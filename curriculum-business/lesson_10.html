📄 Lesson 4.1: Reports, Proposals & Business Documents

Emails move the day; documents move decisions. When you need to present findings, justify a budget, or win approval for a plan, you write a report or a proposal. This lesson gives you the structure, the executive summary, the formal register, and — most importantly — the language of recommendations that actually get approved.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure a business report and a proposal using standard, reader-friendly sections
  • Write a clear, standalone executive summary that a busy decision-maker can act on
  • Use appropriate formal, objective register and a bank of ready-made document phrases
  • Present findings and make persuasive, actionable recommendations tied to your evidence

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Draft a short, structured report or proposal — with an executive summary and prioritised recommendations — on a work-relevant topic.

Reports vs Proposals

These two documents look similar and share much of the same language, but they answer different questions. Knowing which you are writing keeps you focused — and tells the reader what to expect.

  • A report presents information and findings — usually looking backward or analysing the present: what happened, what the data shows, what we learned. It may end with recommendations.
  • A proposal persuades toward a future action or plan: here is what we should do, why it is worth doing, and what it will take. It must sell.
ReportProposal
Core questionWhat happened / what does the evidence show?What should we do next, and why?
OrientationPast / present — analysisFuture — action
Main jobInform (and sometimes advise)Persuade and win approval
ToneObjective, evidence-ledObjective but persuasive
Typical trigger"Give me the findings.""Convince me to fund / approve this."

💡 The overlap is real

The two blur together. A strong report often ends in recommendations (moving toward proposal territory), and a good proposal is grounded in findings (borrowing the report's evidence). The difference is one of emphasis: a report leads with what is; a proposal leads with what should be.

Report & Proposal Structure

Readers of business documents don't read start to finish — they scan, jump, and dip in. Standard structure with clear headings lets them find what they need. Never make a busy reader hunt.

Standard report structure

SectionWhat goes in it
TitleClear, specific subject + author + date
Executive SummaryStandalone recap of purpose, key findings, main recommendations (written last, read first)
IntroductionPurpose, scope (and limits), background, method
Findings / BodyThe evidence, organised by theme, with sub-headings, data, and figures
ConclusionsWhat the findings mean — the interpretation, no new data
RecommendationsSpecific, prioritised actions that follow from the conclusions
AppendicesSupporting detail: full data, tables, references (optional)

Standard proposal structure

SectionWhat goes in it
Background / ProblemThe situation and the need — why this matters now
ObjectivesWhat the plan aims to achieve, ideally measurable
Proposed Solution / PlanWhat you propose to do, concretely
BenefitsThe value and payoff — sell it (link to the reader's goals)
Costs / Resources / TimelineWhat it takes: budget, people, schedule
RisksWhat could go wrong and how you'll manage it (builds trust)
Recommendation / Call to actionThe clear ask: approve, fund, decide by [date]

🔁 Proposals must sell

A proposal is persuasion in document form — everything you learned in Lesson 3.3 about benefits over features applies here. Don't just describe the plan (features); spell out the benefits to this reader's priorities — cost saved, risk reduced, revenue gained, time freed. Facts inform; benefits convince.

The Executive Summary

If you learn one thing from this lesson, learn this: the executive summary is the most-read and most-important part of your document. Decision-makers are busy — many will read only the summary and act on it. Everything else is there to back it up.

⚠️ Written last, read first

You can only summarise a document you've finished, so write the executive summary last — but position it first. It must be standalone: a reader who sees nothing else should still understand the purpose, the key findings, and what you recommend. Don't tease ("this report will discuss…"); state the conclusions.

How to write one

  • Keep it short — a few tight paragraphs, or roughly 5–10% of the document. One page at most.
  • Cover three things: the purpose (why this document exists), the key findings (the headline results), and the main recommendations (what to do).
  • Lead with the answer — put the bottom line up front, not at the end.
  • Use plain language — no jargon a busy senior reader outside your team would stumble over.
  • Make it self-sufficient — no "as discussed above"; it may be read alone.

MODEL — Executive Summary (from a report)

Purpose → This report assesses the causes of rising customer-support wait times in Q2 and recommends a response.
Key findings → Average wait time rose from 4 to 11 minutes, driven by a 38% increase in ticket volume and the loss of two agents. Notably, 60% of tickets concern a single, fixable billing issue.
Recommendations → We recommend three actions: (1) fix the billing error to remove the largest source of tickets; (2) add a self-service FAQ for the top five queries; and (3) hire two support agents by the end of Q3. Together these are projected to return wait times below five minutes within two months.

Notice: it stands alone, states results rather than teasing them, and ends with clear, numbered recommendations. A reader who stops here still knows exactly what to approve.

Formal, Objective Register

Documents sit higher on the formality spectrum than most emails (recall Lesson 1.1). The goal is an impersonal, objective, evidence-based voice — the facts should feel like they speak for themselves, not like your personal opinion.

Email register (often)Document register
I think we lost customers because…The data indicates that customer loss was driven by…
We found out that…It was found that… / The analysis reveals that…
I'm pretty sure this means…This suggests that… / It appears that…
You should hire more people.It is recommended that the team be expanded.
contractions, "I / you", chatty asidesfull forms, impersonal phrasing, neutral tone

💡 Four register habits for documents

1. Be objective — attribute claims to evidence ("The data indicates…", "It was found that…"), not to feelings.
2. Use the passive where it helps — "It was found that…", "The survey was conducted…" keeps the focus on findings, not on who did what.
3. Hedge honest conclusions — "This suggests…", "It appears that…", "The results imply…" are more credible than overclaiming.
4. Signpost and structure — clear headings, short paragraphs, and bullet points make dense content scannable.

⚠️ Clarity beats showing off

Formal does not mean dense, long-winded, or jargon-heavy. The classic document mistake is burying a simple point under complicated words to sound impressive — "utilise", "leverage synergies", "in order to facilitate the operationalisation of…". Busy readers reward clarity. Write to be understood on the first read, not to look clever.

Document Phrase Banks

These are the workhorse phrases of professional documents, grouped by what they do. Keep them handy and adapt them.

Stating the purpose

The aim of this report is to… This report examines / assesses / reviews… This proposal sets out… The purpose of this document is to… This report covers the period… / is based on…

Presenting findings

The results show / indicate that… It was found that… The data reveals a clear trend… Notably, / Significantly, … In contrast, / However, … The majority of respondents…

Referring to data & appendices

As shown in Figure 1, … See Appendix A for full details. The table below summarises… According to the survey, … Full figures are provided in the appendix.

Drawing conclusions

It can be concluded that… These findings suggest that… On balance, … Overall, the evidence points to… It appears that…

Making recommendations

We recommend that… It is recommended that the team be expanded. (subjunctive) The best course of action would be to… We should prioritise… / The team could also consider… In the short term, … ; in the longer term, …

📖 A note on "It is recommended that…"

In formal recommendations the verb after "recommend that", "propose that", or "suggest that" often takes the subjunctive (the base form): "It is recommended that the team be expanded" / "We propose that the deadline be moved." Use should and could to distinguish a firm recommendation ("we should") from an option worth considering ("we could").

Recommendations That Get Approved

The recommendations section is where a document earns its keep. Vague advice ("improve communication", "consider our options") gets nodded at and ignored. Approvable recommendations share five traits:

💡 The five marks of an approvable recommendation

  • Specific — names the actual action, not a vague aspiration. "Add a billing-FAQ page", not "improve support".
  • Actionable — someone could start on Monday. Ideally: who does what, by when.
  • Prioritised — order them, or label short-term vs long-term. Don't hand over an unranked wish-list.
  • Cost/benefit-aware — acknowledge what it takes and what it returns. Decision-makers weigh both.
  • Tied to the findings — every recommendation should trace back to evidence in the body. No surprises.
Vague (ignored)Approvable (specific & actionable)
We should improve customer support.We recommend hiring two support agents by the end of Q3 to bring average wait times below five minutes.
Marketing needs more budget.We propose increasing the digital ad budget by 15% for Q4, targeting the two channels that returned the highest ROI in Q3 (see Appendix B).
Something must be done about turnover.As a priority, we recommend introducing quarterly stay-interviews for at-risk teams; longer term, the review of pay bands could follow in Q1.

Here is the structure of a report and a proposal at a glance:

flowchart TD A["Business document"] --> B["Report (present findings)"] A --> C["Proposal (persuade to act)"] B --> B1["Executive Summary"] B1 --> B2["Introduction (purpose & scope)"] B2 --> B3["Findings / Body"] B3 --> B4["Conclusions"] B4 --> B5["Recommendations"] C --> C1["Background / Problem"] C1 --> C2["Objectives"] C2 --> C3["Proposed Plan"] C3 --> C4["Benefits"] C4 --> C5["Costs, Timeline & Risks"] C5 --> C6["Recommendation / Call to action"]

🧠 Mindset: Clear writing is clear thinking

Nobody is born writing polished reports — every confident business writer once stared at a blank page too. The struggle to make a document clear is really the work of making your thinking clear, and that is a skill you build by doing. Each report you draft, restructure, and tighten trains the muscle. Great communicators are made, not born: your tenth report will be far better than your first, precisely because you wrote the first. Start messy, then shape it.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Order the sections

Objective: Put these report sections into the correct standard order.

  1. Recommendations
  2. Executive Summary
  3. Findings / Body
  4. Introduction
  5. Conclusions
✅ Answer

Correct order: Executive Summary → Introduction → Findings/Body → Conclusions → Recommendations (then Appendices). Remember: the executive summary comes first in the document even though you write it last.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Match the phrase to its function

Objective: Label each phrase — Purpose, Findings, or Recommend.

  1. "It is recommended that the process be automated."
  2. "The aim of this report is to evaluate supplier performance."
  3. "The data indicates a 20% drop in returns."
  4. "The best course of action would be to renegotiate the contract."
  5. "Notably, satisfaction rose fastest among new customers."
✅ Answer

1. Recommend · 2. Purpose · 3. Findings · 4. Recommend · 5. Findings. Notice how the recommend-phrases point to a future action, the purpose-phrase states why the document exists, and the findings-phrases report evidence objectively.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the vague recommendation & tighten the wordy line

Objective: (a) Turn this vague recommendation into a specific, actionable one; (b) tighten this wordy, jargon-heavy passage.

  1. Vague: "The company should try to reduce its costs where possible."
  2. Wordy: "In order to facilitate the operationalisation of enhanced efficiencies going forward, it is our considered view that the utilisation of the new system should perhaps be leveraged."
✅ Answer (models)

1. "We recommend cutting the two lowest-performing ad channels (see Appendix A), saving an estimated €40k in Q4." — specific, actionable, tied to evidence, cost-aware. (Many good versions exist; the test is: could someone act on it Monday?)

2. "We recommend adopting the new system to improve efficiency." — same meaning, half the words, no jargon. Clarity over showing off.

🎯 Your Task: Draft a Structured Report or Proposal

Choose a real, work-relevant topic — e.g. a recurring problem on your team, a tool or process you'd like approved, or findings from something you recently ran.

  1. (5 min) Decide: is this a report (present findings) or a proposal (persuade to act)? Write a clear title and pick the matching structure from this lesson.
  2. (20 min) Draft the body: for a report, write the Introduction, 2–3 themed Findings with at least one piece of evidence each, and Conclusions; for a proposal, write the Problem, Objectives, Plan, Benefits, and Costs/Risks.
  3. (10 min) Write 3 recommendations that pass the five-marks test — specific, actionable, prioritised, cost/benefit-aware, and tied to your findings. Use the recommendation phrase bank.
  4. (10 min) Now write the executive summary last (purpose + key findings + main recommendations), and place it first. Make it standalone.
  5. (5 min) Register pass: remove chatty phrasing and jargon, check headings and bullets, and confirm a busy reader could act on the summary alone.

Options: Do this solo (write and self-review), or swap drafts with a colleague and role-play the decision-maker — read only each other's executive summary and say whether you'd approve it and why.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I chose the right document type and matching structure, with a clear title
  • ☐ My executive summary is standalone and covers purpose, findings, and recommendations
  • ☐ Each finding/claim is backed by evidence or a reference
  • ☐ My recommendations are specific, prioritised, and tied to the findings
  • ☐ The register is formal, objective, and jargon-free — clarity over showing off

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Write the executive summary last and make it stand entirely on its own — assume it's all the reader sees.
  • Structure for scanning — clear headings, short paragraphs, bullets, and one point per section.
  • Tie every recommendation to a finding — no conclusion or recommendation should surprise a reader who read the body.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Density and jargon: the classic document error — long words and complex sentences to sound impressive. Busy readers reward clarity, not effort.
  • Vague recommendations: "improve", "consider", "look into" without an action, owner, or deadline get ignored. Be specific and actionable.
  • A weak or missing executive summary: burying your conclusions on page 6 means the decision-maker never reaches them. Put the answer up front.
  • No structure: a wall of text with no headings forces the reader to hunt. Signpost everything.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A report presents findings (past/present analysis); a proposal persuades toward a future action — and proposals must sell the benefits.
  • Use standard structure with clear headings so busy readers can scan and jump: summary, intro, findings, conclusions, recommendations.
  • The executive summary is the most-read part — standalone, purpose + findings + recommendations, written last and read first.
  • Documents use a formal, objective, evidence-based register — but clarity beats jargon every time.
  • Recommendations that get approved are specific, actionable, prioritised, cost/benefit-aware, and tied to the findings.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How long should a business report or proposal be?

As short as it can be while doing its job — busy readers thank you for brevity. Many workplace documents are 1–4 pages plus appendices. Let the structure, not padding, carry it: if a section adds no decision-relevant information, cut it.

Isn't the passive voice supposed to be bad writing?

In documents it has a real job: "It was found that…" and "The survey was conducted…" keep the focus on findings and lend an objective tone. Use it where it helps objectivity — but don't let it make sentences vague or clumsy. Clarity still wins.

What if I'm not sure whether to include recommendations in a report?

Check what the reader asked for. If they want your judgement, include clear recommendations; if they only want the facts to decide themselves, stop at conclusions. When in doubt, ask — and if you do recommend, keep it a distinct, well-marked section.
