🤝 Lesson 3.2: Negotiations — Language & Strategy

A negotiation isn't a battle to be won — it's a problem two sides solve together. This lesson gives you the stages to move through, the language to propose and bargain, and the strategy to make concessions and close a deal without damaging the relationship. Master the phrase "If you…, we'll…" and you already hold the heart of professional bargaining.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Move confidently through the stages of a negotiation, from opening to close
  • Make, accept, and reject proposals — and counter-propose without causing offence
  • Bargain with conditional language ("If you…, we'll…") and trade concessions strategically
  • Handle deadlock and close a deal professionally, protecting the relationship for next time

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Role-play a short negotiation (price, deadline, or terms) using the stages and conditional bargaining language, aiming for a genuine win–win.

Mindset & the Stages of a Negotiation

Weak negotiators haggle over positions — the thing each side says it wants ("I want 20% off"). Strong negotiators dig for interests — the why behind the position ("I need to hit a budget number my boss set"). Once you know the interest, you can often satisfy it in a way you both prefer. That is the road to a win–win: a deal where both sides leave with enough to say yes and enough goodwill to work together again.

🧠 Mindset: negotiate the problem, not the person

Great negotiators aren't born tough — they're prepared and curious. Their default question isn't "How do I win?" but "What does the other side really need, and how do we both get enough?" You are almost never negotiating a one-off transaction; you're negotiating the next email, the next contract, the next reference. Aim for mutual gain and protect the relationship — a deal that leaves the other side resentful is a deal you'll pay for later.

📖 Key terms

Position = what a party says it wants (the demand on the table).
Interest = why they want it (the underlying need or fear) — the key to unlocking a win–win.
Concession = something you give up to move the deal forward.
BATNA = your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — your walk-away plan if no deal is reached.

Almost every negotiation moves through the same five stages. Knowing where you are keeps you calm and stops you from proposing before you've explored:

Opening & Exploring Interests

The opening sets the temperature of the whole conversation. Start warm and collaborative — you're signalling "we're solving this together," not "prepare to fight." Then spend real time exploring: the side that asks the best questions usually gets the best deal, because they find the interests everyone else missed.

Setting a positive tone

Thanks for making the time — I'm confident we can find something that works for both of us. Shall we start by agreeing what we'd both like to get out of today? I'm hoping we can reach an outcome we're both happy with.

Stating your aims

What we're hoping to achieve is… Our main priority here is… Ideally, we'd like to… The key thing for us is…

Probing for interests (the money questions)

What's most important to you here? What's driving that requirement? Can you help me understand why that matters? If we could solve X, would that change things? What would an ideal outcome look like for you?

Signalling flexibility (without committing yet)

We might be able to… In principle, we'd be open to… There may be some room on that, depending on… That could be possible if a few other things fell into place.

💡 "Signalling" is how you hint before you commit

Words like "might," "in principle," "there may be some room" tell the other side a door could open, without promising anything. Listen for their signals too — "that would be difficult" usually means "not impossible," and is an invitation to ask "what would make it easier?"

Proposing, Accepting & Rejecting

Once interests are on the table, you move to proposals. Keep them clear and, wherever possible, conditional (more on that next). When you reject, reject the proposal, never the person — and always leave a way forward.

Making a proposal

We'd be prepared to… if… How about we…? Our proposal would be… Would you consider…? What if we were to…?

Accepting

That works for us. We can live with that. Yes, I think we have a deal. That sounds fair — let's do it.

Rejecting — without causing offence

I'm afraid that doesn't quite work for us, because… That's a little more than we can do at the moment. I don't think we can go that far, unfortunately. I understand where you're coming from, but…

Counter-proposing (reject, then redirect)

What we could offer instead is… We can't do X, but we could do Y. Let me suggest an alternative… Could we meet in the middle at…?

⚠️ The classic mistake: a flat "no"

A bare "No" or "That's impossible" slams the door and can turn a discussion into a stand-off. Always pair a rejection with a reason and a route forward: "That's a little more than we can do, because our margin is tight — but what we could offer instead is…" You've said no to the number while keeping the relationship, and the deal, alive.

Conditional Bargaining — the Grammatical Heart

This is the single most useful skill in the lesson. In a real negotiation you should almost never give something for nothing. Instead, you trade: every time you offer a concession, you attach a condition. The grammar that does this is the conditional — the "If you…, (then) we'll…" sentence.

⚠️ Golden rule: never give something for nothing

The moment you give a discount, a faster deadline, or an extra service for free, you've taught the other side that pushing works — and they'll push again. Wrap every give in an "if": "If you can do X, we'll do Y." Now every concession you make buys you something in return.

First conditional — real, on-the-table offers

Use the first conditional (If + present, … will / can) for offers you're genuinely prepared to make right now:

If you can commit to a two-year contract, we can offer a 10% discount. If you pay within 14 days, we'll waive the setup fee. We'll include training provided that you order all five licences. As long as delivery stays flexible, we can hold the price.

Second conditional — hypothetical, testing-the-water offers

Use the second conditional (If + past, … would) to float an idea without committing — to test what they'd accept:

If you were to double the order, what sort of discount would you expect? We'd be willing to move on price if you could bring the deadline forward. If we absorbed the shipping, would that change your position? Supposing we extended the warranty — would that help?

💡 First vs second conditional: what your listener hears

"If you sign today, we will discount" = a firm, real offer you can accept now. "If you signed today, we would discount" = a softer, more hypothetical feeler — useful for exploring without locking yourself in. Both make the trade explicit. Use the second conditional early (to explore) and the first conditional late (to close).

The Strategy of Concessions

Concessions are the currency of a negotiation — how you spend them matters as much as what you spend. The pros follow a simple discipline:

  • Small — move in small steps; a big early jump signals you had lots of room
  • Slow — concede gradually; each concession should feel earned
  • Conditional — always attach an "if"; never give for free
  • Labelled — name the value of what you give so it's appreciated
  • Reciprocal — ask for something back every single time
  • Reserved — keep one variable in your pocket to trade at the close

💡 "Label" your concessions so they're valued

A concession the other side doesn't notice is money wasted. Say out loud how much it costs you: "That's a big concession for us," "This is well beyond what we'd normally do," "I've had to push hard internally to offer this." Then immediately ask for the return: "…so in exchange, could you…?" Labelling turns a quiet giveaway into a chip you can cash in.

Concession phrase bank

That's a big concession for us… I can move a little on that, but I'll need something in return. We don't usually do this, but… If we do that for you, could you do this for us? I could stretch to X, but that really is our limit.

Asking for a concession

Is there any flexibility on…? What would it take to get to…? Could you do a little better on the price? If that's your position, is there something else you can give me?

Deadlock & Difficult Moments

Sometimes you get stuck. Deadlock isn't the end of the deal — it's a signal to change something: the pace, the scope, or the room. Stay calm; a negotiator who loses their temper loses their leverage.

Buying time

Let me take that back to my team and come back to you. Can we take a short break and pick this up in ten minutes? I'd like to think that over before I answer. Let me check what's possible and get back to you tomorrow.

Widening the pie (find new value)

Are there other areas where we could add value? If price is fixed, could we look at delivery, support, or terms instead? Let's step back — what else matters to you besides the number? Maybe there's a bigger package that works better for us both.

Staying calm & keeping it professional

I understand this is important to you — it is to us too. Let's not let one point derail the whole deal. We're closer than it feels — let's find the last step.

💡 Know your walk-away point (BATNA)

Your BATNA — your best alternative if this deal falls through — is your quiet source of confidence. If you know you have another supplier, another buyer, or the option to do nothing, you can hold your line calmly and walk away from a bad deal without panic. The side with the stronger alternative has the stronger hand. Work out your BATNA before you sit down, and never accept a deal that's worse than it.

Closing & Confirming

A deal isn't done until it's summarised, confirmed, and written down. Closing badly — leaving terms fuzzy — is how "agreements" fall apart a week later. Finish clean, and finish warm.

Summarising the agreement

So, to confirm what we've agreed… Let me just recap the key points. Are we agreed on all of that? Have I understood everything correctly?

Next steps & putting it in writing

I'll send over the details in an email this afternoon. I'll draft the contract and get it to you by Friday. Shall we say the same time next week to sign off? Once you've confirmed by email, we'll get started.

Preserving goodwill

It's been a pleasure doing business with you. Thanks for working with us on this — I think it's a good outcome for both sides. I'm glad we found something that works for everyone. I'm looking forward to working together.

Model confirmation email (send straight after the meeting):

Subject: Confirming our agreement — Q3 supply terms

Hi Marco,

Thanks for a productive conversation today. Just to confirm what we agreed:
• 10% discount on the standard rate, in return for a two-year commitment.
• Payment within 14 days, with the setup fee waived.
• First delivery in the week of 6 October.

I'll send the contract over by Friday for your review. Please let me know if I've captured anything incorrectly.

It's been a pleasure — I'm looking forward to working together.

Best regards,
Ana

A Negotiation in Action

Watch the stages, the interest-probing, the conditional trade, the labelled concession, and the clean close all come together in one short exchange:

Buyer: Thanks for coming in. I'll be honest — your quote is about 15% over our budget. We really like the product, but that number is a problem.

Seller: I appreciate you being straight with me. Can I ask — is it the total price that's the issue, or the cost this quarter specifically?

Buyer: Honestly, it's this quarter. My budget resets in January, so a big hit now is hard to justify.

Seller: That's really helpful to know. So if we could ease the pressure this quarter, the overall figure is less of a concern?

Buyer: Exactly.

Seller: Then here's what I could suggest. If you commit to the full order today, we could split the payment — 40% now and 60% in January. Would that work?

Buyer: That helps a lot. But I'd still need the headline price to come down a little for my director.

Seller: I can move, but I'll need something in return. If you can give us a testimonial and a reference call, I could offer 5% off. That's genuinely at the edge of what I'm able to do.

Buyer: A testimonial we can do easily. Let's say 5% off, split payments, and you'll have the reference.

Seller: Then I think we have a deal. So, to confirm: full order today, 5% discount, 40/60 payment split, and a testimonial plus a reference call from your side. Have I got that right?

Buyer: That's it.

Seller: Wonderful. I'll send the details over by email this afternoon. It's been a real pleasure — I'm looking forward to working together.

🔍 What just happened

The seller probed for the interest ("is it the total, or this quarter?") and discovered the real problem was timing, not price. That unlocked a solution — split payments — that cost little but solved a lot. When a discount was still needed, the seller never gave it for free: it was traded for a testimonial and reference ("If you can…, I could…"), and labelled ("at the edge of what I'm able to do"). Then a clean summary close and a warm sign-off. Win–win.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match the phrase to its function

Objective: Label each phrase as Propose, Reject, Concede, or Close.

  1. "So, to confirm what we've agreed…"
  2. "How about we split the difference?"
  3. "That's a little more than we can do, I'm afraid."
  4. "I can move a little, but I'll need something in return."
  5. "I'll send over the details in an email."
  6. "What if we were to extend the warranty instead?"
✅ Answer

1. Close (summarising) · 2. Propose · 3. Reject · 4. Concede (conditional) · 5. Close (next steps / in writing) · 6. Propose (counter-proposal).

🏋️ Exercise 2: Turn giveaways into conditional trades

Objective: Each line gives something away for free. Rewrite it as a conditional trade ("If you…, we'll…") that asks for something in return.

  1. "Okay, we'll give you a 10% discount."
  2. "Fine, we'll deliver it a week earlier."
  3. "Sure, we can throw in the training for free."
✅ Answer (models — many good versions)

1. "If you can commit to a two-year contract, we'll give you a 10% discount." · 2. "We can deliver a week earlier as long as you can be flexible on the final quantity." · 3. "We'd be happy to include the training provided that you order all five licences today." The key move in each: attach an "if," and make sure you get something back.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Put the negotiation in order & turn positions into questions

Part A — Order these five moves into a logical negotiation sequence:

  1. "So to confirm, we're agreed on the discount and the timeline."
  2. "Thanks for coming in — I'm sure we can find something that works for us both."
  3. "If you can commit today, we can offer 8% off."
  4. "Can I ask what's most important to you in this deal?"
  5. "That's a little high for us — could you do better on the price?"

Part B — Turn each position into an interest-probing question:

  1. Position: "We need the price down by 15%."
  2. Position: "The deadline has to be the 1st, no later."
✅ Answer

Part A order: 2 (open) → 4 (explore interests) → 5 (react / reject & push) → 3 (propose conditionally) → 1 (close).
Part B (models): 1. "Can you help me understand what's driving the 15% — is it a fixed budget, or a comparison with another quote?" · 2. "What's making the 1st so important — is it a launch date, or something further down the line we could work around?" You're digging for the why so you can solve the real need.

🎯 Your Task: Role-Play a Win–Win Negotiation

  1. (5 min) Choose a scenario and a variable to negotiate — e.g. a supplier and buyer on price, a project deadline, or contract terms (payment, volume, support). Decide your ideal, your limit, one or two variables you can trade, and your BATNA.
  2. (3 min) Write one interest-probing question and two conditional trades ("If you…, we'll…") you could offer.
  3. (8 min) Run the negotiation — with a partner, or solo by voicing both sides — through all five stages: open → explore → propose & bargain → concede conditionally → close.
  4. (2 min) Close properly: summarise the agreement aloud, confirm it, and state you'll put it in writing with a warm sign-off.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I opened warmly and set a collaborative tone
  • ☐ I asked at least one question to find the interest behind a position
  • ☐ Every concession I made was conditional — nothing given for free
  • ☐ I labelled a concession and asked for something in return
  • ☐ I closed by summarising, confirming, and promising it in writing — with goodwill intact

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Explore before you propose — find the interest behind the position first.
  • Trade, don't give — attach an "if" to every concession, and ask for something back.
  • Concede small, slow, and labelled — and keep one variable in reserve for the close.
  • Close in writing — summarise, confirm, and follow up with an email.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Giving something for nothing: free concessions signal weakness and invite more pushing. Always wrap a give in an "if."
  • Arguing positions, not interests: haggling over the number when the real issue was timing (or risk, or reputation) means you'll never find the win–win.
  • Winning the deal but losing the relationship: crushing the other side feels good once — but you'll pay for the resentment on the next contract. Protect the relationship.
  • Leaving it vague: "we're basically agreed" isn't an agreement. Summarise and put it in writing before you leave the room.

💡 Culture note: negotiation styles vary hugely

How people negotiate differs sharply across cultures and companies: directness (blunt vs. indirect), haggling norms (is bargaining expected or slightly rude?), relationship-first vs. deal-first (do we build trust before terms, or get straight to numbers?), pace (fast and transactional vs. slow and deliberate), and who decides (the person in the room, or someone senior who isn't). Don't assume your normal style travels. Observe your counterpart, mirror their pace, and when unsure, ask. As always in this course: read the room.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Negotiate interests, not positions — the why behind the demand is where win–win solutions live.
  • Move through the five stages: prepare → open → explore → propose & bargain → close.
  • Never give something for nothing — trade with conditionals ("If you…, we'll…") and ask for a return every time.
  • Concede small, slow, conditional, and labelled; know your BATNA; close by summarising and putting it in writing — with the relationship intact.

That's the toolkit professional negotiators use every day. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't "win–win" just a soft way of giving too much away?

No. Win–win means both sides get enough to say yes — it doesn't mean you go easy. You still hold your line, know your BATNA, and trade every concession. The point is that a deal the other side is happy with is one they'll honour and repeat, whereas a deal you "won" by crushing them tends to unravel or poison the next negotiation.

What if I genuinely have nothing to trade?

You almost always have something beyond price: timing, volume, contract length, payment terms, a testimonial, a reference, flexibility on scope, speed of decision. "Widening the pie" is exactly this search — when one variable is stuck, put others on the table so there's something to exchange.

The other side is much more powerful than me. Can I still negotiate well?

Yes. Preparation and a clear BATNA level the field more than size does. Know your walk-away point, focus on their interests (even powerful players have needs and constraints), stay calm, and never accept a deal that's worse than your best alternative. Confidence comes from options, not from position.
