👥 Lesson 2.1: Meetings — Participating & Chairing

Meetings are where a lot of real work gets decided — and where good communicators stand out. This lesson gives you the practical language to participate with confidence (share an opinion, agree, disagree politely, ask for clarification), to interrupt and take your turn without being rude, and to chair a meeting professionally: opening it, working through an agenda, keeping to time, and closing with clear action points.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Participate actively — give opinions, agree and disagree politely, and ask for clarification
  • Interrupt and manage turn-taking — come in, hold the floor, and bring others in without being rude
  • Chair a meeting — open it, follow the agenda, keep to time, and close with action points
  • Understand core meeting documents — the agenda, minutes, and action points (who does what by when)

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Plan and role-play a short meeting — one person chairs from an agenda, the others participate — then write up the action points.

Meeting Roles & Documents

Every effective meeting has two roles and three documents. Knowing the vocabulary makes you sound like an insider from your first meeting.

The chair (or chairperson) runs the meeting: they open it, guide the discussion through the agenda, manage time and turn-taking, and close with clear decisions. The participants (or attendees) contribute — sharing information, opinions, and decisions. Sometimes someone is asked to take the minutes (write the record).

DocumentWhat it isWhen
AgendaThe list of topics to be discussed, usually in order, sometimes with timesSent before the meeting
MinutesThe written record of what was said and, above all, what was decidedWritten during / just after
Action pointsWho does what by when — the concrete follow-upsAgreed at the end; tracked afterwards

💡 The one rule that makes meetings useful

A meeting without action points is often a meeting that didn't need to happen. The best question to ask before you close is simple: "So, who's doing what, and by when?" Everything in this lesson builds toward being able to answer that clearly.

Participating: The Phrase Banks

You don't need clever language to contribute well — you need reliable, ready-made phrases for each thing you want to do. Here are the core functions, grouped so you can grab them fast. (Notice how they carry the register and tone we set up in Lesson 1.1 — polite, clear, never blunt. Diplomatic disagreement gets a whole lesson of its own in Lesson 2.3.)

Giving your opinion

In my view, … It seems to me that… I'd suggest (that) we… From my perspective, … I think we should… If you ask me, …

Agreeing

Absolutely. That's a good point. I'm with you on that. Exactly — that's my thinking too. You're right. I completely agree.

Disagreeing politely

I see your point, but… I'm not sure I agree. Have we considered…? I take your point, though I'd add… I'm not convinced that… Could I offer a different view?

Asking for clarification

Could you clarify what you mean by…? Sorry, could you expand on that? Just so I'm clear, are you saying…? Could you give us an example? What exactly do you mean by that?

Making & responding to suggestions

Why don't we…? How about…? Shall we…? What if we…? That could work. I like that idea.

Checking understanding

So, what you're saying is…? If I've understood correctly, … Let me make sure I've got this right… So the plan is to…, is that right?

✅ A tiny trick that sounds professional

When you disagree, acknowledge first, then differ: "I see your point, but…" or "That's fair, though…". Leading with agreement keeps the room warm and makes your objection easy to hear. A flat "No, that's wrong" does the opposite.

Interrupting & Turn-Taking

This is one of the hardest skills for many professionals — especially in a second language, and especially in fast-moving or online meetings. Say nothing and your ideas never land; jump in badly and you seem rude. The fix is a small set of polite "signal phrases" that ask for the floor before you take it.

⚠️ Interrupting is a skill, not a sin

In busy meetings you will have to interrupt to be heard — that's normal and expected. The trick is to flag it first ("Sorry to interrupt, but…") rather than simply talking over someone. A short, polite signal makes the interruption feel like teamwork, not rudeness.

What you want to doPhrases to use
Come in / interrupt politely"Sorry to interrupt, but…" · "Can I just come in here?" · "If I could add something…" · "May I jump in?"
Build on what someone said"Just to build on that…" · "To add to Ana's point…" · "Following on from that, …"
Hold the floor (finish your point)"Let me just finish my point." · "If I could just finish…" · "Give me one second and I'll come to that."
Bring others in"What do you think, Sara?" · "Ana, you've done this before — any thoughts?" · "I'd love to hear from the design side."

💡 Online meetings need this most

On video calls there's a split-second delay, so people talk over each other easily. A quick "Can I just come in here?" — or even physically raising a hand or using the "raise hand" button — prevents the awkward "no, you go / no, you go" loop. And bringing quieter people in ("What do you think, Sara?") is one of the most valued things you can do in any meeting.

Chairing a Meeting

Chairing sounds intimidating, but it's mostly a predictable sequence of stages, each with its own handful of phrases. Learn the stages and the phrases, and you can run a tidy, on-time meeting even in a second language.

StageThe chair's job
1. OpenWelcome people, state the purpose, get started
2. Set / follow the agendaIntroduce each item, move between items, park tangents
3. Manage participation & timeInvite contributions, keep discussion balanced, watch the clock
4. Decide & assign actionSummarise, confirm agreement, record who does what by when
5. CloseRecap action points, thank everyone, end on time

1. Opening the meeting

Thanks everyone for coming. Shall we get started? The purpose of today's meeting is… We've got about 30 minutes, so let's dive in.

2. Setting & following the agenda

First on the agenda is… Let's move on to… Let's come back to that later. The next item is… That's item one covered.

3. Managing participation & time

Let's hear from Tom on this. We're running short on time. Let's park that and take it offline. Can we keep this brief? Let's give everyone a chance to speak.

4. Reaching decisions & assigning action

So, to summarise, … Are we all agreed? The action point is… Can I confirm — you'll handle this by Friday? Let's make that a decision, then.

5. Closing the meeting

Let's wrap up. To recap the action points, … Thanks everyone, that was productive. I'll send the minutes round this afternoon.

Here's the whole shape of a well-run meeting at a glance:

flowchart LR
    A["Open (welcome, purpose)"] --> B["Follow the agenda"]
    B --> C["Discuss each item"]
    C --> D["Decide / agree"]
    D --> E["Assign action points"]
    E --> F["Close (recap, thanks)"]
    C -.->|"tangent?"| G["Park it / take offline"]
    G -.-> B

And here is the skeleton a chair works from — an agenda that becomes an action list. Copy this format for your Task:

AGENDA — Weekly Project Sync — Tue 10:00 (30 min)

1. Launch timeline — are we on track? (10 min)
2. Website copy — sign-off needed (10 min)
3. Budget update (5 min)
4. Any other business (5 min)

— — — after the meeting — — —

ACTION POINTS
☐ Tom — confirm the launch date with the client — by Thu
☐ Ana — finalise the website copy and send for sign-off — by Fri
☐ Priya — circulate the updated budget — by Mon

🧠 Mindset: chairing is served, not seized

New chairs often think their job is to talk the most. It's the opposite: a great chair talks least on the content and most on the process — opening, guiding, inviting others in, summarising, and closing on time. You're the host of the conversation, not its star. That's a learnable role, and every meeting you run makes you better at it. Great communicators are made, not born — and chairing is where it shows.

A Meeting in Action

Watch the language work together — the chair opening, a participant giving an opinion, a polite interruption, a diplomatic disagreement, and an action point being set.

Chair: Thanks everyone for coming. We've got half an hour, so let's get started. First on the agenda is the launch timeline. Ana, where are we?

Ana: In my view, we're on track for the 15th, but it's tight. I'd suggest we lock the website copy this week so design has time.

Tom: Sorry to interrupt, but — can I just come in here? I see your point, Ana, but I'm not sure the copy will be signed off that fast. Have we considered pushing the date by a few days?

Ana: That's a fair point. Maybe the 18th is safer.

Chair: Okay — let's park the exact date for a second. So, to summarise: copy locked this week, and we'll confirm the 15th or 18th once the client replies. Tom, can I make that your action point — confirm the date with the client by Thursday?

Tom: Yes, I'll do that.

Chair: Great. Let's move on to the website copy. We're a little short on time, so let's keep it brief.

🌍 A note on meeting culture

Meeting norms differ a lot between countries and companies: how directly people disagree, whether junior staff are expected to speak up (or wait to be asked), how strictly punctuality is treated, and how much small talk happens before "real" business. In some cultures interrupting shows engagement; in others it's disruptive. When you join a new team or work internationally, observe the room first and adapt — and if you're the chair, actively invite quieter voices, because silence doesn't always mean agreement.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match the phrase to its function

Objective: Label each phrase — opinion, agree, interrupt, clarify, or chair.

  1. "Sorry to interrupt, but can I just come in here?"
  2. "Let's move on to the next item."
  3. "It seems to me that we should wait."
  4. "Could you clarify what you mean by 'soon'?"
  5. "Absolutely — I'm with you on that."
✅ Answer

1. Interrupt · 2. Chair (following the agenda) · 3. Opinion · 4. Clarify · 5. Agree.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Put the meeting flow in order

Objective: These chair moves are jumbled. Put them in the natural order of a meeting.

  1. "To recap the action points, Tom will confirm the date by Thursday…"
  2. "Thanks everyone for coming — shall we get started?"
  3. "First on the agenda is the launch timeline."
  4. "So, are we all agreed on the 18th?"
  5. "Let's move on to the website copy."
✅ Answer

Correct order: 2 (open) → 3 (first agenda item) → 5 (move to next item) → 4 (reach a decision) → 1 (close with action points). Open → agenda → discuss/move → decide → close.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Make it polite

Objective: Rewrite each blunt interruption or disagreement so it's professional and courteous.

  1. "Stop — I want to say something."
  2. "No, that's wrong."
  3. "Your idea is bad. We should do it differently."
✅ Answer (models)

1. "Sorry to interrupt — can I just come in here?" · 2. "I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree — could we look at it again?" · 3. "I take your point, though have we considered a different approach? What if we…?" Many good versions exist — the test is: did you acknowledge before differing, and add a softener?

🎯 Your Task: Plan & Role-Play a Short Meeting

Do this with two or three colleagues (best), or plan and script it solo (write both the chair's lines and the participants').

  1. (8 min) Write a short agenda — a realistic 15-minute meeting with 3 items (e.g. a project update, a decision to make, and "any other business"). Use the agenda skeleton from this lesson.
  2. (2 min) Assign roles: one chair, the rest participants. Decide who takes the minutes.
  3. (10 min) Run the meeting. The chair opens, moves through the agenda, and closes. Participants must each: give an opinion, agree or disagree politely at least once, and interrupt politely at least once.
  4. (5 min) Write up the action points — who does what by when — in the format from the model. Aim for at least two.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ We had a written agenda with 3 items
  • ☐ The chair opened with a purpose and closed with a recap
  • ☐ Someone interrupted politely using a signal phrase
  • ☐ At least one disagreement was handled diplomatically (acknowledge, then differ)
  • ☐ We ended with clear action points: who, what, by when

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Signal before you interrupt — "Can I just come in here?" turns a rude cut-off into a polite request.
  • Acknowledge, then disagree — "I see your point, but…" keeps the room warm.
  • If you're chairing, talk least on content, most on process — and always close with action points.
  • Bring quieter people in — "What do you think, Sara?" is one of the most valued moves in any meeting.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Talking over people with no signal: even a good point sounds rude if you barge in. Flag it first.
  • Blunt disagreement: "No, you're wrong" shuts the room down. Soften and acknowledge — full diplomacy skills come in Lesson 2.3.
  • Meetings with no action points: a great discussion that ends with no "who does what by when" often changes nothing. Always close the loop.
  • Chairing by dominating: if the chair does most of the talking, the meeting fails. Guide, don't perform.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Meetings have two roles — chair and participants — and three documents: the agenda, the minutes, and the action points.
  • Participate with ready-made phrase banks: give opinions, agree, disagree politely (acknowledge, then differ), ask for clarification, suggest, and check understanding.
  • Interrupt and take your turn by signalling first — "Can I just come in here?" — and bring quieter people in.
  • Chair through five stages: open → follow the agenda → manage time & participation → decide → close with action points (who / what / by when).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You now have the language to hold your own in a business meeting — to contribute an opinion, agree and disagree gracefully, and come in without stepping on anyone. And you can run one: open it, steer it through an agenda, keep it on time, and close it with clear action points. That's a genuinely high-value professional skill, and everything from here — negotiations, presentations, difficult conversations — draws on it. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't it rude to interrupt at all?

Not when you signal it. In busy meetings — especially online — you often have to come in to be heard, and a quick "Sorry to interrupt, but…" makes it feel like collaboration rather than rudeness. Silence just means your ideas never get on the table.

What if I'm asked to chair and I'm nervous about my English?

Lean on the stages and phrases. Chairing is a predictable sequence — open, follow the agenda, manage time, decide, close — and each stage has a small set of fixed phrases. You don't need fancy language; you need to keep the meeting moving and close with action points.

How do I disagree with a senior person without seeming difficult?

Acknowledge first, then offer your view as a question: "I take your point — have we considered…?" or "Could I offer a different view?" This keeps the register respectful. We go deep on diplomatic, tactful language in Lesson 2.3.
