Marking principle

Most Module 2 exercises test function, register and tone, so there is usually more than one good answer. Accept any answer that (1) does the job (disagrees, refuses, gives feedback…), (2) is appropriate in register for the reader or listener, (3) keeps the real message (firm on substance), and (4) is grammatically accurate enough to be clear. The models below show one good version; the "Also accept" notes list common alternatives. Reject answers that are only polite on the surface but lose the message ("Maybe, if it's OK, perhaps we could possibly…"), and answers that are clear but blunt. British spellings (summarise, organise, practise) and British phrases (send the minutes round) are fine. When you correct, praise the function first ("Good: you acknowledged first"), then fix the language.

2.1 · Meetings — Participating & Chairing (C5)

Exercise 1 — The weekly sync (Script 2.1-A)

Exercise 2 — What does each phrase do?
1T (interrupt)5Cl9Cl (check understanding)
2D6A10Ch (manage participation)
3O7T (hold the floor)11D
4Ch (follow the agenda)8O12A

Discuss: 11 starts like agreement ("I take your point") but it is a polite disagreement: the acknowledgement is the softener. 10 could also be used by a participant, but it is typical chair language. Expect: 7 labeled as an interruption (it is the opposite: the speaker is protecting their turn).

Exercise 3 — Suggestions and common mistakes
  1. ask
  2. using
  3. move (I'd suggest that we moved = wrong)
  4. take
  5. share or shared (both correct; shared sounds a little more tentative)
  6. postponing (also accept: I suggest that we postpone; not to postpone)
  7. We need to discuss the budget. (or: We need to talk about the budget.)
  8. I agree with Tom.
  9. Can we have / hold a meeting on Friday? (also: Can we meet on Friday?)
Exercise 4 — Put the chair's lines in order

1 b · 2 d · 3 f · 4 h · 5 e · 6 c · 7 a · 8 g. Open (welcome → purpose) → first item → bring someone in → decide → next item → recap action points → thank and close. Accept d and b reversed only if the learner can explain it (some chairs state the purpose first), but the welcome usually comes first. h must come after f and before the decision in e.

Exercise 5 — Make it polite (models)
  1. "Sorry to interrupt, but can I just come in here?" / "If I could just add something…"
  2. "I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree. Could we look at it again?"
  3. "Sorry, could you expand on that?" / "Could you clarify what you mean by…?"
  4. "I take your point, though have we considered a different approach? What if we…?"
  5. "Let's give everyone a chance to speak. Sara, what do you think?" / "Thanks, Marco. Let's hear from Tom on this."

Test for every answer: did it acknowledge or signal first, and does it still do the job? For 5, the chair should never comment on the person ("you're talking too much"); they manage the process (invite others in by name). Expect: "Excuse me, I want to say something" (better, but want is still direct: suggest Could I just…) and over-softened answers for 2 ("Sorry, sorry, maybe I'm wrong, but perhaps…").

Meeting planner — what a good result looks like

An agenda with three items (the third is usually AOB) and realistic times; a meeting that follows it; and at least two action points in the who / what / when format, each with a verb and a deadline ("Ana will book the venue by May 30"). For the Set B scenarios, possible compromises: 1 move on June 7–8 with each team packing its own area, and the design team packing after its June 10 deadline for the last items · 2 keep the $12,000 stand, add online booking and ask for two quotes by Friday · 3 a Saturday rota (maximum two per driver) plus a customer email with holiday dates · 4 one in-person day plus a short online follow-up session on the new ordering software. Any reasonable decision is fine: the task is the process and the action points, not the "right" answer.

Self-check
Q1
A colleague is speaking, but you have an urgent point. Most professional way to come in?
A) "Sorry to interrupt, but can I just come in here?"
B) "Stop, I want to say something."
C) Say nothing and hope to raise it later.
Why: it signals the interruption before taking the floor, so it feels like teamwork. B is a command; C means the urgent point never lands.
Q2
Which one is a complete action point?
A) "We should look at the budget."
B) "Priya — circulate the updated budget — by Friday."
C) "Budget: discussed."
Why: B has who, what and by when. A has no person and no deadline; C records a discussion, not an action.
Q3
Which phrase does the chair use to close the meeting?
A) "First on the agenda is…"
B) "To recap the action points, …"
C) "Could you expand on that?"
Why: recapping the action points is the chair's closing job. A opens the first item; C is a participant's clarification question.
Q4 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) I suggest to wait until Monday.
B) How about to wait until Monday?
C) I'd suggest we wait until Monday.
Why: suggest (that) we + base verb. Correct versions of A and B: I suggest waiting… · How about waiting…?
Errors to expect in 2.1
  • I am agree · Are you agree? → I agree · Do you agree?
  • discuss about · make a meeting · assist a meeting → discuss · have / hold a meeting · attend a meeting
  • I suggest to… · How about to…? → I suggest -ing / that we + verb · How about -ing?
  • Action points without a person or a deadline ("Check the photos.")
  • Correct words with flat, fast intonation that sounds impatient: drill again, contrasting flat and polite.

Exercise 2 — Open it up (models)
  1. What are you enjoying most (about the conference) so far? / How are you finding the conference?
  2. What do you enjoy most about your job / role? / How did you get into your line of work?
  3. How was your trip in? / How did you get here today?
  4. What did you think of the keynote?
  5. What are you working on at the moment?
  6. How do you know the host?

Also accept any How / What / Which / Why question with correct word order. Expect: "What you think of the keynote?" (missing auxiliary did), "How was your journey?" (fine: British journey = American trip).

Exercise 3 — Safe or risky?

Safe: 1 the event · 2 food and coffee · 4 how they got into their field · 6 weekend plans · 9 their trip. Risky: 3 earnings · 5 religion · 7 age · 8 gossip about a competitor · 10 health. "It depends" (discussion, no single answer): family (warm and normal in some cultures, too personal in others: wait for them to mention it); sport (usually safe, but some rivalries are strong); the weather (always safe, but a dead end unless you follow up). The key strategy: start safe, watch the response, follow their lead.

Exercise 4 — Rescue the dead-end reply (models)
  1. "I'm an accountant — I mostly work with small businesses, which keeps it interesting. How about you?"
  2. "Not really, just an hour on the train, so not bad. Are you local, or did you travel in?"
  3. "Really well, thanks — the session on remote teams was great. What's been your highlight?"
  4. "We worked together at a design agency a few years ago. How about you? How do you know her?"

Each answer should add free information and hand a question back. Role-card details are fine. Expect: free information that is too personal too soon (salary, family problems): praise the idea, suggest a work or event detail instead.

Exercise 5 — The graceful exit
1✗ honest but rude: it insults the other person4✓ warm, and turns the exit into a connection
2✓ warm + light reason + stay in touch5✓ light, face-saving reason + warm close
3✗ the silent vanish feels like a snub6✗ too abrupt and cold: no thanks, no warmth
The model follow-up email — label the parts

Paragraph 1 = A (reminder: where they met + something specific: career changes and fresh-food deliveries) · paragraph 2 = B (keeps the promise: "As promised, I've attached…") · paragraph 3 = C (a light next step: a short call, "No pressure at all") · the sign-off = D (name + role + company). Point out: it was sent the next day (within 48 hours), and it is neutral and warm, not formal.

Networking toolkit and follow-up email — model

Toolkit example: opener "Have you been to one of these before?" · topics: the sessions, the trip in, weekend plans · introduction "Hi, I'm Rosa. I lead a small team at Tidewater Packaging, and we're planning our training day, so I'm here for ideas!" · exit "I won't keep you. It was really good to meet you. Let's stay in touch."

Model follow-up (about 75 words): Subject: Great to meet you this morning · Hi Daniel, It was great to meet you at the Riverside Business Breakfast today, and congratulations again on your first half marathon! I really enjoyed hearing how you put together the monthly booking reports. I'd love to hear more about the tool you use: would you have time for a quick coffee or a call next week? No pressure at all. Best regards, Rosa Delgado, Team Leader, Tidewater Packaging

Check: reminder of where they met · one specific reference · a light, low-pressure next step · a neutral, warm register. Expect: "I am writing to inform you that I met you" (too formal), "Dear Mr. Daniel" (use Hi Daniel or Dear Mr. Novak), no specific detail ("Nice to meet you. Let's keep in touch." only), or a hard sell in the first message.

Self-check
Q1
Which opener invites a real answer?
A) "Are you enjoying it?"
B) "What's been the most useful session for you so far?"
C) "Nice weather."
Why: an open What question needs a full answer and gives you something to follow up. A is closed ("Yes."); C is a statement that is hard to continue.
Q2
Which topic is the safest with someone you have just met?
A) their salary
B) the event and the venue
C) whether they are married and have children
Why: the event is shared, neutral ground. Money is risky in most workplace cultures; family status is normal in some cultures but intrusive in many, so wait for them to raise it.
Q3 · new
"What do you do?" Which answer builds rapport best?
A) "I'm in logistics."
B) "I'm in logistics — I actually moved over from teaching last year, so it's all quite new. How about you?"
C) "Why do you ask?"
Why: answer + free information + question back. A is a dead end; C sounds suspicious.
Q4 · new
When is the best time to send a follow-up message?
A) within 24–48 hours
B) after about a month
C) only if they contact you first
Why: while you are still fresh in their memory. After a month, the connection has usually faded.
Errors to expect in 2.2
  • Missing auxiliaries in questions: What you think…? · How you got into…? → What do you think…? · How did you get into…?
  • I am working here since 2019 / since three years → I've worked / I've been working here since 2019 / for three years
  • Nice to meet you to someone already met → Nice to see you again
  • Rising intonation on tags that expect agreement (it sounds like a real, uncertain question)
  • Over-formal follow-ups ("Kindly find attached," "I remain at your disposal") and title + first name (Mr. Hamid)

Exercise 2 — Passive, aggressive or assertive?
1P (says yes, but signals resentment; no real request)5Ag (dismisses the idea bluntly, offers nothing)
2As (firm concern, respectful, invites discussion)6As (honest about capacity, offers an alternative)
3Ag ("always" + blaming the person)7P (over-apologizes and drops the point)
4P (hides a real problem)8As (behavior-focused, invites problem-solving)
Exercise 3 — Add a softener (models)
  1. I think we might be underestimating the timeline.
  2. I have a slight concern about the budget. (also: a small concern / a bit of a concern)
  3. Would it be possible to push the deadline back a week? (also: Could we push…?)
  4. I was hoping we could revisit the deadline. / I was wondering if we could revisit the deadline.
  5. I can definitely get you the draft by Thursday.
  6. I take your point, but I'm not sure that's the best approach, because… (also: With respect, I'd suggest a different approach.)

Expect: I was wondering if we can… (accept in speech, but teach could); item 5 rewritten negatively ("Sorry, only Thursday"): the move is to say what you can do; item 6 with the preface but no reason (the reason is what keeps it firm).

Exercise 4 — Rewrite the blunt messages (models)
  1. To your manager: "I can see why this client is attractive, but I have some concerns about taking them on. Their project is small and needs a lot of changes, so it might take time from our bigger accounts. Could we look at the numbers together before we decide?"
  2. To a colleague: "I'd love to help, but I can't cover your shift on Saturday: I already have plans I can't move. I could swap with you next week, though, or I can ask in the team chat if anyone else is free. Would that help?"
  3. To a teammate: "Can I share some feedback? In Tuesday's meeting, you came in a couple of times while Sara was still explaining the budget, and she didn't get to finish her point. Your ideas were really useful, so it would be great to hear them after she's done. How did you feel the meeting went?"
  4. To a supplier: "I appreciate your team's work on our account, but I need to raise a concern. This is the third order this month with a mistake: this time, 50 boxes were the wrong size. Could we look at what's going wrong and agree how to prevent it? Could you send me a plan by Friday?"

Judge by: acknowledge first · one or two softeners · the real message stays (the "no" is still a no; the complaint still asks for a fix) · a way forward (alternative, proposal, deadline) · no blame words (always, never, you're…). Message 3 should be SBI and behavior-focused; message 4 should be firm and specific, with a clear request and deadline. Expect: passive rewrites that lose the message ("It's fine, maybe next time"), and "Kindly do the needful" style formulas in message 4.

Hard-conversation planner — what a good opening sounds like

Card 2 (push back on a deadline): "I understand the client wants it early next week, and I want to get it right for them. Realistically, a full report needs until Friday. Would it be possible to send a one-page summary on Wednesday and the full report on Friday?" Card 3 (SBI): "Is now a good time for some feedback? Your analysis in the weekly report is really strong. Self-check
Q1
A colleague suggests an approach you think is risky. Which response is assertive?
A) "Sure, sounds good, let's go with yours." (while privately worried)
B) "That's a bad idea, it'll never work."
C) "I see it a little differently — I'm worried about the support cost. Can we look at it together?"
Why: it names the concern with a reason and invites discussion. A is passive (swallows the concern); B is aggressive.
Q2 · new
Which sentence is about behavior, not the person?
A) "You're careless."
B) "The last two reports arrived after the deadline."
C) "You never care about deadlines."
Why: B describes something observable. A judges character; C uses "never" and guesses at feelings.
Q3
You really can't take on a request. Which reply is best?
A) "I'd love to help, but I'm at capacity this week. I could review the draft on Friday if that's useful?"
B) "No, ask someone else."
C) "Um, okay, I'll try to squeeze it in somehow…" (knowing you can't)
Why: honest about capacity, warm, and offers a partial alternative. B closes the door; C is a passive yes that will damage trust later.
Q4 · new
A client is angry about a late delivery. What should you do first?
A) explain whose fault it was
B) acknowledge the feeling
C) offer a discount immediately
Why: a person who feels heard becomes reasonable. Blame (A) makes it worse; a discount (C) may be part of the solution later, but it skips listening and may promise more than you can give.
Errors to expect in 2.3
  • Over-softening that hides the message: "I was just perhaps wondering if it might possibly be a slight concern…" → one softener + the point + a reason.
  • Over-direct modals: You must send it today · I want you send… → Could you send it today? It's holding up the client report.
  • I was wondering if we can… → could; Would it be possible that you send… → Would it be possible for you to send… / to get…
  • Feedback about the person (you're lazy, you're not professional) instead of the behavior; "always / never."
  • Archaic formulas in complaints: Kindly do the needful · Please revert back → Could you… · Please let me know by…
