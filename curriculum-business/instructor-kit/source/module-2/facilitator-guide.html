Module 2 — Meetings & Interpersonal Skills

Three workshops on the spoken, face-to-face side of working life, plus the course's Midpoint Performance Check. First, meetings: giving an opinion, agreeing and disagreeing politely, interrupting with a signal, and chairing from agenda to action points (who does what by when). Then small talk and networking: openers, open questions, "free information," safe topics, graceful exits and a follow-up message that turns a chat into a contact. Last, diplomatic and assertive communication: disagreeing, giving and receiving feedback, saying no and calming tension while staying firm on substance, soft on delivery. C8 checks Modules 1 and 2 with a timed writing paper (a client email and register rewrites) and pair speaking simulations (two calls and a meeting), scored Writing /20 and Speaking /20; the results come back privately in the C9 warmer.

3 lessons + Midpoint Check Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C5–C8 Weeks 3–4 90 min per class Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)

How to Use This Guide

  • One block = one 90-minute class. C5–C7 follow the communication-skills workshop shape used across this kit: warmer (review of the last class) → lead-in and needs link → model (a meeting extract, a networking chat, a feedback conversation) → notice and clarify the language (phrase banks, register, Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions, CCQs) → drill (choral → individual: stress, intonation, polite tone) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a role-play or simulation with cards) → feedback on register, tone and clarity, with delayed error correction → homework. C8 is the Midpoint Performance Check and has its own plan.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-meetings.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C5. It holds the phrase flashcards and tone-matching pairs, the meeting simulation cards (C5), the networking identity cards (C6), the hard-conversation A/B cards (C7), the Register Dominoes game, the C8 kit (pair-order sheet, door sign, an extra quiet task for early finishers) and every listening script you read aloud.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons (UK or US voice, adjustable speed), so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and shadow them.
  • Protect the freer practice. The meeting simulation (C5), the networking mingle (C6) and the hard-conversation carousel (C7) are where learners find out what they can actually do under pressure. If you are running late, shorten your explanation or the controlled stage, never the role-play or its feedback.
  • Feedback is on register, tone and clarity first, accuracy second. During role-plays, take notes (do not interrupt). Afterwards, board 4–6 lines you heard, without names: two good ones to copy, and two or three to fix ("I am agree with you" → I agree; "No, that is wrong" → I see it a little differently…). Learners fix them in pairs, then say the good version aloud with the right tone. Use the four criteria of the course rubric (Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy, Organization & Delivery) as your observation headings from the first day, so they are familiar by C8.
  • Role cards protect privacy. Every personal or work-history task allows a role card: a fictional job and company. No learner has to name a real employer, say whether they are working, or talk about salary or immigration status. Say this at the start of every role-play: "Use your real job, a past job, or the card. It's your choice, and nobody will ask which."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and phrasing, like the kit (summarize, finalize, practice; send the minutes around; How was your trip in?). Accept British forms (summarise, send the minutes round, queue) whenever a learner uses them, and point out that learners will meet both in international workplaces.

The through-line

Acknowledge first, then act. All three lessons rest on one habit that many learners do not use in English: show the other person you have heard them before you say your own thing. In C5 it is a signal before you interrupt ("Sorry to interrupt, but…") and agreement before you differ ("I see your point, but…"). In C6 it is reacting and asking a follow-up question before you add your own story ("Oh really? What was that like?"). In C7 it is acknowledging a view or a feeling before you push back, give feedback, say no or solve a problem ("I understand this is frustrating. Let's look at how we fix it."). Name the habit in C5, point at it every time it appears, and by C7 learners should recognize it themselves. It is also the single fastest way to raise a Register & Tone score in C8.

Face, confidence and experience

Many learners in this class have more professional experience than their English shows: engineers, nurses, accountants and managers rebuilding a career in a new language, sometimes in jobs below their qualifications. Meetings and networking can hit hard, because being quiet in a meeting or awkward at a networking event can feel like losing professional identity. Treat that experience as a resource ("You've run meetings before, in another language; what did a good chair do?"), never as something to apologize for. Keep conflicts in role-plays ordinary (deadlines, budgets, schedules, delivery delays). Do not use layoffs, discrimination, harassment, politics or religion as role-play material, even as "difficult conversation" practice. If a learner brings a real, painful workplace situation into C7, thank them, keep it private, and offer a role card instead: "Let's practice the language on this card first; you can use it for your own situation later."

Meetings — Participating & Chairing

90 min
Objectives — learners can…

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: action point. A task agreed in a meeting, with a person, a task and a deadline. Without all three, it is a hope, not an action point.

"We should look at the budget." Is that an action point? (No.) What's missing? (Who and when.) · "Priya will circulate the budget by Friday." Who? (Priya.) What? (Circulate the budget.) When? (By Friday.) · Who usually recaps the action points at the end? (The chair.)

Meaning: minutes and agenda.

"I'll send the minutes around this afternoon." Is minutes about time here? (No: the written record of the meeting.) · When do people get the agenda: before or after the meeting? (Before.) The minutes? (After.)

Meaning: park it · take it offline. Chair language for stopping a topic now without ignoring it.

"Let's park that and take it offline." Are we discussing it now? (No.) Are we forgetting it? (No: later, outside this meeting, maybe with fewer people.) Does offline mean the internet is off? (No.) Who usually says it? (The chair.)

Meaning: the interruption signal.

"Sorry to interrupt, but can I just come in here?" Is the speaker really sorry? (Not really: it's a polite signal.) Do you say it before or after you talk over someone? (Before: instead of talking over them.) What does just do? (Makes the request smaller and softer.) · "If I could just finish my point…" Who says this: the person interrupting or the person being interrupted? (The person being interrupted: they are holding the floor.)

Meaning: acknowledge, then differ.

"I see your point, but I'm not sure the copy will be ready." Does the speaker agree? (No.) Why start with "I see your point"? (To show respect; it keeps the room warm.) · "Have we considered pushing the date?" Is it a real question? (Partly: it's a polite way to disagree and suggest.)

Pronunciation. Interrupting phrases need a friendly, rising tone and a small pause after them: Sorry to interRUPT, but… ↗ · Can I just come IN here? ↗. Said flat and fast, they sound like an order. In "I see your POINT, but…", a fall-rise on point tells the listener that a "but" is coming. The chair's summaries fall clearly at the end (So, to sumMArize… ↘), which sounds decisive. Weak forms: to /tə/ in "Sorry to interrupt"; can /kən/ in "Can I just…".

Drill script

Meeting simulation — how to run it
  • Groups of four: chair, participant A, participant B, observer. With three in a group, drop the observer and the chair takes the notes; with five, add a second observer or a "participant C" who simply has to bring someone in and ask one clarification question. Put a confident speaker in the chair for the first run; the chair role is also a good stretch for C1 learners.
  • Scenarios (Set B, all fictional): 1 Kestrel Design, the office move date · 2 Brightline Foods, the trade-show stand budget · 3 Northwind Logistics, the holiday delivery schedule · 4 Tidewater Packaging, the team training day. Each participant card has a position, a reason and a hidden goal (for example, "get one action point assigned to someone else"). The positions clash a little, so there is something to disagree about, but a compromise is always possible.
  • Timing: 10 minutes on the timer, visible to the chair. Give a 2-minute warning. The meeting is "finished" only when the chair has recapped at least two action points.
  • Observer checklist (on the observer card, using the rubric criteria): Task & Purpose ☐ agenda followed ☐ a decision made ☐ action points with who / what / when · Register & Tone ☐ disagreement acknowledged first ☐ interruptions signaled ☐ quieter person brought in · Language Range & Accuracy ☐ phrases from three or more functions ☐ suggestion grammar correct · Organization & Delivery ☐ chair signposted each item ☐ time managed ☐ clear close.
  • If time allows (or in a longer class), swap roles and run a second scenario for 8 minutes, so a second learner chairs.

L1 and cross-cultural watch-outs
  • I am agree / Are you agree?: common among learners whose languages express agreement with "be" + an adjective or noun (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, French je suis d'accord, Portuguese estou de acordo, and others). In English, agree is a verb: I agree · Do you agree?
  • discuss about, make a meeting, assist a meeting: direct translations from many languages. Discuss takes a direct object; you have / hold a meeting; you attend it (in English, assist means "help").
  • I suggest to… / How about to…: very frequent. Drill the three patterns (base verb · -ing · that + we + base verb) until they are automatic.
  • Interrupting norms differ. Some workplace cultures see interrupting as a sign of engagement; others expect people to wait to be invited, especially junior staff speaking to senior staff. Neither is "right." Present the signal phrases as a bridge: learners who find interrupting uncomfortable can use a signal, and learners who interrupt freely can soften it. For chairs: silence does not always mean agreement, so invite quieter people by name.
  • First names vs titles. Many English-speaking workplaces use first names in meetings, even with managers. Some learners find this disrespectful at first. Tell them it is normal and not rude, but also that it is always fine to follow the other person's lead.
  • Flat intonation can sound rude or bored in English even when the words are polite; some learners' first languages use a narrower pitch range. The Drill script's "flat vs polite" contrast is worth doing more than once.

Small Talk, Networking & Building Rapport

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • start a conversation with a stranger using shared context (the event, the situation, the surroundings) and turn closed questions into open questions
  • keep a conversation going with the "rapport engine": follow-up questions, active-listening reactions, common ground and free information
  • choose safe topics and recognize risky ones, knowing that "safe" varies across cultures and following the other person's lead
  • introduce themselves in one or two sentences, join a group, exchange contacts and exit gracefully
  • write a short follow-up message within 48 hours: reminder + specific reference + light next step

Networking mingle — how to run it
  • Set the scene. "This is the coffee break at the Riverside Business Breakfast. You don't know anyone." Put a few cups or a jug of water on a table as the "coffee area": it helps learners step into the role.
  • Identity cards (Set C): twelve invented professionals with a name, role, company, a "free information" fact and a hobby or recent experience. Each card shares one point of common ground with two others (the same city trip, the same hobby, the same work challenge), so every learner can find it by asking. For more than twelve learners, print a second set: duplicates are fine. Learners can use their own professional identity instead, as long as they are comfortable.
  • Rounds: ring the bell every 3–4 minutes. Each time, learners must exit with an exit line (not just walk away) and join someone new. In round 2, some must join a pair ("Mind if I join you?"), and the pair must include them.

L1 and cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Topic norms vary. In some workplace cultures, questions about family, marital status, age or even salary are friendly and normal; in many English-speaking workplaces they feel intrusive with a stranger. Present this as a difference in codes, not good vs bad manners. The safe strategy: start on the safe list, watch the other person's response, and follow their lead. Learners should also know how to deflect a question they don't want to answer: "Oh, that's a long story! How about you, what brings you here?"
  • Interview mode. Some learners ask question after question to be polite, which can feel like an interrogation in English. Others answer in one word to be modest. Teach the rhythm: answer + free info + question back.
  • Formality. Learners may use a formal written style in follow-up messages ("I am writing to inform you that it was a pleasure…", "Kindly revert"). A neutral, warm register is expected: "It was great to meet you at…". Titles with first names ("Mr. Hamid") are a common error; use Mr. / Ms. + family name, or the first name alone.
  • Present perfect with for / since: "I am working here since 2019" is very common among learners whose languages use a present tense for this. Model I've worked / I've been working here since 2019.
  • "Nice to meet you" vs "Nice to see you (again)": meet for the first time, see after that. At a networking event, learners who meet the same person twice often repeat "Nice to meet you."
  • Humor and self-deprecation ("I'm probably the reason your deliveries are late!") are common in English small talk but can confuse. Point it out when it appears in Script 2.2-A: it's friendly, not a real confession.

Fitting everyone into the speaking block

Six pairs (twelve learners) fit comfortably in 45 minutes; seven is possible if every pair keeps to time. With an odd number, make one group of three: in Call 1, C takes Card B (receptionist) while B waits; Call 2 runs as normal; everyone joins the meeting with Card C added. Allow about 9 minutes, with 4 for the meeting. With more than seven pairs, finish the remaining pairs in the C9 warmer (and move the results return to C10 for those learners), or ask a colleague to run a second table after you have both scored one pair together. Never take time from Part A: a rushed written task is not a fair measure. Whatever you do, note it on the score sheet and use the same timing for the Final Assessment, so the results can be compared.

Running the speaking simulations — how to do it well
  • Pairing. Pair learners of similar confidence rather than a very strong speaker with a very hesitant one, so nobody's score depends on a partner who dominates. Give Card A (the chair in the meeting) to the learner who needs stretch on chairing, or who hasn't chaired in class yet. Write the pairs and roles on your pair-order sheet before class.
  • The calls. Seat the pair back to back. "A, say 'Ring ring' when you're ready." Partners must not show each other their cards. The receptionist fills in the message pad on their card; check it after the call: it is part of the Task & Purpose evidence (all details correct, read-back done).
  • The meeting. Turn the chairs to face each other. Three minutes on the timer, visible to the chair. Card A opens, goes through the agenda, makes sure everyone gives a view, reaches a decision on each item and closes with action points. In a pair, Card A also holds Card C's views and gives them as their own, so there is always something to disagree about.
  • Prompts. If a learner freezes, give one neutral prompt ("What would you say to the receptionist now?") and note it on the sheet; don't rescue with the answer. Otherwise stay silent and keep your face neutral and friendly.
  • Scoring. Score each learner separately, one whole number 1–5 per criterion, based on all the evidence from both calls and the meeting (use the task columns on the score sheet for quick notes during the talk). Score what you hear, not what you know the learner can do. Accent itself is not scored; clarity is. Use the observer prompt in the performance-check pack for the moves to listen for (opening + purpose, "I see your point, but…", "Sorry to interrupt, but…", action points with names and days).
  • Support and stretch (from the run-sheet). A B1 learner may keep the phrase card from the Module 1 materials on the table: note "phrase card used" on the sheet. Tell a C1 learner before Call 1: "Offer the caller an alternative contact, and deal with one request you can't meet."
  • Consistency. Before class, reread the rubric descriptors for 3 and 4 on each criterion: these are the hardest to separate. After the first two pairs, glance back at your sheets. Do not change earlier scores because a later pair was stronger or weaker.
What to say (and not say) about nerves
"Speaking with the teacher listening feels different. That's normal. Talk to your partner, not to me." · "If you don't know a word, say it another way, as you would at work." · "If you lose your place, pause, look at your card, and carry on." · "This is a check-up, not a final exam. Your results are private."

Avoid: "This really counts," "Don't be nervous," or comparing learners ("The last pair was great"). Many learners have had job interviews, credential assessments or official exams in English that decided important things; a test can bring back those feelings. Thank every pair, and never show a score during or after the simulation. If a learner becomes distressed, stop, let them take a moment or step outside, and offer to do their simulation at the end or before C9.

Supervising the quiet task
  • The Reading & Ranking task ("Choose the Venue": four replies from venues, a manager's brief with must-haves, a ranking, a short recommendation and two register rewrites) is not scored. It recycles the Module 1–2 register work, so it is useful practice, not filler.
  • Learners stop when you call them and continue when they come back. Early finishers do the "Extra" question on the paper, then the extra quiet task in the materials (Set F: rank five replies to a customer) or review the "I can…" lists for Lessons 1.1–2.3.
  • Glance at the room between pairs. If the class can hear the pair at your table, ask them to read with headphones on (no sound) or play quiet background music.
Marking Part A (after class)
  • Use the rubric and the model answers (with checked word counts) in assessment/assessment-key.html. Give one Writing score per criterion that reflects Task 1 and Task 2 together, as the score sheet shows. For Task 1, tick the three content points (apology + reason · Thursday + no charge · contact + confirm request).
  • On each learner's score sheet, write one strength with evidence (a quotation from their work) and one or two targets for Modules 3–4, using the "From Scores to Targets" method in the assessment key: the target is a habit you can see ("Put the ask and the deadline in the first two lines of every email"), not "improve my emails."

Differentiation
Access and support

Keep the tasks the same for everyone, so the results are a true measure. A learner who needs adapted conditions (extra writing time, a typed paper, enlarged print, a seat near you for the calls because of a hearing difficulty) keeps the same tasks; note the change on the paper and in the "Adapted?" box on the score sheet. A B1 learner may use the Module 1 phrase card in Part B (noted on the sheet). Never let a partner's level decide a score: if a stronger partner dominates the meeting, you may ask the quieter learner one short question at the end ("Do you agree with the plan?") and score their answer.

Early finishers and stronger learners

Learners who finish Part A early check against a short list on the board: all three points in Task 1? · a clear ask (confirm Thursday morning)? · greeting and sign-off match? · word count about 120–170? · Task 2: right register for each reader, meaning kept? They stay seated and do not start other work. In Part B, C1 learners get the stretch instruction from the run-sheet and, where possible, Card A (the chair), but they are scored on the same rubric as everyone else.

Watch-outs: habits from other assessment systems
  • Memorized scripts. Learners who prepare long memorized paragraphs sound fluent but don't respond to the partner, which lowers Task & Purpose and Organization & Delivery. Say in the framing: "Listen and respond to your partner; that's part of the score."
  • Longer and more formal is not better. In Task 1, some learners write far more than 170 words in a very formal style ("I am writing to inform you that…", "Kindly…"). In Task 2 (c), the whole point is to move down the register spectrum for a colleague. Remind them: right for the reader, clear and complete.
  • Collaboration norms. In some classrooms, helping a neighbor quietly during a test is tolerated. Explain the rule without judgment before Part A, and seat friends apart.
  • Asking the teacher for words. You can explain instructions but not language. Note which instruction caused questions; it's useful when you brief the Final Assessment in C15.
Returning the results: the C9 warmer

The Module 3 guide begins C9 with this warmer; here is the full procedure. It takes about 10 minutes. Before C9: mark Part A, complete every score sheet (Writing /20, Speaking /20, one strength with evidence, one or two targets) and fold it, or put it in an envelope with the learner's name on the outside. Follow the "Returning Results Kindly" principles in assessment/assessment-key.html: private, evidence before numbers, two scores tell two stories, name what is fixable, respect experience.

  1. Frame it (1 min). "Today you get your midpoint results: two scores, one for writing and one for speaking, and one or two targets. The numbers are useful, but the targets are what we'll work on. Your results are private: you don't have to show anyone."
  2. Return privately (2 min). Hand back each learner's folded score sheet. Learners read it quietly. Circulate: stop briefly with anyone who looks surprised or upset ("Let's talk for two minutes at the break"), and never discuss a score in front of others.
  3. Record (3 min). Learners copy both scores into section 2 of their Performance Tracker (performance-tracker.html), next to their Class 1 diagnostic scores, and copy their targets into section 4. Point out: "Compare yourself only with yourself. Writing and speaking often move at different speeds."
  4. Quiet pair task (4 min). While you circulate, pairs do: "Rewrite one sentence from your email that you'd now write differently." Each learner shows their partner the before and after sentence (never the score) and says why. Take two or three good examples for the board, without names.

If some pairs did not finish Part B in C8, run them now (about 7 minutes each) while the rest of the class does steps 3–4, and return their results in C10. Offer a two-minute conversation at the break to anyone who wants one. Keep the score sheets (or copies) with your records: you will need them to compare with the Final Assessment. For the whole class, use the class record: if one criterion is low for most learners, build it into Module 3 warmers (for example, low Register & Tone: Register Dominoes and tone-swap drills; low Organization & Delivery in speaking: signposting, which Lesson 3.1 teaches directly).

Using the results diagnostically
  • The criteria tell you what to teach. Low Task & Purpose: more work on "purpose and ask in the first two lines" and "every call ends with a read-back; every meeting ends with who / what / when." Low Register & Tone: tone-swap drills and register rewrites in warmers. Low Language Range & Accuracy: short clinics on the five class errors. Low Organization & Delivery: signposting and chair language, which presentations in Module 3 use directly.
  • Writing vs speaking. Tell each learner honestly which is stronger and where the fastest gain is, and point them to the matching online lessons (the example targets table in the assessment key lists one for each criterion).
  • Progress. Compare with the Class 1 diagnostic in the tracker. A rise of one or two points on one criterion is real progress and worth naming, privately.
  • Privacy. Never display scores, rank learners, or share one learner's result with another.

Closing the Module

End of C7 and C9: Module 2 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "acknowledge first, then act": signal before you interrupt · acknowledge, then differ · close with who / what / when (meetings) → open questions · answer + free info + question back · exit warmly and follow up within 48 hours (networking) → firm on substance, soft on delivery · SBI · alternative, trade-off or buy time · acknowledge the feeling, then solve (diplomacy). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists at the end of C7; anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. After C9, each learner's midpoint goal becomes their personal focus for weeks 5–8: check in briefly each week ("How's your goal going?").

Watch for in Module 3: presentations, negotiations and pitching reuse all of this. The chair's signposting in C5 becomes presentation signposting; the question-handling in presentations uses "acknowledge, then answer"; negotiation depends on rapport (C6) and on firm-but-soft pushing back and trade-offs (C7: "Happy to, if…"). Learners who struggled with the carousel will meet the same moves again with more support.

The Final Assessment (C15–C16) uses the same rubric; the Module 4 guide plans those classes. Keep each learner's midpoint score sheet (or a copy) so you can show them their progress on each criterion; learners track it themselves in the Performance Tracker.
