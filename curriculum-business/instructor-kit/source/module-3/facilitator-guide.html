Module 3 — Presenting, Negotiating & Persuading

Three speaking workshops, and the first class after the Midpoint Performance Check. C9 opens by handing back the score sheets and individual targets, then teaches presentations: the three-part shape, the five jobs of an opening, signposting, describing a chart so it means something, and staying calm in the Q&A. C10 turns to negotiation: positions vs interests, the five stages, rejecting without offense and the grammatical heart of bargaining, the conditional trade ("If you…, we'll…"), practiced in a points-based Deal Room simulation with private priorities. C11 brings it together in persuasion: the five-stage pitch, features into benefits, honest credibility language, and handling objections with LAARC, practiced in Pitch & Push-back, where every buyer hides a real concern behind the objection they say out loud.

3 lessons Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C9–C11 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class is a communication-skills workshop with the same shape: warmer (review) → lead-in linked to learners' working lives → model (a read-aloud script and a workbook text) → notice and clarify the language through Meaning / Form / Pronunciation and concept-checking questions (CCQs) → a short drill (choral → individual; stress, pausing, polite intonation) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a simulation with A/B cards) → feedback on register, tone and clarity with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-presenting-negotiating.html (C9 = slides 2–8, C10 = slides 9–13, C11 = slides 14–18). Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C9. It holds the phrase flashcards, the role profiles, the Presentation Carousel brief and question cards, the Deal Room A/B negotiation cards with points tables, the Pitch & Push-back A/B cards, the observer checklists, the Signpost Bingo game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review: 3.1 = lesson_07.html, 3.2 = lesson_08.html, 3.3 = lesson_09.html. Each has 🔊 Listen buttons for the model sentences.
  • Protect the simulation. Every class ends in a timed speaking simulation (the Presentation Carousel in C9, the Deal Room in C10, Pitch & Push-back in C11). Learners can read phrase lists at home; what they cannot do alone is use them live, under mild pressure, with a real listener who pushes back. If you're running late, cut controlled practice (set it for homework), never the simulation or the feedback that follows it.
  • Feedback on the four criteria, not on "good English." This module uses the course's one analytic rubric (the full descriptors are in assessment/assessment-key.html): Task & Purpose (did the talk, deal or pitch achieve its goal: all points covered, a clear action), Register & Tone (appropriate formality, politeness, diplomacy), Language Range & Accuracy (phrases, grammar, vocabulary), Organization & Delivery (structure, signposting, fluency, pronunciation, interaction). During every simulation, take notes in these four columns. In feedback, board two items per column without names, and always one phrase to praise.
  • Observers use the same criteria. In each simulation one learner observes with a checklist card (Set G) built on the four criteria. This does two jobs: it trains learners to hear what the rubric rewards, and it gives you a second pair of ears. Observers give one strength and one "try next time," never a score.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and dollars, like the kit (organized, emphasizing, summarize, leveling off, program, licenses, offense, labeled, elevator pitch; $2m, $4 a day). Never mark a correct British form wrong: in international business both are normal. What matters is being consistent within one document.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Inform → trade → persuade. In C9 learners inform: they give a clear, structured talk that an audience can follow without effort, because every turn is signposted. In C10 they trade: they discover what the other side really needs and swap concessions conditionally, so that both sides leave with enough to say yes. In C11 they persuade: they turn features into benefits, back them with honest evidence, ask for one clear action and stay calm under objections. The same four habits run through all three: structure out loud (signposts, stages, pitch steps), focus on the listener ("what's in it for them"), stay polite under pressure (soften, acknowledge, never a flat "no") and finish with a clear action (a call to action, a written summary, a next step). Each class produces something a listener responds to: a mini-presentation with Q&A, a recorded deal, a pitch that survives two objections.

Confidence, face and speaking in front of others

This is the most exposed module of the course. Many of your learners are highly competent professionals who presented, negotiated and sold with ease in their first language, and now feel judged on their English instead of their expertise. Some are rebuilding a career after a move and may be working below their qualifications. Say these things explicitly in C9 and whenever someone stands up to speak:

  • "Presenters are made, not born." (It's the online lesson's message too.) Nervous first-timers and polished speakers are the same person a few presentations apart.
  • Structure beats accent. An audience forgives an accent and a grammar slip; it doesn't forgive getting lost. Signposting is the fastest route to sounding in control, and learners can plan it in advance.
  • Small groups first. All three simulations run in groups of 2–3, not in front of the whole class. Nobody presents to the full room in this module unless they volunteer. The whole-class capstone comes in C16, after weeks of rehearsal.
  • Their expertise is an asset. Invite learners with professional experience in sales, procurement, teaching or management to share one tip in English. Never ask what their job "used to be" or why they left it; let them volunteer it.
  • Nothing personal is required. Every presentation, negotiation and pitch can use a role profile (Set B): a fictional person, job and company with ready-made figures. Learners may present their real work only if they want to, and should never share confidential information, salaries or a real employer's name. Say so in C9.
Keep the conflicts ordinary

All negotiation and pitch scenarios in this kit are fictional and deliberately low-stakes: delivery prices, launch dates, a training venue, meeting-room booking, a lunch-box order. Don't replace them with salary negotiations, job cuts, disputes about discrimination or anything political: those are real stressors for some learners and poor practice material. If learners want to rehearse a real situation (for example, asking for more time on a project), they may do so in the homework task, privately, on their own terms.

Presentations — Structure & Delivery

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • read their Midpoint Performance Check results criterion by criterion and link their target for the Final Assessment to a Module 3 skill
  • open a presentation with the five jobs (greet · introduce yourself and the topic · state the purpose and the benefit · outline the structure · housekeeping on questions) and a one-sentence hook

Returning the Midpoint Performance Check — how to run it (12 min)

This follows the procedure in the Module 2 guide ("Returning the results: the C9 warmer"), with two extra minutes to link each learner's target to Module 3. You need each learner's completed speaking and writing score sheets from assessment/performance-check.html (Writing /20, Speaking /20, one strength with evidence, one or two targets, set with "From Scores to Targets" in assessment/assessment-key.html), folded, with the learner's name on the outside. Learners need their Performance Tracker (performance-tracker.html). Follow the "Returning Results Kindly" principles in the assessment key.

  • Frame it (1 min): "Today you get your midpoint results: two scores, one for writing and one for speaking, and one or two targets. These results are a map, not a verdict. In C8 you wrote under time pressure and spoke with someone listening and taking notes. That's hard in any language. The numbers don't measure your professional ability, and nobody else will see them. The targets are what we'll work on."
  • Return privately (2 min): hand back each learner's folded score sheets. Learners read quietly. Circulate; stop briefly with anyone who looks surprised or upset ("Let's talk for two minutes at the break"), and never discuss a score in front of others.
  • Record (3 min): learners copy both scores into section 2 of their Performance Tracker, next to their Class 1 diagnostic scores, and their targets into section 4. "Compare yourself only with yourself. Writing and speaking often move at different speeds." Remind them what a 3 means on a criterion: the job is done, with some problems.
  • Quiet pair task (3 min): pairs do "Rewrite one sentence from your C8 email that you'd now write differently" (the "After the check (C9)" box in the Module 2 workbook). Each learner shows the before and after sentence, never the score. Board two or three good examples without names.
  • Link the target to Module 3 (2 min): learners complete "My Midpoint target in Module 3" in Workbook 3.1: they choose the Module 3 skill that matches their target from the target menu, and one action they can check. ✘ "Better English." ✔ "Signpost every move in my C16 presentation and practice it aloud twice." ✔ "Soften every refusal: a reason + an alternative." Point out that Module 3 works directly on Organization & Delivery (signposting, negotiation stages, pitch structure) and Register & Tone (saying no diplomatically).
  • Close (1 min): "In C14 we rehearse the capstone, and I'll ask you: what did you do about your target?" Keep the score sheets (or copies) with your records for the Final Assessment comparison. If some pairs did not finish Part B in C8, the Module 2 guide explains how to run them now and return their results in C10.
  • Individual talks: anyone whose result upset them, or whose score is far below their professional level, gets a few minutes with you at the break or during the Carousel prep, never in front of others. Be specific and kind: "Your Task & Purpose was a 5: your email did everything it needed to do. Your Language score is a 3 because of articles and past tense, and those are very fixable." Learners who scored low in speaking because of nerves: tell them so, and point to the small-group format of this module. Use the class record privately: if one criterion is low for most learners, lean on the matching Module 3 warmers and drills.
Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: topic vs purpose vs benefit. The topic is what the talk is about; the purpose is what the speaker wants to achieve; the benefit is what the audience gets.

"Today I'm going to talk about late invoices." Topic or purpose? (Topic.) "The purpose of my talk is to get your approval for a new reminder system." (Purpose.) "By the end, you'll know how to get paid two weeks faster." Who is that sentence for: the speaker or the audience? (The audience: it's the benefit.) · "Please feel free to ask questions as we go." When can I ask a question: now or at the end? (Now, during the talk.) "I'll take questions at the end." Can I interrupt? (Better not; wait until the end.)

Meaning: signposts by function. A signpost tells the audience where the speaker is going, like a road sign.

"This brings me to my next point." Is the speaker starting, moving forward or finishing? (Moving forward to a new point.) · "As I mentioned earlier…" Forward or back? (Back, to something already said.) "We'll come to that shortly." Now or later? (Later, soon.) · "In conclusion…" Can I say this in the middle of the talk? (No: only at the end.) · "The key point here is…" Is this an example or the most important idea? (The most important idea.) · "For instance…" Same meaning as? (For example.)

Meaning: describing and interpreting a visual.

"On-time deliveries rose sharply in Q3." Big change or small change? (Big.) Fast or slow? (Fast.) "They leveled off in Q4." Did they go up, down or stay about the same? (About the same.) · "They peaked at 93%." Was 93% the highest or the lowest point? (Highest.) · "This graph shows our deliveries." Is that describing or interpreting? (Describing: introducing.) "What this tells us is that the new routes are working." (Interpreting: the meaning for the audience.) Which one does the audience care about most? (The meaning.)

Meaning: Q&A.

"I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up by tomorrow." Does the speaker know the answer now? (No.) Are they going to guess? (No.) What will they do? (Find it and send it later.) · "Can we take that offline?" Discuss it now, in front of everyone, or later, just the two of us? (Later, privately.) · Is it weak to say "I don't know"? (No, it's honest and professional, as long as you offer to follow up.)

Pronunciation. A signpost is a chunk with one main stress and a pause after it: This brings me to my NEXT point. (pause) The cost. · The KEY point here is… (pause) · So, to sum UP… (pause). The pause is the audible paragraph break; nervous speakers skip it. Numbers: thirTEEN vs THIRty, fifTEEN vs FIFty: stress the ending on -teen and repeat important figures ("ninety-three, nine-three, percent"). Nouns vs verbs: an INcrease / to inCREASE. Q&A: a falling tone on That's a great QUEStion sounds calm; a rising tone sounds unsure.

Drill script
  1. Choral, then individual, with the pause: T: "Good morning, everyone, and thank you all for COMing." → Class repeats. "I've divided my talk into THREE parts." · "Let's START with the problem." · "This brings me to my NEXT point." · "The KEY point here is…" · "So, to sum UP…" Tap the table on each pause.
  2. Function → phrase (rapid round): T: "Moving on!" → S1: "Let's move on to the cost." T: "Example!" → S2: "For instance, …" T: "Back!" → S3: "As I mentioned earlier, …" T: "Forward!" → S4: "We'll come to that shortly." T: "Emphasis!" → S5: "What I want to stress is…" T: "Recap!" → S6: "Let me recap the main points."
  3. Trend + meaning chain: T: "Sales, Q1 to Q2, 100 to 150." → S1: "Sales rose sharply in Q2." → S2: "What this tells us is that the campaign worked." T: "Costs, flat all year." → S1: "Costs stayed flat." → S2: "The takeaway here is that our savings held."
  4. Can't-answer frame: T (as audience): "What was the figure for 2023?" → S: "I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up by tomorrow." T: "What about the IT budget?" → S: "That's a bit outside today's scope. Can we take that offline?"
ICQs before the Presentation Carousel
How many people in your group? (Three.) How long do you present? (Two minutes.) What must your opening include? (The five jobs, or at least greeting, topic and purpose, outline and questions.) How many signposts, at least? (One between each point, and one to close.) Do you read from your planner? (No: look at the audience; keywords only.) What does the questioner do? (Asks one question from the card.) What does the observer do? (Ticks the checklist, then says one strength and one "try next time.") Does the observer give a score? (No.) Can you use a role profile instead of your real job? (Yes.)
Running the Presentation Carousel (role-play management)
  • Grouping: threes, mixed levels, and never three learners from the same workplace or first language if you can avoid it. With a group of four, add a second questioner; with a pair, the questioner also observes.
  • Timing: 5 min prep (planner) · then three rounds of 5 minutes: 2 min presenting · 1 min Q&A · 1 min observer feedback · 1 min to rotate. Call the time loudly. A presenter who runs over is stopped at 2:15 with "Close now, please": closing on time is part of the skill.
  • Roles: Presenter (stands if they're comfortable; sitting is fine) · Questioner (draws a card from the Q&A pack, Set C: 8 questions, including one the presenter can't answer and one that is off-topic) · Observer (checklist card, Set G).
  • Observer checklist (the four criteria): Task & Purpose: was the purpose clear? was there a clear call to action or conclusion? · Register & Tone: polite, confident, audience-focused ("you" language)? · Language Range & Accuracy: at least three different signposts? trend words used correctly? · Organization & Delivery: opening with the five jobs? a signpost at every turn? eye contact, pauses, not reading? a calm, short answer to the question?
  • What you do: stand near each group for one full round. Note in four columns. Don't interrupt a presenter, even for a serious error. If a group finishes early, they repeat with a new question card or stretch constraint (below).
  • Face-saving: anyone who is very anxious can present sitting, to one person, using the planner as notes, or can be the observer twice and present last. Everyone presents at least once, in whatever format they can manage today.
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Give the fill-in opening frame in the Presentation planner: "Good ___, everyone. I'm ___ from ___. Today I'm going to talk about ___. By the end, you'll ___. I've divided my talk into two parts: first, ___; then, ___. Please ___." Allow two points instead of three and a 90-second talk. Let them use a role profile, which has the figures ready. Ex 3 items 1–4 only. In the Carousel, they present third, after hearing two models.

Stretch (C1)

Constraints and register-shifting. (1) Present the same content twice: once to senior managers (formal, 90 seconds, numbers first), once to your own team (informal, 90 seconds, benefits first). (2) Include one referring-back and one referring-forward signpost and a rhetorical question. (3) As questioner, ask a hostile or off-topic question; as presenter, park it politely. (4) Take the chair's role: introduce the next speaker and manage the Q&A time ("We have time for one more question").

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "I will present you…": a translation of présenter / presentar / präsentieren + indirect object. Teach I'd like to present to you…, I'll show you…, or simply Today I'm going to talk about…. Also ✘ explain me → ✔ explain to me, and ✘ discuss about → ✔ discuss + noun.
  • "In conclusion" too early: learners from school traditions where every paragraph has a formal conclusion use in conclusion as "in summary of this point." In English it means "the talk is ending." Use So, to sum up this part… mid-talk.
  • Irregular past and articles in trends: ✘ rised, falled, the sales was growing up; ✘ There was sharp rise. Drill rose / fell / peaked and the chunk a sharp rise in. Sales is plural: sales were.
  • Numbers: many European and Latin American learners write 2,5 million (decimal comma) and 1.000 (thousand point); in English it's 2.5 million, 1,000. Some languages count in milliards, so billion can be misheard; and -teen / -ty confusion causes real errors in meetings. Check figures by asking "fifteen, one-five?"
  • Eye contact and self-promotion: in some workplace cultures, steady eye contact with a senior person feels disrespectful, and presenting your own team's success can feel like boasting. Don't frame this as right or wrong. Explain the English-speaking workplace expectation (look around the room, state results plainly: "we cut waiting times by half") and let learners find a version that feels honest to them.
  • Questions during the talk: some learners come from settings where interrupting a speaker, or questioning a senior colleague, is rude; others from settings where it's expected. That's exactly why the housekeeping line ("feel free to ask as we go" / "I'll take questions at the end") exists: it makes the rule explicit for everyone.
  • Flat intonation: speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Tagalog, Turkish and others) may give every word the same weight. In presentations this sounds bored rather than rude. The signpost drill (one stress, one pause) is the fix.

3.2 · C10

Negotiations — Language & Strategy

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain the difference between a position (what someone says they want) and an interest (why they want it), and ask a question that uncovers the interest
  • name the five stages of a negotiation and use phrases to open warmly, propose, accept, reject without offense (reason + route forward) and counter-propose
  • bargain with conditionals: the first conditional for a real offer (If you commit to two years, we'll…), the second for testing the water (If you were to double the order, would you…?), and provided that / as long as
  • make concessions that are small, conditional and labeled, and ask for something back every time
  • close a deal by summarizing it aloud and confirming it in a short email
Materials
  • Workbook 3.2 (Key words, Key phrases, Language box, Model dialogue, Exercises 1–5, Deal Room planner, confirmation email frame, self-check)

Left: position vs interest. POSITION = what they say ("We need 15% off.") · INTEREST = why ("My budget resets in January.") → ask: What's driving that? · What's most important to you here? · If we could solve X, would that change things? Stages: ① prepare (ideal · limit · BATNA) ② open ③ explore ④ propose & bargain ⑤ close.

Center: never give something for nothing. If you commit to two years, we'll waive the fee. (real offer) · If you were to double the order, would you…? (testing) · We'll include training provided that / as long as you order today. Concessions: small · slow · conditional · labeled ("That's a big step for us") · reciprocal · keep one in reserve.

Right: no → not yet. ✘ "No. Impossible." ✔ I'm afraid that doesn't quite work for us, because… What we could offer instead is… · Deadlock: Let me take that back to my team. · If price is fixed, could we look at delivery or terms? Close: So, to confirm what we've agreed… I'll send the details by email this afternoon.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: key terms.

"We need the price down by 15%." Position or interest? (Position: what they say they want.) "My budget resets in January." (Interest: why.) · A concession: do you get something or give something? (Give something up, to move the deal forward.) · Your BATNA is your plan if…? (…there is no deal: your best alternative, for example another supplier.) If a deal is worse than your BATNA, do you accept it? (No.) · Deadlock: are the two sides moving or stuck? (Stuck.)

Meaning: first vs second conditional in bargaining.

"If you sign today, we'll take 5% off." Is this a real offer? (Yes.) Could the buyer say "Deal!" now? (Yes.) · "If you signed today, would you expect a discount?" Is the seller offering a discount? (Not yet: they're testing, finding out.) Which is softer? (The second.) When do you use it, early or late in the negotiation? (Early, to explore.) · "We'll include training provided that you order all five licenses." Do they get training if they order three? (No: provided that = only if.) Same meaning as? (As long as / only if.)

Meaning: rejecting and labeling.

"I'm afraid that doesn't quite work for us, because our margin is tight. What we could offer instead is…" Is this a yes or a no? (A no.) Is the conversation over? (No: there's an alternative.) · "That would be difficult." Does it usually mean impossible? (No: often "not impossible, if…"). What could you ask? ("What would make it easier?") · "That's a big concession for us." Why say this out loud? (So they value it, and so you can ask for something back.)

Form.

First conditional (real offer)If + present simple, … will / can + verbIf you pay within 14 days, we'll waive the setup fee. ✘ If you will pay…
Second conditional (testing)If + past simple / were to + verb, … would / could + verbIf we absorbed the shipping, would that change your position? · If you were to double the order…
Only if… provided (that) / as long as + present simpleWe can hold the price as long as delivery stays flexible.
Unless= if… notWe can't go lower unless you increase the volume.
Soft rejectionI'm afraid + clause · That's a little more than we can do · I don't think we can go that far+ because… + What we could offer instead is… / We can't do X, but we could do Y.
CloseSo, to confirm what we've agreed… · I'll send (over) the details by + time✘ I will send you until Friday → ✔ by Friday

Pronunciation. Contractions make offers sound natural and friendly: we'll /wiːl/, we'd /wiːd/, I'd. Stress the trade, not the grammar: If YOU can commit to TWO years, WE can offer TEN percent. Polite rejection has a fall-rise on the softener and a pause: I'm aFRAID… (pause) that doesn't quite work for us. A flat, fast "No, we can't" sounds much ruder in English than learners intend. Labeling a concession: slow down and lower the pitch: That really is… our LIMit.

Drill script
  1. Giveaway → trade: T: "OK, we'll give you 10% off." → S: "If you commit to two years, we'll give you 10% off." T: "Fine, we'll deliver a week earlier." → S: "We can deliver a week earlier as long as you're flexible on quantity." T: "Sure, free training." → S: "We'll include training provided that you order today." Choral first, then individual; stress YOU and WE.
  2. Real → testing: T: "If you order 500, we'll reduce the price." → S: "If you ordered 500, would you expect a lower price?" T: "If we pay the shipping, will you sign?" → S: "If we paid the shipping, would you sign?"
  3. Soften the no (fall-rise): T: "No. Too expensive." → S: "I'm aFRAID that's a little more than we can do, because… What we could offer instead is…" T: "No. Impossible date." → S: "I don't think we can go that far on the date, but we could…"
  4. Open pairs, chain: S1 asks for something ("Could you do a little better on the price?") → S2 trades ("I can move a little, but I'll need something in return. If you…") → S3 labels and closes ("That's a big step for us. So, to confirm…").
ICQs before the Deal Room
Do you show your card to your partner? (No, never.) Do you know your partner's priorities? (No: you find out by asking questions.) Can you tell your partner your points? (No, but you can tell them what's important to you and why.) Should you accept a deal worth less than your walk-away score? (No.) Is the goal to beat your partner? (No: to get a good deal for you that they can also say yes to.) What do you do at the end? (Summarize aloud, write the deal on the record, both sign.) How long? (Five minutes to prepare, about fifteen to negotiate and close.)
Running the Deal Room (role-play management)
  • Scenarios (Set D): 1 · The delivery contract (Brightline Foods buys store deliveries from Northwind Logistics: price, contract length, payment terms, delivery window) · 2 · The launch deadline (two teams inside Maple & Stone Furniture: launch date, features, contractor budget, testing time) · 3 · The training venue (Silverleaf Clinics books Harbor Point Conference Center: price, dates, catering, deposit). Each scenario has four issues. On every card, two issues matter a lot to that side and two matter little, and the sides don't care about the same ones. That is what makes a win–win possible: the best deals come from each side giving way on what it cares about less.
  • Grouping: pairs, matched roughly by level (a very strong learner against a B1 learner turns into a lecture). With an odd number, add an observer (checklist, Set G) or give a C1 learner the stretch card: two people on one side who must agree privately before each offer (call a "team break"). Scenario 1 is the most straightforward and suits B1 pairs; scenario 2 (internal, no money changing hands) suits confident pairs.
  • Timing: 5 prep · 13 negotiate · 2 close and record · 2 score (22). Warn at 11 minutes. If a pair reaches deadlock, they must use one deadlock phrase and try once more before they walk away. Walking away (no deal) is an allowed result and worth zero points for both: discuss it in feedback without blame.
  • Observer checklist (the four criteria): Task & Purpose: a clear deal on all four issues, or a clear decision to walk away? · Register & Tone: warm opening, no flat "no," goodwill at the end? · Language Range & Accuracy: at least two conditional trades? correct If + present, will? · Organization & Delivery: explored before proposing? summarized the deal? listened and responded to what the other side said?
  • Feedback with the numbers: the teacher's note in Set D gives each scenario's typical "split the difference" deal and its "win–win" deal with combined points (for example, in scenario 1, splitting everything down the middle gives about 130 combined points; trading well gives about 170). Board the pairs' combined scores; ask the highest pair what they learned about the other side, and when. Don't rank individuals.
  • Face-saving: some learners find hard bargaining uncomfortable, and some cultures treat haggling as normal and others as slightly rude. The points table depersonalizes it: "You're protecting your card, not arguing with your classmate."
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Use scenario 1. Give the phrase strip on the back of the card (one phrase per stage) and allow learners to write their two trades in full before they start. Accept If you…, we can… as the only structure; the second conditional is optional. In the Deal Room planner, the partially completed model shows the first trade. Pair them with a patient partner, not the strongest learner.

Stretch (C1)

Tougher constraints. (1) The stretch line on the card: "You must get your director's approval for anything below X: call a short break and 'phone' your director (the observer) before you agree." (2) Use only second conditionals until minute 6, then switch to first conditionals to close. (3) Label every concession, and never make two concessions in a row without getting one back. (4) Afterwards, write the confirmation email in two registers: to the other side (formal) and to their own team on chat (informal).

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "If you will…": because the action is in the future, learners from many first languages put will after if (and after when, as long as, provided that). ✘ If you will sign → ✔ If you sign. Also over-polite ✘ If you would sign, we would… for a real, firm offer.
  • Over-direct requests: ✘ I want you give me discount / You must deliver on Monday (translated directly) → ✔ Could you do a little better on the price? · Would it be possible to deliver on Monday? Missing articles: ✘ give discount → ✔ a discount. Also ✘ I am agree → ✔ I agree / We can live with that.
  • Directness in refusals: some workplace cultures prefer a clear "no" and find hints confusing; others almost never say "no" and signal it with "that would be difficult" or silence. Teach learners to notice both: an indirect "that might be difficult" from their counterpart deserves the question "What would make it easier?"; and their own "no" should come with a reason and an alternative.
  • Haggling norms: in some markets and workplaces bargaining is expected and friendly; in others, pushing on a quoted price can seem rude. Relationship-first vs deal-first: some counterparts expect small talk and trust before numbers. The online lesson's culture note says it well: observe, mirror the pace, and when unsure, ask.
  • Silence: learners from some cultures are comfortable with long pauses; others rush to fill silence and give away a concession. Practice the "strategic pause": make an offer, then stop talking.
  • "Discuss about the price" (Spanish discutir sobre, French discuter de): ✔ discuss the price. And negotiate + object, or negotiate with + person: ✘ negotiate about the price with is acceptable but heavy; ✔ negotiate the price with the supplier.

3.3 · C11

Pitching, Persuasion & Handling Objections

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain the persuasion triangle (trust + clear value + a clear ask) and structure a pitch in five stages: hook/problem → solution → benefit → evidence → call to action
  • turn a feature into a benefit with …which means that… / …so that…, and pass the "So what?" test
  • use value and credibility language honestly (around, in our pilot, on average) and avoid over-hyped claims
  • handle an objection with LAARC: listen, acknowledge, ask a clarifying question, respond (reframe, evidence or a trial), confirm
  • deliver a 30-second elevator pitch: who · what · value · ask
Materials
  • Workbook 3.3 (Key words, Key phrases, Language box, Model pitch, Exercises 1–5, Pitch planner, elevator pitch, self-check) · learners' confirmation emails from C10
  • Slides 14–18 · Feature ↔ benefit flashcards (Set A3) · Pitch & Push-back A/B cards (Set E) · role profiles (Set B) · observer checklists (Set G) · timer · Script 3.3-A (a pitch and an objection)

Left: the pitch. TRUST + VALUE + ASK. ① Hook / problem: You know how…? ② Solution: We've built… / I'd like to introduce… ③ Benefit: So your team… ④ Evidence: In our pilot, … cut… by around 30%. ⑤ Call to action: Could we run a two-week trial? Lead with the problem, not the product.

Center: feature → benefit. FEATURE (what it is / has) + …which means that… / …so that… + BENEFIT (what it does for them). "It works offline" → which means that your drivers never lose data. Test: "So what?" until you reach time, money, stress or looking good.

Right: LAARC. Listen · Acknowledge (That's a fair concern.) · Ask (Can I ask what's driving that? Is it the price or the timing?) · Respond (value / differentiate / cost of waiting / a trial) · Confirm (Does that address your concern?). ✘ "You're wrong." ✘ "OK, 30% off." ✔ acknowledge first.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: feature vs benefit.

"The app works offline." Is that what the app is or what it does for me? (What it is / has: a feature.) "Your drivers can keep working with no signal." (A benefit: what it does for the customer.) Which one does the buyer care about? (The benefit.) · "It has a 10-hour battery." So what? ("You can work all day.") So what? ("You don't hunt for a charger in meetings.") When do you stop asking "So what?" (When you reach something the listener feels: time, money, stress, looking good to their boss.)

Meaning: call to action and honest urgency.

"…anyway, let me know what you think." Is that a clear ask? (No: the listener doesn't know what to do.) "Could we run a two-week trial with your team, starting Monday?" (Yes: one specific, low-risk next step.) · "We can only take two pilots this quarter." Is that OK to say if it's true? (Yes.) If it isn't true? (No: false urgency destroys trust.) · "In our pilot, admin time fell by around 30%." Why "around"? (Honest: it's approximately correct, not invented precision.)

Meaning: objections.

"It's too expensive." Is this a rejection? (Not always: it can be a sign of interest; they're imagining buying.) · "That's a fair concern." Does the speaker agree the product is too expensive? (No: they agree the concern is reasonable.) Why say it first? (It keeps the conversation calm and gives you a moment to think.) · "Can I ask what's driving that?" Answer or question? (A question, to find the real issue.) · "I need to think about it." What's often behind this? (An unspoken concern.) What do you do? (Ask gently what's giving them pause.)

Pronunciation. Stress the benefit words: which means that YOU can work ALL day. Choice questions rise then fall: Is it the ↗PRICE, or the ↘TIMing? Acknowledging phrases need a warm, slower tone: That's a FAIR concern. Said fast and flat, it sounds dismissive.

Drill script

  • Timing: 6 prep · 7 round 1 · 7 round 2 · 4 debrief (24). In each round: 90 seconds pitch · 4 minutes objections and LAARC · 1 minute observer feedback. Warn at 5 minutes in each round: "Buyers: decide on a next step."
  • Observer checklist (the four criteria): Task & Purpose: one clear call to action? a next step agreed? · Register & Tone: confident but not pushy? acknowledged before responding? no over-hyped claims? · Language Range & Accuracy: at least two features turned into benefits? value or credibility phrases? · Organization & Delivery: problem first, all five stages, within 90 seconds? asked a clarifying question?
  • The debrief is the point. Buyers reveal their hidden concern. Most pitchers will have answered the stated objection (price, timing) with arguments; the ones who asked "Can I ask what's driving that?" usually found the real issue. Make that the headline of your feedback.
  • Face-saving: being the buyer is easier than pitching; let anxious learners buy first. A pitcher who freezes can restart once, from "You know how…?"
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Use the partially completed pitch in the Pitch planner (hook and solution given; the learner writes the benefit, evidence and ask). Pitch card 1 or 2. Allow a 45–60 second pitch and one objection instead of two. The phrase strip on the card has one line for each LAARC step. In Ex 1–2, items 1–4 only.

Stretch (C1)

Harder buyers and register shifts. (1) Buyers raise a third objection, and one is a "fake" (a polite excuse); the pitcher must decide which is real. (2) Pitch the same idea twice: to a senior manager (formal, numbers first, 60 seconds) and to a peer over coffee (informal, story first). (3) Use no superlatives at all: persuade only with specific, hedged evidence. (4) Stretch learners can chair the elevator sprint and time everyone.

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Hard sell vs soft sell: in some business cultures a bold, direct "here's why you should buy" is expected; in others, it feels aggressive and trust is built slowly through modest claims and relationship. Describe both as tendencies and point to the lesson's advice: confident, honest, read the buyer. Neither extreme is the "English" way.
  • Modesty and self-promotion: learners from workplace cultures that value modesty may understate their evidence ("maybe it helped a little"). Teach honest-but-confident: In our pilot, processing time fell by around a third. Learners who come from strongly promotional cultures may overclaim: ✘ the best in the world, 100% guaranteed. Both are fixed by the same rule: specific, hedged evidence.
  • Superlatives and "very": ✘ very unique, most best, more cheaper. ✔ unique · the best · cheaper. Also ✘ It permits to save time (French permettre de, Spanish permitir) → ✔ It lets you save time / It helps you save time.
  • "Which means that" as a filler: learners who learn the bridge well may repeat the feature after it ("It has offline mode, which means that it works offline"). Use the "So what?" test.
  • Arguing with objections: in some professional cultures, correcting a client ("You're wrong, it's not expensive") is a sign of expertise. In most English-speaking business settings it sounds defensive. Acknowledge first, then give the evidence.
  • Explain me / suggest me: ✘ Let me explain you the benefits · Can I suggest you a trial? → ✔ Let me explain the benefits (to you) · Can I suggest a trial?

End of C11: Module 3 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "inform, trade, persuade": open with the five jobs · signpost every turn · chart = introduce → direct → describe → interpret (inform) → ask for the interest · "If you…, we'll…" · label it · confirm in writing (trade) → problem first · feature, which means that… · LAARC · one clear ask (persuade). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you.

Before C12, read the confirmation emails you collected. Don't correct every error: check the four terms, the next step and the tone, and write one line using the criteria ("Task & Purpose: all four terms and a clear date. Register & Tone: warm and professional. Next time: check by vs until."). These emails are a bridge to Module 4, which is about writing.

Watch for in Module 4: C12 (reports and proposals) uses the same persuasion logic in writing: a proposal is a pitch on paper (problem → solution → benefit → evidence → recommendation). Make the link out loud. C13 (CVs, cover letters and interviews) uses features → benefits for a person: "I led a team of five" → "which means I can…".
