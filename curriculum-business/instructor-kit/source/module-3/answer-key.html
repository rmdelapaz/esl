Module 3 — Presenting, Negotiating & Persuading: Answers

Answers to every Module 3 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model answers for the open tasks (a 2-minute presentation, a confirmation email, a pitch with objection handling, an elevator pitch), the Deal Room points guide, and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 3.1–3.3Classes C9–C119 self-check items15 exercises · 5 models
Marking principle

Speaking and persuasion have criteria, not single answers. For closed items (matching, ordering, verb forms, feature vs benefit) there is one best answer and the key gives it. For open items (rewrites, trades, pitches, emails), the models are examples. Accept any version that (1) does the job the task sets (a reason + a way forward; an if on every concession; a benefit that is a payoff for the listener, not another fact), (2) is accurate (If you pay…, we'll…, not If you will pay), and (3) sits in a polite, professional register. Refer to the course's four criteria when you comment: Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy, Organization & Delivery. American English is the model; accept British spellings and forms (organised, summarise, levelled off, licence, programme, offence), and accept any currency.

3.1 · Presentations — Structure & Delivery (C9)

My Midpoint target in Module 3

No answers: each learner's target is private. Check two things as you circulate: the target matches the learner's score sheet (and is copied into section 4 of the Performance Tracker; see "From Scores to Targets" in assessment/assessment-key.html), and the "one action" is observable. ✘ "Improve my English." ✘ "Speak better." ✔ "In every talk I'll use one signpost between each point and pause after it." ✔ "Every email I write ends with an action and a date."

Exercise 1 — The five jobs (Tomás's opening)

1 greeting a · 2 introduce yourself b · 3 hook c · 4 topic + purpose + benefit d · 5 outline e · 6 housekeeping f

7 A question followed by a striking fact (52 days). Accept "a question" or "a surprising number." · 8 At the end ("I'll take any questions at the end").

Exercise 2 — Put the opening in order (Amira)

2 → 5 → 4 → 6 → 1 → 3: greeting ("Good afternoon…") → introduce ("I'm Amira Haddad…") → hook ("In January, one delivery in five…") → topic + purpose ("Today I'd like to show you…") → outline ("two parts…") → housekeeping ("Please feel free to ask…").

Also accept the hook after the topic (2 → 5 → 6 → 4 → 1 → 3), and the hook first, before the greeting, for confident learners: some presenters open cold with a fact. Not acceptable: the outline before the topic, or housekeeping in the middle.

Exercise 3 — Choose the signpost

1 b (moving on) · 2 a (giving an example) · 3 b (referring back) · 4 a (referring forward: "We'll come to that shortly") · 5 b (emphasizing) · 6 a (summarizing)

Why the distractors fail: In conclusion ends the whole talk, so it can't introduce point two · So much for cost means "I've finished talking about cost," the opposite of item 4 · Last but not least introduces the final point, not a look back · Firstly starts a list.

Exercise 4 — Describe the chart

Listening (Script 3.1-B): the two wrong figures are "79%" in Q1 (the chart says 81%) and "95%" in Q3 (the chart says 93%).

Gap-fill: 1 shows · 2 If you look at · 3 rose steadily · 4 rose sharply · 5 peaked at · 6 leveled off · 7 What this tells us is

3 and 4 are the only pair that could be confused: 81 → 84 over a quarter is small and even (steadily); 84 → 93 is big and fast (sharply). Accept British levelled off.

Exercise 5 — From slide-reading to speaking (models)
  1. "This slide shows customer complaints over the last two years. If you look at the 2024 figure, you'll see complaints fell sharply, from 240 to 130, almost half. What this tells us is that the new returns process is working."
  2. "I'd like to draw your attention to fuel. As you can see, it makes up almost half of our costs, at 45%, and drivers are another third. Repairs are only 12%. The takeaway here is that a small saving on fuel would have the biggest impact."
  3. "This graph shows visits to our website this year. Notice how they tripled between January and June, from about 3,000 to 9,000. In other words, the new online catalog is bringing people in, so now we need to turn those visits into orders."

Accept any version with the four steps: introduce · direct attention · describe the trend (with at least one accurate figure) · interpret. Watch for: reading every number; interpretation missing; ✘ the complaints were decreased (✔ fell / decreased); ✘ the 45% of costs (✔ 45% of our costs).

Dialogue — a question you can't answer

(a) buy time: "That's a great question." · (b) clarify: "Just to make sure I understand: do you mean the monthly fee, or the setup cost as well?" · (c) promise a follow-up: "I don't have the setup cost to hand, but I'll check with finance and follow up by Thursday." · (d) park: "That's a bit outside today's scope, I'm afraid. Can we take that offline?"

Presentation Carousel — a model 2-minute talk (role profile: Amira)

"Good afternoon, everyone, and thanks for coming. I'm Amira Haddad, and I coordinate operations here at Northwind. In January, one delivery in five arrived late. In October, it was one in fourteen. Today I'd like to show you how that happened, and why I think we should use the new route plan in every region. I've divided my talk into two parts: what changed, and what we should do next. Please feel free to ask questions as we go.

Let's start with what changed. In July we started using a route-planning app in the North region. As you can see from this chart, on-time deliveries rose steadily in the first half of the year, from 81% to 84%. Then, in Q3, they rose sharply and peaked at 93%. What this tells us is that the app, not the season, made the difference. For instance, one driver cut forty minutes from her morning route.

This brings me to my next point: what we should do next. The key point here is that the other regions still plan routes by hand. So, to sum up: the app works, it's already paid for in one region, and it could work everywhere. I'd like your approval to try it in two more regions next month. Thank you. Are there any questions?"

What makes it work (for your feedback): Task & Purpose: a clear request at the end · Register & Tone: neutral-professional, "you"-focused · Language: trend verbs used accurately · Organization & Delivery: five jobs, a signpost at every turn, about 260 words ≈ 2 minutes at presentation speed.

Self-check 3.1
Q1
Moving from the problem to the solution: which signpost?
A) "That's the problem. This brings me to my solution…"
B) "In conclusion, here's my solution…"
C) "As I mentioned earlier, here's my solution…"
Why: "This brings me to…" moves forward to the next point. In conclusion signals the end of the talk; As I mentioned earlier refers back.
Q2
A question you don't have the numbers for?
A) A long, general answer.
B) "That's not really relevant to my talk."
C) "I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up with you by tomorrow."
Why: honest, short and committed to a follow-up. A damages credibility (guessing, waffling); B sounds defensive and dismissive.
Q3 · new
The best way to present a chart of costs?
A) "This slide shows a table of costs."
B) "As you can see, costs fell sharply in Q2. What this tells us is that the new supplier is saving us money."
C) Read every number from top to bottom.
Why: B introduces, describes the trend and interprets it. A only labels the slide; C reads it and loses the audience.
Errors you will meet most (3.1)

✘ I will present you my project · ✘ Today I will talk about to reduce costs · ✘ discuss about · ✘ In conclusion used mid-talk · ✘ rised / was risen / the sales was growing up · ✘ the 45% of costs · decimal commas (2,5 million) · fifteen / fifty confusion · no purpose or benefit stated · two points with no signpost between them · reading from notes · rambling answers in Q&A. For range, praise a real hook, This brings me to… with a pause, What this tells us is…, and a calm "I'll follow up."

3.2 · Negotiations — Language & Strategy (C10)

Model dialogue — twenty desks
  1. Position: all twenty desks by the 1st. Interest: a new team of twelve starts on the 1st and needs somewhere to sit.
  2. "Can I ask what's driving the 1st?" (also: "So it's twelve desks that are urgent, not all twenty?" checks the interest)
  3. "If you can accept two deliveries, we'll send twelve desks by the 1st…" → Rafael gets flexibility on delivery (two drops instead of one rush). "If you pay within 14 days, I'll waive the second charge…" → he gets faster payment.
  4. "I'm afraid we can't cover both, because our drivers are fully booked that week. What we could offer instead is half price on the second delivery."
  5. "That's a big step for us, honestly."
  6. Open: "Thanks for making the time…" · Explore: "Can I ask what's driving the 1st?" · Propose and bargain: "Then here's an idea. If you can accept two deliveries…" · Close: "Wonderful. So, to confirm…" (Preparing happened before the meeting.)
Exercise 1 — Name the move

1 Open · 2 Probe · 3 Propose · 4 Reject · 5 Concede (a conditional concession) · 6 Accept · 7 Close (summarizing) · 8 Probe · 9 Accept · 10 Close (next steps, in writing)

Accept Propose for 5 if the learner argues "I'll need something in return" invites a trade; the key idea is that it isn't a free concession.

Exercise 2 — Don't give it away: trade it (models)
  1. "We can include free shipping if you increase the order to 500 units." / "…as long as you order before the end of the month."
  2. "We can start a week earlier provided that you sign the contract by Friday." / "…if you can be flexible on the final deadline."
  3. "If you commit to a two-year contract, we'll add two extra training days."
  4. "We could extend payment to 60 days if you place a repeat order in the spring." / "…as long as the first order is paid in full on delivery."

Accept any version that attaches a real condition and asks for something of value in return. ✘ "If you want, we'll give free shipping" (the if asks for nothing). ✘ "If you will order 500…".

Exercise 3 — A real offer or testing the water?
  1. commit · 'll reduce / will reduce (also can reduce) · R
  2. offered · would you consider · T
  3. 'll hold / will hold / can hold · order · R
  4. extended · would that help · T
  5. were · would you expect · T
  6. unless · R (a real condition)

Watch for: ✘ If you will commit · ✘ as long as you will order · ✘ If you was to double (accept was in speech from strong learners, but teach were as the safer form) · ✘ If we would offer.

Exercise 4 — Say no without closing the door (models)
  1. "I'm afraid 20% is more than we can do, because our margins on this product are already very tight. What we could offer instead is 8%, if you commit to a year."
  2. "I don't think we can manage Saturday, unfortunately, because our warehouse is closed on weekends. We could deliver first thing on Friday or Monday morning. Would either of those work?"
  3. "I understand where you're coming from, but that price is a little higher than our budget allows. Could we look at a smaller first order, or a longer contract, to bring it down?"

Each answer needs a softener (I'm afraid, unfortunately, I understand…but), a reason (because…) and a way forward (an alternative or a question). Register: no Forget it, no bare No.

Exercise 5 — From position to interest (models)
  1. "Can you help me understand what's behind the 10%? Is it a fixed budget, or a lower quote from someone else?"
  2. "What's making the 30th so important? Is it a launch, or something later that we could plan around?"
  3. "Can I ask why one year works better for you? Is it about flexibility, or about cost?"

Accept any open, polite question that asks why. Offering two possible reasons ("Is it X or Y?") makes it easier to answer. ✘ "Why do you want this?" in a flat tone can sound suspicious: add Can I ask… or Can you help me understand….

Deal Room — points guide (teacher only)

Walk-away score on every card = 40 points. "Meet in the middle" = the middle option on every issue (scenario 3 has only two date options). "Win–win" = each side gives way on the issues it cares less about.

ScenarioA "meet in the middle" deal (no exploring)A + B pointsA win–win dealA + B points
1 · Delivery contract
(A Brightline Foods · B Northwind Logistics)		$170 · 2 years · 30 days · 7–10 a.m.50 + 80 = 130$160 · 3 years · 30 days · before 7 a.m.80 + 90 = 170
2 · Launch deadline
(A Marketing · B Digital team, Maple & Stone)		March 15 · 4 features · $5,000 · 2 weeks' testing50 + 55 = 105March 1 · 3 features · $5,000 · 3 weeks' testing70 + 80 = 150
3 · Training venue
(A Silverleaf Clinics · B Harbor Point)		$1,800 · Thu–Fri · coffee only · 25%55 + 43 = 98$1,800 · Mon–Tue · full lunch · 25%80 + 90 = 170

Why the win–win works: (1) Northwind's drivers start at 5 a.m. anyway, so an early window costs them almost nothing, and Brightline is happy with a long contract because it plans to grow. (2) Marketing only needs three features for the trade show; the digital team only fears building five in time. (3) Harbor Point's Mondays and Tuesdays are empty; lunch costs its in-house kitchen very little, and Silverleaf cares most about feeding 60 staff. Pairs who asked "What's most important to you?" usually find these. Any deal above 40 for both sides is a valid result; "no deal" is 0 + 0.

Confirmation email — model (scenario 1, from Northwind's side; 112 words)

Subject: Confirming our agreement — store deliveries

Hi Tomás,

Thanks for a productive conversation today. Just to confirm what we agreed:

• a price of $160 per delivery
• a three-year contract, starting on March 1
• payment within 30 days of each invoice
• all deliveries to arrive before 7 a.m.

I'll send the contract over by Friday for your review. Please let me know if I've captured anything incorrectly.

It's been a pleasure working this out with you, and I'm looking forward to a long partnership.

Best regards,
Amira

Check with the criteria: Task & Purpose: all four terms, a next step with a date · Register & Tone: warm, neutral-professional (not Kindly do the needful, not Hey!) · Language: by Friday (✘ until Friday), articles (a three-year contract) · Organization: subject line, bullet points, close.

Self-check 3.2
Q1
The strongest conditional trade?
A) "OK, fine, we'll take another 5% off."
B) "We could do another 5% if you commit to a two-year contract."
C) "No, that's the price. Take it or leave it."
Why: B attaches an if and gets something back. A gives the concession away and teaches the buyer that pushing works; C slams the door and risks deadlock.
Q2
The best way to close?
A) Shake hands and leave.
B) Ask for one more concession.
C) Summarize, confirm, and send the details by email.
Why: a deal isn't done until it's summarized and written down. A leaves the terms vague; B reopens a settled deal and damages goodwill.
Q3 · new
"If you signed today, would you expect a discount?"
A) A firm offer.
B) Testing the water without promising anything.
C) A rejection.
Why: the second conditional (If + past, would) is hypothetical: the speaker explores what the buyer wants without committing. A firm offer would be "If you sign today, we'll take 5% off."
Errors you will meet most (3.2)

✘ If you will sign… · ✘ as long as you will pay · ✘ I want you give me discount · ✘ give discount (article) · ✘ I am agree · ✘ discuss about the price · ✘ until Friday for a deadline · a bare "No" or "Impossible" · free concessions ("OK, OK, free shipping") · proposing before asking any questions · agreeing to everything to be polite · a deal with no summary. For range, praise What's driving that?, a well-placed second conditional, a labeled concession, and a clean spoken summary.

3.3 · Pitching, Persuasion & Handling Objections (C11)

Model pitch (Amira)

(1) hook / problem · (2) solution · (3) benefit · (4) evidence · (5) call to action

Why the ask is easy to say yes to: it's specific (two regions, one month), low-risk (a trial, not a full rollout), and it includes a review ("review the numbers together"), so the director stays in control.

Exercise 1 — Feature or benefit?

1 F · 2 B · 3 F · 4 B · 5 F · 6 B · 7 F · 8 B

Each odd item is a fact about the product; each even item is its payoff for a person. Point out the pattern: benefits usually start with you / your.

Exercise 2 — Build the bridge (models)
  1. "…which means that fewer people forget their bookings, so rooms don't sit empty while others are looking for one."
  2. "…so that your cafés can order once a week and throw away far less food."
  3. "…which means that your staff can arrive by train without worrying about parking."
  4. "…so that your team spends its time on the difficult questions, and customers get simple answers in seconds, even at night."

Accept any bridge that ends in a human payoff (time, money, stress, reputation). ✘ "…which means that it sends reminders automatically" (the feature again).

Exercise 3 — Order the pitch and add the ask

b → d → c → a: hook/problem → solution → benefit → evidence.

Model call to action: "Could we switch on online booking at two more clinics for three months and compare the waiting times?" Also good: "I'd love your go-ahead to add it to the Westside clinic next month." A strong ask is specific (where, how long), low-risk (a trial) and asks for one next step. ✘ "So… what do you think?"

Exercise 4 — Answer the objection

A. 1 reframe to value (ROI) · 2 show what's different (differentiate) · 3 the cost of waiting (cost of inaction; also accept "offer a trial at a better time") · 4 find the real concern

B. Models: 1 "That's a fair concern: budgets are tight. Can I ask whether it's the total cost or the monthly cost that's the problem?" · 4 "Of course, it's an important decision. Can I ask what's giving you pause? Is it the price, the timing, or something else?"

Both lines must acknowledge before they ask, and neither should argue or answer yet. ✘ "But it's cheap!" ✘ "What is your problem?" (sounds rude: use Can I ask what's driving that?)

Exercise 5 — Confident, not over-hyped (models)
  1. "In our six-month pilot with three small shops, the app cut stock-taking time by about a third, and two of them saw sales rise by around 10%."
  2. "More than forty businesses in our region already use it. The sooner you start, the sooner you'll see the time savings, and we can set it up for you this month."
  3. "It's very reliable: in the last year it was available 99.9% of the time, and if anything goes wrong, our support team answers 24 hours a day."

Replace absolute claims (best, guaranteed, never, everybody) with specific, hedged evidence (in our pilot, about, around, more than forty) and honest urgency. Accept any invented figures that are plausible and marked as the learner's example.

Dialogue — "We already use spreadsheets"

Listen: the seller lets the client finish ("It looks nice, but…"). · Acknowledge: "That's a fair point. Spreadsheets are flexible." · Ask: "Can I ask what you like most about them?" and "Just so I understand: is the main worry learning a new tool, or losing your old data?" · Respond: "We import all your old spreadsheets for you, free of charge… Would it help if we tested it with last month's expenses first?" · Confirm: "Does that address your concern?"

Real concern: losing five years of data (not the tool itself).

Pitch & Push-back — a model with LAARC (card 2: lunch boxes)

Pitcher: You know how your cafés run out of lunch options by one o'clock, and customers walk out? We make ready-to-cook lunch boxes at Brightline Foods: your staff heat them in three minutes. That means you can offer a hot lunch all afternoon without a full kitchen. Cafés in our pilot sold an extra forty lunches a week on average. Could we start with a small order for two of your cafés next month?

Buyer: They look good, but honestly, it's too expensive for us.

Pitcher: That's a fair concern. Can I ask what's driving that: is it the price per box, or the size of the first order?

Buyer: Well, it's more that if we don't sell them, we throw them away. That's money in the bin.

Pitcher: I completely understand. So the real risk is waste. The boxes stay fresh for five days, and for the first month we can offer sale-or-return: you only pay for what you sell. Does that address your concern?

Buyer: If we only pay for what we sell, then yes. Let's try two cafés.

Notice: five stages in under 60 seconds; the objection is acknowledged and clarified before any answer; the hidden concern (waste) is found with one "or" question; the response removes the risk rather than cutting the price.

Elevator pitch — models (30 seconds)

Role profile (Priya Nair): "Hi, I'm Priya. I look after events at Harbor Point Conference Center, near the station. We help companies run training days without the stress: rooms, lunch and equipment all in one booking. Most of our clients rebook within the year. You mentioned your team training in the spring. Could I send you two dates and a price this week?"

An idea at work: "I'm Tomás from accounts. I've got an idea that could get us paid two weeks faster: automatic payment reminders. We tested it on twenty customers last month, and most of them paid on time. Could I have ten minutes at Thursday's team meeting to show you?"

Check the four beats: who · what · value · ask. It should sound like the learner, not like a script.

Self-check 3.3
Q1
Which is a benefit, not a feature?
A) It has a 500-page paper capacity.
B) So you can print all day without stopping to reload paper.
C) It's built with a two-tray design.
Why: B names the payoff for the user (no interruptions). A and C describe what the printer has: features.
Q2
"It's too expensive." The best first move?
A) "That's a fair concern. Can I ask what's driving that: the total or the monthly cost?"
B) "It's actually very reasonable. You're wrong about that."
C) "OK, I'll take 30% off right now."
Why: acknowledge, then clarify: the heart of LAARC. B argues and pushes the client away; C gives away value instantly and suggests the price was never real.
Q3 · new
Which claim is honest and persuasive?
A) "This is guaranteed to double your sales."
B) "In our three-month pilot, the team cut admin time by around 30%."
C) "Everyone in the industry is using it."
Why: B is specific (a pilot, three months), measurable and hedged (around). A overpromises; C is vague social proof that the listener can't check.
Errors you will meet most (3.3)

Starting with the product instead of the problem · listing features with no bridge · "which means that" followed by the same feature · no call to action ("…anyway, let me know") · several asks at once · ✘ It permits to save time · ✘ Let me explain you · ✘ very unique, more cheaper, most best · arguing with objections ("You're wrong") · dropping the price at the first objection · over-hyped claims (guaranteed, the best in the world). For range, praise a vivid "You know how…?", Imagine your team no longer having to…, an "X or Y?" clarifying question, and a confident falling-tone ask.
