Marking principle

Many Module 4 tasks are rewrites and open writing, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any rewrite that (1) keeps the meaning, (2) fits the register the task asks for (objective document style, a positive interview tone, a formal and warm client tone) and (3) is clear on the first reading. For recommendations and CV bullets, accept any version that is specific and uses the facts given; learners should not invent facts that contradict them. Where learners write about their own work history, mark the English only, never the history itself. American English is the model; accept British spellings and forms (prioritise, organise, programme, behavioural, CV, "We recommend that the team should be expanded").

4.1 · Reports, Proposals & Business Documents (C12)

Model — the executive summary (Northwind Logistics)

P (purpose): "This report examines the causes of late deliveries in the Northern Region in the first quarter and recommends a response." · F (findings): "Late deliveries rose from 6% to 14%… It was found that two causes account for most of the delay… Notably, 70% of late orders were on Monday and Tuesday routes." · R (recommendations): "We recommend three actions: (1)… (2)… (3)…" plus the expected result in the last sentence.

  1. Three actions: introduce the route-planning software by the end of May, lease a replacement van for the early-week routes, and review driver schedules in Q3. She also knows the expected result (below 6% by July). Accept any answer that names at least the software and the van.
  2. Notably.
  3. "It was found that…" (also accept "routes are still planned by hand" and "these steps are expected to…").
  4. Numbering shows priority and order, makes the actions easy to refer to in a meeting ("Let's approve 1 and 2"), and shows they are separate, concrete steps.

Exercise 2 — Order the sections

A. Report: g Title (1) → b Executive Summary (2) → d Introduction (3) → c Findings (4) → e Conclusions (5) → a Recommendations (6) → f Appendices (7).

B. Proposal: c Background / Problem (1) → e Objectives (2) → f Proposed plan (3) → a Benefits (4) → g Costs, resources & timeline (5) → b Risks (6) → d Recommendation / Call to action (7).

Accept Costs and Risks in either order (5/6), and accept an executive summary placed before Background if a learner adds one to the proposal: long proposals often have one. Written last, placed first.

Exercise 3 — What does the phrase do?
12345678910
RecommendPurposeFindingsDataConclusionRecommendFindingsDataPurposeConclusion

Signals: recommended / best course of action (a future action) · The aim of / sets out (why the document exists) · indicates / Notably (evidence) · As shown in Figure / Appendix (pointing to data) · On balance / suggest (interpretation). Accept "Data" for item 3 if the learner explains that it reports a figure; the key point is that it reports, and does not interpret or recommend.

Exercise 4 — Rewrite for a report (models)
  1. "The data indicates that customer loss was driven mainly by the price increase." / "The analysis suggests that the price increase caused most of the customer loss."
  2. "It was found that most complaints concern packaging." / "The analysis shows that the majority of complaints relate to packaging."
  3. "This suggests that the problem is growing." / "It appears that the problem is getting worse."
  4. "We recommend hiring two additional agents." / "It is recommended that the team be expanded."
  5. "In the trial, the new supplier's boxes reduced damage by 50%." / "The new supplier's packaging performed significantly better." (A number is better than an adjective.)
  6. "We recommend adopting the new system to improve efficiency." (27 words → 9, no jargon.)

Not yet: rewrites that keep I think / pretty sure / basically / way better; rewrites that become longer and more abstract ("It is our considered opinion that…"); passives that hide the meaning ("It was decided that it should be looked into"). Errors to expect: "It was found that most complaints are concerning about packaging," "The datas show," "It is recommended that the team is expand."

Exercise 5 — From vague to approvable

A (models).

  1. "We recommend introducing the Southern Region's route-planning software for Monday and Tuesday routes by the end of May, with the aim of reducing late deliveries from 14% to below 6%."
  2. "We propose moving 20% of the social media budget to email campaigns in Q4, as email returned $6 for every $1 spent, compared with $1.50 for social media ads."
  3. "As a priority, we recommend a two-week buddy program for all new staff, since 40% of order errors are made in employees' first three months."

Check each for S-A-P-C-T: a named action, an owner or a date where possible, a link to the fact given. Numbers may differ (e.g. 15% or 25% of the budget); any plausible, specific choice is fine.

B. 1 be renewed · 2 be moved · 3 should · 4 could · 5 hiring

Accept "should be renewed / should be moved" (common in British English). Don't accept "is renew," "will be renewed" or "to hire" (✘ We recommend to hire).

Writing task — Executive summary (model, Brightline brief card / Exercise 1 notes)
Model · 104 words

Reducing Damaged Online Orders — Brightline Foods

This proposal sets out a plan to reduce customer complaints about online orders. Complaints doubled from 120 to 240 per quarter, and a survey of 400 customers shows that 55% concern damaged packaging. Notably, an eight-week trial at the Eastfield warehouse cut damage by half. We recommend (1) switching all three warehouses to the new boxes by September, at an extra cost of about $3,600 a quarter; (2) keeping the current supplier as a backup for three months; and (3) reviewing complaint figures in Q4. As refunds for damaged orders currently cost about $9,000 a quarter, the change is expected to pay for itself.

Mark with the rubric. A strong summary has: the purpose in sentence 1; at least two numbers; numbered or prioritized recommendations; an expected result; no "This report will discuss…"; no I think. Support (B1) target: purpose + two numbers + two recommendations, about 80 words.

Speaking — The decision-maker (what usually happens)

Northwind: year-one cost is $9,600 (software) + $14,400 (van lease: $1,200 × 12) = $24,000; the director can approve only $15,000 without the board. Good outcomes: "approve with changes" (the software now; the van as phase two, or taken to the board with the penalty savings of about $36,000 a year as the argument). Brightline: about $3,600 a quarter extra against refunds of about $9,000 a quarter, of which the trial suggests half can be saved; the decision-maker's concerns are the trial size and supplier reliability (answered by the backup supplier). Kestrel: the director approves only if the tool replaces something: writers who mentioned retiring the four spreadsheets get a quick yes. Clearwater: the page ($6,000) fits the one-off budget; the new agent needs a separate hiring decision. Any reasoned decision is correct; praise writers who answered with a number and decision-makers who gave a condition rather than a flat no.

Self-check
Q1
Which best describes a good executive summary?
A) A short, standalone recap of purpose, key findings and recommendations: written last, placed first.
B) A teaser that lists what the report will discuss.
C) A section that only makes sense after reading the whole document.
Why: busy decision-makers may read only the summary, so it must stand alone and state the conclusions. B withholds the answer; C fails the reader who reads nothing else.
Q2
Which recommendation is most likely to get approved?
A) The company should improve customer support somehow.
B) We must leverage synergies to operationalize a best-in-class support paradigm…
C) We recommend hiring two agents by the end of Q3 to bring wait times below five minutes (see Appendix A).
Why: C is specific, actionable, time-bound, states the benefit and points to the evidence. A is vague; B is jargon that says nothing concrete.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is in formal, objective document style?
A) I'm pretty sure customers left because of the prices.
B) The data indicates that customer loss was driven mainly by price increases.
C) Customers left, basically because stuff got too expensive.
Why: B attributes the claim to evidence and uses neutral vocabulary. A is personal and uncertain in a chatty way; C is informal (basically, stuff).
Q4 · new
"Convince me to fund the new training program." What are you writing?
A) A report
B) A proposal
C) A meeting agenda
Why: the manager wants to be persuaded toward a future action and a budget: that's a proposal. A report would present findings; an agenda plans a meeting.
Errors you will meet most (4.1)

"It is recommended that the team is expand / expanded" (✔ be expanded) · "We recommend to hire" (✔ hiring / that we hire) · "discuss about," "emphasize on" · "informations, researches, feedbacks, equipments" · "14 percents," "an increase of 14%" said as "forty" · "As shown in the Figure 1" · decimal commas (14,5%) · old formulas (Please find herewith, Kindly do the needful) · summaries that end before the recommendations · "This report will discuss…" · recommendations with no action, owner or date. For range, praise Notably, … · This suggests that… · As a priority, … · In the short term…; in the longer term… and any well-formed subjunctive.

4.2 · CVs, Cover Letters & Job Interviews (C13)

Model — the CV extract (Nadia Ferreira)
  1. Duty bullet: "Responsible for the weekly stock report." Models: "Streamlined the weekly stock report, cutting preparation time from three hours to one." / "Produced a weekly stock report that reduced out-of-stock items by 25%." Any version with an action verb and a plausible result.
  2. The gap appears as a normal entry, "Career break," with dates, a short reason ("Family responsibilities," with no private detail) and what she did (a 120-hour certificate). It's honest, brief and positive.
  3. The degree is listed like any other, with the credential evaluation in brackets; the earlier job says "(overseas)" without apology. Both are facts, presented neutrally.
  4. Any three of: route planning, stock control, staff training, dispatch, logistics, supply chain, warehouse, food distribution.
Exercise 1 — Two answers, one question (Script 4.2-A)
S"Last spring, one of our biggest clients brought their product launch forward by two weeks."
T"As the project coordinator, I had to get forty store displays designed, printed and delivered…"
A"So I split the job into daily targets, I asked the printer to run two shifts…, and I checked progress with the team every morning…"
R"As a result, all forty displays arrived a day early, the launch went ahead on time, and the client has since signed a two-year contract with us."

Joy is the one to hire. Why Rafael is weaker (accept any two): no clear situation or task; "we" and "everybody" instead of "I," so we don't know what he did; no specific actions ("we did many things"); no clear result and no number ("in the end it was OK, I think"); he talks about the company, not the story; it ends with a vague claim ("We are very good with deadlines").

Exercise 2 — Duty → achievement (models)
  1. "Handled about 50 customer emails a day, maintaining a 95% same-day response rate."
  2. "Coordinated four company events a year for up to 120 guests, all delivered on budget."
  3. "Updated and improved the company website, increasing traffic by 25% in six months."
  4. "Streamlined monthly reporting by automating two spreadsheets, saving the team about six hours a month."
  5. "Trained 12 new hires, reducing errors in their first three months by 30%."

Accept any strong verb (answered, resolved, organized, planned, redesigned, automated, onboarded…). Not yet: a bullet starting with I ("I handled…": acceptable in speech, not on a CV), a bullet with no number, or Responsible for / Helped with kept at the start. Errors to expect: present tense for a past job ("Handle 50 emails"), "maintaining a 95% of response," "increased the traffic of 25%" (✔ by 25%).

Exercise 4 — Order the STAR answer

b → d → e → a → f → c (Situation: b, d · Task: e · Action: a, f · Result: c)

Model for "Tell me about a time you solved a problem" (a role-card version): "(S) In my last role, customers kept calling to ask where their orders were, and our phone waits had grown to over ten minutes. (T) As team lead, I was responsible for bringing the wait time down without extra budget. (A) So I analyzed a week of calls, found that most were about order tracking, and worked with IT to add tracking links to every confirmation email. I also wrote a short FAQ for the website. (R) As a result, tracking calls dropped by about 40% in a month, and average waits fell below five minutes." Accept any answer with a brief S and T, an Action in the first person with at least two steps, and a concrete Result.

Exercise 5 — Fix the weak line (models)
  1. Problem: generic ("any role") and empty adjectives. → "I'm applying for the Operations Coordinator role because my experience cutting delivery errors by 22% matches your need for someone who can improve process accuracy."
  2. Problem: badmouthing a past employer makes the candidate look like the problem. → "I'd achieved what I could there, and I was looking for a role with more responsibility, which is what drew me to this position."
  3. Problem: a fake weakness; it doesn't show self-awareness. → "I used to find it hard to delegate. Now I agree clear responsibilities at the start of a project and check in regularly, and it's made our team faster."
  4. Problem: "we" hides what the candidate did, and there's no detail. → "I called a short meeting, asked each person to explain their approach, and suggested a quick test to compare the two options."
  5. Problem: having no questions sounds like low interest. → "Yes, thank you. What does success look like in this role in the first six months?" / "What are the next steps in the process?"
Writing task — Summary and three bullets (model, role card 3: Chris Morgan)
Model · applying for Customer Service Team Leader, Clearwater Health Supplies

Summary: Customer service team leader with eight years' experience, including five years leading a team of seven in a busy contact center. Skilled in scheduling, coaching and reducing wait times. Returning to full-time work after a planned career break, with a new certificate in customer experience and a strong focus on service quality.

• Led a team of 7 agents, cutting average phone wait times from 9 to 4 minutes in six months.
• Introduced weekly coaching sessions, raising customer satisfaction scores from 81% to 90%.
• Redesigned the shift schedule, reducing overtime costs by 20%.

Check: summary = who + strengths + what they offer, no "hard-working team player"; each bullet = action verb + result; at least two keywords from the advert (team leader, wait times, scheduling, coaching).

Dialogue — the end of an interview

Chris's questions: "What does success look like in this role in the first six months?" and "What are the next steps in the process?" After the first answer, Chris links it to their own interest ("That's exactly the kind of project I enjoy"): it shows real engagement and reminds the interviewer of a strength. The close thanks the interviewer and mentions something specific (learning about the team).

Speaking — Mock interview trios

No fixed answers. A strong candidate: Tell me about yourself in under a minute (present → past → future, ending with this job); a STAR answer with "I" in the Action and a result with a number or a clear outcome; positive language about past employers; one or two genuine questions at the end. For role card 3 or any role card with a gap or an overseas qualification, a short, positive sentence and then back to strengths. Observers should quote, not judge.

Self-check
Q1
Which is the strongest CV bullet point?
A) Responsible for the sales team and its targets.
B) Led an 8-person sales team to exceed target by 18% in a year.
C) Hard-working salesperson who is great with people.
Why: B starts with an action verb and proves the result with numbers. A is a duty; C is empty adjectives.
Q2
What should the STAR Action step focus on?
A) The specific steps you personally took.
B) A detailed background of the whole company situation.
C) The final outcome and what you learned.
Why: the Action is the longest part and shows what you did. B belongs (briefly) in the Situation; C is the Result.
Q3 · new
A candidate mentions their last job. Which sentence is best?
A) "My boss was terrible."
B) "I'd achieved what I could there, and I was looking for a bigger challenge…"
C) "I'd rather not say."
Why: B is positive, honest and forward-looking, and links to the new role. A badmouths a past employer; C sounds like something is hidden. (If the real reason was a layoff or a move, a short neutral sentence is fine: "The company restructured, and my role ended.")
Q4 · new
Your CV shows a two-year gap. What's the best approach?
A) Change the dates so nobody notices.
B) Explain it briefly and positively, and mention anything you did or learned during that time.
C) Give a long, detailed explanation of your private life.
Why: B is honest and keeps the focus on what you offer. A is dishonest and can end an application; C shares more than an employer needs and uses interview time badly.
Errors you will meet most (4.2)

"I have an experience of five years" / "five years of experiences" (✔ five years' experience) · "I work there since 2019" (✔ I've worked there since 2019) · "responsible of," "in charge for" · CV bullets in the present tense for past jobs · "I am agree with the company values" · "I want this job because I need money" (register) · STAR answers in "we" with no result · rising intonation on every statement · "Do you have any question?" (✔ questions) · over-formal openings ("Respected Sir"). For range, praise streamlined, secured, exceeded, as a result, which led to, I'm particularly drawn to… and any calm, positive framing of a gap or a change.

4.3 · Review Project — The Complete Business Communicator (C14)

My toolkit

No right answers. Check that each learner has circled two strongest and two weakest areas; the weakest two feed the action plan (journal) and the Final Assessment page.

Exercise 5 — Order the follow-up email

d (1) → b (2) → g (3) → f (4) → c (5) → a (6) → e (7)

So: a = 6, b = 2, c = 5, d = 1, e = 7, f = 4, g = 3. Point out the structure: subject line with purpose → greeting → warm thanks → signpost ("To confirm what we discussed") → bullet-point decisions → an invitation to correct + a no-pressure next step → sign-off.

Dialogue — the negotiation

Acknowledgment: "I completely understand that budget is important, and I want to make this work for both of us." · Conditional trade: "if your team can commit to a two-year agreement, we could offer a 10% reduction and hold that rate." · Question inviting a reply: "Would that be a helpful direction to explore?" · Confirm in writing: "I'll put the proposal in writing so your team has everything to consider."

Speaking — The client scenario and the capstone planner

Scenario: no fixed script. Round 1 succeeds if the chair welcomes, makes brief small talk, proposes a light agenda and invites the client to begin. Round 2 needs at least one feature → benefit link and a call to action that respects consensus ("we can share a draft plan by Friday for your team to review"). Round 3 needs an acknowledgment, a conditional offer within the limit (10%, not 15%), space to decide, and a written follow-up. Capstone planner (model, from Script 4.3-B, expanded to 5 minutes): topic: a self-service order-tracking page · audience: the Clearwater leadership team · opening: greet, topic, hook ("Six out of every ten calls ask the same question"), what's in it for them, a three-part outline · problem: waits up from 4 to 11 minutes · solution: a tracking page + FAQ in six weeks · benefits: shorter waits, happier customers, agents free for complex calls ("…which means that…") · evidence: IT has built similar pages; the share of tracking calls (60%) · close: "So, to sum up… What I'm asking you to approve today is $6,000, so IT can start on Monday." · objections: adoption ("What if customers don't use it?" → agents mention it on every call; weekly figures for a month) and cost ("Why pay now?" → a one-off $6,000 against long waits every day). Check against the Capstone Pitch Brief checklist in assessment/final-assessment.html: signposts between every part, a specific call to action, two objections prepared.

Self-check
Q1
First call with a formal, consensus-driven, relationship-first team: which approach fits best?
A) First names, no small talk, a decision by the end of the call.
B) Open warmly and formally, make some small talk, and offer a written summary so their team can decide together.
C) Keep it casual with plenty of idioms.
Why: B starts formal, builds the relationship and gives time for a group decision, confirmed in writing. A clashes with all three preferences; C's idioms confuse and sound too casual.
Q2
A client asks for a bigger discount than you can offer.
A) Simply refuse and repeat the original price.
B) Immediately agree to the full discount.
C) Acknowledge the concern, then offer a conditional trade.
Why: C protects both the relationship and the deal: the client gets something in return for giving something. A can end the conversation; B gives away value for nothing.
Q3 · new
"That may be somewhat difficult for us." What does it most likely mean?
A) Yes, definitely.
B) Probably "no," or there's a real problem: ask gently.
C) They didn't understand you.
Why: in many workplaces, especially more indirect ones, this is a polite way to refuse or to flag a serious problem. The safe move is to check gently and offer an alternative.
Q4 · new
You're not sure what someone's communication norms are.
A) Use your own norms: they're the same everywhere.
B) Start more formal, observe and mirror, and ask when you're unsure.
C) Use lots of idioms to sound friendly.
Why: B combines three golden rules. A is the classic cross-cultural mistake (assuming your norm is universal); C makes you harder to understand.
Errors you will meet most (4.3)

"I am agree / We are agree with this condition" · "discuss about the price" · "If you will commit to two years, we will can…" (✔ If you commit / can commit…, we could…) · first names with a new senior contact · over-direct requests ("I want you send the contract," "Give me discount") · idioms (touch base, circle back, ballpark) · pushing for a yes after a soft signal · national generalizations in discussion ("They are always…") · capstones with no call to action or no number · reading a full script aloud. For range, praise There's no need to decide today · Would it help if I put this in writing? · I sense this may be difficult · If you can…, we could….

Final Assessment Page (C15–C16)

Guidance for the workbook page
  • Before Class 16 checklist. Check it at the end of C15 (the close stage) or at the start of C16: learners without a call to action or an interview slot need a quick word before the pitches start.
  • Performance profile. Learners fill it in after you return results privately (within a week of C16), copying the four criterion scores for Writing and Speaking at Class 1, Class 8 and the Final Assessment from their score sheets. It mirrors performance-tracker.html. If a learner missed a task, the row stays blank; never estimate a score for a missing task. If there was no scored interview, the Speaking score comes from the negotiation and the pitch (noted on the score sheet). Remind learners to read the profile by criterion across time, not as one rising line.
  • Action plan. A good plan names one real goal, two habits that are specific and frequent ("I'll put the ask in the first line of every email," "I'll tell one STAR story a week and record it"), a real situation in the next month and a review date. Push back gently on vague plans ("practice more English") with "When? With whom? How will you know?"
  • Final Assessment answers. Model answers for the C15 writing, the shape of a good deal for both negotiation scenarios and the scoring guidance for pitches and interviews are in assessment/assessment-key.html and the teacher pages of assessment/final-assessment.html.
