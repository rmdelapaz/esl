Module 4 — Business Writing & Career

The last five classes of the course. Three workshop classes, then the two-class Final Assessment. First, the documents that move decisions: reports and proposals, a standalone executive summary, a formal and objective document register, and recommendations that get approved. Then the documents and conversations that move a career: achievement-focused CV bullets, a targeted cover letter and job interviews with the STAR method, including how to talk about career gaps, career changes and qualifications from another country with confidence. Last, the review project: cross-cultural awareness, a rehearsal of the integrated client scenario, and the planning and first rehearsal of each learner's capstone presentation. C15 runs the timed writing and the negotiations; C16 runs the capstone pitches, the mock job interviews, a debrief, certificates and a celebration.

3 lessons + Final Assessment Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C12–C16 Weeks 6–8 90 min per class Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)

How to Use This Guide

  • One block = one 90-minute class. C15 and C16 are assessment classes with their own plans.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-writing-career.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C12.   • The Final Assessment is a separate document: assessment/final-assessment.html (learner tasks) and assessment/assessment-key.html (the one shared rubric, model answers, negotiation role cards and scoring sheets). Read both before C15. The task wording, word counts and timings printed there overrule any number in this guide.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and copy the stress and intonation.
  • This is the course's writing module. C12 and C15 ask learners to write under time pressure, and many working adults rarely write more than an email. Give real writing time in class (at least 10 minutes in C12, a full hour in C15) and keep the room quiet while people write. Protect the freer practice in every class: if you're running late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the role-play or the writing.
  • Feedback in three columns. During freer practice, take notes in three columns: register & tone (too direct, too casual, or too old-fashioned: You must approve this · Kindly do the needful · Hey, need a yes today), language (errors that cost clarity: discuss about, I am agree, responsible of, an experience of five years, informations, It is recommended that the team is expand) and clarity & structure (a buried recommendation, no clear ask, a STAR answer with no result, a pitch with no call to action). In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names, and always include one excellent phrase to spread.
  • One rubric, everywhere. All observer checklists and score slips use the course's single analytic rubric (full version in assessment/assessment-key.html): Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy and Organization & Delivery, each scored 1–5, total /20. Learners have used it since C1 and at the C8 midpoint check; in this module they use it to give each other feedback, and you use it for the Final Assessment.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling, like the kit (prioritize, organize, summarize, analyze, program, behavioral), but keep the word CV. Note that in the US and Canada a job-application CV is usually called a résumé; the kit says CV to match the online lessons. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them, and never mark an accent as "wrong."

The through-line

Write it so they can decide → present yourself so they can say yes → bring it all together. In C12 learners write for a busy reader: the answer first, the evidence behind it, and recommendations someone could act on on Monday. In C13 the same logic applies to their own career: prove results instead of listing duties, and answer interview questions with a structured story that ends in a result. In C14 they add the skill that ties the course together, adapting to other people's norms, rehearse the whole client scenario (email → meeting → pitch → negotiation → follow-up) and plan their capstone. C15–C16 turn that work into evidence: a timed writing task, a negotiation, a capstone presentation with Q&A and a short mock interview, all scored on the same four criteria learners have used since C1.

Career stories: the learner decides what to share

C13 and C16 ask learners to talk about work history, and your class may include job seekers, people between jobs, people working below their qualification level, and newcomers whose degrees and licenses are still being recognized. Nobody has to disclose their salary, employment status, immigration status or a real employer. Every CV and interview task offers three equally good options: your real history, a candidate role card (a fictional person with a fictional job and company), or a mix (your real skills in a fictional company). Say it every class: "You're practicing the English, not handing in your life story." Keep the tone respectful when a learner's history includes a career gap, a career change or a step to a more junior role in a new country: these are normal professional stories, and the language box gives positive, factual ways to tell them. Qualifications from another country are qualifications: never call them "not real" or "not valid"; talk about recognition, credential evaluation and local licensing as practical steps. Never collect, display or share a learner's real CV. If a learner offers to show you theirs, look at it privately. Interviewer cards contain only job-related questions: never salary, visa or immigration status, nationality, age, family, health or religion, and never why someone left a job or a gap in employment (the same rule as the Final Assessment interviewer card). A learner may choose to mention a gap or a career change; the C13 language box helps them do it positively. We don't role-play unfair or illegal interviews. For help with credential recognition or job searching, point learners to your program's career or employment services; don't give legal or immigration advice.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: report or proposal? The difference is emphasis: a report leads with what is; a proposal leads with what should be. A report may end with recommendations; a proposal must sell the benefits.

"Give me the findings from the customer survey." Report or proposal? (Report.) "Convince me to fund a second delivery van." (Proposal.) Can a report include recommendations? (Yes, often.) Which one must sell? (The proposal.) Which one looks mainly at the past and present? (The report.)

Meaning: the executive summary.

Do you write the executive summary first? (No: last.) Where does it go in the document? (First.) The director reads only this page. Can she make a decision? (She must be able to.) Can it say "This report will discuss the causes"? (No: state the causes.) Can it say "as discussed above"? (No: it must stand alone.) How long? (Short: a few sentences, about 5–10% of the document.)

Meaning: the subjunctive and should / could.

"It is recommended that the team be expanded." Has the team been expanded yet? (No.) Is this an order? (No: a formal recommendation.) Is it more or less formal than "We should hire more people"? (More formal.) Why "be" and not "is"? (After recommend / propose / suggest that…, formal American English uses the base form.) "We should prioritize the billing fix." Firm or optional? (Firm.) "We could also consider a weekend shift." (Optional: worth considering.)

Form.

PurposeThe aim / purpose of this report is to + verb · This report examines / assesses… · This proposal sets out…This report examines the causes of late deliveries in the first quarter.
FindingsThe results show / indicate that… · It was found that… (passive) · Notably, …It was found that 70% of late orders were on Monday and Tuesday routes.
DataAs shown in Figure 1, … (no article before Figure 1) · See Appendix AAs shown in Figure 1, costs peaked in March.
Conclusion (hedged)This suggests that… · It appears that… · On balance, …This suggests that the problem is seasonal.
RecommendWe recommend + -ing · We recommend that + subject + base form · It is recommended that + subject + be + past participleWe recommend leasing a second van. · It is recommended that the contract be renewed.
PriorityAs a priority, … · In the short term, …; in the longer term, … · numbered listIn the short term, we should fix the billing error; in the longer term, we could review pay bands.

Pronunciation. Findings live or die on numbers. Contrast the stress: fourTEEN vs FORty, fifTEEN vs FIFty, and say percent with stress on the second syllable (perCENT), never "percents." Recommendations are spoken in chunks with the key word stressed: We reCOMmend | HIRing two Agents | by the END of Q3. Data is DAY-ta or DAH-ta: both are fine.

  3. Transform it (individual, fast): T: "I think we lost customers because of the price." → S: "The data indicates that customer loss was driven by price." T: "We found out that most calls are about tracking." → "It was found that most calls concern tracking." T: "Firm: prioritize the billing fix." → "We should prioritize the billing fix." T: "Option: a weekend shift." → "We could also consider a weekend shift."
The decision-maker — how to run it
  • Grouping. Pairs. Give half the pairs brief pair 1 (Northwind Logistics / Brightline Foods) and the other half brief pair 2 (Kestrel Design / Clearwater Health Supplies), so neighbors can't copy. Odd numbers: one trio in which the third learner is an observer with the checklist and becomes a second decision-maker in round 2.
  • The information gap. Each card has two parts: Your proposal (the facts for your own summary) and When you are the decision-maker (your budget, priorities and two questions to ask your partner). The writer can't see the decision-maker's limits, so negotiation happens naturally (for example, the Northwind proposal costs more in its first year than the director can approve alone).
  • Writing (10 min). 80–120 words, in the workbook frame, with a title. Walk round and check three things only: the purpose is in the first sentence, there is at least one number, and the recommendations are numbered. Don't correct grammar now.
  • Decision rounds (6 min each). 1 minute of silent reading (the decision-maker must stop at 60 seconds: "That's all a director has"), 4 minutes of questions, answers and a decision, 1 minute of feedback. Decision language is on slide 7: I'm happy to approve this on one condition… · Could you clarify…? · I'd need to see… before I can approve… · Let's start with phase one.
  • Observer / partner checklist (in the workbook and on the card): Task & Purpose: purpose, findings and recommendations are all there; the ask is clear. Register & Tone: objective, no chatty phrases, no jargon; the spoken answers stay polite under pressure. Language Range & Accuracy: document phrases from the bank; recommend + -ing or the subjunctive used correctly; numbers accurate. Organization & Delivery: purpose → findings → recommendations; recommendations numbered or prioritized; clear, calm answers.
  • You sit with a different pair each round and take three-column notes. If a decision-maker says only "OK, approved," prompt: "Ask one of your questions first."
ICQs: How many words in your summary? (80 to 120.) What comes first? (The purpose.) When you're the decision-maker, how long do you read? (60 seconds only.) Can you show your partner your budget? (No: ask questions and decide.) What are the three possible decisions? (Approve, approve with changes, not yet.) What must you give with your decision? (One reason.)
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Give B1 learners the partially completed frame in the workbook: This proposal sets out… · Currently, … · Notably, … · We recommend (1)… (2)… · Together, these steps are expected to… They fill it from their brief card; 80 words is enough. Fewer items: Ex 3 items 1–6, Ex 4 items 1–4. As decision-makers, they read the two questions printed on their card and choose a decision from the three phrases on the slide. Target: a summary with a purpose, two numbers and two numbered recommendations.

Stretch (C1)

Strong writers tend to write too much. Set a strict 100-word ceiling and ask them to include (1) one hedged finding (This suggests…), (2) one risk with its mitigation (The main risk is…; to manage this, we will…) and (3) a recommendation in the subjunctive. As decision-makers, they must push back with a counter-condition ("I can approve the software now if the van is phased into Q3"). Extra: rewrite one recommendation for two readers, a finance director (cost first) and the team (workload first).

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Old-fashioned formulas taught as "business English" in some school systems: Kindly do the needful · Please find herewith · We humbly request · I am writing to inform you that… at the start of every document. Formal documents today are clear and modern: This report examines… · We recommend…
  • Density as a sign of respect. Some academic and civil-service traditions reward long sentences and heavy nouns (the implementation of the optimization of…). Say plainly: in most English-speaking workplaces, a short, clear document is the respectful one, because it saves the reader's time.
  • The recommendation at the end, or not at all. In some workplace cultures, telling a senior reader what to do feels presumptuous, so learners bury the recommendation or hedge it away. Explain that "bottom line up front" is the expected, polite norm in many English-speaking organizations, and that We recommend… is professional, not rude.
  • Uncountable nouns and verb patterns: informations, researches, feedbacks, equipments, advices; discuss about, emphasize on, recommend to do (✔ recommend doing / recommend that we do). The data shows / show: accept both.
  • Numbers: decimal commas (14,5%) and thousands points (9.600 $) from many European and Latin American systems. In US documents: 14.5%, $9,600, currency symbol first. Stress -teen / -ty clearly when presenting.
  • Articles: the data, the results, the appendix, but no article before a label: as shown in Figure 1, see Appendix A.

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Modesty norms. In many workplace cultures, saying "I achieved…" feels like boasting, and learners say "we" throughout or give credit to the team. Explain that interviewers in many English-speaking countries need to hear your contribution; "I" in the Action is expected, and you can still thank the team in the Result.
  • CV conventions differ by country: a photo, date of birth, marital status or nationality on the CV is normal in some markets and is left off in the US, Canada, the UK and others to reduce bias. Present it as "local norms," never as one right way. Learners decide what fits the market they're applying in.
  • "I have an experience of five years" and "since five years" (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Arabic patterns): teach five years' experience · I've worked there for five years. "Responsible of" / "in charge for": responsible for, in charge of.
  • Titles and first names. Some learners will call the interviewer "Sir" or "Madam" throughout; others use a first name immediately. Teach the middle path: Ms. / Mr. + surname until invited, or the first name if the interviewer introduces themselves that way.
  • Handshakes and eye contact vary by culture and by person. Don't require a handshake in the role-play; a greeting, a smile and "Nice to meet you" work everywhere. Steady eye contact is expected in many English-speaking interviews but is uncomfortable for some learners: suggest looking at the interviewer's face while listening and at the end of each answer.
  • Weakness answers. Learners from systems where admitting a weakness is risky may say "I have no weakness" or "I'm a perfectionist." Show the pattern: a real, non-essential weakness + what you do about it.

  • Over-direct requests from L1 patterns: "I want you send the contract," "You must decide today," "Give me discount." Soften with Could you…? · Would it be possible to…? · We'd need….
  • "I am agree" / "We are agree with this condition" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Arabic, Farsi and others): I agree · We agree to this condition. "Discuss about": discuss the price.
  • Idioms from the internet (touch base, circle back, low-hanging fruit, ballpark): learners pick them up to sound fluent. Remind them of the golden rule: with international partners, plain English is clearer and more professional.
  • Presentation nerves can be especially high for learners who've been judged on accent before. Say it plainly: "Clear beats perfect. Your accent is part of who you are; we score how clearly you communicate."

Class size

Six pairs fit in three rounds. With four pairs or fewer, run two rounds of 9 minutes and use the spare time for a quick debrief. Running the negotiations
  • Before round 1, say (from the run-sheet): "You have seven minutes. Start with a friendly opening, find out what matters to the other side, and trade: nothing for nothing. Before time is up, summarize what you've agreed. A deal isn't required. A clear, polite 'we can't agree today, so here's the next step' is a good result too."
  • Don't intervene. If a pair reaches a deal in 3 minutes, say only: "Check every item on your card: have you agreed all of them?" If a pair drifts into a personal argument, pause them: "Let's go back to the terms on your cards." Keep the conflict commercial.
  • Card A (seller / agency) protects a minimum and pushes for a long-term commitment: give it to a learner who needs stretch. For support, allow the Lesson 3.2 phrase card on the table and note it on the score sheet.
  • Scoring. You score the language and the process, not the numbers: exploring interests, trading, firm-but-polite register, conditionals, a clear summary (and the deal memo). A learner who reaches a modest deal politely and clearly can score well; a learner who "wins" by refusing to trade or by being rude can't score high on Register & Tone. The shape of a good deal and "what to listen for" are in the run-sheet and the assessment key: don't tell learners in advance.
  • Two scores, not five. The writing (Tasks 1 + 2) becomes one Writing /20; the negotiation, the C16 pitch and the interview together become one Speaking /20, on the four criteria. Make task notes now; decide the criterion scores after C16.

Running a fair assessment in a community or workplace class
  • Conditions matter more than the room. Quiet, spaced seats if possible, a visible clock, a sign on the door. A learner who arrives late starts late and finishes with everyone else; note it on their paper.
  • Accommodations. If a learner has an agreed accommodation in your program (extra time, enlarged print, a computer for writing), give it here too and note it.
  • Distress. If a learner becomes very upset, let them step out and decide whether to continue or finish at home as untimed practice; mark the paper "untimed." An assessment is never worth more than a person's wellbeing.
  • Role cards count the same. Task 2 Option A can use the learner's own experience or the role-card profile printed on the paper; both are scored the same way.

Watch-outs
  • No scores in C16. The final assessment document asks you to name improvements, never scores, in class. Keep all results private.
  • Real job news. Some learners may be waiting for a real interview result or may have just lost a job. Keep the practice interviews light and role-card-friendly, and never ask about real applications in front of the group.
  • Freezing in a pitch. Say quietly, "Take a breath. What's your next point?" or ask an easy question to restart. Score what they did, not the pause.
  • Food at the celebration. Check allergies, dietary rules and fasting periods before anyone brings food. A celebration can be just words, certificates and thanks.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C14: the module wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "write it so they can decide, present yourself so they can say yes, bring it all together": the answer first · a standalone executive summary · S-A-P-C-T recommendations (write it so they can decide) → action verb + result · STAR with I and a result · your path in one positive sentence · two questions at the end (present yourself) → start formal · read the soft signal · trade conditionally · confirm in writing (bring it all together). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you: use those notes when you write each learner's next step in C16.

What the Final Assessment tells you. It's the course's third set of evidence: a Writing /20 and a Speaking /20, set beside C1 and C8 on each learner's score sheet and performance tracker. The trend on each criterion is the finding, not a single total. Expect Organization & Delivery and Register & Tone to move most (structures and habits), and Language Range & Accuracy to move more slowly.

If you teach the course again: keep your three-column notes from C12–C14. The most frequent errors (discuss about, I am agree, five years of experiences, responsible of) and the most common clarity problems (the buried recommendation, the STAR answer with no result, the pitch with no call to action) make the best warmers for the next group.
