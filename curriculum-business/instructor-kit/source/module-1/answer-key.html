Marking principle

This is a communication course, so mark closed items (matching, ordering, sorting, dictation) as right or wrong, and open items (rewrites, subject lines, emails, pitches) against purpose and register. For open items, the models below are examples. Accept any version that (1) keeps the full message (the ask, the deadline, the numbers), (2) is in a register that suits the reader, (3) is polite and clear (one softener, not five), and (4) is accurate enough not to confuse. When a learner's version is correct but misses the lesson target, accept it and point to the target: "Good. Can you also frame it positively?" American English is the model; accept British spellings and conventions (Ms Alvarez, apologise, Yours faithfully, 12 September) whenever a learner uses them consistently. Use the four rubric criteria by name when you give feedback: Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy, Organization & Delivery.

C1 · Welcome pages (orientation)

How we work together, My goals, progress tracker

No answers: these belong to the learner. Check only that (1) each goal names a situation and something the learner wants to be able to do, with a time ("by C8") — "improve my English" is not yet a goal; help them narrow it ("Which situation? Emails to clients? Then: by C8, write a request email to a new client in ten minutes"), (2) goals are realistic for eight weeks, and (3) the progress tracker is filled in privately after you return the diagnostic notes in C3. Learners between jobs may write goals about interviews or networking: that's exactly right.

Warm-up — Three ways to ask

Most → least formal: B (1) → C (2) → A (3). Client: B (a first or formal contact) · Manager: C (neutral; B for a sensitive request or a very formal workplace) · Close colleague: A (or C). All three are acceptable at work; the choice depends on the reader and the relationship. "When you get a sec" is friendly-professional; avoid it with clients and senior people you don't know well.

1.1 · Business Register, Tone & First Impressions (C2)

Exercise 1 — Rank the register
Set1 (most formal)23 (least formal)
1b) Would it be possible to arrange…a) Can we meet tomorrow?c) Free tomorrow for a quick chat?
2b) …your assistance in this matter.a) Thanks for your help with this.c) Thanks a million!
3c) Unfortunately, I will be unable to attend…a) I'm afraid I won't be able to attend.b) Can't make it, sorry!

Clues to point out: would / it be possible · assistance · in this matter · unable · will be (full forms) = formal; contractions and dropped subjects ("Can't make it," "Free tomorrow?") = informal. "Can't make it, sorry!" is fine in a chat with a close colleague, not in a reply to a client.

Exercise 2 — Formal, neutral or friendly?

1 F · 2 FP · 3 F · 4 N · 5 FP · 6 F · 7 N · 8 FP. Accept N for 4 or 7 only; if a learner argues 5 is N, accept it if they explain ("in my team, 'just a quick one' is normal"): the point is that register is a spectrum. "Please do not hesitate to contact me" is formal and a little old-fashioned in email; "Let me know if you have any questions" is the everyday version.

Exercise 3 — Soften the tone (models)
  1. Could you give me a quick update when you have a moment?
  2. I'm not sure that approach would work here. Could we look at another option? (also: I see it a little differently…)
  3. Would it be possible to have this by 3? It would really help me meet the deadline.
  4. Would it be possible to arrange a meeting with your manager? (also: I'd like to set up a meeting with your manager, if possible.)
  5. I'm afraid we can't deliver on Friday, but we can deliver first thing on Monday. (positive framing: lead with or add what you can do)

Accept any version with one clear softener and the full message (the deadline "by 3" must stay). Don't accept over-softened versions ("Could you maybe possibly perhaps…") or ones that lose the request ("An update would be nice someday"). Number 2 must still disagree: diplomacy changes the tone, not the message.

Exercise 4 — Two first meetings (Script 1.1-A)
  1. Meeting 2.
  2. Any two: he answers "Yeah. Rafael." (no full name, no company, no "pleasure to meet you") · he describes his job vaguely ("that kind of stuff") · he asks for coffee and the meeting room before any courtesy · he just says "Hi" to Dev · he asks "how long will this take?"   3. He's the account manager for Brightline Foods: he looks after all their deliveries from Northwind's side.

Phrases heard in Meeting 2: ✔ It's a pleasure to meet you · ✔ Likewise · ✘ How do you do? · ✔ Thanks for making the time · ✔ I've heard great things about… · ✘ Have you two met?

Exercise 5 — Respond naturally

1 d · 2 a · 3 e · 4 b · 5 f · 6 c. "How do you do?" is a formal greeting, not a question: the answer is the same words. Accept "Likewise" for 1 as well.

Dialogue — Moving to first names

First names: Lena: "Please, call me Lena." → Sam: "Then please call me Sam." (Sam used "Ms. Ortiz" until invited.) Introduction: "Have you met my colleague? This is Mei Lin, who looks after our events." Softened request: "Would it be possible to go through them after the meeting?" Also point out "It's great to finally meet you in person" (a warm opener after phone or email contact) and Lena's role line: "I'm responsible for the design of…"

My elevator pitch and the Welcome Reception (models)

Model (role card 2, Amira Haddad): "Hi, I'm Amira Haddad. I'm an accounts assistant at Harbor Point Supplies, where I make sure our suppliers are paid correctly and on time. I'm a qualified accountant, and I'm currently completing my local certification. I understand your team is moving to a new invoicing system. I'd love to hear how it's going." Check: all four parts (name, role, what I do / value, hook); 3–4 sentences; no life story; a hook that invites a reply. The reception has no answers: use the observer checklist and the four rubric criteria. Good exit lines to praise: "It was great to meet you. I'll let you get back to the others." · "Let's continue this over coffee after the meeting."

Self-check
Q1
First email to a client. Which opening fits best?
A) Hey! Quick one for you…
B) Need to talk about the contract.
C) Dear Ms. Alvarez, I would like to introduce myself…
Why: a first external contact calls for a formal, respectful register. A is too casual; B has no greeting and sounds abrupt.
Q2
Which response best softens a refusal while staying professional?
A) No, I can't.
B) I'm afraid I can't make Tuesday, but I'm free Thursday if that helps.
C) Um, maybe I possibly might not be able to…
Why: it declines clearly and offers an alternative (positive framing). A is blunt; C is over-softened and sounds unsure.
Q3 · new
Which is the best neutral request to a colleague?
A) Could you send me the figures when you have a moment?
B) Send me the figures.
C) I would be most grateful if you could possibly perhaps send the figures, if it is not too much trouble.
Why: one modal question + one hedge = polite and clear. B sounds like an order; C is over-formal and over-softened for a colleague.
Q4 · new
A senior visitor says, "How do you do?" What do you answer?
A) I'm fine, thank you.
B) How do you do?
C) Not bad, and you?
Why: "How do you do?" is a very formal greeting, not a real question, so you repeat it. A and C answer a question that wasn't asked; C is also too casual for a senior visitor.
Errors you will meet most (1.1)

"I want you send me…" / "Give me…" (direct translation) · flat intonation on correct words · "I am agree" · "I am engineer" (missing article) · "I work here since 2019" (→ I've worked) · "Nice to see you" at a first meeting (→ Nice to meet you; see is for people you already know) · "I'm fine" in reply to "How do you do?" · "Mr. David" (title + first name) · stacked softeners ("Could you maybe possibly…") · over-formal set phrases ("It is an honor to make your acquaintance"). For range, praise Thanks for making the time · I've heard great things about… · I'm responsible for… · Would it be possible to…? · I'm afraid…, but…

1.2 · Professional Emails & Messaging (C3)

Model email — questions

1. Parts: 1 Subject: Training room booking… · 2 Dear Ms. Grant, · 3 Thank you for sending your conference brochure last week. · 4 We would like to book… I would be grateful if you could… by Friday, March 6. (+ the attachment line "Please find attached…", which is part of the body) · 5 Please let me know if you need any further details. I look forward to hearing from you. · 6 Sincerely, · 7 Sofia Brandt, Office Manager, Kestrel Design. 2. Purpose: to book the Harbor Room / confirm availability and the full price for the March 18 training day. 3. Ask: "I would be grateful if you could confirm availability and the full price…"; deadline: Friday, March 6. 4. Yes, formal throughout: Dear Ms. Grant … Sincerely · I would be grateful · We would like (full forms) · Please find attached · further details.

Exercise 1 — Put the email in order

c → b → e → d → f → a (subject → greeting → opening line → body / clear ask → closing line → sign-off + name). "Dear Mr. Patel … Best regards" is a normal, polite pairing in modern business email: neutral-formal and consistent. (Sincerely would be slightly more formal.)

Exercise 2 — Match the phrase to its function

1 opening · 2 attachment · 3 request · 4 follow-up · 5 bad news · 6 closing · 7 follow-up · 8 request. Accept "request" for 4 or 7 if the learner explains that a follow-up also asks for something: they overlap. "Regrettably" is formal; "Unfortunately" is the everyday version.

Exercise 3 — Fix the subject line (models)
  1. Slides for Friday's review: your comments by Wed? (also: Please review my slides by Wednesday)
  2. Question about invoice #3390 (also: Quick question re: invoice #3390)
  3. Action needed: approve weekend overtime by 4 p.m. today
  4. Move Thursday team meeting to Fri 10 a.m.? (also: Team meeting: new time Friday 10 a.m.)
  5. FYI: warehouse closed Monday (inspection)

Accept any subject line that names the topic and signals the action or deadline (or "FYI" when no action is needed). Short is good: 4–9 words. Don't accept "URGENT" on 5: nothing is required, so it isn't urgent.

Exercise 4 — Rewrite the blunt email (model)

Subject: Sales figures + a quick slide fix, today if possible

Hi Marco,

Could you send me the latest sales figures when you get a moment? I'd need them by the end of the day for tomorrow's review, if that's doable.

One more thing: I think a couple of numbers on the slides need updating. I'm happy to point them out on a quick call.

Thanks so much,
Ana

What changed: a specific subject; a greeting; a softened request with a deadline; the criticism reframed diplomatically ("need updating," not "are wrong") with an offer of help; a warm sign-off. Friendly-professional or neutral are both fine here (an internal colleague). Strong learners may split it into two emails (one purpose each) or number the two asks: both acceptable.

Exercise 5 — Chat or email? Reply or Reply All?

1 chat (quick, informal, right now) · 2 email (external, formal, needs a record) · 3 CC · 4 Reply (only the sender needs your thanks) · 5 BCC (protects people's addresses) · 6 Reply All (everyone genuinely needs it). For 5, some organizations use a mailing tool instead; BCC is the answer for ordinary email.

Exercise 6 — The manager's voicemail (Script 1.2-A)
Write toMr. Daniel Okoro (O-K-O-R-O), Harbor Point Supplies
What we needan updated quote; the price must include delivery
Quantity and item40 standing desks, model KD-20
Deadline (and why)by Friday the 14th, because the budget meeting is the following Monday
What to attachour original specification
Registerformal: it's the first time working with them

Traps: forty (desks) vs fourteenth (the date): check learners didn't write "14 desks" or "Friday the 40th"; "not the KD-12" (Lena mentions an old model).

The formal request (model)

Subject: Request for updated quote: 40 KD-20 standing desks, by Friday the 14th

Dear Mr. Okoro,

I am writing on behalf of Kestrel Design to request an updated quote for 40 standing desks, model KD-20.

I would be grateful if you could include delivery in the price and send us the quote by Friday the 14th, as our budget meeting takes place the following Monday.

Please find attached our original specification for your reference.

Please let me know if you need any further information. I look forward to hearing from you.

Sincerely,
Kenji Mori
Graphic Designer, Kestrel Design

The voicemail doesn't name the learner's company: they use their real company (optional), a role card or an invented one. Check: all six facts; a specific subject with the deadline; a formal frame (Dear Mr. Okoro … Sincerely / Yours sincerely / Kind regards); one purpose; the ask near the top. Accept "Thank you in advance for your help" as the closing. Don't accept "Dear Daniel" (too familiar for a first contact) or "Hi Mr. Okoro" (mixed register).

Reply in role (models for the four Supplier Reply cards)
  1. Yes, on time. "Dear Mr./Ms. ___, Thank you for your email. Please find attached our updated quote for 40 KD-20 standing desks at $389 per desk, including delivery (total $15,560). The quote is valid for 30 days. Please let me know if you have any questions. Sincerely, Daniel Okoro"
  2. Yes, but one detail is missing. "…Thank you for your request. Before I can send the final quote, could you please confirm the delivery address and the color you would like (black or white)? I will then send the quote within one working day. Kind regards, Daniel Okoro"
  3. A delay, with an alternative. "…Unfortunately, the KD-20 is out of stock until next month. We can deliver it in five weeks, or we can offer the KD-24, a very similar model, at $405 per desk including delivery, available now. Please let me know which option you prefer, and I will send the quote by Friday. Sincerely, Daniel Okoro"
  4. A question before quoting. "…Thank you for your email. Could you let me know whether you would like the desks assembled on site? Assembly costs an additional $25 per desk. As soon as I hear from you, I will send the full quote, within 24 hours. Kind regards, Daniel Okoro"

$389 × 40 = $15,560. Look for: the reply answers the request; bad news uses Unfortunately / I'm afraid and positive framing (card 3); questions are softened (cards 2 and 4); the frame stays formal.

Self-check
Q1
Which subject line best follows the "specific and useful" rule?
A) Budget approval needed by Thu 5 p.m.
B) Important!!
C) Budget
Why: it says the topic and the action and deadline, so the reader knows exactly what to do. B and C tell the reader almost nothing.
Q2
"Dear Sir or Madam," (British style). Which sign-off matches?
A) Yours faithfully,
B) Yours sincerely,
C) Cheers,
Why: an unnamed reader pairs with "Yours faithfully" (never two S's together: Sir → faithfully). A named reader pairs with "Yours sincerely." In American style, "Sincerely," is used for both, so accept a learner who explains that. C is far too casual.
Q3 · new
An email about a room change went to 30 people. You want to thank the sender.
A) Reply All.
B) Forward it to your team with "Thanks!"
C) Reply only to the sender.
Why: the Reply-All test: does every person need to read this? No, only the sender. A fills 29 inboxes; B sends it to people who didn't need it.
Q4 · new
Which closing line is correct?
A) I look forward to hear from you.
B) I look forward to hearing from you.
C) I am looking forward for your reply.
Why: in look forward to, to is a preposition, so it takes a noun or -ing: to hearing / to your reply. C has the wrong preposition (for).
Errors you will meet most (1.2)

Vague or blank subject lines · the ask in the last paragraph, or no deadline · mismatched frame ("Dear Mr. Okoro … Cheers," "Hi Mr. Okoro") · "Dear Mr. Daniel" (title + first name) · archaic or regional formulas ("Kindly do the needful," "Please revert," "Thanks and regards") · "I'm writing for ask" · "look forward to hear" · "discuss about" · "informations / advices / equipments" · "Please find attach" · "I send you the file" (→ I've attached / I'm sending) · "until Friday" for a deadline (→ by Friday) · several purposes in one email. For range, praise I would be grateful if you could… · Further to our conversation · As a gentle reminder · Please find attached… for your reference · Unfortunately…, but we can…

1.3 · Telephoning & Virtual Calls (C4)

Exercise 1 — Match the phrase to the stage

a 3 (connect / hold) · b 4 (unavailable) · c 1 (answer / identify) · d 2 (purpose) · e 5 (message) · f 6 (end) · g 3 · h 2. "Is this a good time to talk?" belongs to the caller's opening: it's a courtesy before stating the purpose. Accept 1 for h if the learner explains it as part of the opening.

Exercise 2 — Put the call in order

b → d → f → h → a → e → g → c. Priya answers and identifies (b); Rafael gives his name, company and who he wants (d); Priya signposts the hold and offers a message (f); Rafael gives the reason for the callback (h); Priya asks for his details (a); Rafael spells his name and gives the number (e); Priya reads it back (g); Rafael closes (c).

Dictation — names, numbers, dates (Script 1.3-C)
  1. Tomasz Nowak
  2. (206) 555-0172
  3. 7700-B
  4. 15 boxes by the 14th
  5. s.brandt@kestreldesign.com
  6. Thursday, April 3, at 2:30 (Eastern)

Accept any clear format for numbers (206-555-0172; 3 April; 2:30 p.m.). Common errors: 50 for 15, 40th for 14th, 770B (a missed "double oh"), Brand for Brandt, a missing dot in the email. Accept "Tomas" only if the learner asks you to spell it: that's the point.

Exercise 4 — Fix the message (model)

(a) Missing: who called (name), which company, their number, which order / what it's about, when they called and by when they need a callback. (b) Model: "Sam Bexley, from Harbor Point Supplies, called at 10:15 about order #4471 (the delivery is two boxes short). Please call back at (415) 555-0126 before 5 p.m. today." (c) Saying it: "Bexley: B as in Bravo, E as in Echo, X as in X-ray, L as in Lima, E as in Echo, Y as in Yankee. Four-one-five, five-five-five, oh-one-two-six." Then: "So that's Bexley, B-E-X-L-E-Y, at (415) 555-0126. Is that right?" Any clear spelling words are fine.

Exercise 5 — Video-call trouble

1 c · 2 a · 3 e · 4 f · 5 b · 6 d. Accept "Can you hear me?" type answers as extra language for 1, and "Could you drop it in the chat?" as a helpful follow-up for 3.

Dialogue — Opening a video meeting

Audio check: "Can everyone hear me OK?" · Mute: "Priya, I think you're on mute." · Screen share: "I'll share my screen so we're all looking at the same agenda." · Ground rule: "Let's use the chat for any questions as we go" (also "I'll pause after each item") · Handover by name: "Tom, over to you for item one."

Self-check
Q1
A caller asks for a colleague who is in a meeting. Which response fits best?
A) One moment, I'll put you through.
B) He's not here. Call back later.
C) I'm afraid he's in a meeting right now. Can I take a message?
Why: it explains the absence politely and offers a next step. A connects to someone who isn't available; B is blunt and gives the caller nothing.
Q2
You didn't quite catch a client's phone number.
A) Write down your best guess and move on.
B) "Sorry, could you repeat the last three digits? Let me read it back to check."
C) Say nothing and hope it arrives in an email.
Why: ask specifically, then read it back. Guessing (A) or hoping (C) risks a wrong callback, which costs far more than five seconds of checking.
Q3 · new
What is the best way to start a call that you make?
A) This is Ana from Northwind Logistics.
B) I am Ana. Who are you?
C) Hello, here is Ana speaking from Northwind.
Why: on the phone, English uses This is… + name + company. B is a direct translation and "Who are you?" sounds rude (→ Who's calling, please? is for the receiver). C mixes here is (a translation) with speaking, which is for answering.
Q4 · new
On a video call, a colleague's audio keeps cutting out.
A) Nothing. Pretend you heard and continue.
B) "Your internet is terrible."
C) "Sorry, you're breaking up a little. Could you repeat that last part?"
Why: name the problem neutrally ("breaking up a little") and ask for a repeat. A risks a misunderstanding; B blames the person and sounds rude.
Errors you will meet most (1.3)

"I am Ana / Here is Ana" (→ This is Ana / Ana speaking) · "Who are you? / Who is speaking?" (→ Who's calling, please?) · answering with only "Hello?" · a silent hold · no read-back · fifteen / fifty and fourteen / forty confusion · "call me back to this number" (→ at / on) · letter names A / E / I and G / J confused when spelling · "Speak slowly!" (→ Would you mind speaking a little more slowly?) · "Would you mind to spell" (→ spelling) · 03/04-style dates read the local way. For range, praise Bear with me a moment · I'm afraid…, can I take a message? · Let me read that back to you · B as in Bravo · Sorry, you go ahead · Over to you, Priya.
