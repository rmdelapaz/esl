Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 1 — Professional Communication Foundations

Four classes that give learners a starting point and the three everyday tools of working life. The course opens with an orientation class, a workplace-needs survey and a short diagnostic (C1), framed as a map, not a verdict. Then three communication workshops: register, tone and first impressions, the "how" behind everything else in the course (C2); professional emails and messaging, the most-used skill at work (C3); and telephoning and virtual calls, where learners lose the safety net of body language and must clarify and confirm out loud (C4).

3 lessons + orientation Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C4 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C1 is the orientation, needs-analysis and diagnostic class; C2–C4 teach online lessons 1.1–1.3. These are communication-skills workshops, so every lesson class follows the same shape: warmer (review) → lead-in linked to learners' real needs → a model (dialogue, email, call) → notice and clarify the language (phrase banks, register, Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions, CCQs) → drill key phrases (choral → individual; stress, intonation, polite tone) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a role-play or simulation with A/B cards) → feedback on register, tone and clarity, with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes, and the numbers in the Time column are minutes. If you're running late, cut the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the role-play or the feedback: using the language under a little pressure, then hearing how it landed, is what learners can't get from the online course.
  • Present from slides/module-1-foundations.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the word-only phrase flashcards, the six role cards, the C1 Find Someone Who and speaking-sample interviewer card, the C2 Welcome Reception cards, the C3 Supplier Reply cards and Register Dominoes, the C4 phone-call A/B cards, video-meeting cards and observer checklist, and all the listening scripts.
  • The needs survey and the diagnostic tasks are in assessment/needs-analysis.html; the course rubric, model answers and scoring sheets are in assessment/assessment-key.html. Both are written separately from this guide. Always follow the timings and instructions printed on those papers; the C1 plan below budgets about 15 minutes for the survey and 30 minutes for the writing sample.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and choose a US or UK voice. The classroom is where they use the language with a real listener and get feedback on how it sounded.
  • One rubric, all course. Every speaking and writing task in this kit is observed with the same four criteria from assessment/assessment-key.html, each scored 1–5: Task & Purpose (did it achieve the goal: all points covered, a clear action), Register & Tone (the right formality, politeness, diplomacy), Language Range & Accuracy (phrases, grammar, vocabulary) and Organization & Delivery (structure, signposting, fluency, pronunciation, interaction). In Module 1 you don't give scores in lesson classes; you use the criteria as your note-taking columns and teach learners to use them on each other through the observer checklist.
  • Feedback on register, tone and clarity. In every freer task, walk around and note (1) lines that were too blunt or too casual for the listener ("I want the report," "hey, need those numbers"), (2) lines that were over-formal or over-softened ("I am writing to inform you that I would possibly maybe like…"), (3) accuracy slips that blur meaning ("discuss about," "I am agree," missing articles), and (4) good language to praise and spread. In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, never with names, and let learners fix them in pairs.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and conventions, like the kit (practice, apologize, finalize, Ms. Alvarez, US-style phone numbers such as (312) 555-0147). Lesson 1.2 also shows the British letter pairing (Dear Sir or Madam … Yours faithfully) next to American Sincerely. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them: many learners will work with British, Irish, Indian, Australian or international colleagues. The one rule: be consistent inside one email.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Choose the register → write it clearly → say it clearly. C1 finds out what each learner actually needs English for at work and where they are now. C2 builds the idea that runs through the whole course: the same message can be formal, neutral or friendly-professional, and tone (softeners, positive framing, intonation) decides whether it sounds courteous or blunt. C3 applies register to writing: the seven-part email, matching greeting and sign-off, one purpose and a clear, dated ask. C4 applies it to the voice: call stages, message-taking and, above all, clarifying and confirming names, numbers and dates. Every class ends with something learners keep: goals and a starting profile (C1), an elevator pitch (C2), an email template (C3), a call "cheat sheet" (C4).

Adults with careers: respect, privacy and the role-card option

Your learners are working adults. Some use English at work every day and want polish; some are looking for work; some are newcomers who were accountants, engineers, nurses or managers in another country and are rebuilding a professional career here, sometimes in a job below their experience. Treat every learner's experience as expertise ("You've managed a team of twelve: tell us how you ran meetings"), and treat foreign credentials with respect: never "back home," always "in your previous role" or "in your field."

Nobody has to disclose anything. Learners never have to say where they work, whether they are employed, how much they earn, their immigration status or why they are in the class. Every personal or work-history task has a role-card option: one of six fictional professionals with a job, a company and contact details (materials, set B). Put the cards on a side table in every class so anyone can take one without asking. Say it on day one and repeat it before each role-play: "Your real job, a role card, or a job you invent. All three are equally good."

All companies, people and products in this kit are fictional (Northwind Logistics, Brightline Foods, Kestrel Design, Harbor Point Supplies, Crestview Hotels). Keep role-play conflicts ordinary: deadlines, budgets, schedules, delivery delays. Don't use layoffs, discrimination or politics as practice material, even if a learner suggests it.

Using the diagnostic and the two performance checks

The course has three measurement points: the diagnostic (C1: a reply email and a 3-minute speaking sample), the Midpoint Performance Check (C8: a timed email + chat-message rewrite and pair phone and meeting simulations) and the Final Assessment (C15–C16: writing, negotiation, presentations and mock interviews). All three use the same rubric, so learners can see real progress on the same four criteria. Present each as information, not judgment. The diagnostic is short and cold, and learners haven't yet met the course's phrase banks, so it usually underestimates them; say so. What matters is the pattern: is the learner's main gap Register & Tone (too blunt, too formal), Language Range & Accuracy, or Organization & Delivery (a buried request, no structure, hesitant delivery)? Return a short private note to each learner by C3, and let them record their scores in the workbook progress tracker.

  • Print the role cards (set B) twice and put them on a side table from C1 onward. Print one Find Someone Who sheet per learner and the speaking-sample interviewer card (set C). Bring name cards, a phone timer and one folder per learner for the diagnostic.
  • For C2: the register pair flashcards (set A1) and the Welcome Reception situation cards (set D, one set per group of 3).
  • For C3: the email-function flashcards (set A2), the Supplier Reply cards (set E, one per pair) and Register Dominoes (set G, one set per group of 3–4) for the warmer or early finishers.
  • For C4: the call-stage flashcards (set A3), the phone-call A/B cards (set F, one set per pair), the video-meeting cards (one set per group of 4) and one observer checklist per learner. Arrange chairs back to back for the phone role-play if the room allows.

A professional, low-stress first class — what to say and do
  • Model the course in the way you run the room. Greet at the door, use names, start on time, finish on time. Learners notice, and C2 will name these habits ("Be punctual; use names").
  • Your four promises (read them aloud; they're printed in the workbook): 1. Your results are private: I'll never share or compare them. 2. I'll tell you honestly what's working and what to focus on. 3. You never have to share personal information: your job, your employer, your status or your salary. A role card is always fine. 4. Every mistake in this room is useful: we correct the language, never the person.
  • Respect experience. Many learners had senior roles before. When someone says "I was a finance director," respond with interest ("Then you'll have a lot to teach us about meetings") and move on; don't probe why they're here.
  • If a learner becomes anxious during the speaking sample, slow down, smile and say: "That's fine. Take your time; there's no hurry." If they freeze, offer the role card: "Would you like to be Daniel from Northwind Logistics instead?" Many learners find it easier to speak as someone else.
Running the speaking sample
  • Use the tasks printed in assessment/needs-analysis.html and the interviewer card (materials, set C). Three minutes per learner: about one minute for the self-introduction (real or role card), then a short phone-message role-play in which you are the receptionist and the learner leaves a message, as the paper describes. Keep the phone timer where the learner can't see it.
  • Sit at the side of the table, not opposite. Be warm but neutral: don't finish sentences or correct. Take notes on the four rubric criteria only after the learner leaves.
  • Class of up to about 8: you'll reach everyone across the two stages. Larger class: reach as many as you can, then take the rest at the side table during C2's controlled-practice stage (never during the role-play). Alternatively, a learner can record the self-introduction and a phone message on their phone at home and bring it to C2.
  • With the learner's permission, record the sample on your phone so you can mark it later and play a few seconds back in Module 4 as a "before" recording. Say that you'll delete recordings at the end of the course, and do.
ICQs before the survey and the diagnostic
Survey: Do you have to write the name of your company? (No.) If you're not working at the moment, what can you do? (Answer for the job I want, or use a role card.) What do you compare with your partner? (Only my top three situations.) · Diagnostic: Is this a test with a grade? (No, a starting map.) Who sees your results? (Only me and the teacher.) What do you write? (A reply email.) If I call your name, what happens? (Three minutes of speaking at the side table.) Do you lose writing time? (No, I get the minutes back.)
Differentiation
Support

B1 learners may find the survey's vocabulary heavy. Read the situation list aloud and gloss quickly (chair a meeting = lead it; negotiate = agree a deal). In the writing sample, say privately: "If you can't finish, that's useful information. Write what you can." On My goals, give a frame on the board: "By ___, I want to be able to ___ in English when I ___." A learner can dictate their goals to you.

Stretch

C1-level learners who finish early should reread for tone: "Would the reader feel respected? Is the request easy to find?" In My goals, ask them to make one goal about register-shifting (for example, "I want to sound less formal with my own team without sounding careless") or about leading (chairing, presenting). Ask them to notice one email this week that they think is too formal.

Watch-outs for the first class
  • Status and disclosure. "What do you do?" can be a painful question for someone between jobs or working below their qualifications. Never go round the room asking it. The icebreaker and the survey both allow a pass or a role card.
  • Names and titles. Some learners expect to call you "Teacher," "Sir," "Madam" or "Mr./Ms. + first name." Tell them what you'd like to be called and practice it; C2 will explain first names versus titles in English-speaking workplaces as a tendency that varies by company.
  • The writing sample will show you the patterns you'll meet all module: very formal or archaic formulas ("Kindly do the needful," "I am writing to inform you that…"), very direct requests translated from another language ("I want you send me…"), a missing subject line or sign-off. Don't correct them today; note them for C2 and C3.
  • Handshakes, eye contact and distance vary across cultures and individuals. At the door, let learners lead: offer a hand only if they do, or simply smile and nod.

Business Register, Tone & First Impressions

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain register (the level of formality) and tone (the attitude words carry), and place a message on the spectrum: formal · neutral · friendly-professional · too casual

  • greet people, introduce themselves and others, and respond naturally ("Likewise," "How do you do?" → "How do you do?")
  • deliver a 3–4 sentence elevator pitch (name + role + what you do / value + a friendly hook), real or from a role card

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: register. The level of formality, chosen for the reader and the situation. It isn't "good" or "bad": the right register depends on who, how well you know them, and how sensitive the topic is.

"I would be grateful if you could send me the report." Is this rude? (No.) Is it wrong for a close colleague? (Not wrong, but a little cold or distant.) When is it the best choice? (First contact, a senior or external person, a sensitive topic.) · If you don't know which register to use, which is safest? (Neutral.) Is it easier to become more formal or less formal later? (Less formal: relax later.)

Meaning: tone. The attitude your words carry: warm, confident, apologetic, firm. The same register can have a good or a bad tone.

"You're wrong." Is it clear? (Yes.) Is it professional? (No, it sounds like an attack.) "I see it a little differently. Could we look at it again?" Does the speaker still disagree? (Yes.) What changed? (The tone, not the message.) · "Could you possibly maybe send it sometime if that's OK?" Polite? (Yes, too polite.) How does the speaker sound? (Unsure.) How many softeners do we usually need? (One.)

Meaning: positive framing. Lead with what you can do.

"I can't do it today." vs "I can have it ready by Monday." Which one gives the reader a plan? (The second.) Is the answer still "not today"? (Yes.)

Pronunciation. Polite requests rise and fall: the voice starts a little higher and moves on the stressed word: "Could you SEND me the rePORT?" A flat, low voice turns the same words into an order. Stress: REGister · proFESsional · COURteous /ˈkɝːtiəs/ · imPRESsion · reSPONsible. Weak forms: Could you /kʊdʒə/, to meet you /tə miːtʃə/. "Likewise" = LIKEwise.

Drill script
  1. Polite or flat? (choral, then individual): T says "Could you send me the report?" with a warm rise-fall; S repeat. T says it flat; S say "Flat!" Then: "Would it be POSSible to arRANGE a quick MEETing?" · "I'm aFRAID that won't be POSSible, but here's what I CAN do." · "I see it a LITtle DIFferently."
  2. Up the ladder: T gives the too-casual line, S give the neutral version, then the formal one. T: "need the numbers" → S: "Could you send me the numbers, please?" → S: "I would be grateful if you could send me the numbers." T: "can't come" → "I'm afraid I can't make it." → "Unfortunately, I will not be able to attend." T: "help me with this?" → "Could you help me with this?" → "I would appreciate your assistance with this."

Running the Welcome Reception
  • Groups of 3, chairs in a small standing circle if possible (receptions happen standing up). Each learner holds their own pitch or a role card; each group gets one situation card (a supplier's first visit, a new team member's first day, a conference coffee break…).
  • Timing: 5 minutes per round × 3 rounds + 5 minutes for observer feedback and the whole-class "best line." Ring a bell (or clap) at 4 minutes: "One more minute: find a polite way to end the conversation." Teach the exit line on the spot: "It was great to meet you. I'll let you get back to the others."

  • Stretch roles: give the Host card to a stronger learner in round 1 so the group hears a good model; give C1-level learners the constraint on their situation card ("The visitor is very senior: stay formal until they invite you to use their first name").

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Direct translation: "I want you send me the report," "Give me the number." In many languages an imperative or "I want" is neutral at work; in English it sounds like an order. The fix is structural: Could you…? + please. Note also the missing to ("want you to send"), common across many L1s.
  • Flat intonation sounds rude. Speakers of languages with a narrower pitch range, or of tone languages, may use correct words with a flat voice. Drill the rise-fall on requests; it matters as much as the words.
  • "I am agree" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Arabic, Hindi and others) → I agree. "I am engineer" (many L1s without articles, including Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Persian) → I'm an engineer. "I work here since 2019" → I've worked here since 2019.
  • First names vs titles. Some workplace cultures use titles and family names for a long time; many English-speaking workplaces move to first names quickly, but not with everyone and not always at the start. Teach the safe route: start with the title (Ms. Grant) and switch when invited ("Please, call me Karen"). Avoid Mr./Ms. + first name ("Mr. David").
  • Over-formal or old-fashioned phrases learned from textbooks or letter templates: "It is an honor to make your acquaintance," "How do you do?" used as a real question. Present them as very formal, not wrong, and show the neutral choice.
  • Small talk and first impressions vary. Some workplace cultures prefer a longer warm-up before business; others get to the point fast. Punctuality expectations, handshakes and eye contact also vary. Describe these as tendencies and teach the strategy: observe and mirror; when unsure, start a little more formal.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: one email, one purpose. Each email does one job. Several unrelated asks → several emails, or a short numbered list.

An email asks for a quote, invites the reader to a party and complains about an invoice. How many purposes? (Three.) What usually happens? (The reader answers one and forgets the others.) L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Archaic or regional formulas: "Kindly do the needful," "Please revert at the earliest," "Thanks and regards," "I am writing to inform you that…" at the start of every email. Some are normal in certain regional varieties of English (revert = reply is common in parts of South Asia) but may confuse international readers. Offer the widely understood version: Could you please…? · Could you reply by…? · Kind regards. Don't mock the originals: they were taught as correct.
  • Title + first name ("Dear Mr. David," "Dear Ms. Sara") is common across many languages. English uses title + family name or first name alone. If you don't know the gender, use the full name: "Dear Sam Okafor."
  • "discuss about," "informations," "advices," "an equipment": discuss takes a direct object; information, advice, equipment, feedback are uncountable. Very common across L1s.
  • Tense in emails: "I send you the file" (present simple for now) → "I'm sending / I've attached the file." "I am waiting your answer" → "I look forward to your reply" (and ✘ "look forward to hear").
  • Directness varies. Some workplace cultures expect a long, warm opening before the request; others expect the ask in the first line. In most English-speaking workplaces, one short courtesy line and then the ask is safe. Describe it as a tendency, and tell learners to mirror the emails they receive.
  • British vs American conventions: Ms Alvarez / Ms. Alvarez; Yours faithfully is rare in American email; dates (12 September / September 12; 03/04 can mean March 4 or April 3). Accept both; advise writing the month as a word in international email.

Running the phone calls
  • Back to back. Chairs back to back removes lip-reading and gestures, which is exactly what makes phone calls hard. If the room doesn't allow it, learners hold a book up between their faces, or sit side by side looking straight ahead. Real phones are fine too: pairs can call each other from opposite corners of the room.
  • Cards. Card A (caller) has the details to give, including one name to spell and one number or date to confirm. Card B (receiver) has what's happening in the office (the person is unavailable, a colleague can help, the person is back at 3 p.m.). The receiver writes on the message pad in the workbook. After each call, A checks B's pad against card A: any detail wrong is a signal to practice the read-back.
  • Timing: 5 minutes per call (about 2 minutes to call, 1 to check the pad, 2 for the observer or partner feedback). Stronger pairs do Call 3 (an invoice query with two numbers that could be confused).
  • Observer checklist (set F), for groups of 3 or the repeat call: Task & Purpose: were all five message parts captured and correct? Register & Tone: warm, polite phone voice; no "Who are you?" Language Range & Accuracy: stage phrases; This is…; accurate numbers. Organization & Delivery: stages in order, a signposted hold, a read-back, a clear ending.
ICQs before the calls and the video meeting
Phone: Can you look at your partner? (No, we sit back to back.) Can you show your card? (No.) Who writes the message? (The receiver, on the pad.) What must the receiver do before ending the call? (Read the details back.) What does the caller check afterwards? (The message pad against my card.) · Video: Who opens the meeting? (The chair.) Do you tell the group your problem card? (No, you act it.) What must the chair do? (Check audio, handle the problems, set a ground rule, hand over by name.)
Differentiation
Support

B1 learners start as the caller in Call 1 (the caller's lines are on the card). Give them a call cheat sheet: the workbook Language box folded so the six stages face up, one phrase per stage highlighted. In the dictation, read each item three times. In the video meeting, they take a participant role first ("You're on mute" is the easiest problem). Allow them to write their first two lines before the call.

Stretch

C1-level learners take the receiver role with the "difficult caller" constraint (the caller speaks fast and gives the number only once; the receiver must control the pace politely). In the video meeting, they chair with two problems at once and must also hand over to a quiet participant by name ("Priya, did you want to come in on that?"). Extension: leave a 30-second voicemail on a partner's phone with all five parts, spoken without notes.

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "I am Ana" / "Here is Ana" (a direct translation in many European and other languages) → This is Ana or Ana speaking. "Who are you?" / "Who is speaking?" → Who's calling, please?
  • Teen / ty and number grouping: speakers of Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Arabic and others often need extra work on the stress contrast. Number grouping also varies: some learners read phone numbers in pairs or as whole numbers ("thirty-one, twenty…"). Teach the local grouping and the digit-by-digit confirmation.
  • Letter names: learners whose first languages name vowels differently often confuse A / E / I when spelling aloud; G / J and B / V are common too. The spelling-with-words habit solves it.
  • Dates: 03/04 is March 4 in the US and 3 April in much of the world. On calls and in emails, say and write the month as a word: "April third" / "the third of April." Time zones: name the zone ("3 p.m. Eastern").
  • Silence and turn-taking: some speakers are comfortable with longer pauses; others overlap as a sign of engagement. On the phone and on video, both can cause confusion. Teach the signposts ("Bear with me," "Sorry, you go ahead") rather than one "correct" rhythm.
  • Camera norms vary by company and by person. Some learners may prefer cameras off for privacy, bandwidth or family reasons. In class, never require a real home background: the video meeting is simulated.
