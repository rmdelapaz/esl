Marking principle

This is a communication course, so mark closed items (matching, ordering, sorting, dictation) as right or wrong, and open items (rewrites, subject lines, emails, pitches) against purpose and register. For open items, the models below are examples. Accept any version that (1) keeps the full message (the ask, the deadline, the numbers), (2) is in a register that suits the reader, (3) is polite and clear (one softener, not five), and (4) is accurate enough not to confuse. When a learner's version is correct but misses the lesson target, accept it and point to the target: "Good. Can you also frame it positively?" American English is the model; accept British spellings and conventions (Ms Alvarez, apologise, Yours faithfully, 12 September) whenever a learner uses them consistently. Use the four rubric criteria by name when you give feedback: Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy, Organization & Delivery.

C1 · Welcome pages (orientation)

How we work together, My goals, progress tracker

No answers: these belong to the learner. Check only that (1) each goal names a situation and something the learner wants to be able to do, with a time ("by C8") — "improve my English" is not yet a goal; help them narrow it ("Which situation? Emails to clients? Then: by C8, write a request email to a new client in ten minutes"), (2) goals are realistic for eight weeks, and (3) the progress tracker is filled in privately after you return the diagnostic notes in C3. Learners between jobs may write goals about interviews or networking: that's exactly right.

Warm-up — Three ways to ask

Most → least formal: B (1) → C (2) → A (3). Client: B (a first or formal contact) · Manager: C (neutral; B for a sensitive request or a very formal workplace) · Close colleague: A (or C). All three are acceptable at work; the choice depends on the reader and the relationship. "When you get a sec" is friendly-professional; avoid it with clients and senior people you don't know well.

Exercise 1 — Rank the register
Set1 (most formal)23 (least formal)
1b) Would it be possible to arrange…a) Can we meet tomorrow?c) Free tomorrow for a quick chat?

Clues to point out: would / it be possible · assistance · in this matter · unable · will be (full forms) = formal; contractions and dropped subjects ("Can't make it," "Free tomorrow?") = informal. "Can't make it, sorry!" is fine in a chat with a close colleague, not in a reply to a client.

Exercise 2 — Formal, neutral or friendly?

1 F · 2 FP · 3 F · 4 N · 5 FP · 6 F · 7 N · 8 FP. Accept N for 4 or 7 only; if a learner argues 5 is N, accept it if they explain ("in my team, 'just a quick one' is normal"): the point is that register is a spectrum. "Please do not hesitate to contact me" is formal and a little old-fashioned in email; "Let me know if you have any questions" is the everyday version.

Exercise 3 — Soften the tone (models)
  1. Could you give me a quick update when you have a moment?
  2. I'm not sure that approach would work here. Could we look at another option? (also: I see it a little differently…)
  3. Would it be possible to have this by 3? It would really help me meet the deadline.
  4. Would it be possible to arrange a meeting with your manager? (also: I'd like to set up a meeting with your manager, if possible.)
  5. I'm afraid we can't deliver on Friday, but we can deliver first thing on Monday. (positive framing: lead with or add what you can do)

Accept any version with one clear softener and the full message (the deadline "by 3" must stay). Don't accept over-softened versions ("Could you maybe possibly perhaps…") or ones that lose the request ("An update would be nice someday"). Number 2 must still disagree: diplomacy changes the tone, not the message.

First names: Lena: "Please, call me Lena." → Sam: "Then please call me Sam." (Sam used "Ms. Ortiz" until invited.) Introduction: "Have you met my colleague? My elevator pitch and the Welcome Reception (models)

Model (role card 2, Amira Haddad): "Hi, I'm Amira Haddad. I'm an accounts assistant at Harbor Point Supplies, where I make sure our suppliers are paid correctly and on time. Self-check
Q1
First email to a client. Which opening fits best?
A) Hey! Quick one for you…
B) Need to talk about the contract.
C) Dear Ms. Alvarez, I would like to introduce myself…
Why: a first external contact calls for a formal, respectful register. A is too casual; B has no greeting and sounds abrupt.
Q2
Which response best softens a refusal while staying professional?
A) No, I can't.
B) I'm afraid I can't make Tuesday, but I'm free Thursday if that helps.
C) Um, maybe I possibly might not be able to…
Why: it declines clearly and offers an alternative (positive framing). A is blunt; C is over-softened and sounds unsure.
Q3 · new
Which is the best neutral request to a colleague?
A) Could you send me the figures when you have a moment?
B) Send me the figures.
C) I would be most grateful if you could possibly perhaps send the figures, if it is not too much trouble.
Why: one modal question + one hedge = polite and clear. B sounds like an order; C is over-formal and over-softened for a colleague.
Q4 · new
A senior visitor says, "How do you do?" What do you answer?
A) I'm fine, thank you.
B) How do you do?
C) Not bad, and you?
Why: "How do you do?" is a very formal greeting, not a real question, so you repeat it. A and C answer a question that wasn't asked; C is also too casual for a senior visitor.
Errors you will meet most (1.1)

"I want you send me…" / "Give me…" (direct translation) · flat intonation on correct words · "I am agree" · "I am engineer" (missing article) · "I work here since 2019" (→ I've worked) · "Nice to see you" at a first meeting (→ Nice to meet you; see is for people you already know) · "I'm fine" in reply to "How do you do?" · "Mr. David" (title + first name) · stacked softeners ("Could you maybe possibly…") · over-formal set phrases ("It is an honor to make your acquaintance"). For range, praise Thanks for making the time · I've heard great things about… · I'm responsible for… · Would it be possible to…? · I'm afraid…, but…

Exercise 1 — Put the email in order

c → b → e → d → f → a (subject → greeting → opening line → body / clear ask → closing line → sign-off + name). "Dear Mr. Patel … Best regards" is a normal, polite pairing in modern business email: neutral-formal and consistent. (Sincerely would be slightly more formal.)

Exercise 2 — Match the phrase to its function

1 opening · 2 attachment · 3 request · 4 follow-up · 5 bad news · 6 closing · 7 follow-up · 8 request. Accept "request" for 4 or 7 if the learner explains that a follow-up also asks for something: they overlap. "Regrettably" is formal; "Unfortunately" is the everyday version.

Exercise 3 — Fix the subject line (models)
  1. Slides for Friday's review: your comments by Wed? (also: Please review my slides by Wednesday)
  2. Question about invoice #3390 (also: Quick question re: invoice #3390)
  3. Action needed: approve weekend overtime by 4 p.m. today
  4. Move Thursday team meeting to Fri 10 a.m.? (also: Team meeting: new time Friday 10 a.m.)
  5. FYI: warehouse closed Monday (inspection)

Accept any subject line that names the topic and signals the action or deadline (or "FYI" when no action is needed). Short is good: 4–9 words. Don't accept "URGENT" on 5: nothing is required, so it isn't urgent.

Exercise 4 — Rewrite the blunt email (model)

Subject: Sales figures + a quick slide fix, today if possible

Hi Marco,

Could you send me the latest sales figures when you get a moment? I'd need them by the end of the day for tomorrow's review, if that's doable.

One more thing: I think a couple of numbers on the slides need updating. I'm happy to point them out on a quick call.

Thanks so much,
Ana

What changed: a specific subject; a greeting; a softened request with a deadline; the criticism reframed diplomatically ("need updating," not "are wrong") with an offer of help; a warm sign-off. Friendly-professional or neutral are both fine here (an internal colleague). Strong learners may split it into two emails (one purpose each) or number the two asks: both acceptable.

Exercise 5 — Chat or email? Reply or Reply All?

1 chat (quick, informal, right now) · 2 email (external, formal, needs a record) · 3 CC · 4 Reply (only the sender needs your thanks) · 5 BCC (protects people's addresses) · 6 Reply All (everyone genuinely needs it). For 5, some organizations use a mailing tool instead; BCC is the answer for ordinary email.

The formal request (model)

Dear Mr. Okoro,

I am writing on behalf of Kestrel Design to request an updated quote for 40 standing desks, model KD-20.

I would be grateful if you could include delivery in the price and send us the quote by Friday the 14th, as our budget meeting takes place the following Monday.

The voicemail doesn't name the learner's company: they use their real company (optional), a role card or an invented one. Check: all six facts; a specific subject with the deadline; a formal frame (Dear Mr. Okoro … Sincerely / Yours sincerely / Kind regards); one purpose; the ask near the top. Accept "Thank you in advance for your help" as the closing. Don't accept "Dear Daniel" (too familiar for a first contact) or "Hi Mr. Okoro" (mixed register).

Reply in role (models for the four Supplier Reply cards)
  1. Yes, on time. "Dear Mr./Ms. ___, Thank you for your email. Please find attached our updated quote for 40 KD-20 standing desks at $389 per desk, including delivery (total $15,560). The quote is valid for 30 days. Please let me know if you have any questions. Sincerely, Daniel Okoro"
  2. Yes, but one detail is missing. "…Thank you for your request. Before I can send the final quote, could you please confirm the delivery address and the color you would like (black or white)? I will then send the quote within one working day. Kind regards, Daniel Okoro"
  3. A delay, with an alternative. "…Unfortunately, the KD-20 is out of stock until next month. We can deliver it in five weeks, or we can offer the KD-24, a very similar model, at $405 per desk including delivery, available now. Please let me know which option you prefer, and I will send the quote by Friday. Sincerely, Daniel Okoro"
  4. A question before quoting. "…Thank you for your email. Could you let me know whether you would like the desks assembled on site? Assembly costs an additional $25 per desk. As soon as I hear from you, I will send the full quote, within 24 hours. Kind regards, Daniel Okoro"

$389 × 40 = $15,560. Look for: the reply answers the request; bad news uses Unfortunately / I'm afraid and positive framing (card 3); questions are softened (cards 2 and 4); the frame stays formal.

Self-check
Q1
Which subject line best follows the "specific and useful" rule?
A) Budget approval needed by Thu 5 p.m.
B) Important!!
C) Budget
Why: it says the topic and the action and deadline, so the reader knows exactly what to do. B and C tell the reader almost nothing.
Q2
"Dear Sir or Madam," (British style). Which sign-off matches?
A) Yours faithfully,
B) Yours sincerely,
C) Cheers,
Why: an unnamed reader pairs with "Yours faithfully" (never two S's together: Sir → faithfully). A named reader pairs with "Yours sincerely." In American style, "Sincerely," is used for both, so accept a learner who explains that. C is far too casual.
Q3 · new
An email about a room change went to 30 people. You want to thank the sender.
A) Reply All.
B) Forward it to your team with "Thanks!"
C) Reply only to the sender.
Why: the Reply-All test: does every person need to read this? No, only the sender. A fills 29 inboxes; B sends it to people who didn't need it.
Q4 · new
Which closing line is correct?
A) I look forward to hear from you.
B) I look forward to hearing from you.
C) I am looking forward for your reply.
Why: in look forward to, to is a preposition, so it takes a noun or -ing: to hearing / to your reply. C has the wrong preposition (for).
Errors you will meet most (1.2)

Vague or blank subject lines · the ask in the last paragraph, or no deadline · mismatched frame ("Dear Mr. Okoro … Cheers," "Hi Mr. Okoro") · "Dear Mr. Daniel" (title + first name) · archaic or regional formulas ("Kindly do the needful," "Please revert," "Thanks and regards") · "I'm writing for ask" · "look forward to hear" · "discuss about" · "informations / advices / equipments" · "Please find attach" · "I send you the file" (→ I've attached / I'm sending) · "until Friday" for a deadline (→ by Friday) · several purposes in one email. For range, praise I would be grateful if you could… · Further to our conversation · As a gentle reminder · Please find attached… for your reference · Unfortunately…, but we can…

Self-check
Q1
A caller asks for a colleague who is in a meeting. Which response fits best?
A) One moment, I'll put you through.
B) He's not here. Call back later.
C) I'm afraid he's in a meeting right now. Can I take a message?
Why: it explains the absence politely and offers a next step. A connects to someone who isn't available; B is blunt and gives the caller nothing.
Q2
You didn't quite catch a client's phone number.
A) Write down your best guess and move on.
B) "Sorry, could you repeat the last three digits? Let me read it back to check."
C) Say nothing and hope it arrives in an email.
Why: ask specifically, then read it back. Guessing (A) or hoping (C) risks a wrong callback, which costs far more than five seconds of checking.
Q3 · new
What is the best way to start a call that you make?
A) This is Ana from Northwind Logistics.
B) I am Ana. Who are you?
C) Hello, here is Ana speaking from Northwind.
Why: on the phone, English uses This is… + name + company. B is a direct translation and "Who are you?" sounds rude (→ Who's calling, please? is for the receiver). C mixes here is (a translation) with speaking, which is for answering.
Q4 · new
On a video call, a colleague's audio keeps cutting out.
A) Nothing. Pretend you heard and continue.
B) "Your internet is terrible."
C) "Sorry, you're breaking up a little. Could you repeat that last part?"
Why: name the problem neutrally ("breaking up a little") and ask for a repeat. A risks a misunderstanding; B blames the person and sounds rude.

"I am Ana / Here is Ana" (→ This is Ana / Ana speaking) · "Who are you? / Who is speaking?" (→ Would you mind speaking a little more slowly?) · "Would you mind to spell" (→ spelling) · 03/04-style dates read the local way. Marking principle

Most Module 2 exercises test function, register and tone, so there is usually more than one good answer. Accept any answer that (1) does the job (disagrees, refuses, gives feedback…), (2) is appropriate in register for the reader or listener, (3) keeps the real message (firm on substance), and (4) is grammatically accurate enough to be clear. The models below show one good version; the "Also accept" notes list common alternatives. Reject answers that are only polite on the surface but lose the message ("Maybe, if it's OK, perhaps we could possibly…"), and answers that are clear but blunt. British spellings (summarise, organise, practise) and British phrases (send the minutes round) are fine. When you correct, praise the function first ("Good: you acknowledged first"), then fix the language.

Exercise 2 — What does each phrase do?

Discuss: 11 starts like agreement ("I take your point") but it is a polite disagreement: the acknowledgement is the softener. 10 could also be used by a participant, but it is typical chair language. Expect: 7 labeled as an interruption (it is the opposite: the speaker is protecting their turn).

Exercise 3 — Suggestions and common mistakes
  1. ask
  2. using
  3. move (I'd suggest that we moved = wrong)
  4. take
  5. share or shared (both correct; shared sounds a little more tentative)
  6. postponing (also accept: I suggest that we postpone; not to postpone)
  7. We need to discuss the budget. (or: We need to talk about the budget.)
  8. I agree with Tom.
  9. Can we have / hold a meeting on Friday? (also: Can we meet on Friday?)
Exercise 4 — Put the chair's lines in order

1 b · 2 d · 3 f · 4 h · 5 e · 6 c · 7 a · 8 g. Open (welcome → purpose) → first item → bring someone in → decide → next item → recap action points → thank and close. Accept d and b reversed only if the learner can explain it (some chairs state the purpose first), but the welcome usually comes first. h must come after f and before the decision in e.

Exercise 5 — Make it polite (models)
  1. "Sorry to interrupt, but can I just come in here?" / "If I could just add something…"
  2. "I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree. Could we look at it again?"
  3. "Sorry, could you expand on that?" / "Could you clarify what you mean by…?"
  4. "I take your point, though have we considered a different approach? What if we…?"
  5. "Let's give everyone a chance to speak. Sara, what do you think?" / "Thanks, Marco. Let's hear from Tom on this."

Test for every answer: did it acknowledge or signal first, and does it still do the job? For 5, the chair should never comment on the person ("you're talking too much"); they manage the process (invite others in by name). Expect: "Excuse me, I want to say something" (better, but want is still direct: suggest Could I just…) and over-softened answers for 2 ("Sorry, sorry, maybe I'm wrong, but perhaps…").

Meeting planner — what a good result looks like

An agenda with three items (the third is usually AOB) and realistic times; a meeting that follows it; and at least two action points in the who / what / when format, each with a verb and a deadline ("Ana will book the venue by May 30"). For the Set B scenarios, possible compromises: 1 move on June 7–8 with each team packing its own area, and the design team packing after its June 10 deadline for the last items · 2 keep the $12,000 stand, add online booking and ask for two quotes by Friday · 3 a Saturday rota (maximum two per driver) plus a customer email with holiday dates · 4 one in-person day plus a short online follow-up session on the new ordering software. Any reasonable decision is fine: the task is the process and the action points, not the "right" answer.

Presentation Carousel — a model 2-minute talk (role profile: Amira)

"Good afternoon, everyone, and thanks for coming. I'm Amira Haddad, and I coordinate operations here at Northwind. In January, one delivery in five arrived late. In October, it was one in fourteen. Today I'd like to show you how that happened, and why I think we should use the new route plan in every region. I've divided my talk into two parts: what changed, and what we should do next. Please feel free to ask questions as we go.

Let's start with what changed. In July we started using a route-planning app in the North region. As you can see from this chart, on-time deliveries rose steadily in the first half of the year, from 81% to 84%. Then, in Q3, they rose sharply and peaked at 93%. What this tells us is that the app, not the season, made the difference. For instance, one driver cut forty minutes from her morning route.

This brings me to my next point: what we should do next. The key point here is that the other regions still plan routes by hand. So, to sum up: the app works, it's already paid for in one region, and it could work everywhere. I'd like your approval to try it in two more regions next month. Thank you. Are there any questions?"

What makes it work (for your feedback): Task & Purpose: a clear request at the end · Register & Tone: neutral-professional, "you"-focused · Language: trend verbs used accurately · Organization & Delivery: five jobs, a signpost at every turn, about 260 words ≈ 2 minutes at presentation speed.

  3. "Can I ask why one year works better for you? Is it about flexibility, or about cost?"

Accept any open, polite question that asks why. Offering two possible reasons ("Is it X or Y?") makes it easier to answer. ✘ "Why do you want this?" in a flat tone can sound suspicious: add Can I ask… or Can you help me understand….

3.3 · Pitching, Persuasion & Handling Objections (C11)

Model pitch (Amira)

(1) hook / problem · (2) solution · (3) benefit · (4) evidence · (5) call to action

Why the ask is easy to say yes to: it's specific (two regions, one month), low-risk (a trial, not a full rollout), and it includes a review ("review the numbers together"), so the director stays in control.

Exercise 1 — Feature or benefit?

1 F · 2 B · 3 F · 4 B · 5 F · 6 B · 7 F · 8 B

Each odd item is a fact about the product; each even item is its payoff for a person. Point out the pattern: benefits usually start with you / your.

Exercise 2 — Build the bridge (models)
  1. "…which means that fewer people forget their bookings, so rooms don't sit empty while others are looking for one."
  2. "…so that your cafés can order once a week and throw away far less food."
  3. "…which means that your staff can arrive by train without worrying about parking."
  4. "…so that your team spends its time on the difficult questions, and customers get simple answers in seconds, even at night."

Accept any bridge that ends in a human payoff (time, money, stress, reputation). ✘ "…which means that it sends reminders automatically" (the feature again).

Exercise 3 — Order the pitch and add the ask

b → d → c → a: hook/problem → solution → benefit → evidence.

Model call to action: "Could we switch on online booking at two more clinics for three months and compare the waiting times?" Also good: "I'd love your go-ahead to add it to the Westside clinic next month." A strong ask is specific (where, how long), low-risk (a trial) and asks for one next step. ✘ "So… what do you think?"

Exercise 4 — Answer the objection

A. 1 reframe to value (ROI) · 2 show what's different (differentiate) · 3 the cost of waiting (cost of inaction; also accept "offer a trial at a better time") · 4 find the real concern

B. Models: 1 "That's a fair concern: budgets are tight. Can I ask whether it's the total cost or the monthly cost that's the problem?" · 4 "Of course, it's an important decision. Can I ask what's giving you pause? Is it the price, the timing, or something else?"

Both lines must acknowledge before they ask, and neither should argue or answer yet. ✘ "But it's cheap!" ✘ "What is your problem?" (sounds rude: use Can I ask what's driving that?)

Exercise 5 — Confident, not over-hyped (models)
  1. "In our six-month pilot with three small shops, the app cut stock-taking time by about a third, and two of them saw sales rise by around 10%."
  2. "More than forty businesses in our region already use it. The sooner you start, the sooner you'll see the time savings, and we can set it up for you this month."
  3. "It's very reliable: in the last year it was available 99.9% of the time, and if anything goes wrong, our support team answers 24 hours a day."

Replace absolute claims (best, guaranteed, never, everybody) with specific, hedged evidence (in our pilot, about, around, more than forty) and honest urgency. Accept any invented figures that are plausible and marked as the learner's example.

Dialogue — "We already use spreadsheets"

Listen: the seller lets the client finish ("It looks nice, but…"). · Acknowledge: "That's a fair point. Spreadsheets are flexible." · Ask: "Can I ask what you like most about them?" and "Just so I understand: is the main worry learning a new tool, or losing your old data?" · Respond: "We import all your old spreadsheets for you, free of charge… Would it help if we tested it with last month's expenses first?" · Confirm: "Does that address your concern?"

Real concern: losing five years of data (not the tool itself).

Pitch & Push-back — a model with LAARC (card 2: lunch boxes)

Pitcher: You know how your cafés run out of lunch options by one o'clock, and customers walk out? We make ready-to-cook lunch boxes at Brightline Foods: your staff heat them in three minutes. That means you can offer a hot lunch all afternoon without a full kitchen. Cafés in our pilot sold an extra forty lunches a week on average. Could we start with a small order for two of your cafés next month?

Buyer: They look good, but honestly, it's too expensive for us.

Pitcher: That's a fair concern. Can I ask what's driving that: is it the price per box, or the size of the first order?

Buyer: Well, it's more that if we don't sell them, we throw them away. That's money in the bin.

Pitcher: I completely understand. So the real risk is waste. The boxes stay fresh for five days, and for the first month we can offer sale-or-return: you only pay for what you sell. Does that address your concern?

Buyer: If we only pay for what we sell, then yes. Let's try two cafés.

Notice: five stages in under 60 seconds; the objection is acknowledged and clarified before any answer; the hidden concern (waste) is found with one "or" question; the response removes the risk rather than cutting the price.

Q1
Which is a benefit, not a feature?
A) It has a 500-page paper capacity.
B) So you can print all day without stopping to reload paper.
C) It's built with a two-tray design.
Why: B names the payoff for the user (no interruptions). A and C describe what the printer has: features.

A) "That's a fair concern. Can I ask what's driving that: the total or the monthly cost?"

Q3 · new

B) "In our three-month pilot, the team cut admin time by around 30%."

Model — the executive summary (Northwind Logistics)

Writing task — Executive summary (model, Brightline brief card / Exercise 1 notes)
Model · 104 words

Reducing Damaged Online Orders — Brightline Foods

This proposal sets out a plan to reduce customer complaints about online orders. Complaints doubled from 120 to 240 per quarter, and a survey of 400 customers shows that 55% concern damaged packaging. Notably, an eight-week trial at the Eastfield warehouse cut damage by half. We recommend (1) switching all three warehouses to the new boxes by September, at an extra cost of about $3,600 a quarter; (2) keeping the current supplier as a backup for three months; and (3) reviewing complaint figures in Q4. As refunds for damaged orders currently cost about $9,000 a quarter, the change is expected to pay for itself.

Mark with the rubric. A strong summary has: the purpose in sentence 1; at least two numbers; numbered or prioritized recommendations; an expected result; no "This report will discuss…"; no I think. Support (B1) target: purpose + two numbers + two recommendations, about 80 words.

Speaking — The decision-maker (what usually happens)

Northwind: year-one cost is $9,600 (software) + $14,400 (van lease: $1,200 × 12) = $24,000; the director can approve only $15,000 without the board. Good outcomes: "approve with changes" (the software now; the van as phase two, or taken to the board with the penalty savings of about $36,000 a year as the argument). Brightline: about $3,600 a quarter extra against refunds of about $9,000 a quarter, of which the trial suggests half can be saved; the decision-maker's concerns are the trial size and supplier reliability (answered by the backup supplier). Kestrel: the director approves only if the tool replaces something: writers who mentioned retiring the four spreadsheets get a quick yes. Clearwater: the page ($6,000) fits the one-off budget; the new agent needs a separate hiring decision. Any reasoned decision is correct; praise writers who answered with a number and decision-makers who gave a condition rather than a flat no.

Dialogue — the negotiation

Acknowledgment: "I completely understand that budget is important, and I want to make this work for both of us." · Conditional trade: "if your team can commit to a two-year agreement, we could offer a 10% reduction and hold that rate." · Question inviting a reply: "Would that be a helpful direction to explore?" · Confirm in writing: "I'll put the proposal in writing so your team has everything to consider."

Speaking — The client scenario and the capstone planner

Scenario: no fixed script. Round 1 succeeds if the chair welcomes, makes brief small talk, proposes a light agenda and invites the client to begin. Round 2 needs at least one feature → benefit link and a call to action that respects consensus ("we can share a draft plan by Friday for your team to review"). Round 3 needs an acknowledgment, a conditional offer within the limit (10%, not 15%), space to decide, and a written follow-up. Capstone planner (model, from Script 4.3-B, expanded to 5 minutes): topic: a self-service order-tracking page · audience: the Clearwater leadership team · opening: greet, topic, hook ("Six out of every ten calls ask the same question"), what's in it for them, a three-part outline · problem: waits up from 4 to 11 minutes · solution: a tracking page + FAQ in six weeks · benefits: shorter waits, happier customers, agents free for complex calls ("…which means that…") · evidence: IT has built similar pages; the share of tracking calls (60%) · close: "So, to sum up… What I'm asking you to approve today is $6,000, so IT can start on Monday." · objections: adoption ("What if customers don't use it?" → agents mention it on every call; weekly figures for a month) and cost ("Why pay now?" → a one-off $6,000 against long waits every day). Self-check
Q1
First call with a formal, consensus-driven, relationship-first team: which approach fits best?
A) First names, no small talk, a decision by the end of the call.
B) Open warmly and formally, make some small talk, and offer a written summary so their team can decide together.
C) Keep it casual with plenty of idioms.
Why: B starts formal, builds the relationship and gives time for a group decision, confirmed in writing. A clashes with all three preferences; C's idioms confuse and sound too casual.
Q2
A client asks for a bigger discount than you can offer.
A) Simply refuse and repeat the original price.
B) Immediately agree to the full discount.
C) Acknowledge the concern, then offer a conditional trade.
Why: C protects both the relationship and the deal: the client gets something in return for giving something. A can end the conversation; B gives away value for nothing.
Q3 · new
"That may be somewhat difficult for us." What does it most likely mean?
A) Yes, definitely.
B) Probably "no," or there's a real problem: ask gently.
C) They didn't understand you.
Why: in many workplaces, especially more indirect ones, this is a polite way to refuse or to flag a serious problem. The safe move is to check gently and offer an alternative.
Q4 · new
You're not sure what someone's communication norms are.
A) Use your own norms: they're the same everywhere.
B) Start more formal, observe and mirror, and ask when you're unsure.
C) Use lots of idioms to sound friendly.
Why: B combines three golden rules. A is the classic cross-cultural mistake (assuming your norm is universal); C makes you harder to understand.
