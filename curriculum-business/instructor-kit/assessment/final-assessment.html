Final Assessment — The Whole Course

Two classes, five realistic tasks, one rubric. Part A (Class 15): a 60-minute timed writing paper (a short proposal section with an executive summary, then a cover letter or a formal email) and a pair negotiation with private priorities and tradeable variables. Part B (Class 16): a capstone pitch (5 minutes + Q&A) and a mock job interview with STAR questions, followed by a debrief and celebration. Each learner receives a Writing /20 and a Speaking /20, comparable with Class 1 and Class 8.

C15 · Writing 60 min + negotiation ~7 min C16 · Pitch 5 + 2–3 min · Interview ~8 min Writing /20 · Speaking /20 Rubric & models: assessment-key.html
How to frame it

"These are the tasks you'll really meet at work or in a job search: a proposal, a formal letter, a negotiation, a pitch and an interview. You've practiced every one. I'm scoring the same four criteria as always. What matters most is that you finish each task: cover the points, keep the register right, and make the next step clear." Remind learners that any task can be done with a role card, and that the interview never asks about salary, visas, age, family or gaps in employment.

Scheduling the scored interviews
  • A teacher-led interview takes about 8 minutes plus 2 for notes: allow 10-minute slots.
  • Option A: two learners during the Class 16 interview block, the rest in slots before or after Classes 15–16 or on a video call within a week.
  • Option B: a second interviewer (co-teacher, trained volunteer, or a colleague who hires people) runs interviews in parallel during the pitch block, using this pack and the rubric.
  • Option C: if you can free time in Class 14's rehearsal block, hold three or four scored interviews there.
  • The interview is scored with the pitch and negotiation into one Speaking /20, so a learner who can't attend a slot still gets a speaking score (note "no interview" on the sheet).
You need
  • C15: per learner, the writing paper; one negotiation card each (cut out); the score sheet; a phone timer
  • C16: the pitch brief and the interview preparation sheet (give both out in Class 14); question slips; the objection cards and interviewer card (teacher pages); a screen if learners use one slide
  • The rubric, the model answers and "what to listen for": assessment/assessment-key.html
  • Each learner's Class 1 and Class 8 scores, for the progress row
Fairness and sensitivity

All companies, people, products and figures are fictional. The cover letter and interview can use the learner's real experience or the role-card profile; both are scored the same way. Learners with foreign qualifications or a career change may describe them as they wish ("My degree was earned abroad and has been evaluated as…"). Never ask why they left a job, why there is a gap, or about immigration or family. In the negotiation, keep the conflict commercial (price, dates, terms); if a pair drifts into a personal argument, pause them with "Let's go back to the terms on your cards."

Kestrel Design is a design studio of 40 people. We are looking for an Operations Coordinator to keep our projects running smoothly.

You will: coordinate project schedules and supplier deliveries · prepare weekly status reports · be the first point of contact for clients' logistics questions.

Negotiation · Scenario 1 · Delivery contract renewal

Scenario 1 · Card A · seller

Account Manager, Northwind Logistics

Brightline Foods' one-year delivery contract ends next month. You're meeting their purchasing lead to renew it. Current terms: $85 per delivery, standard 4-hour delivery window, payment in 30 days.

  • High priority: a two-year contract (it lets you plan trucks and staff).
  • Price: ideally keep $85. Your absolute minimum is $80.
  • Payment: you prefer 30 days; you can accept 45 days if they sign for two years. Never 60.
  • Morning 2-hour windows cost you a little more to plan, but you can offer them.
  • In reserve: free pickup of returned goods (it costs you very little).
  • Your alternative (BATNA): another client wants this truck capacity. Below $80, walk away politely.

Planning: opening line · one question about their interests · two "If you…, we'll…" offers · what you keep for the close

Scenario 1 · Card B · buyer

Purchasing Lead, Brightline Foods

You're renewing your delivery contract with Northwind Logistics. Current terms: $85 per delivery, standard 4-hour delivery window, payment in 30 days.

  • Price: your budget has been cut by 8%. Target: $78. You'd like to stay below $83.
  • High priority (the real problem): deliveries that arrive after 11 a.m. disrupt shelf-stocking in your stores. A guaranteed 2-hour morning window is worth a lot to you.
  • Contract length: you can commit to two years if the price is fixed for both years.
  • Payment: your finance team wants 60 days; 45 is acceptable.
  • Nice to have: pickup of returned goods.
  • Your alternative (BATNA): another carrier offered $78, but with no morning windows.

Deal memo · write on the back of your card after the negotiation (3–5 minutes)

Write the confirming email you'd send straight after the meeting: Subject · greeting · "Just to confirm what we agreed:" · 3–4 bullet points (every term, with numbers) · the next step · a warm close. If you did not reach a deal, write what was agreed so far and the next step.

1. Choose your topic

  • Your own idea: an improvement at your workplace (real or role card), or, if you're looking for work, an idea for a company you'd like to join.
  • The Review Project: the approach you prepared for Lesson 4.3.
  • A brief from the list below (all figures are fictional; you may invent sensible extra details).
Brief 1 · Northwind Logistics: "Arrival alerts"

18% of home deliveries fail the first time because nobody is home. Each failed delivery costs about $22. Idea: an automatic text message to the customer 30 minutes before the driver arrives. A test on two routes cut failed deliveries by half. Ask: approve a 3-month trial on 10 routes ($2,400).

Brief 2 · Brightline Foods: "Crates, not cardboard"

Brightline spends about $4,100 a month on cardboard boxes for deliveries to grocery stores. Idea: 1,500 reusable plastic crates ($9,000) that stores return with the next delivery; crates last about three years. One store has already agreed to try. Ask: approve a 6-month pilot with two stores.

Brief 3 · Kestrel Design: "Focus Friday mornings"

Designers spend about 14 hours a week in internal meetings; in a staff survey, 68% said they can't find time for focused work. Idea: no internal meetings on Friday mornings. One team tried it: on-time project delivery rose from 71% to 85%. Cost: none. Ask: approve an 8-week trial for the whole studio.

Give Card A (seller / agency) to the learner who needs stretch: they must protect a minimum and push for a long-term commitment. For support, allow the Lesson 3.2 phrase card on the table and note it on the sheet.

Say, before round 1
You have seven minutes. Start with a friendly opening, find out what matters to the other side, and trade: nothing for nothing. Before time is up, summarize what you've agreed. A deal isn't required. A clear, polite "we can't agree today, so here's the next step" is a good result too.

Don't intervene in the negotiation. If a pair reaches a deal in 3 minutes, say "Check every item on your card: have you agreed all of them?"

The shape of a good deal (don't tell learners in advance)

Scenario 1: the buyer's real interest is the morning window, not only price. A strong outcome: about $80–82, two years with the price fixed, a 2-hour morning window, 45 days' payment, and (traded at the close) free returns pickup. Scenario 2: the client's real interest is launching before the spring sale. A strong outcome: a phased launch (shop and opening-hours pages in about 6–7 weeks, the blog later), about $22,000–23,000, 3 revision rounds, 40% upfront, and a support contract at $350–400. You score the language and process, not the numbers: a learner who reaches a poor deal politely and clearly can still score well; a learner who "wins" by refusing to trade or by being rude cannot score high on Register & Tone.

Objection Cards · Class 16 Q&A (you ask one per pitch)

Objection · cost

"It sounds expensive. Why should we pay for this now?"

Objection · adoption

"What if people don't actually use it?"

Objection · history

"We tried something similar two years ago and it didn't work."

Objection · timing

"This isn't a good time. Why not wait until next year?"

Objection · evidence

"How will we know it's working? What will you measure?"

Objection · ownership

"Who is going to manage this, and how much of their time will it take?"

Choose the card that fits the pitch best. For support, use the evidence or ownership card (they invite a factual answer). For stretch, use the history or timing card and add one follow-up: "And if the pilot fails?"

Interviewer Card · scored interview (about 8 minutes)

Before you start
Thanks for coming in. This is a practice interview for the [job] role, so treat me as the hiring manager. It will take about eight minutes. You can use your real experience or your role card. Ready?
  • Stand up to greet, offer a handshake if culturally comfortable for the learner, and invite them to sit: the opening is part of the interview.
  • Ask 6–8 questions: always 1, 2, 3 and 8; choose the others to fit the job and the time.
  • Keep a neutral, friendly face. Use one follow-up per STAR answer if the Result is missing: "And what was the result?"
Never ask
  • Salary history or expectations
  • Visa, immigration or citizenship status; nationality; "Where are you really from?"
  • Age, family, children, religion, health
  • Why they left a job, or about gaps in employment
  • Anything about a real current employer they haven't mentioned

If a learner raises one of these themselves (for example, a career change after a move), respond briefly and respectfully and move to the next question.

Score Sheet · Final Assessment · one per learner

If the tasks point to different scores for a criterion, give the score that most of the evidence supports, and prefer the longer, more demanding task when it's evenly split (the proposal for writing; the pitch for speaking).
