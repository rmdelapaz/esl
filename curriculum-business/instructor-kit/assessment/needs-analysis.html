Needs Analysis & Diagnostic — Where Are We Starting?

A friendly first-class pack. It has three parts: a workplace-needs survey (which situations, how often, how confident, what goals), a 20-minute diagnostic reply email to a fictional message, and a 3-minute speaking sample (a short introduction and a phone-message role-play). Learners finish with a goal-setting box. Together these give you a baseline on the course rubric, a map of what this group actually needs at work, and the information to plan support and stretch from Class 2. It is a starting point, not a verdict.

~60 min of the 90-minute class Writing sample /20 · Speaking sample /20 Rubric: assessment-key.html Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)
How to frame it (say this before anything is handed out)

Adults in a Business English class often carry a lot of professional pride, and some have had to start again below the level they worked at before. Make it clear straight away that this is about the course, not about them: "Nothing today is pass or fail. I want to know which work situations matter to you and how your English works in them right now, so the next eight weeks fit this group. Everyone will find something difficult today. That's useful information, not a problem." Tell them that results are private, that nobody's score is shared with the class, and that every personal question on the survey is optional.

You need
  • One learner paper per person (the middle section of this pack), printed at 100%
  • The teacher pages: speaking script and cards (cut out), one note grid per learner, the class overview, the profile sheet
  • A clock the class can see and a phone timer for speaking
  • The course rubric and model email: assessment/assessment-key.html
What each part tells you
  • Survey: which situations matter and how often, where confidence is low, and practical constraints (time, devices, comfort with role-play)
  • Reply email /20: all four rubric criteria in writing: did they answer everything, in a suitable register, with control of language and a clear email structure?
  • Speaking sample /20: the same four criteria in speech, including listening, clarifying and reading back details on a call
  • Goals: what each learner wants, in their words, so targets later are theirs, not only yours
Personal information is always optional

No task in this pack asks for salary, employment status, immigration status, nationality, age or the name of a real employer. Every personal item can be answered with a role card (four are on the teacher pages; a learner can also invent one). Say: "You can use your real job, the job you want, or a role card. I'm listening to your English, not checking your CV." Some learners worked at a senior level before a move or a career change. If a learner mentions this, respond with respect and interest ("That's a lot of experience to bring. Let's make sure your English shows it."), never with pity, and don't ask follow-up questions about why.

Adapting the conditions, not the content

If a learner has a visual impairment, dyslexia, or reads more slowly than they speak, keep the same tasks and change the conditions: enlarge the paper, give 10 extra minutes for the email, or let them type it. Write the change on the paper ("Adapted: +10 min, typed"). Treat the result as the same kind of evidence; the aim of a diagnostic is to see what they can do, not how fast they can handwrite.

Part 2 · Reply to an email

Teacher only · do not hand out

Speaking Sample · 3 minutes per learner

Sit beside the learner rather than opposite, if the room allows; it feels less like a job interview. Keep your own talk to the script. Don't correct, don't teach, and don't react to mistakes; nod and move on. Make notes on the grid after the learner leaves (or in single words while they speak, if you must). Time with a phone.

Part 1 · Introductions · about 1 minute
Thanks for coming over. Imagine we've just met at a work event. Could you introduce yourself? You can use your real job, the job you want, or a role card.

Offer the role cards (below) face up. After the introduction, ask one or two follow-ups:

  1. What does a normal day look like for you? (or: What would a normal day look like in that job?)
  2. What's one situation at work where you'd like to feel more confident in English?

Listen for: a clear one-line role (name + role + what you do), "free information", and whether they ask anything back. A learner who answers in five words or fewer: ask once, "Could you tell me a bit more?" and note it.

Part 2 · Phone-message role-play · about 2 minutes
Now a short phone call. Here's your card. Take thirty seconds to read it. I'm the receptionist. When you're ready, say "Ring ring" and I'll answer.

Give the learner card. Play the receptionist from your card. Stop kindly after the goodbye: "Thank you. That's all."

The deliberate mistake in your read-back tests the single most useful call habit (Lesson 1.3): does the learner notice and correct it? Note exactly what they say.

Learner card · phone message

You work at Kestrel Design, a design studio.

Call Brightline Foods. Ask to speak to Ms. Rosa Lindgren in Marketing.

Why: the photos for her new menu cards are ready. You need her to approve them by Friday.

If she is not available, leave a message: your name (spell your family name), your number 555-0148, the reason, and ask her to call back before 5 p.m. tomorrow.

Check that the receptionist has the details right.

Teacher card · receptionist

You answer the phone at Brightline Foods.

  1. "Good morning, Brightline Foods, [your name] speaking. How can I help?"
  2. "I'm afraid Ms. Lindgren is in a meeting until three. Can I take a message?"
  3. "Can I take your name, please? … Could you spell your family name?"
  4. "And the best number to reach you on?" · "What's it regarding?"
  5. Read back with one mistake: say the number as 555-0184 (not 0148). "Is that right?"
  6. After any correction: "Thanks. I'll make sure she gets the message. Have a good day."

Reading the survey as a class
  • Count the ★ for each situation. The top three are where to add extra practice time or examples (the course order stays the same).
  • Note which situations are "Not yet" for many learners (often negotiating or chairing). Give them more scaffolding: a model first, then a card with sentence frames.
  • Group "What makes it hard?" ticks. If many ticked knowing how formal to be, spend longer on the register spectrum in Class 2.
  • Check role-play comfort. For "I prefer to watch one first," demo each role-play with a stronger learner before pairs start.
Using the scores for support and stretch
  • Around 6–10 / 20: plan support: sentence frames, a partly completed model, fewer items, the easier role card.
  • Around 11–15 / 20: the core tasks as written; push one criterion (usually Register & Tone or Organization & Delivery).
  • Around 16–20 / 20: plan stretch: tougher role-card constraints, register-shifting tasks, chairing roles.
  • Mixed profiles are normal (for example, strong writing and hesitant speaking). Pair them with someone whose profile is the reverse.

Talking about the starting point kindly
  • Start with a real strength, with evidence ("Your email answered every question, in a clear order").
  • Give two or three targets at most. Five targets means none.
  • Connect each target to a learner goal when you can ("You want to feel confident on the phone, so let's start with reading back numbers").
  • Never compare learners, and never read scores aloud. A learner with senior experience and a low speaking score needs to hear that the gap is language, not professional ability.
