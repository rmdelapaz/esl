Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 1 — Professional Communication Foundations

Four classes that give learners a starting point and the three everyday tools of working life. The course opens with an orientation class, a workplace-needs survey and a short diagnostic (C1), framed as a map, not a verdict. Then three communication workshops: register, tone and first impressions, the "how" behind everything else in the course (C2); professional emails and messaging, the most-used skill at work (C3); and telephoning and virtual calls, where learners lose the safety net of body language and must clarify and confirm out loud (C4).

3 lessons + orientation Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C4 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class Level B1+ (B1–C1 mixed)

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C1 is the orientation, needs-analysis and diagnostic class; C2–C4 teach online lessons 1.1–1.3. These are communication-skills workshops, so every lesson class follows the same shape: warmer (review) → lead-in linked to learners' real needs → a model (dialogue, email, call) → notice and clarify the language (phrase banks, register, Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions, CCQs) → drill key phrases (choral → individual; stress, intonation, polite tone) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a role-play or simulation with A/B cards) → feedback on register, tone and clarity, with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes, and the numbers in the Time column are minutes. If you're running late, cut the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the role-play or the feedback: using the language under a little pressure, then hearing how it landed, is what learners can't get from the online course.
  • Present from slides/module-1-foundations.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the word-only phrase flashcards, the six role cards, the C1 Find Someone Who and speaking-sample interviewer card, the C2 Welcome Reception cards, the C3 Supplier Reply cards and Register Dominoes, the C4 phone-call A/B cards, video-meeting cards and observer checklist, and all the listening scripts.
  • The needs survey and the diagnostic tasks are in assessment/needs-analysis.html; the course rubric, model answers and scoring sheets are in assessment/assessment-key.html. Both are written separately from this guide. Always follow the timings and instructions printed on those papers; the C1 plan below budgets about 15 minutes for the survey and 30 minutes for the writing sample.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and choose a US or UK voice. The classroom is where they use the language with a real listener and get feedback on how it sounded.
  • One rubric, all course. Every speaking and writing task in this kit is observed with the same four criteria from assessment/assessment-key.html, each scored 1–5: Task & Purpose (did it achieve the goal: all points covered, a clear action), Register & Tone (the right formality, politeness, diplomacy), Language Range & Accuracy (phrases, grammar, vocabulary) and Organization & Delivery (structure, signposting, fluency, pronunciation, interaction). In Module 1 you don't give scores in lesson classes; you use the criteria as your note-taking columns and teach learners to use them on each other through the observer checklist.
  • Feedback on register, tone and clarity. In every freer task, walk around and note (1) lines that were too blunt or too casual for the listener ("I want the report," "hey, need those numbers"), (2) lines that were over-formal or over-softened ("I am writing to inform you that I would possibly maybe like…"), (3) accuracy slips that blur meaning ("discuss about," "I am agree," missing articles), and (4) good language to praise and spread. In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, never with names, and let learners fix them in pairs.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and conventions, like the kit (practice, apologize, finalize, Ms. Alvarez, US-style phone numbers such as (312) 555-0147). Lesson 1.2 also shows the British letter pairing (Dear Sir or Madam … Yours faithfully) next to American Sincerely. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them: many learners will work with British, Irish, Indian, Australian or international colleagues. The one rule: be consistent inside one email.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Choose the register → write it clearly → say it clearly. C1 finds out what each learner actually needs English for at work and where they are now. C2 builds the idea that runs through the whole course: the same message can be formal, neutral or friendly-professional, and tone (softeners, positive framing, intonation) decides whether it sounds courteous or blunt. C3 applies register to writing: the seven-part email, matching greeting and sign-off, one purpose and a clear, dated ask. C4 applies it to the voice: call stages, message-taking and, above all, clarifying and confirming names, numbers and dates. Every class ends with something learners keep: goals and a starting profile (C1), an elevator pitch (C2), an email template (C3), a call "cheat sheet" (C4).

Adults with careers: respect, privacy and the role-card option

Your learners are working adults. Some use English at work every day and want polish; some are looking for work; some are newcomers who were accountants, engineers, nurses or managers in another country and are rebuilding a professional career here, sometimes in a job below their experience. Treat every learner's experience as expertise ("You've managed a team of twelve: tell us how you ran meetings"), and treat foreign credentials with respect: never "back home," always "in your previous role" or "in your field."

Nobody has to disclose anything. Learners never have to say where they work, whether they are employed, how much they earn, their immigration status or why they are in the class. Every personal or work-history task has a role-card option: one of six fictional professionals with a job, a company and contact details (materials, set B). Put the cards on a side table in every class so anyone can take one without asking. Say it on day one and repeat it before each role-play: "Your real job, a role card, or a job you invent. All three are equally good."

All companies, people and products in this kit are fictional (Northwind Logistics, Brightline Foods, Kestrel Design, Harbor Point Supplies, Crestview Hotels). Keep role-play conflicts ordinary: deadlines, budgets, schedules, delivery delays. Don't use layoffs, discrimination or politics as practice material, even if a learner suggests it.

Using the diagnostic and the two performance checks

The course has three measurement points: the diagnostic (C1: a reply email and a 3-minute speaking sample), the Midpoint Performance Check (C8: a timed email + chat-message rewrite and pair phone and meeting simulations) and the Final Assessment (C15–C16: writing, negotiation, presentations and mock interviews). All three use the same rubric, so learners can see real progress on the same four criteria. Present each as information, not judgment. The diagnostic is short and cold, and learners haven't yet met the course's phrase banks, so it usually underestimates them; say so. What matters is the pattern: is the learner's main gap Register & Tone (too blunt, too formal), Language Range & Accuracy, or Organization & Delivery (a buried request, no structure, hesitant delivery)? Return a short private note to each learner by C3, and let them record their scores in the workbook progress tracker.

A professional, low-stress first class — what to say and do
  • Model the course in the way you run the room. Greet at the door, use names, start on time, finish on time. Learners notice, and C2 will name these habits ("Be punctual; use names").
  • Your four promises (read them aloud; they're printed in the workbook): 1. Your results are private: I'll never share or compare them. 2. I'll tell you honestly what's working and what to focus on. 3. You never have to share personal information: your job, your employer, your status or your salary. A role card is always fine. 4. Every mistake in this room is useful: we correct the language, never the person.
  • Respect experience. Many learners had senior roles before. When someone says "I was a finance director," respond with interest ("Then you'll have a lot to teach us about meetings") and move on; don't probe why they're here.
  • If a learner becomes anxious during the speaking sample, slow down, smile and say: "That's fine. Take your time; there's no hurry." If they freeze, offer the role card: "Would you like to be Daniel from Northwind Logistics instead?" Many learners find it easier to speak as someone else.
Running the speaking sample
  • Use the tasks printed in assessment/needs-analysis.html and the interviewer card (materials, set C). Three minutes per learner: about one minute for the self-introduction (real or role card), then a short phone-message role-play in which you are the receptionist and the learner leaves a message, as the paper describes. Keep the phone timer where the learner can't see it.
  • Sit at the side of the table, not opposite. Be warm but neutral: don't finish sentences or correct. Take notes on the four rubric criteria only after the learner leaves.
  • Class of up to about 8: you'll reach everyone across the two stages. Larger class: reach as many as you can, then take the rest at the side table during C2's controlled-practice stage (never during the role-play). Alternatively, a learner can record the self-introduction and a phone message on their phone at home and bring it to C2.
  • With the learner's permission, record the sample on your phone so you can mark it later and play a few seconds back in Module 4 as a "before" recording. Say that you'll delete recordings at the end of the course, and do.

Differentiation
Support

B1 learners may find the survey's vocabulary heavy. Read the situation list aloud and gloss quickly (chair a meeting = lead it; negotiate = agree a deal). In the writing sample, say privately: "If you can't finish, that's useful information. Write what you can." On My goals, give a frame on the board: "By ___, I want to be able to ___ in English when I ___." A learner can dictate their goals to you.

Stretch

C1-level learners who finish early should reread for tone: "Would the reader feel respected? Is the request easy to find?" In My goals, ask them to make one goal about register-shifting (for example, "I want to sound less formal with my own team without sounding careless") or about leading (chairing, presenting). Ask them to notice one email this week that they think is too formal.

Watch-outs for the first class
  • Status and disclosure. "What do you do?" can be a painful question for someone between jobs or working below their qualifications. Never go round the room asking it. The icebreaker and the survey both allow a pass or a role card.
  • Names and titles. Some learners expect to call you "Teacher," "Sir," "Madam" or "Mr./Ms. + first name." Tell them what you'd like to be called and practice it; C2 will explain first names versus titles in English-speaking workplaces as a tendency that varies by company.
  • The writing sample will show you the patterns you'll meet all module: very formal or archaic formulas ("Kindly do the needful," "I am writing to inform you that…"), very direct requests translated from another language ("I want you send me…"), a missing subject line or sign-off. Don't correct them today; note them for C2 and C3.
  • Handshakes, eye contact and distance vary across cultures and individuals. At the door, let learners lead: offer a hand only if they do, or simply smile and nod.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: register. The level of formality, chosen for the reader and the situation. It isn't "good" or "bad": the right register depends on who, how well you know them, and how sensitive the topic is.

"I would be grateful if you could send me the report." Is this rude? (No.) Is it wrong for a close colleague? (Not wrong, but a little cold or distant.) When is it the best choice? (First contact, a senior or external person, a sensitive topic.) · If you don't know which register to use, which is safest? (Neutral.) Is it easier to become more formal or less formal later? (Less formal: relax later.)

Meaning: tone. The attitude your words carry: warm, confident, apologetic, firm. The same register can have a good or a bad tone.

"You're wrong." Is it clear? (Yes.) Is it professional? (No, it sounds like an attack.) "I see it a little differently. Could we look at it again?" Does the speaker still disagree? (Yes.) What changed? (The tone, not the message.) · "Could you possibly maybe send it sometime if that's OK?" Polite? (Yes, too polite.) How does the speaker sound? (Unsure.) How many softeners do we usually need? (One.)

Meaning: positive framing. Lead with what you can do.

"I can't do it today." vs "I can have it ready by Monday." Which one gives the reader a plan? (The second.) Is the answer still "not today"? (Yes.)

Pronunciation. Polite requests rise and fall: the voice starts a little higher and moves on the stressed word: "Could you SEND me the rePORT?" A flat, low voice turns the same words into an order. L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Direct translation: "I want you send me the report," "Give me the number." In many languages an imperative or "I want" is neutral at work; in English it sounds like an order. The fix is structural: Could you…? + please. Note also the missing to ("want you to send"), common across many L1s.
  • Flat intonation sounds rude. Speakers of languages with a narrower pitch range, or of tone languages, may use correct words with a flat voice. Drill the rise-fall on requests; it matters as much as the words.
  • "I am agree" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Arabic, Hindi and others) → I agree. "I am engineer" (many L1s without articles, including Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Persian) → I'm an engineer. "I work here since 2019" → I've worked here since 2019.
  • First names vs titles. Some workplace cultures use titles and family names for a long time; many English-speaking workplaces move to first names quickly, but not with everyone and not always at the start. Teach the safe route: start with the title (Ms. Grant) and switch when invited ("Please, call me Karen"). Avoid Mr./Ms. + first name ("Mr. David").
  • Over-formal or old-fashioned phrases learned from textbooks or letter templates: "It is an honor to make your acquaintance," "How do you do?" used as a real question. Present them as very formal, not wrong, and show the neutral choice.
  • Small talk and first impressions vary. Some workplace cultures prefer a longer warm-up before business; others get to the point fast. Punctuality expectations, handshakes and eye contact also vary. Describe these as tendencies and teach the strategy: observe and mirror; when unsure, start a little more formal.

Running the phone calls
  • Back to back. Chairs back to back removes lip-reading and gestures, which is exactly what makes phone calls hard. If the room doesn't allow it, learners hold a book up between their faces, or sit side by side looking straight ahead. Real phones are fine too: pairs can call each other from opposite corners of the room.
  • Cards. Card A (caller) has the details to give, including one name to spell and one number or date to confirm. Card B (receiver) has what's happening in the office (the person is unavailable, a colleague can help, the person is back at 3 p.m.). The receiver writes on the message pad in the workbook. After each call, A checks B's pad against card A: any detail wrong is a signal to practice the read-back.
  • Timing: 5 minutes per call (about 2 minutes to call, 1 to check the pad, 2 for the observer or partner feedback). Stronger pairs do Call 3 (an invoice query with two numbers that could be confused).
  • Observer checklist (set F), for groups of 3 or the repeat call: Task & Purpose: were all five message parts captured and correct? Register & Tone: warm, polite phone voice; no "Who are you?" Stretch

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "I am Ana" / "Here is Ana" (a direct translation in many European and other languages) → This is Ana or Ana speaking. "Who are you?" / "Who is speaking?" → Who's calling, please?
  • Teen / ty and number grouping: speakers of Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Arabic and others often need extra work on the stress contrast. Number grouping also varies: some learners read phone numbers in pairs or as whole numbers ("thirty-one, twenty…"). Teach the local grouping and the digit-by-digit confirmation.
  • Letter names: learners whose first languages name vowels differently often confuse A / E / I when spelling aloud; G / J and B / V are common too. The spelling-with-words habit solves it.
  • Dates: 03/04 is March 4 in the US and 3 April in much of the world. On calls and in emails, say and write the month as a word: "April third" / "the third of April." Time zones: name the zone ("3 p.m. Eastern").
  • Silence and turn-taking: some speakers are comfortable with longer pauses; others overlap as a sign of engagement. On the phone and on video, both can cause confusion. Teach the signposts ("Bear with me," "Sorry, you go ahead") rather than one "correct" rhythm.
  • Camera norms vary by company and by person. Some learners may prefer cameras off for privacy, bandwidth or family reasons. In class, never require a real home background: the video meeting is simulated.

The through-line

Acknowledge first, then act. All three lessons rest on one habit that many learners do not use in English: show the other person you have heard them before you say your own thing. In C5 it is a signal before you interrupt ("Sorry to interrupt, but…") and agreement before you differ ("I see your point, but…"). In C6 it is reacting and asking a follow-up question before you add your own story ("Oh really? What was that like?"). In C7 it is acknowledging a view or a feeling before you push back, give feedback, say no or solve a problem ("I understand this is frustrating. Let's look at how we fix it."). Name the habit in C5, point at it every time it appears, and by C7 learners should recognize it themselves. It is also the single fastest way to raise a Register & Tone score in C8.

Face, confidence and experience

Many learners in this class have more professional experience than their English shows: engineers, nurses, accountants and managers rebuilding a career in a new language, sometimes in jobs below their qualifications. Meetings and networking can hit hard, because being quiet in a meeting or awkward at a networking event can feel like losing professional identity. Treat that experience as a resource ("You've run meetings before, in another language; what did a good chair do?"), never as something to apologize for. Keep conflicts in role-plays ordinary (deadlines, budgets, schedules, delivery delays). Do not use layoffs, discrimination, harassment, politics or religion as role-play material, even as "difficult conversation" practice. If a learner brings a real, painful workplace situation into C7, thank them, keep it private, and offer a role card instead: "Let's practice the language on this card first; you can use it for your own situation later."

L1 and cross-cultural watch-outs
  • I am agree / Are you agree?: common among learners whose languages express agreement with "be" + an adjective or noun (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, French je suis d'accord, Portuguese estou de acordo, and others). In English, agree is a verb: I agree · Do you agree?
  • discuss about, make a meeting, assist a meeting: direct translations from many languages. Discuss takes a direct object; you have / hold a meeting; you attend it (in English, assist means "help").
  • I suggest to… / How about to…: very frequent. Drill the three patterns (base verb · -ing · that + we + base verb) until they are automatic.
  • Interrupting norms differ. Some workplace cultures see interrupting as a sign of engagement; others expect people to wait to be invited, especially junior staff speaking to senior staff. Neither is "right." Present the signal phrases as a bridge: learners who find interrupting uncomfortable can use a signal, and learners who interrupt freely can soften it. For chairs: silence does not always mean agreement, so invite quieter people by name.
  • First names vs titles. Many English-speaking workplaces use first names in meetings, even with managers. Some learners find this disrespectful at first. Tell them it is normal and not rude, but also that it is always fine to follow the other person's lead.
  • Flat intonation can sound rude or bored in English even when the words are polite; some learners' first languages use a narrower pitch range. The Drill script's "flat vs polite" contrast is worth doing more than once.

Could I help next week?" · T: "Your email was confusing." → S: "One suggestion: could we put the main question at the top?"
  3. Receiving (pairs). A gives a one-line piece of feedback from a list on slide 15; B answers with "Thanks for telling me. Could you give me an EXample?" or "That's fair. Let me take that on BOARD." No defending allowed: if B starts "Yes, but…", A says "Buzz!" and B tries again.
Hard-conversation carousel — how to run it
  • Groups of three (A, B, C), each with one set of Set D cards, face down. Each round, A takes the next card and reads the A side; B reads the B side (how to react: for example, "At first, don't accept. Accept if A offers a reasonable alternative."). C takes the observer role.
  • Round shape (5 minutes): 1 minute to plan (A writes the opening line in the workbook planner using a frame: acknowledge → view / SBI → reason → proposal) · 3 minutes to talk · 1 minute for C's feedback. Rotate roles. Groups of two: skip the observer and give feedback to each other with the checklist.
  • Observer checklist (rubric criteria): Task & Purpose ☐ the real message was clear ☐ a way forward was agreed · Register & Tone ☐ acknowledged first ☐ one or two softeners, not too many ☐ no blame words (always, never, you're…) · Language Range & Accuracy ☐ toolkit phrases used correctly · Organization & Delivery ☐ calm pace ☐ the firm point was stressed, not swallowed.
  • All situations are ordinary and fictional (deadlines, workloads, late reports, a delivery delay). Learners who want to prepare for a real conversation may adapt a card privately in the planner, but no one is asked to share a real situation.

L1 and cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Directness varies widely. In some workplaces, disagreement and feedback are expected to be explicit; in others, a "small concern" or a silence is the strong message. Neither is more professional. Teach learners to calibrate: with a very direct counterpart, get to the point; with an indirect one, lead with more acknowledgement and listen for what is not said. Avoid national labels in class discussion; say "some workplaces" and "some people."
  • Over-direct translations: "I want you send the report today," "You must correct this," "Do it by Friday." In many languages these are neutral; in English workplaces they can sound like orders. Model Could you… · Would it be possible to… · I'd need it by….
  • Over-formal or archaic formulas: "Kindly do the needful," "Please revert back," "I am writing to inform you that…" in every message. Show the neutral modern version beside each one.
  • "Yes" that means no. In some workplace cultures, "I'll try" or "It may be difficult" is a polite refusal; in others it is heard as a promise. Teach the clear-but-kind no: "I'm afraid I can't…, but I could…" and the time-buyer: "Let me check and get back to you this afternoon."
  • Feedback to seniors. Some learners come from workplaces where giving feedback upward is not done. Keep the cards peer-to-peer or manager-to-team-member for feedback, and use "pushing back on a plan" (not "criticizing a person") for upward situations.
  • Over-apologizing ("Sorry, sorry, I'm probably wrong…") undermines the message. One "sorry" for a real inconvenience is enough; do not apologize for having a view.

Running the speaking simulations — how to do it well
  • Pairing. Pair learners of similar confidence rather than a very strong speaker with a very hesitant one, so nobody's score depends on a partner who dominates. Give Card A (the chair in the meeting) to the learner who needs stretch on chairing, or who hasn't chaired in class yet. Write the pairs and roles on your pair-order sheet before class.
  • The calls. Seat the pair back to back. "A, say 'Ring ring' when you're ready." Partners must not show each other their cards. The receptionist fills in the message pad on their card; check it after the call: it is part of the Task & Purpose evidence (all details correct, read-back done).
  • The meeting. Turn the chairs to face each other. Three minutes on the timer, visible to the chair. Card A opens, goes through the agenda, makes sure everyone gives a view, reaches a decision on each item and closes with action points. In a pair, Card A also holds Card C's views and gives them as their own, so there is always something to disagree about.
  • Prompts. If a learner freezes, give one neutral prompt ("What would you say to the receptionist now?") and note it on the sheet; don't rescue with the answer. Otherwise stay silent and keep your face neutral and friendly.
  • Scoring. Score each learner separately, one whole number 1–5 per criterion, based on all the evidence from both calls and the meeting (use the task columns on the score sheet for quick notes during the talk). Score what you hear, not what you know the learner can do. Accent itself is not scored; clarity is. Use the observer prompt in the performance-check pack for the moves to listen for (opening + purpose, "I see your point, but…", "Sorry to interrupt, but…", action points with names and days).
  • Support and stretch (from the run-sheet). A B1 learner may keep the phrase card from the Module 1 materials on the table: note "phrase card used" on the sheet. Tell a C1 learner before Call 1: "Offer the caller an alternative contact, and deal with one request you can't meet."
  • Consistency. Before class, reread the rubric descriptors for 3 and 4 on each criterion: these are the hardest to separate. After the first two pairs, glance back at your sheets. Do not change earlier scores because a later pair was stronger or weaker.

Watch-outs: habits from other assessment systems
  • Memorized scripts. Learners who prepare long memorized paragraphs sound fluent but don't respond to the partner, which lowers Task & Purpose and Organization & Delivery. Say in the framing: "Listen and respond to your partner; that's part of the score."
  • Longer and more formal is not better. In Task 1, some learners write far more than 170 words in a very formal style ("I am writing to inform you that…", "Kindly…"). The through-line

Inform → trade → persuade. In C9 learners inform: they give a clear, structured talk that an audience can follow without effort, because every turn is signposted. In C10 they trade: they discover what the other side really needs and swap concessions conditionally, so that both sides leave with enough to say yes. In C11 they persuade: they turn features into benefits, back them with honest evidence, ask for one clear action and stay calm under objections. The same four habits run through all three: structure out loud (signposts, stages, pitch steps), focus on the listener ("what's in it for them"), stay polite under pressure (soften, acknowledge, never a flat "no") and finish with a clear action (a call to action, a written summary, a next step). Each class produces something a listener responds to: a mini-presentation with Q&A, a recorded deal, a pitch that survives two objections.

Confidence, face and speaking in front of others

This is the most exposed module of the course. Many of your learners are highly competent professionals who presented, negotiated and sold with ease in their first language, and now feel judged on their English instead of their expertise. Some are rebuilding a career after a move and may be working below their qualifications. Say these things explicitly in C9 and whenever someone stands up to speak:

  • "Presenters are made, not born." (It's the online lesson's message too.) Nervous first-timers and polished speakers are the same person a few presentations apart.
  • Structure beats accent. An audience forgives an accent and a grammar slip; it doesn't forgive getting lost. Signposting is the fastest route to sounding in control, and learners can plan it in advance.
  • Small groups first. All three simulations run in groups of 2–3, not in front of the whole class. Nobody presents to the full room in this module unless they volunteer. The whole-class capstone comes in C16, after weeks of rehearsal.
  • Their expertise is an asset. Invite learners with professional experience in sales, procurement, teaching or management to share one tip in English. Never ask what their job "used to be" or why they left it; let them volunteer it.
  • Nothing personal is required. Every presentation, negotiation and pitch can use a role profile (Set B): a fictional person, job and company with ready-made figures. Learners may present their real work only if they want to, and should never share confidential information, salaries or a real employer's name. Say so in C9.
Keep the conflicts ordinary

All negotiation and pitch scenarios in this kit are fictional and deliberately low-stakes: delivery prices, launch dates, a training venue, meeting-room booking, a lunch-box order. Don't replace them with salary negotiations, job cuts, disputes about discrimination or anything political: those are real stressors for some learners and poor practice material. If learners want to rehearse a real situation (for example, asking for more time on a project), they may do so in the homework task, privately, on their own terms.

3.1 · C9

Presentations — Structure & Delivery

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • read their Midpoint Performance Check results criterion by criterion and link their target for the Final Assessment to a Module 3 skill
  • open a presentation with the five jobs (greet · introduce yourself and the topic · state the purpose and the benefit · outline the structure · housekeeping on questions) and a one-sentence hook
  • choose the right signpost for each move (moving on, sequencing, referring back or forward, giving an example, emphasizing, summarizing) and say it with clear stress and a pause
  • describe a simple chart in four steps: introduce → direct attention → describe the trend → say what it means
  • handle a question they can't answer: clarify, answer briefly or promise a follow-up, and park an off-topic question politely

Returning the Midpoint Performance Check — how to run it (12 min)

This follows the procedure in the Module 2 guide ("Returning the results: the C9 warmer"), with two extra minutes to link each learner's target to Module 3. You need each learner's completed speaking and writing score sheets from assessment/performance-check.html (Writing /20, Speaking /20, one strength with evidence, one or two targets, set with "From Scores to Targets" in assessment/assessment-key.html), folded, with the learner's name on the outside. Learners need their Performance Tracker (performance-tracker.html). Follow the "Returning Results Kindly" principles in the assessment key.

  • Frame it (1 min): "Today you get your midpoint results: two scores, one for writing and one for speaking, and one or two targets. These results are a map, not a verdict. In C8 you wrote under time pressure and spoke with someone listening and taking notes. That's hard in any language. The numbers don't measure your professional ability, and nobody else will see them. The targets are what we'll work on."
  • Return privately (2 min): hand back each learner's folded score sheets. Learners read quietly. Circulate; stop briefly with anyone who looks surprised or upset ("Let's talk for two minutes at the break"), and never discuss a score in front of others.
  • Record (3 min): learners copy both scores into section 2 of their Performance Tracker, next to their Class 1 diagnostic scores, and their targets into section 4. "Compare yourself only with yourself. Writing and speaking often move at different speeds." Remind them what a 3 means on a criterion: the job is done, with some problems.
  • Quiet pair task (3 min): pairs do "Rewrite one sentence from your C8 email that you'd now write differently" (the "After the check (C9)" box in the Module 2 workbook). Each learner shows the before and after sentence, never the score. Board two or three good examples without names.
  • Link the target to Module 3 (2 min): learners complete "My Midpoint target in Module 3" in Workbook 3.1: they choose the Module 3 skill that matches their target from the target menu, and one action they can check. ✘ "Better English." ✔ "Signpost every move in my C16 presentation and practice it aloud twice." ✔ "Soften every refusal: a reason + an alternative." Point out that Module 3 works directly on Organization & Delivery (signposting, negotiation stages, pitch structure) and Register & Tone (saying no diplomatically).
  • Close (1 min): "In C14 we rehearse the capstone, and I'll ask you: what did you do about your target?" Keep the score sheets (or copies) with your records for the Final Assessment comparison. If some pairs did not finish Part B in C8, the Module 2 guide explains how to run them now and return their results in C10.
  • Individual talks: anyone whose result upset them, or whose score is far below their professional level, gets a few minutes with you at the break or during the Carousel prep, never in front of others. Be specific and kind: "Your Task & Purpose was a 5: your email did everything it needed to do. Your Language score is a 3 because of articles and past tense, and those are very fixable." Learners who scored low in speaking because of nerves: tell them so, and point to the small-group format of this module. Use the class record privately: if one criterion is low for most learners, lean on the matching Module 3 warmers and drills.
Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: topic vs purpose vs benefit. The topic is what the talk is about; the purpose is what the speaker wants to achieve; the benefit is what the audience gets.

"Today I'm going to talk about late invoices." Topic or purpose? (Topic.) "The purpose of my talk is to get your approval for a new reminder system." (Purpose.) "By the end, you'll know how to get paid two weeks faster." Who is that sentence for: the speaker or the audience? (The audience: it's the benefit.) · "Please feel free to ask questions as we go." When can I ask a question: now or at the end? (Now, during the talk.) "I'll take questions at the end." Can I interrupt? (Better not; wait until the end.)

Meaning: signposts by function. A signpost tells the audience where the speaker is going, like a road sign.

"This brings me to my next point." Is the speaker starting, moving forward or finishing? (Moving forward to a new point.) · "As I mentioned earlier…" Forward or back? (Back, to something already said.) "We'll come to that shortly." Now or later? (Later, soon.) · "In conclusion…" Can I say this in the middle of the talk? (No: only at the end.) · "The key point here is…" Is this an example or the most important idea? (The most important idea.) · "For instance…" Same meaning as? (For example.)

Meaning: describing and interpreting a visual.

"On-time deliveries rose sharply in Q3." Big change or small change? (Big.) Fast or slow? (Fast.) "They leveled off in Q4." Did they go up, down or stay about the same? (About the same.) · "They peaked at 93%." Was 93% the highest or the lowest point? (Highest.) · "This graph shows our deliveries." Is that describing or interpreting? (Describing: introducing.) "What this tells us is that the new routes are working." (Interpreting: the meaning for the audience.) Which one does the audience care about most? (The meaning.)

Meaning: Q&A.

"I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up by tomorrow." Does the speaker know the answer now? (No.) Are they going to guess? (No.) What will they do? (Find it and send it later.) · "Can we take that offline?" Discuss it now, in front of everyone, or later, just the two of us? (Later, privately.) · Is it weak to say "I don't know"? (No, it's honest and professional, as long as you offer to follow up.)

Pronunciation. A signpost is a chunk with one main stress and a pause after it: This brings me to my NEXT point. (pause) The cost. · The KEY point here is… (pause) · So, to sum UP… (pause). The pause is the audible paragraph break; nervous speakers skip it. Numbers: thirTEEN vs THIRty, fifTEEN vs FIFty: stress the ending on -teen and repeat important figures ("ninety-three, nine-three, percent"). Nouns vs verbs: an INcrease / to inCREASE. Q&A: a falling tone on That's a great QUEStion sounds calm; a rising tone sounds unsure.

Drill script
  1. Choral, then individual, with the pause: T: "Good morning, everyone, and thank you all for COMing." → Class repeats. "I've divided my talk into THREE parts." · "Let's START with the problem." · "This brings me to my NEXT point." · "The KEY point here is…" · "So, to sum UP…" Tap the table on each pause.
  2. Function → phrase (rapid round): T: "Moving on!" → S1: "Let's move on to the cost." T: "Example!" → S2: "For instance, …" T: "Back!" → S3: "As I mentioned earlier, …" T: "Forward!" → S4: "We'll come to that shortly." T: "Emphasis!" → S5: "What I want to stress is…" T: "Recap!" → S6: "Let me recap the main points."
  3. Trend + meaning chain: T: "Sales, Q1 to Q2, 100 to 150." → S1: "Sales rose sharply in Q2." → S2: "What this tells us is that the campaign worked." T: "Costs, flat all year." → S1: "Costs stayed flat." → S2: "The takeaway here is that our savings held."
  4. Can't-answer frame: T (as audience): "What was the figure for 2023?" → S: "I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up by tomorrow." T: "What about the IT budget?" → S: "That's a bit outside today's scope. Can we take that offline?"

Running the Presentation Carousel (role-play management)
  • Grouping: threes, mixed levels, and never three learners from the same workplace or first language if you can avoid it. With a group of four, add a second questioner; with a pair, the questioner also observes.
  • Timing: 5 min prep (planner) · then three rounds of 5 minutes: 2 min presenting · 1 min Q&A · 1 min observer feedback · 1 min to rotate. Call the time loudly. A presenter who runs over is stopped at 2:15 with "Close now, please": closing on time is part of the skill.
  • Roles: Presenter (stands if they're comfortable; sitting is fine) · Questioner (draws a card from the Q&A pack, Set C: 8 questions, including one the presenter can't answer and one that is off-topic) · Observer (checklist card, Set G).
  • Observer checklist (the four criteria): Task & Purpose: was the purpose clear? was there a clear call to action or conclusion? · Register & Tone: polite, confident, audience-focused ("you" language)? · Language Range & Accuracy: at least three different signposts? trend words used correctly? · Organization & Delivery: opening with the five jobs? a signpost at every turn? eye contact, pauses, not reading? a calm, short answer to the question?
  • What you do: stand near each group for one full round. Note in four columns. Don't interrupt a presenter, even for a serious error. If a group finishes early, they repeat with a new question card or stretch constraint (below).
  • Face-saving: anyone who is very anxious can present sitting, to one person, using the planner as notes, or can be the observer twice and present last. Everyone presents at least once, in whatever format they can manage today.
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Give the fill-in opening frame in the Presentation planner: "Good ___, everyone. I'm ___ from ___. Today I'm going to talk about ___. By the end, you'll ___. I've divided my talk into two parts: first, ___; then, ___. Please ___." Allow two points instead of three and a 90-second talk. Let them use a role profile, which has the figures ready. Ex 3 items 1–4 only. In the Carousel, they present third, after hearing two models.

Stretch (C1)

Constraints and register-shifting. (1) Present the same content twice: once to senior managers (formal, 90 seconds, numbers first), once to your own team (informal, 90 seconds, benefits first). (2) Include one referring-back and one referring-forward signpost and a rhetorical question. (3) As questioner, ask a hostile or off-topic question; as presenter, park it politely. (4) Take the chair's role: introduce the next speaker and manage the Q&A time ("We have time for one more question").

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "I will present you…": a translation of présenter / presentar / präsentieren + indirect object. Teach I'd like to present to you…, I'll show you…, or simply Today I'm going to talk about…. Also ✘ explain me → ✔ explain to me, and ✘ discuss about → ✔ discuss + noun.
  • "In conclusion" too early: learners from school traditions where every paragraph has a formal conclusion use in conclusion as "in summary of this point." In English it means "the talk is ending." Use So, to sum up this part… mid-talk.
  • Irregular past and articles in trends: ✘ rised, falled, the sales was growing up; ✘ There was sharp rise. Drill rose / fell / peaked and the chunk a sharp rise in. Sales is plural: sales were.
  • Numbers: many European and Latin American learners write 2,5 million (decimal comma) and 1.000 (thousand point); in English it's 2.5 million, 1,000. Some languages count in milliards, so billion can be misheard; and -teen / -ty confusion causes real errors in meetings. Check figures by asking "fifteen, one-five?"
  • Eye contact and self-promotion: in some workplace cultures, steady eye contact with a senior person feels disrespectful, and presenting your own team's success can feel like boasting. Don't frame this as right or wrong. Explain the English-speaking workplace expectation (look around the room, state results plainly: "we cut waiting times by half") and let learners find a version that feels honest to them.
  • Questions during the talk: some learners come from settings where interrupting a speaker, or questioning a senior colleague, is rude; others from settings where it's expected. That's exactly why the housekeeping line ("feel free to ask as we go" / "I'll take questions at the end") exists: it makes the rule explicit for everyone.
  • Flat intonation: speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Tagalog, Turkish and others) may give every word the same weight. In presentations this sounds bored rather than rude. The signpost drill (one stress, one pause) is the fix.

3.2 · C10

Negotiations — Language & Strategy

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain the difference between a position (what someone says they want) and an interest (why they want it), and ask a question that uncovers the interest
  • name the five stages of a negotiation and use phrases to open warmly, propose, accept, reject without offense (reason + route forward) and counter-propose
  • bargain with conditionals: the first conditional for a real offer (If you commit to two years, we'll…), the second for testing the water (If you were to double the order, would you…?), and provided that / as long as
  • make concessions that are small, conditional and labeled, and ask for something back every time
  • close a deal by summarizing it aloud and confirming it in a short email
Materials
  • Workbook 3.2 (Key words, Key phrases, Language box, Model dialogue, Exercises 1–5, Deal Room planner, confirmation email frame, self-check)
  • Slides 9–13 · Signpost Bingo (Set F) for the warmer · Negotiation flashcards (Set A2) · Deal Room A/B cards with points tables + teacher's deal record (Set D) · observer checklists (Set G) · timer · Script 3.2-A (a negotiation extract)

Left: position vs interest. POSITION = what they say ("We need 15% off.") · INTEREST = why ("My budget resets in January.") → ask: What's driving that? · What's most important to you here? · If we could solve X, would that change things? Stages: ① prepare (ideal · limit · BATNA) ② open ③ explore ④ propose & bargain ⑤ close.

Center: never give something for nothing. If you commit to two years, we'll waive the fee. (real offer) · If you were to double the order, would you…? (testing) · We'll include training provided that / as long as you order today. Concessions: small · slow · conditional · labeled ("That's a big step for us") · reciprocal · keep one in reserve.

Right: no → not yet. ✘ "No. Impossible." ✔ I'm afraid that doesn't quite work for us, because… What we could offer instead is… · Deadlock: Let me take that back to my team. · If price is fixed, could we look at delivery or terms? Close: So, to confirm what we've agreed… I'll send the details by email this afternoon.

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • "If you will…": because the action is in the future, learners from many first languages put will after if (and after when, as long as, provided that). ✘ If you will sign → ✔ If you sign. Also over-polite ✘ If you would sign, we would… for a real, firm offer.
  • Over-direct requests: ✘ I want you give me discount / You must deliver on Monday (translated directly) → ✔ Could you do a little better on the price? · Would it be possible to deliver on Monday? Missing articles: ✘ give discount → ✔ a discount. Also ✘ I am agree → ✔ I agree / We can live with that.
  • Directness in refusals: some workplace cultures prefer a clear "no" and find hints confusing; others almost never say "no" and signal it with "that would be difficult" or silence. Teach learners to notice both: an indirect "that might be difficult" from their counterpart deserves the question "What would make it easier?"; and their own "no" should come with a reason and an alternative.
  • Haggling norms: in some markets and workplaces bargaining is expected and friendly; in others, pushing on a quoted price can seem rude. Relationship-first vs deal-first: some counterparts expect small talk and trust before numbers. The online lesson's culture note says it well: observe, mirror the pace, and when unsure, ask.
  • Silence: learners from some cultures are comfortable with long pauses; others rush to fill silence and give away a concession. Practice the "strategic pause": make an offer, then stop talking.
  • "Discuss about the price" (Spanish discutir sobre, French discuter de): ✔ discuss the price. And negotiate + object, or negotiate with + person: ✘ negotiate about the price with is acceptable but heavy; ✔ negotiate the price with the supplier.

C12–C14 follow the course's workshop shape: warmer (review of the last class) → lead-in linked to learners' real needs → model (an executive summary, a CV extract, an interview answer, a client call) → notice and clarify the language (phrase banks, register, Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions, CCQs) → drill (choral → individual; stress, intonation, polite tone) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a role-play or simulation with A/B cards) → feedback on register, tone and clarity, with delayed error correction → homework. C15 and C16 are assessment classes with their own plans.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-writing-career.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C12. It holds the word cards (document phrases, register pairs, action verbs, STAR, cross-cultural phrases), the proposal brief cards (A/B) for C12, the interview kit (candidate role cards, job adverts, interviewer cards, observer checklist) for C13 and C16, the integrated scenario cards for C14, the capstone kit (planner card, objection-style Q&A cards, peer feedback slips), the C16 kit (pitch running order, audience question slips, scored-interview slot schedule, performance profile cards), the Toolkit Bingo game and the listening scripts.
  • The Final Assessment is a separate document: assessment/final-assessment.html (learner tasks) and assessment/assessment-key.html (the one shared rubric, model answers, negotiation role cards and scoring sheets). Read both before C15. The task wording, word counts and timings printed there overrule any number in this guide.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and copy the stress and intonation.
  • This is the course's writing module. C12 and C15 ask learners to write under time pressure, and many working adults rarely write more than an email. Give real writing time in class (at least 10 minutes in C12, a full hour in C15) and keep the room quiet while people write. Protect the freer practice in every class: if you're running late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the role-play or the writing.
  • Feedback in three columns. During freer practice, take notes in three columns: register & tone (too direct, too casual, or too old-fashioned: You must approve this · Kindly do the needful · Hey, need a yes today), language (errors that cost clarity: discuss about, I am agree, responsible of, an experience of five years, informations, It is recommended that the team is expand) and clarity & structure (a buried recommendation, no clear ask, a STAR answer with no result, a pitch with no call to action). In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names, and always include one excellent phrase to spread.
  • One rubric, everywhere. All observer checklists and score slips use the course's single analytic rubric (full version in assessment/assessment-key.html): Task & Purpose, Register & Tone, Language Range & Accuracy and Organization & Delivery, each scored 1–5, total /20. Learners have used it since C1 and at the C8 midpoint check; in this module they use it to give each other feedback, and you use it for the Final Assessment.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling, like the kit (prioritize, organize, summarize, analyze, program, behavioral), but keep the word CV. Note that in the US and Canada a job-application CV is usually called a résumé; the kit says CV to match the online lessons. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them, and never mark an accent as "wrong."
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Write it so they can decide → present yourself so they can say yes → bring it all together. In C12 learners write for a busy reader: the answer first, the evidence behind it, and recommendations someone could act on on Monday. In C13 the same logic applies to their own career: prove results instead of listing duties, and answer interview questions with a structured story that ends in a result. In C14 they add the skill that ties the course together, adapting to other people's norms, rehearse the whole client scenario (email → meeting → pitch → negotiation → follow-up) and plan their capstone. C15–C16 turn that work into evidence: a timed writing task, a negotiation, a capstone presentation with Q&A and a short mock interview, all scored on the same four criteria learners have used since C1.

Career stories: the learner decides what to share

C13 and C16 ask learners to talk about work history, and your class may include job seekers, people between jobs, people working below their qualification level, and newcomers whose degrees and licenses are still being recognized. Nobody has to disclose their salary, employment status, immigration status or a real employer. Every CV and interview task offers three equally good options: your real history, a candidate role card (a fictional person with a fictional job and company), or a mix (your real skills in a fictional company). Say it every class: "You're practicing the English, not handing in your life story." Keep the tone respectful when a learner's history includes a career gap, a career change or a step to a more junior role in a new country: these are normal professional stories, and the language box gives positive, factual ways to tell them. Qualifications from another country are qualifications: never call them "not real" or "not valid"; talk about recognition, credential evaluation and local licensing as practical steps. Never collect, display or share a learner's real CV. If a learner offers to show you theirs, look at it privately. Interviewer cards contain only job-related questions: never salary, visa or immigration status, nationality, age, family, health or religion, and never why someone left a job or a gap in employment (the same rule as the Final Assessment interviewer card). A learner may choose to mention a gap or a career change; the C13 language box helps them do it positively. We don't role-play unfair or illegal interviews. For help with credential recognition or job searching, point learners to your program's career or employment services; don't give legal or immigration advice.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: report or proposal? The difference is emphasis: a report leads with what is; a proposal leads with what should be. A report may end with recommendations; a proposal must sell the benefits.

"Give me the findings from the customer survey." Report or proposal? (Report.) "Convince me to fund a second delivery van." (Proposal.) Can a report include recommendations? (Yes, often.) Which one must sell? (The proposal.) Which one looks mainly at the past and present? (The report.)

Meaning: the executive summary.

Do you write the executive summary first? (No: last.) Where does it go in the document? (First.) The director reads only this page. Can she make a decision? (She must be able to.) Can it say "This report will discuss the causes"? (No: state the causes.) Can it say "as discussed above"? (No: it must stand alone.) How long? (Short: a few sentences, about 5–10% of the document.)

Meaning: the subjunctive and should / could.

"It is recommended that the team be expanded." Has the team been expanded yet? (No.) Is this an order? (No: a formal recommendation.) Is it more or less formal than "We should hire more people"? (More formal.) Why "be" and not "is"? (After recommend / propose / suggest that…, formal American English uses the base form.) "We should prioritize the billing fix." Firm or optional? (Firm.) "We could also consider a weekend shift." (Optional: worth considering.)

Form.

PurposeThe aim / purpose of this report is to + verb · This report examines / assesses… · This proposal sets out…This report examines the causes of late deliveries in the first quarter.
FindingsThe results show / indicate that… · It was found that… (passive) · Notably, …It was found that 70% of late orders were on Monday and Tuesday routes.
DataAs shown in Figure 1, … (no article before Figure 1) · See Appendix AAs shown in Figure 1, costs peaked in March.
Conclusion (hedged)This suggests that… · It appears that… · On balance, …This suggests that the problem is seasonal.
RecommendWe recommend + -ing · We recommend that + subject + base form · It is recommended that + subject + be + past participleWe recommend leasing a second van. · It is recommended that the contract be renewed.
PriorityAs a priority, … · In the short term, …; in the longer term, … · numbered listIn the short term, we should fix the billing error; in the longer term, we could review pay bands.

Pronunciation. Findings live or die on numbers. Contrast the stress: fourTEEN vs FORty, fifTEEN vs FIFty, and say percent with stress on the second syllable (perCENT), never "percents." Recommendations are spoken in chunks with the key word stressed: We reCOMmend | HIRing two Agents | by the END of Q3. Data is DAY-ta or DAH-ta: both are fine.

Drill script
  1. Phrase chain (choral, then three individuals): "The AIM of this REport is to…" → "The reSULTS inDIcate that…" → "NOTEably, …" → "This sugGESTS that…" → "We reCOMmend that…". Learners finish each one with a word from the Brightline story ("…that complaints doubled").
  2. -teen or -ty? (T–S, then pairs): T says "fourteen percent" or "forty percent"; learners hold up one finger for -teen, two for -ty. Six items (14/40, 15/50, 16/60, 13/30, 18/80, 19/90). Then pairs do the same with each other.
  3. Transform it (individual, fast): T: "I think we lost customers because of the price." → S: "The data indicates that customer loss was driven by price." T: "We found out that most calls are about tracking." → "It was found that most calls concern tracking." T: "You should hire two more people." → "We recommend hiring two more agents." / "It is recommended that two agents be hired." T: "Firm: prioritize the billing fix." → "We should prioritize the billing fix." T: "Option: a weekend shift." → "We could also consider a weekend shift."
The decision-maker — how to run it
  • Grouping. Pairs. Give half the pairs brief pair 1 (Northwind Logistics / Brightline Foods) and the other half brief pair 2 (Kestrel Design / Clearwater Health Supplies), so neighbors can't copy. Odd numbers: one trio in which the third learner is an observer with the checklist and becomes a second decision-maker in round 2.
  • The information gap. Each card has two parts: Your proposal (the facts for your own summary) and When you are the decision-maker (your budget, priorities and two questions to ask your partner). The writer can't see the decision-maker's limits, so negotiation happens naturally (for example, the Northwind proposal costs more in its first year than the director can approve alone).
  • Writing (10 min). 80–120 words, in the workbook frame, with a title. Walk round and check three things only: the purpose is in the first sentence, there is at least one number, and the recommendations are numbered. Don't correct grammar now.
  • Decision rounds (6 min each). 1 minute of silent reading (the decision-maker must stop at 60 seconds: "That's all a director has"), 4 minutes of questions, answers and a decision, 1 minute of feedback. Decision language is on slide 7: I'm happy to approve this on one condition… · Could you clarify…? · I'd need to see… before I can approve… · Let's start with phase one.
  • Observer / partner checklist (in the workbook and on the card): Task & Purpose: purpose, findings and recommendations are all there; the ask is clear. Register & Tone: objective, no chatty phrases, no jargon; the spoken answers stay polite under pressure. Language Range & Accuracy: document phrases from the bank; recommend + -ing or the subjunctive used correctly; numbers accurate. Organization & Delivery: purpose → findings → recommendations; recommendations numbered or prioritized; clear, calm answers.
  • You sit with a different pair each round and take three-column notes. If a decision-maker says only "OK, approved," prompt: "Ask one of your questions first."
ICQs: How many words in your summary? (80 to 120.) What comes first? (The purpose.) When you're the decision-maker, how long do you read? (60 seconds only.) Can you show your partner your budget? (No: ask questions and decide.) What are the three possible decisions? (Approve, approve with changes, not yet.) What must you give with your decision? (One reason.)
Differentiation
Support (B1)

Give B1 learners the partially completed frame in the workbook: This proposal sets out… · Currently, … · Notably, … · We recommend (1)… (2)… · Together, these steps are expected to… They fill it from their brief card; 80 words is enough. Fewer items: Ex 3 items 1–6, Ex 4 items 1–4. As decision-makers, they read the two questions printed on their card and choose a decision from the three phrases on the slide. Target: a summary with a purpose, two numbers and two numbered recommendations.

Stretch (C1)

Strong writers tend to write too much. Set a strict 100-word ceiling and ask them to include (1) one hedged finding (This suggests…), (2) one risk with its mitigation (The main risk is…; to manage this, we will…) and (3) a recommendation in the subjunctive. As decision-makers, they must push back with a counter-condition ("I can approve the software now if the van is phased into Q3"). Extra: rewrite one recommendation for two readers, a finance director (cost first) and the team (workload first).

L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Old-fashioned formulas taught as "business English" in some school systems: Kindly do the needful · Please find herewith · We humbly request · I am writing to inform you that… at the start of every document. Formal documents today are clear and modern: This report examines… · We recommend…
  • Density as a sign of respect. Some academic and civil-service traditions reward long sentences and heavy nouns (the implementation of the optimization of…). Say plainly: in most English-speaking workplaces, a short, clear document is the respectful one, because it saves the reader's time.
  • The recommendation at the end, or not at all. In some workplace cultures, telling a senior reader what to do feels presumptuous, so learners bury the recommendation or hedge it away. Explain that "bottom line up front" is the expected, polite norm in many English-speaking organizations, and that We recommend… is professional, not rude.
  • Uncountable nouns and verb patterns: informations, researches, feedbacks, equipments, advices; discuss about, emphasize on, recommend to do (✔ recommend doing / recommend that we do). The data shows / show: accept both.
  • Numbers: decimal commas (14,5%) and thousands points (9.600 $) from many European and Latin American systems. In US documents: 14.5%, $9,600, currency symbol first. Stress -teen / -ty clearly when presenting.
  • Articles: the data, the results, the appendix, but no article before a label: as shown in Figure 1, see Appendix A.

Mock interview trios — how to run it
  • Roles. The interviewer reads only from the interviewer card: friendly, professional, no teaching. The candidate answers (real history, a role card, or a mix: say it again). The observer keeps time and fills the checklist. Give confident talkers the observer role first: it asks them to listen.
  • Materials. Each trio gets the three job adverts, one interviewer card (A for rounds 1 and 3, B for round 2, so questions vary) and three observer checklists. Candidates choose the advert that best fits their history or role card.
  • One round (7½ min): the interviewer opens ("Thank you for coming in. Please have a seat.") → Tell me about yourself (under 1 min) → one STAR question from the card → one more question from the card → "Do you have any questions for us?" → close ("Thank you, we'll be in touch.") → observer feedback: one strength and one next step, both quoted (1½ min).

Salary, visa or immigration status, nationality, age, family, health, religion, why someone left a job, or gaps in employment: the same list as the Final Assessment interviewer card. L1 & cross-cultural watch-outs
  • Modesty norms. In many workplace cultures, saying "I achieved…" feels like boasting, and learners say "we" throughout or give credit to the team. Explain that interviewers in many English-speaking countries need to hear your contribution; "I" in the Action is expected, and you can still thank the team in the Result.
  • CV conventions differ by country: a photo, date of birth, marital status or nationality on the CV is normal in some markets and is left off in the US, Canada, the UK and others to reduce bias. Present it as "local norms," never as one right way. Learners decide what fits the market they're applying in.
  • "I have an experience of five years" and "since five years" (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Arabic patterns): teach five years' experience · I've worked there for five years. "Responsible of" / "in charge for": responsible for, in charge of.
  • Titles and first names. Some learners will call the interviewer "Sir" or "Madam" throughout; others use a first name immediately. Teach the middle path: Ms. / Mr. + surname until invited, or the first name if the interviewer introduces themselves that way.
  • Handshakes and eye contact vary by culture and by person. Don't require a handshake in the role-play; a greeting, a smile and "Nice to meet you" work everywhere. Steady eye contact is expected in many English-speaking interviews but is uncomfortable for some learners: suggest looking at the interviewer's face while listening and at the end of each answer.
  • Weakness answers. Learners from systems where admitting a weakness is risky may say "I have no weakness" or "I'm a perfectionist." Show the pattern: a real, non-essential weakness + what you do about it.

Class size

Six pairs fit in three rounds. With four pairs or fewer, run two rounds of 9 minutes and use the spare time for a quick debrief. With seven or eight pairs, seat three pairs per round and rely more on the deal memos, or move the last round to the start of C16 and use pair pitches there. Running the negotiations
  • Before round 1, say (from the run-sheet): "You have seven minutes. Start with a friendly opening, find out what matters to the other side, and trade: nothing for nothing. Before time is up, summarize what you've agreed. A deal isn't required. A clear, polite 'we can't agree today, so here's the next step' is a good result too."
  • Don't intervene. If a pair reaches a deal in 3 minutes, say only: "Check every item on your card: have you agreed all of them?" If a pair drifts into a personal argument, pause them: "Let's go back to the terms on your cards." Keep the conflict commercial.
  • Card A (seller / agency) protects a minimum and pushes for a long-term commitment: give it to a learner who needs stretch. For support, allow the Lesson 3.2 phrase card on the table and note it on the score sheet.
  • Scoring. You score the language and the process, not the numbers: exploring interests, trading, firm-but-polite register, conditionals, a clear summary (and the deal memo). A learner who reaches a modest deal politely and clearly can score well; a learner who "wins" by refusing to trade or by being rude can't score high on Register & Tone. The shape of a good deal and "what to listen for" are in the run-sheet and the assessment key: don't tell learners in advance.
  • Two scores, not five. The writing (Tasks 1 + 2) becomes one Writing /20; the negotiation, the C16 pitch and the interview together become one Speaking /20, on the four criteria. Make task notes now; decide the criterion scores after C16.

Running a fair assessment in a community or workplace class
  • Conditions matter more than the room. Quiet, spaced seats if possible, a visible clock, a sign on the door. A learner who arrives late starts late and finishes with everyone else; note it on their paper.
  • Accommodations. If a learner has an agreed accommodation in your program (extra time, enlarged print, a computer for writing), give it here too and note it.
  • Distress. If a learner becomes very upset, let them step out and decide whether to continue or finish at home as untimed practice; mark the paper "untimed." An assessment is never worth more than a person's wellbeing.
  • Role cards count the same. Task 2 Option A can use the learner's own experience or the role-card profile printed on the paper; both are scored the same way.

  • Topics follow the Capstone Pitch Brief: an improvement at the learner's workplace (real or role card) or an idea for a company they'd like to join; the Review Project approach from C14; or Brief 1 (Northwind "Arrival alerts"), Brief 2 (Brightline "Crates, not cardboard") or Brief 3 (Kestrel "Focus Friday mornings"). Differentiation
Support (B1)

Offer B1 learners a pair pitch with a supportive partner, or a ready-made Brief, and let them choose an early or a late slot (ask privately in C15). They may keep their one notes card in hand. Stretch (C1)

C1 learners who opt in on their preparation sheet get the stretch interview questions ("You have more experience than this role requires. Why this job?" · "Where do you see yourself in five years?") and a harder objection with a follow-up. Ask one to chair the Q&A for another presenter (introduce the speaker, take the question slip, thank them).

Watch-outs
  • No scores in C16. The final assessment document asks you to name improvements, never scores, in class. Keep all results private.
  • Real job news. Some learners may be waiting for a real interview result or may have just lost a job. Keep the practice interviews light and role-card-friendly, and never ask about real applications in front of the group.
  • Freezing in a pitch. Say quietly, "Take a breath. What's your next point?" or ask an easy question to restart. Score what they did, not the pause.
  • Food at the celebration. Check allergies, dietary rules and fasting periods before anyone brings food. A celebration can be just words, certificates and thanks.

If your program runs a follow-up session a month later, invite learners to bring one real email, meeting or interview story: it's the best motivation for the next group.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C14: the module wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "write it so they can decide, present yourself so they can say yes, bring it all together": the answer first · a standalone executive summary · S-A-P-C-T recommendations (write it so they can decide) → action verb + result · STAR with I and a result · your path in one positive sentence · two questions at the end (present yourself) → start formal · read the soft signal · trade conditionally · confirm in writing (bring it all together). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you: use those notes when you write each learner's next step in C16.

What the Final Assessment tells you. It's the course's third set of evidence: a Writing /20 and a Speaking /20, set beside C1 and C8 on each learner's score sheet and performance tracker. The trend on each criterion is the finding, not a single total. Expect Organization & Delivery and Register & Tone to move most (structures and habits), and Language Range & Accuracy to move more slowly.

If you teach the course again: keep your three-column notes from C12–C14. The most frequent errors (discuss about, I am agree, five years of experiences, responsible of) and the most common clarity problems (the buried recommendation, the STAR answer with no result, the pitch with no call to action) make the best warmers for the next group.
