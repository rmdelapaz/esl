B · Proposal Brief Cards — The Decision-Maker (4.1)

Pair 1 = cards 1A + 1B. Pair 2 = cards 2A + 2B. Each learner writes an 80–120-word executive summary of their own proposal, then plays the decision-maker for their partner, using the box at the bottom of their own card. Don't let partners see each other's cards. B1 support: the sentence starters are in the workbook frame.

Brief 1A · Northwind Logistics

You: Operations Coordinator, Northern Region

The facts (January–March):

  • Late deliveries rose from 6% to 14% of all orders.
  • 70% of late orders were on Monday and Tuesday routes.
  • Routes are planned by hand (about 2 hours a day).
  • One of three vans was out of service for 5 weeks.
  • Late-delivery penalties to customers: about $3,000 a month.
  • The Southern Region uses route-planning software: 4% late deliveries.

Your proposal: (1) route-planning software, $9,600 a year, live by the end of May; (2) lease an extra van for Monday–Tuesday routes, $1,200 a month; (3) review driver schedules in Q3.

When you are the decision-maker (your partner's Brightline proposal): You are Head of Operations at Brightline Foods. You care most about complaints and the brand. Rule: any extra cost must be paid back by savings within 12 months. Ask: 1 How big was the trial, and how long? 2 What happens if the new supplier delivers late?

Brief 1B · Brightline Foods

You: Online Orders Team Lead

The facts (last two quarters):

  • Survey of 400 online customers.
  • Complaints doubled: 120 → 240 per quarter.
  • 55% about damaged packaging; 25% about late delivery.
  • Refunds for damaged orders: about $9,000 a quarter.
  • 8-week trial at the Eastfield warehouse (4,000 boxes): a new supplier's boxes cut damage by half.
  • New boxes cost $0.12 more; about 30,000 boxes a quarter = about $3,600 a quarter.

Your proposal: (1) switch all three warehouses to the new boxes by September; (2) keep the current supplier as a backup for 3 months; (3) review complaint figures in Q4.

When you are the decision-maker (your partner's Northwind proposal): You are Regional Director at Northwind Logistics. You can approve up to $15,000 this year without going to the board. You care most about on-time delivery. Ask: 1 Why lease a van instead of buying one? 2 How quickly will we see results?

Brief 2A · Kestrel Design

You: Studio Manager (25 staff)

The facts (last quarter):

  • 11 of 40 projects were late.
  • Staff survey: 68% said "I didn't know the deadline had changed."
  • Deadlines are tracked in 4 different spreadsheets.
  • Each late project costs about $1,500 in overtime.
  • A shared project calendar tool costs $8 per user per month (25 users = $2,400 a year).
  • The tool can replace all four spreadsheets.

Your proposal: (1) introduce the shared calendar tool from July; (2) a half-day training session in June; (3) check late projects again at the end of Q3.

When you are the decision-maker (your partner's Clearwater proposal): You are Customer Service Director at Clearwater Health Supplies. Your budget for one-off projects this year is $10,000. New hires need a separate decision by HR. Ask: 1 Who will build the page, and when? 2 Why one new agent, not two?

Brief 2B · Clearwater Health Supplies

You: Customer Service Team Lead

The facts (Q2):

  • Average phone wait rose from 4 to 11 minutes.
  • 60% of calls ask one question: "Where is my order?"
  • Two agents left in the spring.
  • IT can build an order-tracking page + FAQ: $6,000 (one-off), 6 weeks.
  • One new agent costs about $45,000 a year.

Your proposal: (1) build the tracking page and FAQ by the end of August; (2) hire one agent by the end of Q3; (3) review wait times in October. Expected result: waits below 5 minutes within two months.

When you are the decision-maker (your partner's Kestrel proposal): You are Studio Director at Kestrel Design. Staff already complain about "too many tools." You'll approve a new tool only if it replaces something. Ask: 1 How and when will staff learn it? 2 What will we stop using?

Interviewer card · rules

Before you start

Be friendly and professional. Ask only the questions on your card. Never ask about salary, visa or immigration status, nationality, age, family, health, religion, why someone left a job, or gaps in employment. If the candidate mentions one of these, respond briefly and kindly and move on. Say "Last question" at 5 minutes.

Interviewer card A

Rounds 1 and 3

  1. Open: "Thank you for coming in. Please have a seat."
  2. "Tell me about yourself."
  3. STAR (choose one): "Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline." · "Tell me about a time you solved a problem for a customer."
  4. One more (choose one): "What's your greatest strength? Can you give an example?" · "Why do you want to work for us?"
  5. Optional: "Can you tell me about your qualifications?"
  6. "Do you have any questions for us?" → "Thank you. We'll be in touch."
Interviewer card B

Round 2

  1. Open: "Thanks for coming in today. Please take a seat."
  2. "So, tell me about yourself."
  3. STAR (choose one): "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague." · "Tell me about a time you showed leadership."
  4. One more (choose one): "Why are you interested in this role?" · "What's one weakness you're working on?"
  5. Optional: "What would you bring to this team that others might not?"
  6. "Is there anything you'd like to ask us?" → "Thank you for your time."

D · Integrated Scenario Cards — Kestrel Design & Harborview Retail (4.3)

Trios: A (Kestrel), B (Harborview), observer. One stage per round (meeting → pitch → negotiation); rotate so everyone plays A once. Harborview's style is described as its company culture: it isn't linked to any country.

Card A · Kestrel Design

You: the account lead (your name, or Sam Ortiz)

The project: Kestrel Design will redesign Harborview Retail's store signs and online catalog. You're the main contact. Harborview's team prefers a formal style, builds the relationship first and decides by consensus.

Round 1 · Meeting: welcome and thank everyone · brief small talk · a light agenda (introductions · shared goals · next steps) · invite Ms. Quinn to begin.

Round 2 · Pitch: Feature: a phased approach (the 5 largest stores first, then the other 20) with one dedicated contact. → Benefit: … → Call to action: "If that approach feels right, we can share a draft plan by Friday for your team to review together."

Round 3 · Negotiation: The price is $80,000. Your limit: up to 10% off, only for a two-year agreement (including catalog updates). Never 15%. Don't push for a decision today; offer to confirm in writing.

Useful: Thank you for making the time. · Which means you… · I understand budget is important. · If your team can commit to…, we could… · Please take the time you need.

Card B · Harborview Retail

You: Ms. Laura Quinn (or your own name), Head of Store Operations

Your style: formal (titles until you know people well) · you like some small talk first · you can't agree anything today: your team decides together.

Round 1 · Meeting: respond warmly · answer the small talk (a busy start to the year, a new store opening) · agree to the agenda.

Round 2 · Pitch: listen · ask one question: "How soon would we see results in our stores?" · don't commit.

Round 3 · Negotiation: start with "Before we go further, we would need the price reduced by 15%." If Kestrel offers a fair condition: "That could work. Let us discuss it internally." If Kestrel pushes for a decision: "That may be somewhat difficult on our side today."

Useful: Thank you, that's kind. · We'd be glad to share our thinking. · We would need… · Let us discuss it internally.

Observer card

Listen for

Round 1: title + surname · thanks · small talk before business · a clear, light agenda · an invitation to speak

Round 2: feature → benefit ("which means you…") · a call to action that gives the team time

Round 3: acknowledge → conditional trade (if… could) · 10% or less · notices the soft signal · offers to confirm in writing

Rubric: Task & Purpose · Register & Tone · Language Range & Accuracy · Organization & Delivery. Give one quoted strength in the last 30 seconds.

Stretch complications (C1)

Add one per round

Round 1: a second Harborview manager (the observer) joins late. Welcome them and recap in one sentence.

Round 2: the second manager worries about disruption in stores during the holiday season. Answer the objection without dropping the call to action.

Round 3: Harborview also wants the first five stores finished two weeks earlier. Trade again, then give a two-line spoken recap of what has (and hasn't) been agreed.

H · Listening Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

Read at a natural speed. Read each script twice unless the plan says otherwise: once for the main idea, once to check. Change your voice for each speaker, or give one speaker's lines to a confident learner. The 🔊 buttons in the online lessons give learners extra listening at home.

4.1-A · The findings briefing (Workbook 4.1, Ex 1)

First listening: report or proposal? What's the main problem? Second listening: the six numbers. Say fifty-five and twenty-five clearly, with stress on the first part.

Grace: Thanks for making the time, Tomás. So, what did the survey tell us about online orders? · Tomás: Quite a lot. We surveyed four hundred customers who ordered online last quarter. The headline is that complaints have doubled. We had a hundred and twenty complaints in the first quarter and two hundred and forty in the second. · Grace: Doubled. That's serious. What are people complaining about? · Tomás: Mainly packaging. Fifty-five percent of complaints are about damaged boxes: crushed boxes, broken jars, that kind of thing. Another twenty-five percent are about late delivery, and the rest are a mix of smaller issues. · Grace: So packaging is the big one. Do we know why? · Tomás: We think so. The current boxes are too thin for glass jars. In May we ran a trial at the Eastfield warehouse with a new supplier's boxes. Over eight weeks, damage went down by half. · Grace: By half? That's impressive. What's the catch? · Tomás: The cost. The new boxes are twelve cents more per box. We send about thirty thousand boxes a quarter, so that's about three thousand six hundred dollars extra a quarter. · Grace: And what do damaged orders cost us now? · Tomás: Refunds are about nine thousand dollars a quarter. · Grace: Then the numbers look good. Can you put it in a proposal for Thursday? Keep it short: I'll only read the first page before the leadership meeting. · Tomás: Will do.

Answers: 1 400 · 2 240 · 3 55 · 4 25 · 5 half (50%) · 6 12 cents. It's a report of findings; Grace then asks for a proposal. Note Grace's last line: the reason the executive summary matters.

4.1-B · A voicemail from a busy director (C12 lead-in · slide 2)

Hi, this is Jordan Blake from Finance, calling for Ana in Operations. It's Tuesday, about four o'clock. Thanks for sending the route-planning report. I have to be honest: it's twelve pages, and I've got about ten minutes before Thursday's budget meeting. So could you put a short executive summary at the front? I'll only read the summary. I need three things: what the problem is, with the key numbers; what exactly you want us to approve; and what it costs and by when. If it's clear, I'll take it to the board. You can reach me on extension four-four-one-seven. Thanks, Ana.

Ask: What does Jordan want? (A short executive summary at the front.) What will he read? (Only the summary.) What three things does he need? (The problem with numbers · what to approve · the cost and the deadline.)

4.2-A · Two answers, one question (Workbook 4.2, Ex 1)

Read Rafael quickly, with hesitations; read Joy calmly, with a short pause between the four parts. Second listening: Joy only.

Interviewer: Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline. · Rafael: Oh, deadlines, yes, we have them all the time. My company is very big, it's in logistics, we have many clients, and, um, one time there was a big order, and we all worked very hard, everybody, the whole team, and we did many things, we worked late, and in the end it was OK, I think. Yes. We are very good with deadlines. · Interviewer: Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline. · Joy: Sure. Last spring, one of our biggest clients brought their product launch forward by two weeks. As the project coordinator, I had to get forty store displays designed, printed and delivered in half the usual time. So I split the job into daily targets, I asked the printer to run two shifts in exchange for a larger order, and I checked progress with the team every morning for ten minutes. As a result, all forty displays arrived a day early, the launch went ahead on time, and the client has since signed a two-year contract with us.

S "Last spring…" · T "As the project coordinator, I had to…" · A "So I split the job…" · R "As a result…". Rafael: no clear situation or task, "we" not "I," no specific actions, no result or number.

4.2-B · "Tell me about yourself": Nadia (C13 "your path" stage · slide 11)

Nadia is the person in the workbook's model CV. Listen for how she mentions her degree and her career break: briefly, factually, then back to what she offers.

Interviewer: So, Nadia, tell me about yourself. · Nadia: Sure. I'm an operations assistant at Brightline Foods, where I've worked since 2023. I'm an industrial engineer by training. I studied and worked in my home country, and my degree has been assessed as equivalent to a U.S. bachelor's degree. There, I spent five years as a warehouse supervisor, managing a team of twelve across two shifts. From 2021 to 2023, I took a career break for family reasons, and during that time I completed a certificate in supply chain management. At Brightline, I've reduced picking errors by eighteen percent. Now I'm looking to move back into a coordinator role, where I can use my planning and team experience more fully, and that's why this position caught my eye.

Ask: Does she apologize for the gap? (No.) How long does she spend on it? (One sentence.) What does she say about her degree? (A fact: it has been assessed.) How does she end? (With what she wants to do in this job.)

4.3-A · First call: misstep and repair (Workbook 4.3, Ex 1 · slide 14)

Harborview's team prefers a formal style, builds the relationship first and decides by consensus. Read version 1, pause for learners to write the problems, then read version 2.

Version 1. Sam: Hi Laura! Great, everyone's here, so let's skip the chit-chat and dive straight into the numbers. Bottom line, we need a decision today. · Ms. Quinn: …I see. That may be somewhat difficult on our side today. · Sam: Oh, it's just a quick yes or no, really. Shall we say yes? · Version 2. Sam: Good morning, Ms. Quinn, and thank you for making the time. It's a pleasure to finally meet your team. Before we turn to the details, I'd love to hear a little about how your side is approaching the year. · Ms. Quinn: Thank you, that's kind. We'd be glad to share our thinking. It's been a busy start to the year for us. · Sam: I can imagine. And please, there's no need to decide anything today. After the call, we'll send a written summary so your team has time to consider it together. What timeline would suit you? · Ms. Quinn: Thank you. Two weeks would be ideal.

Problems: first name without an invitation · no rapport · pressure for a decision today · the soft "no" ignored (and pushed). Fixes: title + surname and thanks · a relationship-first question · no pressure + a written summary + "What timeline would suit you?"

4.3-B · A model capstone pitch (C14 capstone planning · a short model, about 3 minutes; learners' pitches are 5)

Learners tick the parts on the Capstone Pitch Brief checklist as they hear them: opening + hook · problem · solution · benefits · evidence · close + call to action. Ask: "What would you add to make it five minutes?" (more evidence, a figure described, an outline in the opening).

Good afternoon, everyone. Six out of every ten calls to our customer service team ask exactly the same question: "Where is my order?" Today I'd like to propose a simple way to answer that question before customers ever pick up the phone. First, the problem. In the second quarter, the average wait on our phone line rose from four minutes to eleven. Two agents left in the spring, and call volume went up. Customers are waiting longer, and our agents spend a large part of their day reading tracking numbers aloud. My proposal is a self-service order-tracking page, with a short FAQ, linked from every confirmation email. Our IT team has built similar pages before, and they estimate six weeks and six thousand dollars. So what does this mean for you? Three things. First, shorter waits: if even half of the tracking calls move online, we expect average waits to fall below five minutes within two months. Second, happier customers: they get an answer at any time of day, without calling. And third, our agents are free for the complex calls, the ones where a person really makes a difference. What about the cost and the risks? The cost is six thousand dollars, once. The main risk is that some customers won't use the page. To manage that, our agents will mention it at the end of every call, and we'll check the numbers every week for the first month. So, to sum up: one question takes up much of our time; a simple page can answer it; and it's a one-off cost. What I'm asking you to approve today is six thousand dollars for the tracking page, so that IT can start on Monday. Thank you. I'm happy to take any questions.

Signposts to notice: First, the problem. · My proposal is… · So what does this mean for you? Three things. · What about the cost and the risks? · So, to sum up: … · What I'm asking you to approve today is… Ask learners: "Which sentence is the call to action? What number does the hook use?"
