A1 · Flashcards — Signposting: Function ↔ Phrase (3.1)

Groups of 3. Put the six blue function cards face up. Deal the twelve phrase cards. Players place each phrase under its function and say it aloud with the stress on the key word. C · Presentation Carousel — Brief Cards & Q&A Cards (3.1)

Groups of 3: presenter · questioner · observer. The presenter gives a 2-minute mini-presentation: the opening (five jobs), two signposted points, and a close. The questioner draws one Q&A card and asks it. The observer uses checklist G1. Rotate. Learners may use a brief card, their role profile or a real, non-confidential topic.

Brief · 1 · team update

Customer calls answered within one minute

January 60% · March 72% · May 88%. What changed: a new call schedule (more staff at lunchtime). Next step: add one person on Monday mornings.

Brief · 2 · proposal

Move the weekly team meeting

Now: Monday 9:00. Attendance 70%; one person in four arrives late. Proposal: Tuesday 10:00, 30 minutes, with an agenda sent the day before.

Brief · 3 · recommendation

Print less, spend less

Paper use: 40 boxes a year. Double-sided printing as the default would cut this by about 20%. Recycled paper costs 4% more per box. Overall saving: about $300 a year.

Brief · 4 · project update

New warehouse shelves

Time to pick one order: 6 minutes → 4 minutes. Problem: the project is two weeks behind schedule because a delivery of shelves was late. New finish date: the end of next month.

Brief · 5 · results

Customer survey

Satisfaction: 7.1 out of 10 last year → 8.3 this year. Top compliment: friendly staff. Top complaint: no delivery tracking. Recommendation: add tracking by text message.

Brief · 6 · proposal

Shared cars for site visits

Taxi costs for site visits: about $2,400 a month. Two shared company cars would cost about $1,500 a month in total. Staff survey: 8 of 10 would use them.

Q&A card · 1

"What was the figure last year?"

Presenter: you don't know it. Follow up.

Q&A card · 2

"How much will this cost, exactly?"

Presenter: clarify first, then answer or follow up.

Q&A card · 3

"Sorry, can you go back to that number? What did it mean?"

Presenter: refer back and interpret.

Q&A card · 4

"What about the problems in the other department?"

Presenter: off-topic. Park it politely.

Q&A card · 5

"Why should we do this now and not next year?"

Presenter: answer briefly, then stop.

Q&A card · 6

"Who will be responsible for this?"

Presenter: answer, or say you'll check.

Q&A card · 7

"Could you give us an example?"

Presenter: "For instance…"

Q&A card · 8

"Have you asked the team what they think?"

Presenter: be honest; offer a next step.

D · The Deal Room — Negotiation Cards (3.2)

Pairs. Give A and B of the same scenario to two different learners. Never show your card. Each card has four issues with points: some matter a lot to you and some matter little, and your partner's priorities are different. Your aim is a deal worth as many points as possible for you that your partner can also accept. Any deal under 40 points is worse than your alternative: walk away. Prep 5 min · negotiate 13 min · summarize and record 2 min · add up your points 2 min.

Deal Room · Scenario 1 · A

You are: purchasing manager, Brightline Foods

You need a company to deliver your fresh ready meals to 40 grocery stores, six days a week. You're meeting the sales manager of Northwind Logistics.

IssueOptions and your points
Price per delivery$180 = 0 · $170 = 20 · $160 = 40 · $150 = 60
Contract length1 year = 10 · 2 years = 5 · 3 years = 0
Payment terms14 days = 0 · 30 days = 10 · 60 days = 20
Delivery windowbefore 7 a.m. = 30 · 7–10 a.m. = 15 · any time before noon = 0
Private: Your stores open at 8 a.m., and fresh meals must be on the shelves before the doors open, so an early delivery really matters. Price matters most: your budget is tight. Your company is growing fast, so a long contract is fine for you. Slower payment would help, but it isn't essential.

Your alternative: another company offered a deal worth about 40 points to you. Below 40, walk away.

Useful: What's most important to you here? · If you could…, we could… · I'm afraid that doesn't quite work for us, because… · So, to confirm…  ·  Stretch: you can't agree to anything above $165 without your director: call a short "phone break."

E · Pitch & Push-back — A/B Cards (3.3)

Pairs. A pitches for 60–90 seconds: problem → solution → benefit → evidence → call to action. B listens, then raises the stated objection, then the second one. B's real concern is hidden: B reveals it only if A asks a good clarifying question. B agrees to a next step only when the real concern is addressed. Then swap roles with a new pair of cards.

Pitch · 1 · A (pitcher)

A room-booking calendar — to the office manager, Northwind Logistics

You are: an office assistant. Staff often book the same meeting room twice, using the paper sheets on the doors.

Features: shows free rooms on every phone · sends automatic reminders · frees a room if nobody arrives within 10 minutes

Evidence: a one-month test on the second floor: double bookings fell from 12 a month to 1.

Your ask: a one-month trial for the whole building.

Pitch · 1 · B (buyer)

Office manager, Northwind Logistics

Say first: "We already have a system: the paper sheet on each door."

Say second: "I'm not sure people will use it."

Hidden concern: your 80 staff are very busy, and you have no time to organize training sessions for a new tool.

You'll agree if: the pitcher offers a very short demo (for example, 15 minutes at the staff meeting) or a one-page guide, so you don't have to organize training.

Pitch · 2 · A (pitcher)

Ready-to-cook lunch boxes — to the buyer for a café chain

You are: a sales representative for Brightline Foods. The café chain (12 cafés) runs out of lunch options by early afternoon.

Features: ready in three minutes in an oven or microwave · stay fresh for five days · six recipes that change every month

Evidence: cafés in a pilot sold about 40 extra lunches a week on average.

Your ask: a first order for two cafés next month.

Pitch · 2 · B (buyer)

Buyer, a chain of 12 cafés

Say first: "It's too expensive for us."

Say second: "Our customers prefer fresh sandwiches."

Hidden concern: waste. If the boxes don't sell, you throw them away and lose money. The price per box is actually OK.

You'll agree if: the pitcher offers sale-or-return for the first month, or a much smaller first order.

G · Observer Checklists (the four rubric criteria)

One per group. The observer ticks what they see and hears, then gives one strength and one "try next time." Observers don't give scores.

Language Range & Accuracy ☐ 3+ different signposts ☐ trend words used correctly

Organization & Delivery ☐ opening with the five jobs ☐ a signpost at every turn ☐ eye contact, pauses, not reading ☐ a short, calm answer to the question

Task & Purpose ☐ a clear deal on all four issues (or a clear decision to walk away)

Register & Tone ☐ warm opening ☐ no flat "no" ☐ goodwill at the end

H · Listening Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

3.1-A · Priya's opening (C9 model · learners tick the five jobs on slide 5 and note the hook)

Good morning, everyone, and thank you all for coming. For those who don't know me, I'm Priya Nair from the events team here at Harbor Point. Let me start with a number: on Mondays and Tuesdays, only thirty percent of our rooms are used. (pause) Thirty percent. Today I'm going to talk about how we can fill those empty days. By the end, you'll see one simple package we could launch next month. I've divided my talk into three parts: first, why Mondays and Tuesdays are quiet; second, the package; and finally, what it could earn us. This should take about ten minutes, and please feel free to ask questions as we go.

Answers: greeting ✔ · introduce ✔ · hook = a striking fact (30%), repeated for emphasis · topic + purpose + benefit ✔ ("By the end, you'll see…") · outline = three parts ✔ · housekeeping = questions as we go ✔.

3.1-B · The on-time deliveries chart (C9 · learners look at the chart in Workbook 3.1, Ex 4)

Look at the chart in your workbook. I'm going to describe it, but I'll make two mistakes with the figures. When you hear a mistake, raise your hand. · This graph shows our on-time deliveries for last year. If you look at the first quarter, you'll see we started at seventy-nine percent. In the second quarter, the figure rose steadily to eighty-four percent. Then, in Q3, when the new route plan started, it rose sharply and peaked at ninety-five percent. In Q4, it leveled off at around ninety-two percent. What this tells us is that the new route plan works, so we should use it in every region.

Answers: Q1 was 81%, not 79% · Q3 peaked at 93%, not 95%. Then learners complete the gap-fill (Ex 4).

I'll be direct: I love the machine, but six thousand dollars is a lot for a small café. · Leo: I understand. Can I ask: is it the total price that's the problem, or paying it all at once? · Dana: Honestly, paying it all at once. We've just renovated, so cash is tight until the summer. · Leo: That's helpful to know. So if we could spread the cost, the price itself is OK? · Dana: More or less, yes. · Leo: Then how about this: if you sign a two-year coffee-bean contract with us, we can spread the machine over twenty-four monthly payments of two hundred and fifty dollars, with no interest. · Dana: That sounds good. Could you include the installation too? · Leo: Installation is normally three hundred dollars. I can include it, but I'll need something in return. If you put our logo on your menu board, I'll waive the installation fee. That's really the best I can do. · Dana: The logo is fine. OK, we have a deal. · Leo: Great. So, to confirm: the machine on twenty-four monthly payments of two hundred and fifty dollars, a two-year bean contract, free installation, and our logo on your menu board. Have I got that right? · Dana: Perfect. · Leo: I'll email you the details this afternoon. Thanks, Dana. It's a pleasure.

Answers: the real problem = cash flow (paying all at once), not the price · interest question: "Is it the total price… or paying it all at once?" · trades: bean contract → monthly payments; logo → free installation · labeled concession: "That's really the best I can do." · close: spoken summary + email.

3.3-A · The newsletter pitch (C11 model · Mei Lin pitches to her studio manager)

Mei Lin: You know how clients often forget about us between projects? I'd like to start a short monthly email newsletter with our latest work and one design tip. That means clients see our work regularly and think of us first when they need a designer. When I sent a trial email to twenty past clients last month, three of them asked for a quote. Could I send the first newsletter in May, and we review the results after three months? · Manager: I like it, but honestly, I don't think we have time for this. · Mei Lin: That's a fair concern. Everyone's very busy. Can I ask: is it the writing that worries you, or the design and sending? · Manager: The writing. Nobody here likes writing. · Mei Lin: I understand. I'd write it myself. It takes about two hours a month, and I'd use a simple template, so nobody else needs to do anything. Does that address your concern? · Manager: If it's two hours of your time, then yes. Let's try it.

Answers: stages = hook/problem ("You know how clients forget…") → solution (newsletter) → benefit (they think of us first) → evidence (3 of 20 asked for a quote) → call to action (first issue in May, review after three months) · stated objection: time · real concern: the writing · LAARC: listens → "That's a fair concern" → "is it the writing… or the design and sending?" → "I'd write it myself…" → "Does that address your concern?"
