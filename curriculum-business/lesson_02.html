📧 Lesson 1.2: Professional Emails & Messaging

Email is still the backbone of professional life — and a well-written one gets a fast, helpful reply while a messy one gets ignored. In this lesson you'll learn a reliable structure for any business email, the right greetings and sign-offs for each register, ready-to-use phrase banks for every common purpose, and the etiquette of email and workplace chat. Everything you learned about register and tone in Lesson 1.1 now goes to work on the page.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure a clear business email from subject line to sign-off, following the "one email, one purpose" principle
  • Choose the right greeting, sign-off, and register — and match them correctly
  • Use functional phrases for requests, replies, follow-ups, bad news, and attachments
  • Apply etiquette for subject lines, CC/BCC, reply-all, and instant messaging

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Task: Write two emails — a formal external request and a neutral internal update — applying the structure and phrase banks, then self-check them against a checklist.

The Anatomy of a Business Email

A good business email is predictable — and that's a feature, not a flaw. The reader knows exactly where to look for the point, the ask, and the deadline. Every professional email follows the same seven building blocks, top to bottom.

PartJob it doesExample
1. Subject lineTells the reader what it's about and whether to act — clear and specificQ3 report — feedback needed by Fri
2. GreetingOpens with the right register for the readerDear Ms Alvarez, / Hi Sara,
3. Opening lineStates your reason or references a previous contactI'm writing to confirm the delivery date.
4. BodyOne main point, with a clear ask — short paragraphs or bulletsCould you approve the budget by Thursday?
5. Closing lineSignals what happens next and invites a replyPlease let me know if you have any questions.
6. Sign-offCloses with a register that matches the greetingKind regards, / Yours sincerely,
7. Name / signatureSays who you are and how to reach youAna Ruiz · Marketing, Meridian

✅ The golden rule: one email, one purpose

Each email should do one job — a single request, a single update, a single question. If you have three unrelated asks, the reader will act on one and forget the rest. Send separate emails, or use a short numbered list so nothing gets lost. A focused email is easier to write, easier to answer, and easier to find later.

Here's that flow as a diagram — write in this order and you'll rarely leave anything out:

flowchart TD
    A["Subject line (clear + specific)"] --> B["Greeting (right register)"]
    B --> C["Opening line (reason / reference)"]
    C --> D["Body (one point + clear ask)"]
    D --> E["Closing line (next step)"]
    E --> F["Sign-off (matches greeting)"]
    F --> G["Name / signature"]

Greetings & Sign-offs by Register

Your greeting and sign-off are the frame around the message — they set the register before the reader has read a word of the body. The key skill is to match them: a formal greeting needs a formal sign-off, a friendly one a friendly close. Mixing them (a "Dear Sir" opening with a "Cheers" close) looks careless.

RegisterGreetingSign-offWhen to use
FormalDear Mr / Ms Lastname,
Dear Sir or Madam,		Yours sincerely,
Yours faithfully,		First contact, senior or external people, official or sensitive messages
NeutralDear Sara,
Hi Sara,		Kind regards,
Best regards,		Most everyday work; colleagues and contacts you know a little
Friendly-professionalHi Sara,
Hello Sara,		Best,
Thanks,		Close colleagues; a warm, established relationship

💡 The classic formal rule: sincerely vs. faithfully

In formal British-style letters and emails there's a simple pairing:

Named person → "Dear Ms Alvarez," … "Yours sincerely,"
Unknown person → "Dear Sir or Madam," … "Yours faithfully,"

An easy memory hook: never two "s" words together — Sir goes with faithfully; a named person goes with sincerely. (American style often just uses "Sincerely," for both.)

⚠️ Watch out: "Dear" is more formal than many learners expect — "Dear Sara," is perfectly neutral and warm in email, not stiff. And avoid opening a work email with "Hey" or no greeting at all with someone you don't know well: it reads as too casual and can undermine your credibility on a first contact.

Functional Phrase Banks

This is the heart of the lesson — a toolkit of ready-made phrases grouped by what you're trying to do. Learn a couple from each group and you can build almost any professional email quickly and confidently. Bold key words are the parts you keep; swap the rest for your own details.

Opening the email

I'm writing to confirm / ask about / let you know… Thank you for your email of 12 March. Thank you for getting back to me. Further to our conversation, … I hope you're well. It was great to meet you at the conference.

Making requests

Could you please send me…? I would be grateful if you could… Would it be possible to…? When you have a moment, could you…? I'd appreciate it if you could confirm by Friday.

Referring to attachments

Please find attached the report. I've attached the figures for your review. The document is attached below. I'm attaching a copy of the invoice.

Following up / chasing politely

I'm just following up on my email below. I wanted to check in regarding the proposal. As a gentle reminder, the deadline is Friday. I appreciate you're busy — any update when you get a chance?

Giving bad news / apologising

Unfortunately, we won't be able to meet the deadline. I'm afraid there's been a delay. Please accept my apologies for the confusion. I'm sorry for the late reply. Regrettably, the order is out of stock.

Closing the email

Please let me know if you have any questions. I look forward to hearing from you. Thanks in advance for your help. Do let me know if you need anything else. Looking forward to your reply.

💡 Mix and match by register

The same function has a formal and a friendly version. Need something? "I would be grateful if you could…" (formal) or "Could you…?" (neutral) or "Could you pop that over?" (friendly). Keep the whole email on one register level — don't open formally and then close with "Cheers!"

Subject Lines & the Clear Ask

Two things decide whether your email gets a fast, useful reply: the subject line (does the reader open it and know what to do?) and the clarity of your ask (do they know exactly what you want, and by when?).

⚠️ The classic email mistake: a vague or blank subject line

A subject like "Report", "Question", "Hi", or nothing at all tells the reader nothing — it's easy to skip and impossible to find again later. Make it specific and useful, and signal any action or deadline:

❌ Weak✅ Strong
ReportQ3 report — feedback needed by Fri
MeetingReschedule Tue budget meeting to Thu 2pm?
QuestionQuick question re: invoice #4821
(blank)Action needed: approve travel by 5pm today

A good tag like [Action needed], [FYI], or [Reply by Fri] at the front tells a busy reader instantly how to treat the email.

State the ask — and the deadline

Busy readers skim. Put your one main request near the top, say what you need and by when, and use short paragraphs or bullets. Compare:

❌ Too long, no clear ask:

Hi Tom,
I hope you had a good weekend. I've been thinking about the project and there are lots of things on my mind, the timeline, the budget, whether we have the right people, and I saw the client sent some notes which I haven't fully read yet but they seemed to have some concerns, anyway I just wanted to touch base and see where things are and maybe we can figure out next steps at some point soon…
Thanks

✅ Crisp, with a clear ask:

Hi Tom,

Could you send me the updated project timeline by Wednesday? The client raised two concerns in their notes and I'd like to reflect them before our Friday review.

Happy to jump on a quick call if that's easier.

Thanks,
Ana

🧠 Mindset: clarity is a kindness

A clear email isn't about sounding clever — it's about respecting the reader's time. When you make the ask obvious and the deadline explicit, you're doing the thinking for them, and they'll answer faster and like working with you more. Nobody is born writing crisp emails; it's a habit built one message at a time. Every email you tidy up today makes the next one quicker to write.

Model Emails

Here are two complete emails you can adapt. The [labels in brackets] point out each structural part — they aren't part of the email.

Model 1 — Formal request (external)

[Subject] Request for updated pricing — order #7745

[Greeting — formal, named] Dear Ms Alvarez,

[Opening — reference] Thank you for your email of 3 September regarding our recent order.

[Body — one clear ask + deadline] Before we confirm, I would be grateful if you could send us the updated pricing for order #7745, including any bulk discount. Would it be possible to receive this by Friday 12 September, so we can finalise the purchase order next week?

[Attachment reference] Please find attached our original specification for your reference.

[Closing] Please let me know if you need any further details. I look forward to hearing from you.

[Sign-off — matches formal greeting] Yours sincerely,
David Chen
Procurement, Meridian Ltd

Model 2 — Neutral internal update

[Subject] Website launch update — on track for 20 Sept

[Greeting — neutral] Hi team,

[Opening — reason] Quick update on where we are with the website launch.

[Body — one purpose, bulleted for clarity] We're on track for Friday 20 September. Two things to flag:

• Design is signed off. ✅
• We still need final product photos from Marketing — could you send these by Tuesday?

[Closing] I'll share the test link on Wednesday. Let me know if you have any questions.

[Sign-off — matches neutral greeting] Kind regards,
Ana Ruiz

💡 Notice what both models do

The subject says exactly what it's about. The opening gives a reason in one line. The ask is bolded and dated. The register is consistent from greeting to sign-off. And each email has one purpose — request pricing; give a launch update. Copy that shape and you're most of the way to a professional email.

Chat, CC & Reply-All Etiquette

Instant messaging (Slack / Teams)

Workplace chat is more informal than email, but it's still professional — and still permanent. A few norms keep you on the right side of it:

  • One message, not ten — write your whole thought in a single message; don't send "Hi" … "you there?" … "so" … one line at a time (each pings the reader)
  • Chat vs. email — chat for quick, informal, "right now" things; email for anything detailed, formal, external, or that needs a record
  • Respect async norms — people aren't always at their desk; don't expect an instant reply, and use threads to keep topics tidy
  • Emojis in moderation — a 👍 or 🙂 is friendly and normal; a wall of emojis or reactions on serious topics isn't
  • Get to the point — a quick "Hi Sam, could you approve the £200 spend on Canva? Link here 👍" beats a slow warm-up

⚠️ Nothing is truly private

Don't put anything in a chat or email you wouldn't want forwarded, screenshotted, or read aloud in a meeting. Messages get shared, threads get added to, and "delete" rarely means gone. Vent offline; keep the written record professional.

CC, BCC & Reply-All — the quick rules

Field / actionUse it when…
ToThe people you need action or a reply from
CC (carbon copy)People who should be kept informed but don't need to act — keep it minimal
BCC (blind copy)Emailing a large group who shouldn't see each other's addresses, or quietly removing someone from a long thread
ReplyYour answer is only for the sender (the default and usually right choice)
Reply AllEveryone genuinely needs your reply — otherwise you're spamming inboxes
💡 Reply-All test: before you hit Reply All, ask "does every person on this thread need to read this?" If the answer is "just the sender," use Reply. The classic mistake is a whole company getting your "Thanks!" — twenty times over.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the email in order

Objective: These lines are jumbled. Put them in the correct order for a business email.

  1. Kind regards, Priya
  2. Dear Mr Okafor,
  3. Subject: Contract renewal — decision needed by 30 Sept
  4. Could you confirm whether you'd like to renew by 30 September, please?
  5. Thank you for your email about your current contract.
  6. Please let me know if you have any questions.
✅ Answer

3 (subject) → 2 (greeting) → 5 (opening line) → 4 (body / clear ask) → 6 (closing line) → 1 (sign-off + name). Notice the formal greeting "Dear Mr Okafor," is correctly paired with "Kind regards," — a clean, consistent register.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Match the phrase to its function

Objective: Match each phrase to its purpose (request / follow-up / bad news / closing).

  • "I'm afraid there's been a delay with the shipment."
  • "I look forward to hearing from you."
  • "I would be grateful if you could review the draft."
  • "I'm just following up on my email from Monday."
✅ Answer

A = bad news / apologising · B = closing · C = making a request · D = following up / chasing politely. Learn one or two go-to phrases for each function and you can assemble an email in minutes.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Rewrite the blunt email

Objective: This internal email is too blunt and has no clear structure. Rewrite it professionally, with a proper subject, greeting, softened ask, and sign-off.

Subject: stuff

send me the sales numbers. need them today. also the slides are wrong.

✅ Answer (model — many good versions exist)

Subject: Sales figures + a quick slide fix — today if possible

Hi Marco,

Could you send me the latest sales figures when you get a moment? I'd need them by end of day for tomorrow's review, if that's doable.

One more thing — I think a couple of numbers on the slides need updating. Happy to point them out on a quick call.

Thanks so much,
Ana

What changed: a specific subject, a greeting, a softened request ("Could you… when you get a moment?"), a clear deadline, the criticism reframed diplomatically ("need updating" not "are wrong"), and a warm sign-off.

🎯 Your Task: Write Two Emails

  1. (12 min) Formal external request. Imagine you need a supplier to send you an updated quote by next Friday. Write a full email — subject line, formal greeting, opening, one clear ask with a deadline, an attachment reference, closing line, and a matching formal sign-off. Use the phrase banks.
  2. (10 min) Neutral internal update. Write a short update to your team about the status of a project you're working on. Include a specific subject line, one main purpose, and a clear action item for someone (with a deadline), using bullets if helpful.
  3. (5 min) Self-check. Read both aloud. Is each subject line specific? Does each email have one purpose and a clear, dated ask? Is the register consistent from greeting to sign-off?
  4. (Optional) Swap with a colleague and reply to each other's emails in role — did the ask come across clearly enough to answer without follow-up questions?

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Both emails have a specific, useful subject line (not "Update" or blank)
  • ☐ The formal email uses a formal greeting and a matching sign-off (e.g. Yours sincerely / Kind regards)
  • ☐ Each email has exactly one main purpose
  • ☐ The ask is clear and includes a deadline ("by Friday", "end of day")
  • ☐ I used at least one phrase from the opening, request, and closing banks
  • ☐ The register is consistent from greeting to sign-off

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Write a specific subject line and put your main ask near the top, with a deadline.
  • Keep one email to one purpose, and use short paragraphs or bullets.
  • Match your greeting and sign-off to the same register, and reuse the phrase banks.
  • Reread before sending — check the tone, the attachment is actually attached, and the recipients are right.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Vague or blank subject lines — the number-one reason emails go unanswered or unfound.
  • No clear ask — a rambling email where the reader can't tell what you actually want, or by when.
  • Too blunt — a bare "Send me the file" with no greeting or softener reads as an order.
  • Mismatched register — a "Dear Sir" opening with a "Cheers" close, or "Hey!" to a first-time external contact.
  • Reply-All spam and the forgotten attachment — small slips that look careless. Reread, then send.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Every business email follows a seven-part structure: subject → greeting → opening → body → closing → sign-off → name.
  • One email, one purpose — and put a clear ask, with a deadline, near the top.
  • Match greeting and sign-off to one register; remember named → sincerely, Sir/Madam → faithfully.
  • Phrase banks by function (opening, requests, attachments, follow-ups, bad news, closing) let you build any email fast.
  • Etiquette counts: a specific subject line, careful CC/Reply-All, and professional, single-message chat.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How long should a business email be?

As short as it can be while still being clear and courteous. Most work emails are three to six sentences: a one-line opening, the ask or update, and a closing. If it's getting long, it may be trying to do more than one job — split it, or use bullets.

Do I really need "I hope you're well" and other pleasantries?

A light opening line ("I hope you're well," "Thanks for getting back to me") is warm and normal, especially with external contacts. But it's optional — skip it for quick internal messages and get straight to the point. Never let pleasantries bury the ask.

When should I use chat instead of email?

Use chat for quick, informal, "right now" questions with people you work with closely. Use email when it's detailed, formal, external, or needs to be a record you (or others) can find later. When in doubt about tone or permanence, email.
