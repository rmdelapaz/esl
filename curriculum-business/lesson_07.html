📊 Lesson 3.1: Presentations — Structure & Delivery

Sooner or later, someone will ask you to "say a few words" — to a team, a client, or a room full of stakeholders. A good presentation isn't about being a natural performer; it's about structure and signposting. When your talk has a clear shape and you tell the audience where you are at every turn, they can follow you effortlessly — and you look calm and in control. This lesson gives you the framework and the exact language to deliver a professional presentation and handle the questions that follow.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure a presentation — a strong opening, a clearly divided body, and a memorable close
  • Use signposting language so the audience always knows where you are and can follow easily
  • Describe visuals and data clearly — charts, graphs, trends, and figures
  • Engage the audience, deliver with confidence, and handle the Q&A professionally

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Plan and deliver (or record) a short 3–4 minute business presentation using the structure and signposting language from this lesson, then complete a self-review.

The Shape of a Presentation

Every effective business presentation follows the same time-tested formula, often summed up as: "Tell them what you're going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them." Repetition and structure are not padding — they help a listening audience (who can't re-read a paragraph the way they would an email) hold onto your message.

PartWhat it doesWhat goes in it
Opening"Tell them what you'll tell them"Greeting · introduce yourself + your topic · state the purpose (what's in it for them) · outline the structure · housekeeping on questions
Body"Tell them"2–3 clear sections / main points, each with a transition signposting the move from one to the next
Close"Tell them what you told them"Summary of key points · conclusion / call to action · thanks · invite questions

Here is that shape as a flow:

flowchart TD
    A["Opening: greet, topic, purpose, outline"] --> B["Body: Point 1"]
    B --> C["Signpost / transition"]
    C --> D["Body: Point 2"]
    D --> E["Signpost / transition"]
    E --> F["Body: Point 3"]
    F --> G["Close: summary + call to action"]
    G --> H["Thanks + invite questions"]
    H --> I["Q&A"]

⚠️ The classic mistake: no clear structure

A talk with no visible structure or signposting is the single biggest reason audiences switch off. If listeners can't tell where you are — beginning, middle, or end — or how your points connect, they stop following and start checking their phones. Structure is a courtesy to your audience, and it makes you look organised and credible.

Opening Strong

Your first 30 seconds set the tone. A strong opening does five jobs quickly: greet, introduce yourself and your topic, state the purpose (and the benefit to them), outline the structure, and handle housekeeping (when to ask questions).

Good morning, thank you all for coming. For those who don't know me, I'm… Today I'm going to talk about… The purpose of my presentation is to… By the end, you'll know exactly how to… I've divided my talk into three parts. First… then… and finally… This should take about ten minutes. Please feel free to ask questions as we go. I'll take any questions at the end.

💡 Start with a hook

Before (or just after) the housekeeping, grab attention with a hook:

  • A question: "How much do you think we lose to late invoices each year?"
  • A striking fact: "Last quarter, one in three customers left within 90 days."
  • A short story: "Last month a client called me at 6 p.m., furious. Here's what that taught us…"

A hook makes the audience want the rest of the talk. Keep it to one or two sentences.

Here's a complete opening you can adapt — notice how it moves through all five jobs plus a hook:

Model opening — a proposal to speed up invoicing

"Good morning, everyone, and thank you all for coming. [greeting]
For those who don't know me, I'm Ana Ruiz from the finance team. [introduce]
Here's a number to start with: last year we waited an average of 47 days to get paid. [hook — striking fact]
Today I'm going to talk about how we can cut that in half. By the end, you'll see three concrete changes we can make this quarter. [topic + purpose + benefit]
I've divided my talk into three parts: first, where the delays come from; second, the fix; and finally, what it would cost. [outline]
This should take about ten minutes, and please feel free to ask questions as we go." [housekeeping]

Signposting: The Core Skill

Signposting is the language that tells the audience where you are and where you're going — like road signs on a journey. It is the single most important verbal skill in presenting, because it does the work that paragraph breaks and headings do in a document. Master these and even a simple talk becomes easy to follow.

Moving on to a new point

Let's move on to… This brings me to my next point… Now I'd like to turn to… So much for X — let's look at Y.

Sequencing (ordering your points)

Firstly… secondly… finally… Let's start with… The next thing to consider is… Last but not least…

Referring back & forward

As I mentioned earlier… As you'll remember… We'll come to that shortly. I'll say more about this in a moment.

Giving examples

For instance… For example… A good example of this is… To give you an idea…

Emphasising a key point

The key point here is… What I want to stress is… If you take one thing away, it's this: This is really important…

Summarising & concluding

So, to sum up… Let me recap the main points. In conclusion… To bring it all together…

See how a few signposts turn three bare points into a talk the audience can follow:

Signposted mini-outline

"Let's start with where the delays come from. [point 1] …
The key point here is that most delays happen before the invoice is even sent.
This brings me to my next point — the fix. [point 2] For instance, an automated reminder alone recovered two weeks last month.
Finally, let's look at cost. [point 3] As I mentioned earlier, the delay costs us far more than the tool would.
So, to sum up: fix the front end, automate reminders, and we halve our wait." [close]

Describing Visuals & Data

Slides, charts, and graphs support your message — they don't replace it. Your job is to guide the audience through a visual: introduce it, point them to what matters, describe the figures, and tell them what it means.

Introducing a slide or visual

As you can see from this chart… This graph shows… On this slide, you can see… Let's take a look at the figures.

Directing attention

If you look at the top line… I'd like to draw your attention to… Notice the sharp jump here. The column on the right shows…

Describing trends & figures

Sales rose steadily / rose sharply. Costs fell / dropped over the year. Revenue peaked in Q3. It stayed flat / levelled off. roughly / around 40% the majority of customers

Interpreting — what it means

What this tells us is… The takeaway here is… In other words… This means that…

💡 Guide, don't just label

Weak: "This is a chart of our sales." Strong: "This graph shows our monthly sales. If you look at the spring, you'll see revenue rose sharply, then peaked in June at roughly £2m. What this tells us is that our campaign is working — so we should run it again." Move from what the visual shows to what it means for the audience.

⚠️ Don't read your slides word-for-word

Slides are a support, not a script. Reading dense text off the screen is the fastest way to lose a room — the audience can read faster than you can speak, and you lose all eye contact. Put keywords and visuals on the slide; keep the sentences in your mouth. If a slide could be emailed instead of presented, it has too many words.

Delivery & Engaging the Audience

Structure gets your message across; delivery makes people care about it. You don't need to be a performer — you need to be clear, present, and audience-focused. A few reliable habits do most of the work.

✅ Do❌ Don't
Make eye contact — look at people, not the screenRead from a script or stare at your slides
Slow your pace and pause after key pointsRush nervously through everything at one speed
Use stress and voice to highlight what mattersSpeak in a flat monotone
Ask rhetorical questions to pull the audience inTalk at the room with no engagement
Use "you"-focused language — benefits to themMake it all about you and your department
Show enthusiasm for your own topicSound bored — if you don't care, they won't

💡 Handling nerves

Nerves are normal, even for experienced speakers — they mean you care. Channel them: breathe before you start, slow down (nervous speakers speed up), and plant your feet. Knowing your opening cold is the best cure — once the first 30 seconds go well, the nerves fade. And remember: pauses feel long to you but look confident to the audience.

🧠 Mindset: presenters are made, not born

Nobody is born a confident presenter. The people who look effortless on stage have simply structured their talk well, prepared their opening, and practised out loud — often more than you'd guess. Every talk you give makes the next one easier. Treat this not as a talent you either have or don't, but as a skill you're building rep by rep. The nervous first-timer and the polished pro are the same person a few presentations apart.

Handling Questions (Q&A)

The Q&A is where many presenters lose their composure — but with a handful of phrases you can stay calm and in control, even for a question you didn't expect (or can't answer).

Inviting questions

I'd be happy to take any questions now. Does anyone have any questions? What would you like me to expand on?

Buying time & clarifying

That's a great question. Just to make sure I understand your question… If I've understood you correctly, you're asking… Could you say a little more about what you mean?

When you can't answer

I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll follow up. That's outside my area, but I can find out. Let me check and get back to you by tomorrow.

Redirecting or parking

That's a bit outside today's scope — let's discuss it afterwards. Good point — can we take that offline? I'll come back to that at the end if we have time.

⚠️ Don't waffle

The biggest Q&A trap is rambling. Answer concisely, then stop — a short, confident answer beats a long, wandering one. If you don't know, say so and offer to follow up; that reads as honest and professional, not weak. Guessing or padding, on the other hand, damages your credibility. It's perfectly fine to say, "I'll come back to you on that."

Audience: What happens if the vendor raises their prices next year?

Presenter: That's a great question. Just to make sure I understand — you're asking about the risk if the annual fee goes up?

Audience: Exactly.

Presenter: We've locked the rate for two years in the contract, so we're covered until 2028. I don't have the renewal terms to hand, but I'll follow up with the detail by tomorrow.

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These signposting lines guide your audience smoothly through a presentation.

  • Good morning everyone, and thank you for coming.
  • Today I'll be covering three main points.
  • Let's move on to the next part of the presentation.
  • As you can see from this chart, sales rose sharply in the spring.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the opening in order

Objective: These lines from a presentation opening are jumbled. Put them in the natural order.

  1. "I've divided my talk into three parts: the problem, the solution, and the cost."
  2. "Good afternoon, everyone, and thanks for coming."
  3. "Please feel free to ask questions as we go."
  4. "Today I'm going to talk about how we can reduce customer wait times."
  5. "I'm Marco from the operations team."
✅ Answer

Natural order: 2 (greeting) → 5 (introduce yourself) → 4 (topic + purpose) → 1 (outline the structure) → 3 (housekeeping on questions). Greet, introduce, state the topic, outline, then handle questions — the five jobs of an opening.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the right signpost

Objective: Pick the best signposting phrase for each transition.

  1. You've finished point one and want to start point two. → (a) "In conclusion…" (b) "This brings me to my next point…" (c) "As I mentioned earlier…"
  2. You want to give a concrete illustration of your claim. → (a) "For instance…" (b) "Let's move on…" (c) "To sum up…"
  3. You're at the end and want to recap. → (a) "Firstly…" (b) "I'd like to draw your attention to…" (c) "So, to sum up…"
  4. You want to stress the one thing that matters most. → (a) "We'll come to that shortly." (b) "The key point here is…" (c) "This graph shows…"
✅ Answer

1 → (b) moving on · 2 → (a) giving an example · 3 → (c) summarising · 4 → (b) emphasising. Each function has its own family of phrases — matching the phrase to your intention is what makes a talk easy to follow.

🏋️ Exercise 3: From slide-reading to engaging speech

Objective: Rewrite each flat, slide-reading line into an engaging spoken line that directs attention and interprets the data.

  1. "This is a graph of sales."
  2. "Bar chart: Q1 100, Q2 150, Q3 250, Q4 240."
  3. "Table of costs."
✅ Answer (models — many good versions exist)

1. "This graph shows our monthly sales — and if you look at the spring, you'll see they really took off."
2. "As you can see, sales rose steadily through the year and peaked in Q3 at around 250 before levelling off. What this tells us is the summer push worked — so let's repeat it."
3. "I'd like to draw your attention to the bottom row: the majority of our costs sit in just one area. The takeaway here is that's where a small saving would have the biggest impact."
Notice the pattern: introduce the visual → direct attention → describe the trend → say what it means.

🎯 Your Task: Plan & Deliver a 3–4 Minute Presentation

  1. (10 min) Choose a real work topic (a proposal, a project update, a recommendation). Plan the structure: a strong opening (greet, introduce, topic + purpose, outline, housekeeping) and 2–3 body points.
  2. (10 min) Write your signposting into the plan — one transition phrase between each point, at least one "example" phrase, and one "key point" emphasis. Add a close (summary + call to action + thanks + invite questions).
  3. (5 min) If you have a chart or figure, script how you'll describe the visual: introduce it → direct attention → describe the trend → interpret it.
  4. (10 min) Deliver or record it — to a colleague, a partner, or your phone camera — aiming for 3–4 minutes. Then have a partner ask you two questions, or write two likely questions and answer them aloud using the Q&A phrases.
  5. (5 min) Self-review against the checklist below. Watch or replay if you recorded it.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My opening does all five jobs (greet, introduce, topic/purpose, outline, housekeeping)
  • ☐ I signposted every transition — the audience could always tell where I was
  • ☐ I described any visual by guiding attention and interpreting it, not just reading it
  • ☐ I made eye contact, slowed my pace, and paused after key points
  • ☐ I closed with a summary and a clear call to action, then invited questions
  • ☐ I answered questions concisely, and parked or followed up on ones I couldn't answer

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Structure first, slides later — decide your 2–3 points and their order before you open any slide software.
  • Signpost every transition — one phrase between points keeps the audience with you the whole way.
  • Speak to the audience, not the screen — make eye contact, slow down, and let slides support you.
  • Prepare your opening and close cold — the two moments people remember most.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • No structure or signposting: the audience can't follow and switches off. Announce your structure and mark every turn.
  • Reading the slides word-for-word: kills eye contact and engagement. Keywords on screen, sentences in your mouth.
  • Rushing and monotone: nerves speed you up and flatten your voice. Slow down, pause, and stress what matters.
  • Waffling in the Q&A: answer concisely; if you don't know, say so and offer to follow up.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Structure is everything: open (tell them what you'll tell them), body of 2–3 points, close (tell them what you told them).
  • Signposting is the core skill — phrases for moving on, sequencing, referring back/forward, giving examples, emphasising, and summarising make a talk easy to follow.
  • Describe visuals by guiding attention and interpreting the data — introduce → direct → describe the trend → say what it means. Never just read your slides.
  • Delivery and Q&A: make eye contact, slow down, engage with "you"-language, and answer questions concisely — following up honestly on anything you can't answer.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You now have a complete, reusable framework for any business presentation — a structure to hang your ideas on, the exact signposting language to keep an audience with you, a method for turning charts into insight, and the phrases to handle questions with poise. This is the difference between "having to present" and being someone others trust to explain things clearly. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How many points should my presentation have?

Two or three main points is the sweet spot for most business talks. Audiences can't hold much more, and three is a naturally memorable number. If you have more, group them under two or three headings — better to cover three points well than seven badly.

Should I take questions during the talk or at the end?

Both are fine — just decide in advance and say so in your housekeeping. Questions-as-you-go suits small, informal groups and keeps it interactive; questions-at-the-end suits larger audiences or tight timing and stops you being derailed. The key is telling the audience which it will be.

What if I go blank in the middle?

Pause, breathe, and glance at your outline or slide — a short silence looks composed, not lost. This is exactly why a clear structure helps: your signposts ("this brings me to…") double as memory prompts that steer you back on track.
