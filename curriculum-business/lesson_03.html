📞 Lesson 1.3: Telephoning & Virtual Calls

On a call, the other person can't see your face, your notes, or your screen — your voice and your words carry everything. This lesson gives you the phrase banks for every stage of a phone call, the skill of clarifying and confirming when you can't be sure you heard right, and the etiquette that makes video meetings run smoothly. By the end you'll answer, make, transfer, and end calls like a pro — on the phone and on camera.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Make and receive professional phone calls using the right phrase for each stage — answering, stating your purpose, connecting, and ending
  • Take and leave clear messages, capturing name, company, number, and reason accurately
  • Clarify and confirm information — spelling, numbers, dates — when you can't see the person
  • Handle video-call etiquette and common tech problems with confident, natural language

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Task: Role-play (or record both sides of) a business phone call and a video-meeting opening, using the phrase banks from this lesson.

Why Calls Are Different

Everything you learned about register and tone in Lesson 1.1 still applies — but a call removes the safety nets you rely on in person or in writing. There's no body language, no subject line to reread, no chance to polish your wording before it lands. That changes how you communicate:

  • Your voice does the work. Warmth, pace, and clarity all travel through tone alone.
  • You can't check you heard right by looking. Names, numbers, and dates must be actively confirmed.
  • Turn-taking is harder. Without visual cues, people talk over each other — pauses and signposting matter more.
  • Calls follow predictable stages. That's good news: learn the phrase for each stage and you're never lost for words.
⚠️ The classic call mistake: guessing instead of confirming. You think the client said "Thursday at 3" and "50 units" — but you're not sure, and you don't want to seem slow, so you don't ask. The wrong day and the wrong quantity cost far more than a five-second "Let me just check I've got that right." On a call, confirming isn't weakness — it's professionalism.

The Call, Stage by Stage

A business call moves through a reliable sequence. Here is the functional language for each stage — receiver on one side, caller on the other. Learn a couple of phrases from each bank and you can handle almost any call.

Here's the typical flow of an incoming call that needs to be transferred:

flowchart TD
    A["Answer & identify"] --> B["Caller states purpose"]
    B --> C{"Is the person available?"}
    C -->|"Yes"| D["Connect / put through"]
    C -->|"No"| E["Offer to take a message"]
    E --> F["Confirm the details"]
    D --> G["End the call politely"]
    F --> G

1. Answering & identifying

Good morning, Meridian Logistics, Ana speaking. How can I help? Hello, this is Ana Ruiz. Sales department, Tom speaking. Thanks for calling Meridian — how can I help you today?

2. Making a call & stating your purpose

Hello, this is Ana from Meridian. I'm calling about the invoice for last month. The reason I'm calling is… Could I speak to Sara Alvarez, please? I was hoping to reach someone in accounts. Is this a good time to talk?

3. Connecting & holding

I'll put you through. One moment, I'll transfer you. Could you hold the line, please? Bear with me a moment. I'm just connecting you now. Thanks for holding.

4. When the person is unavailable

I'm afraid she's not at her desk right now. He's in a meeting at the moment. She's away until Monday. Can I take a message? Would you like to leave a message? Shall I ask her to call you back?

5. Leaving a message

Could you ask her to call me back on 020 7946 0532? Could you let him know that the delivery is delayed? Please tell her Ana from Meridian called. Could you pass on a message for me? I'll try again later, thanks.

6. Ending the call

Thanks for your help. That's everything — thanks very much. I'll send that over by email to confirm. Speak soon. Have a good day. Thanks for calling — bye for now.

💡 Signpost so no one talks over you

Because you can't see each other, tell the person what's happening: "Let me just check that for you," "I'll put you on hold for a second," "Bear with me." A short spoken cue prevents awkward silences and the "are you still there?" moment. When you come back: "Sorry to keep you" or "Thanks for waiting."

Taking & Leaving Messages

A good message captures five things so the right person can act without calling back for details: who called, from where, their number, why, and what they want to happen next. Say them back to confirm before you hang up.

What to getHow to ask for it
NameCan I take your name, please? / Who's calling, please?
CompanyAnd which company are you calling from?
NumberWhat's the best number to reach you on?
ReasonCan I ask what it's regarding? / What's it in connection with?
Callback / next stepWould you like her to call you back, or shall I pass on a message?

Here is a clean message-taking script you can adapt whenever you answer for a colleague:

Model — taking a message (receiver's side):

"I'm afraid Sara's not at her desk at the moment. Can I take a message?
… Of course. Can I take your name and company?
… And the best number to reach you on?
… Let me read that back to you: Miguel Torres, from Delta Freight, on 020 7946 0532, about the shipping quote, and you'd like a call back before Friday. Is that right?
… Perfect — I'll make sure she gets it. Thanks for calling, and have a good day."

✅ Always read it back

The single habit that prevents almost every message error is reading the details back before you end the call: "So that's Miguel Torres, Delta Freight, on 020 7946 0532 — is that correct?" It takes ten seconds and saves a wrong callback, a lost lead, or a missed deadline.

Clarifying & Confirming

⚠️ This is the heart of good telephoning: because you can't see the person, you must actively check what you heard. There's no shame in asking someone to repeat, slow down, or spell something — professionals do it constantly. The trick is to ask politely and specifically.

Asking someone to repeat or slow down

Sorry, could you repeat that? Could you say that again, please? Would you mind speaking a little more slowly? Sorry, the line's not great — could you repeat the last part? Would you mind spelling that?

Confirming & summarising

So, just to confirm… Let me just check I've got that right… Let me read that back to you. Can I just double-check the spelling? So that's the 14th, not the 40th?

Spelling clearly — the phonetic idea

When a letter could be misheard (b/p/d, m/n, f/s), spell it with a word: "M for Mike," "A for Alpha." You don't need the official alphabet by heart — any clear common word works. Here's the widely used NATO set as a reliable reference:

💡 Confirm the risky bits, not everything

You don't need to read the whole conversation back — just the parts that cost money or time if wrong: names, phone numbers, quantities, dates, and prices. Zero in on those: "So the order's for fifty, five-zero, units — correct?" That's targeted, fast, and reassuring rather than tedious.

Model Call in Action

Here's a full, realistic business call: a caller reaches a receptionist, the person they want is unavailable, and a message is taken — followed by a short clarifying exchange to spell a name and confirm a time. Notice the stage phrases and the read-back in bold.

Receptionist: Good morning, Meridian Logistics, Ana speaking. How can I help?

Caller: Good morning. This is Miguel Torres from Delta Freight. Could I speak to Sara Alvarez, please?

Receptionist: Let me just check for you — bear with me a moment. … I'm afraid Sara's in a meeting right now. Can I take a message?

Caller: Yes, please. Could you ask her to call me back about the shipping quote?

Receptionist: Of course. Can I take your name and the best number to reach you on?

Caller: It's Miguel Torres — that's Torres, T for Tango, O for Oscar, double R, E for Echo, S for Sierra. On 020 7946 0532.

Receptionist: Let me read that back to you: Miguel Torres, T-O-R-R-E-S, Delta Freight, on 020 7946 0532, about the shipping quote. Is that right?

Caller: That's perfect. And could she call before Thursday?

Receptionist: So that's a callback before Thursday the eleventh — that's one-one — not the twelfth?

Caller: Correct, before Thursday the eleventh.

Receptionist: Great — I'll make sure she gets it. Thanks for calling, and have a good day.

Caller: Thanks very much for your help. Bye for now.

📖 What made this call work

The receptionist identified the company on answering, signposted the hold ("bear with me"), offered a message when Sara was unavailable, and read the details back — spelling and date included. The caller stated who and why immediately and spelled his surname without being asked. Nothing was left to guesswork.

Video & Virtual Calls

Video meetings are now the norm for many teams. They add a screen, a mute button, and a whole layer of etiquette — but the goal is the same: clear, courteous communication where everyone can take part.

Etiquette essentials

  • Join on time — or a minute early; latecomers disrupt the room
  • Mute when not speaking — background noise is the No.1 distraction
  • Camera on if that's the norm — match the team's convention
  • Good lighting & a tidy background — face the light, not a bright window
  • Don't talk over people — pause a beat before jumping in
  • Use names — "Over to you, Priya" replaces the eye contact you'd use in person
  • Look at the camera occasionally — it reads as eye contact to others

Opening & managing a video call

Can everyone hear me okay? Shall we get started? I think we're just waiting for one more person. You're on mute. Sorry, you go ahead. Priya, did you want to come in on that? Could you share your screen? Let's use the chat for questions.

Handling tech problems

Sorry, you're breaking up a little. I think we've lost you. Your audio keeps cutting out. Let me reconnect — one second. Can you turn your camera off? It might help the connection. Could you drop it in the chat instead? Sorry, could you repeat that last part? You froze for a moment.

A short, natural video-call opening might sound like this:

Chair: Hi everyone, thanks for joining. Can everyone hear me okay?

Tom: Loud and clear.

Chair: Great. I think we're just waiting for Priya… oh, there she is. Priya, you're on mute.

Priya: Sorry about that — can you hear me now?

Chair: Perfectly. Right, shall we get started? I'll share my screen so we're all looking at the same agenda. Could you see that okay?

Tom: Yes, that's come up fine.

Chair: Wonderful. Let's use the chat for any questions as we go, and I'll pause after each item. Over to item one.

🧠 Mindset: Great communicators are made, not born

Almost everyone feels awkward on their first calls — the pauses, the "can you hear me?", the fear of mishearing a number. That awkwardness isn't a sign you're bad at this; it's the sign of a skill still forming. Every professional who sounds effortless on the phone once fumbled through the same stages. Use the phrase banks as training wheels, make a few calls on purpose, and confidence follows. You're not born knowing how to run a video meeting — you learn it, one call at a time.

💡 Register & culture on calls

Smile — it's audible. A smile literally changes the shape of your voice and comes across as warmth, so smile as you answer. Keep your pace a touch slower than in person and enunciate. Small-talk norms vary: some cultures open with a minute of pleasantries ("How was your weekend?"), others get straight to business — mirror your counterpart, and on the phone keep it briefer and clearer than you would face to face. When in doubt, follow the register lessons from 1.1: neutral-professional, warm, and clear.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match the phrase to its stage

Objective: Match each phrase (A–E) to the call stage it belongs to (1–5).

Phrases: A) "I'll put you through." · B) "Can I take a message?" · C) "Good morning, Meridian, Ana speaking." · D) "Thanks for your help — have a good day." · E) "The reason I'm calling is…"

Stages: 1) Answering/identifying · 2) Stating your purpose · 3) Connecting/holding · 4) Person unavailable · 5) Ending the call

✅ Answer

A → 3 (connecting) · B → 4 (unavailable) · C → 1 (answering) · D → 5 (ending) · E → 2 (stating purpose).

🏋️ Exercise 2: Put the call in order

Objective: These lines are jumbled. Put them in a logical order for a phone call.

  1. "I'm afraid he's not at his desk. Can I take a message?"
  2. "Good afternoon, Delta Freight, Tom speaking. How can I help?"
  3. "Thanks very much for your help. Bye for now."
  4. "Hello, this is Ana from Meridian. Could I speak to Miguel, please?"
  5. "Yes please — could you ask him to call me back on 020 7946 0532?"
  6. "Of course. Let me read that back: Ana from Meridian, on 020 7946 0532."
✅ Answer

Logical order: 2 → 4 → 1 → 5 → 6 → 3. Tom answers and identifies (2), Ana states who she is and who she wants (4), Tom reports Miguel is unavailable and offers a message (1), Ana leaves the callback message (5), Tom reads it back to confirm (6), and Ana closes the call (3).

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the message & say it aloud

Objective: A colleague scribbled this message. It's missing key details and would be risky to act on. (a) List what's unclear or missing, then (b) write how you'd say the name and number aloud to confirm them on a call.

Message left on a sticky note: "Someone called about an order. Call them back."

Now spell & say this aloud, phone-style: Name: Bexley · Number: 07700 900142

✅ Answer (models)

(a) Missing: who called (name), which company, their number, which order/what it's about, and by when they need a callback. A usable version: "Sam Bexley, from Orion Supplies, called at 10:15 about order #4471 — please call back on 07700 900142 before 5pm today."

(b) Saying it aloud: Name — "Bexley: B for Bravo, E for Echo, X for X-ray, L for Lima, E for Echo, Y for Yankee." Number — "oh-double-seven-double-oh, nine-oh-oh, one-four-two" (or "zero seven seven zero zero, nine zero zero, one four two"). Then confirm: "So that's Bexley, B-E-X-L-E-Y, on 07700 900142 — correct?" Other clear versions are fine, as long as risky letters and digits are spelled out.

🎯 Your Task: Role-Play a Call and a Video-Meeting Opening

  1. (3 min) Choose a realistic scenario — e.g. you call a supplier to chase a delayed delivery, and the contact is unavailable so you leave a message.
  2. (10 min) Using the stage phrase banks, script or improvise both sides of the phone call: answering & identifying → stating purpose → person unavailable → leaving a message → reading it back → ending. Include one clarifying moment (spell a name and confirm a number or date).
  3. (3 min) Perform it — role-play with a partner or record both voices yourself. Smile as you speak; keep your pace slightly slow.
  4. (6 min) Now open a video meeting: greet, check audio ("Can everyone hear me?"), handle a "you're on mute" moment, share your screen, and set one ground rule (chat for questions). Handle one tech hiccup ("you're breaking up — could you repeat that?").
  5. (3 min) Play it back or debrief with your partner: Did you read the risky details back? Did you signpost holds? Was your tone warm and clear?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Identify yourself immediately — company and name when you answer; name and purpose when you call.
  • Signpost holds and pauses so the other person is never left wondering if you're still there.
  • Read the risky details back — names, numbers, dates, quantities, prices — every time.
  • On video: join on time, mute when not speaking, and use people's names to hand over.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Guessing instead of confirming: the classic call error. If you're not 100% sure of a number, name, or date, ask and read it back — it's professional, not slow.
  • Silent holds: putting someone on hold without a word ("Bear with me") feels like being cut off. Always signpost.
  • Speaking too fast or without a smile: a call strips away your body language, so pace and warmth in the voice matter more, not less.
  • Talking over people on video: without visual cues it's easy to collide. Pause a beat, and use "Sorry, you go ahead."
  • Ignoring tech trouble: when someone's breaking up, say so ("you're cutting out — could you repeat that?") rather than pretending you heard.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Business calls follow predictable stages — answer/identify, state purpose, connect/hold, handle unavailability, take/leave a message, end — each with its own phrase bank.
  • A good message captures name, company, number, reason, and next step, and you read it back to confirm.
  • Because you can't see the person, clarify and confirm actively — ask to repeat/slow down, spell with clear words ("M for Mike"), and say numbers, dates, and emails carefully.
  • Video calls add etiquette (on time, mute, camera, names) and their own phrases for opening, managing, and fixing tech problems.
  • Smile — it's audible; keep your pace slower and clearer than in person, and mirror your counterpart's small-talk norms.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now walk into any call with the language ready: you know how to answer and identify, put someone through, take a message and read it back, spell a tricky name, and confirm a date without guessing. You can open and steer a video meeting, hand over with names, and rescue a call when the connection drops. These are exactly the situations that used to feel stressful — and now you have the phrases to handle them. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't asking someone to repeat themselves unprofessional?

The opposite. Acting on a number or date you're unsure of is the real risk. A polite "Sorry, could you repeat the last part?" or "Let me read that back" shows you're careful and thorough — qualities every client and colleague values on a call.

Do I really need the phonetic alphabet?

You don't need the official NATO set memorised — any clear, common word works ("D for David," "M for Mango"). Keep the table handy for the letters that get confused most (b/p/d, m/n, f/s), and reach for a spelling word whenever a name or code could be misheard.

Should my camera always be on in video meetings?

Follow the team's norm. Many workplaces expect cameras on for internal and client meetings to build rapport, but some large or informal calls are camera-optional. When you're new, match what the majority are doing and default to on for smaller, important meetings.
