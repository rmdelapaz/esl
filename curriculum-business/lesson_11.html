📝 Lesson 4.2: CVs, Cover Letters & Job Interviews

This is the lesson where all your Business English comes together for one high-stakes purpose: landing the job. You'll learn to write a CV that gets read, a cover letter that gets you an interview, and to answer the questions that decide whether the offer lands. The secret across all three is the same — stop describing your duties and start proving your results.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure an effective CV/résumé with strong, results-focused, quantified bullet points
  • Write a targeted cover letter that fits the role — not a generic template
  • Prepare for and answer common interview questions, including behavioural questions with the STAR method
  • Present yourself with confidence, ask good questions, and close the interview well

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Draft or upgrade a CV professional summary + three achievement bullets, prepare STAR answers to two common interview questions, and role-play the interview.

The CV / Résumé: Structure

First, a word on terminology. In the UK, Ireland, and much of the Commonwealth, the document is a CV (curriculum vitae). In the US and Canada, the same one- or two-page job-application document is a résumé — there, "CV" means a long academic record. Whatever you call it, the goal is identical: a scannable summary of your value. Keep it to 1–2 pages; recruiters skim, they don't read.

A reliable, recruiter-friendly order:

SectionWhat goes in it
1. Contact infoName, phone, professional email, city, LinkedIn. No photo/age in many markets (see culture note).
2. Professional summary / profile2–3 lines: who you are, key strengths, what you offer. Your written elevator pitch.
3. Work experienceReverse chronological (most recent first): role, company, dates, then 3–5 achievement bullets each.
4. EducationDegree, institution, year. Move above experience only if you're a recent graduate.
5. SkillsRelevant technical, language, and software skills — tailored to the job's keywords.
6. Optional extrasCertifications, languages, projects, volunteering — only if relevant to the role.

💡 Tailor it to the job (and the robots)

Many companies filter applications through an ATS (Applicant Tracking System) before a human ever sees them. It scans for keywords from the job advert. So mirror the language of the posting: if they want "stakeholder management" and "project delivery," and you've done exactly that, use their words. One tailored CV beats fifty generic ones.

Powerful CV Bullet Points

This is the skill of the whole lesson. Most CVs fail for one reason: they list duties ("Responsible for sales") instead of achievements ("Grew regional sales 20% in 12 months"). Duties tell the reader your job title; achievements prove you were good at it. Every bullet should do two things:

  1. Start with a strong action verb (not "Responsible for…").
  2. Be results-focused and quantified — a number, a %, a time frame, a scale.

A simple formula: Action verb + what you did + measurable result.

Action-verb wordlist

Swap tired phrases for these. Group them by what they prove:

  • Led / Managed — leadership
  • Delivered / Launched — you shipped it
  • Increased / Grew / Boosted — growth
  • Reduced / Cut / Saved — efficiency
  • Streamlined / Improved — process
  • Built / Developed / Created — you made it
  • Negotiated / Won / Secured — deals
  • Trained / Mentored / Coached — people
  • Achieved / Exceeded / Hit — targets

Weak → Strong: turning duties into achievements

❌ Responsible for sales in my region.
✅ Grew regional sales 20% in 12 months, exceeding target for three consecutive quarters.

❌ Was in charge of a team.
✅ Managed a team of 8, cutting project delivery time by 15%.

❌ Helped with customer complaints.
✅ Resolved 40+ customer issues weekly, lifting satisfaction scores from 78% to 91%.

❌ Did the monthly reports.
✅ Streamlined monthly reporting, saving the team ~6 hours a month.

Annotation: every strong version opens with a verb and lands on a number. No number handy? Use scale ("40+"), frequency ("weekly"), or a before→after ("78% to 91%").

⚠️ The classic CV mistake

Starting bullets with "Responsible for…", "Duties included…", or "In charge of…". These describe the job, not you. Anyone with that title could write them. Lead with a verb and prove the result — that's what makes a recruiter stop skimming.

The Professional Summary

The professional summary (or "profile") sits at the very top of your CV — 2–3 lines that make a recruiter want to keep reading. Think of it as your elevator pitch from Lesson 1.1, written down: who you are + your key strengths + what you offer. Tailor it to each role.

Model professional summary

"Results-driven marketing analyst with 5 years' experience turning data into revenue. Skilled in campaign analytics, stakeholder reporting, and cross-team collaboration. Looking to bring a track record of measurable growth to a data-led marketing team."

Annotation: line 1 = who + a strength ("results-driven"); line 2 = concrete skills (keywords!); line 3 = what you offer + the direction you're heading. No fluff, no "hard-working team player."

💡 Avoid empty adjectives

"Hard-working, motivated, dynamic team player" says nothing — everyone claims it. Replace vague adjectives with evidence: instead of "excellent communicator," write "presented quarterly results to senior stakeholders." Show, don't label.

The Cover Letter

The cover letter answers the question the CV can't: why you, and why here? Keep it to one page, keep the register formal (Lesson 1.2), and — crucially — make it targeted. A four-part structure works every time:

PartPurpose
1. OpeningName the role, say where you saw it, and open with a hook — a genuine reason you're excited.
2. Why youMatch your evidence to their needs. Pick 2–3 requirements and prove them.
3. Why themShow you know the company — a project, value, or achievement you admire.
4. ClosingA confident call to action + your availability for interview.

Phrase bank — cover letter

I am writing to apply for the position of… as advertised on… I was excited to see your opening for… In my current role at…, I have… This directly matches your requirement for… I am particularly drawn to your company because… I admire your recent work on… I would welcome the opportunity to discuss… I am available for interview at your convenience. Thank you for your time and consideration. I look forward to hearing from you.

Model cover-letter excerpt (the "why you" paragraph)

"In my current role at Meridian, I increased qualified leads by 30% by rebuilding our campaign-tracking process — directly relevant to your need for a data-led marketer who can show, not guess. I was drawn to this role because I admire how your team uses experimentation to drive decisions; it's exactly the environment where I do my best work."

Annotation: it quotes the job ("data-led marketer"), gives evidence ("30%"), and shows real knowledge of the company — not a single reused sentence.

⚠️ Don't just restate your CV — and don't go generic

Two failure modes: (1) copying your CV into paragraphs (the recruiter already has it), and (2) the one-size-fits-all letter you send everywhere. A hiring manager spots a generic letter instantly. If you could swap in any company's name without changing a word, it's not targeted enough. Name their role, their needs, their work.

Interview Prep & Common Questions

You got the interview — now the same rule applies: evidence beats claims. Prepare answers to the handful of questions that come up almost every time. Prepared doesn't mean robotic; it means you're never caught blank.

"Tell me about yourself."

The most common opener — and the most commonly fumbled. It's not "tell me your life story." It's a structured 60-second pitch: present (who you are now) → past (relevant experience) → future (why this role). Link it to your elevator pitch and CV summary.

Model answer — "Tell me about yourself."

"I'm a marketing analyst with five years turning data into growth. Most recently at Meridian I rebuilt our campaign tracking and grew qualified leads by 30%. I'm looking to bring that measurable, data-led approach to a bigger, experiment-driven team — which is exactly why this role caught my eye."

Annotation: present → past (with a number) → future. Under a minute. It ends by pointing at their job.

Other common questions — model answers

"Why do you want this role / company?" → "Because your focus on experimentation matches how I work — I grow through testing and measuring, and your recent product launch showed that culture. I'd contribute my track record of data-led growth."

"What are your strengths?" → "Turning messy data into clear decisions. For example, I cut our reporting time by 15% while making the numbers easier for non-analysts to act on."

"What's your weakness?" → "I used to take on too much myself rather than delegate. I've worked on it by setting clear team ownership up front — now I delegate deliberately and check in, rather than doing it all."

"Where do you see yourself in five years?" → "Leading a small analytics team and mentoring others — building on the hands-on work I love now. A role like this is the natural next step."

💡 The weakness question — get it right

Give a real weakness (not the fake "I'm a perfectionist"), then show how you manage it. That proves self-awareness and growth — which is what the question is actually testing. Never pick a weakness that's core to the job.

Behavioural Questions & the STAR Method

Behavioural questions start with "Tell me about a time when you…" — handled a conflict, hit a tight deadline, showed leadership, dealt with a difficult client. The logic: past behaviour predicts future behaviour. Rambling kills these answers. The fix is the STAR method — a four-step structure that keeps you focused and lands on a result.

StepWhat to coverKick-off phrase
S — SituationSet the scene briefly — where, when, the context."In my last role, we were…"
T — TaskYour specific responsibility or the challenge."I was responsible for…"
A — ActionWhat you did — the biggest part. Use "I," not "we.""So I decided to… / I organised…"
R — ResultThe outcome — quantified if possible + what you learned."As a result, we… / This led to…"

Full STAR answer — "Tell me about a time you hit a tight deadline."

(S) "In my last role, a key client moved their campaign launch forward by two weeks, leaving us very little time. (T) As the lead analyst, I had to deliver the full performance dashboard they needed to go live. (A) So I broke the work into daily milestones, negotiated to automate two manual reports, and pulled in a colleague for the data checks so nothing slipped. (R) We delivered a day early, the launch went ahead on time, and the client renewed for another year. I also kept the automation, which saved us hours every month afterwards."

Annotation: brief S and T, a detailed A in the first person, and an R with a concrete outcome plus a lasting benefit.

A short exchange showing STAR in a real interview:

Interviewer: Can you tell me about a time you handled a conflict on your team?

Candidate: Sure. On a recent project, two colleagues disagreed sharply about our approach and it was stalling the work. (Situation)

Interviewer: What was your role in that?

Candidate: I was leading the project, so unblocking it was on me. (Task) I set up a short meeting, let each explain their reasoning, and we agreed a quick test to compare both ideas with real data rather than opinions. (Action) The test settled it in a day, the team got moving again, and we actually shipped ahead of schedule. (Result)

Interviewer: I like that you let the data decide — thank you.

🧠 Mindset: interview skill is built, not born

Nobody is naturally brilliant at interviews — confident candidates are simply prepared and practised ones. The nerves you feel are normal and shared by everyone in the waiting room. Every strong answer you rehearse, every STAR story you bank, makes the next interview easier. Treat each one as reps at the gym: the more you do, the stronger you get. A "no" isn't a verdict on your worth — it's practice for the "yes."

Asking Questions & Closing the Interview

Interviews almost always end with "Do you have any questions for us?" This is not the end — it's your final chance to impress. Having no questions signals low interest. Thoughtful questions show you're engaged and already picturing the role.

Phrase bank — good questions to ask them

What does success look like in this role in the first six months? How would you describe the team culture? What are the biggest challenges the team is facing right now? What are the opportunities for growth and development? What do you enjoy most about working here? What are the next steps in the process?

Closing well & following up

Thank you — this has been really helpful. I'm even more excited about the role after our conversation. Thank you for taking the time to meet me today. I enjoyed learning more about the team and would be glad to bring my experience to it.

Within 24 hours, send a short thank-you email: thank them, mention one thing you discussed, and reaffirm your interest. It's brief, courteous, and surprisingly rare — so it stands out.

💡 Confidence & body language

Your delivery speaks before your words do: make eye contact, offer a firm handshake, sit up, and smile. Slow down — a small pause to think reads as composure, not weakness. On video calls, look at the camera, not the screen, and check your lighting and background beforehand.

⚠️ Two things that sink strong candidates

  • Badmouthing a past employer or boss. Even if it was awful, stay professional: "It wasn't the right fit for my goals, so I'm looking for…" Complaining makes you look like the problem.
  • Having no questions at the end. It reads as "I'm not that interested." Always bank two or three in advance.

🌍 A note on culture

Norms vary widely by market. In some countries a CV includes a photo, date of birth, and marital status; in the US, UK, and others these are deliberately left off to avoid bias — and "references available on request" is often assumed, not listed. Length expectations (one page vs. two), how much you may self-promote, and interview formality all differ too. Research the norms of the country and company you're applying to, and adapt — there's no single global standard.

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These lines help you present your experience and answer interview questions with confidence.

  • I led a team of five and increased sales by twenty percent.
  • I'm particularly drawn to this role because it fits my strengths.
  • In my current position, I manage the entire onboarding process.
  • My greatest strength is staying calm and organised under pressure.
  • Thank you for your time; I'm very excited about this opportunity.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Duty → achievement bullets

Objective: Rewrite each duty as an achievement bullet — start with an action verb and add a (plausible) number.

  1. Responsible for answering customer emails.
  2. Helped organise company events.
  3. In charge of updating the website.
✅ Answer (models)

1. "Handled 50+ customer emails daily, maintaining a 95% same-day response rate." · 2. "Coordinated 4 company events for up to 120 attendees, all delivered on budget." · 3. "Redesigned and maintained the company website, increasing traffic by 25%." Your numbers will differ — the point is verb + result. Many good versions exist.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Build a STAR answer

Objective: The question is "Tell me about a time you showed leadership." Turn these notes into a STAR answer:

Notes: team behind on a report · manager was off sick · I stepped up · split the tasks, set a plan · we finished on time · manager praised it.

✅ Answer (model)

(S) "Our team was falling behind on an important quarterly report and, midway through, our manager went off sick. (T) Someone needed to step up to keep it on track, so I volunteered to coordinate it. (A) I split the report into clear sections, assigned each to the right person, and set two short check-ins so we could catch problems early. (R) We delivered it on time and to standard, and our manager specifically praised how smoothly the team had run in her absence." Note the first-person "I" in the Action, and the result at the end.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the weak line

Objective: Spot and fix the problem in each.

  1. (Cover letter opening) "I am a hard-working team player and would be a great fit for any role at your company."
  2. (Interview answer to "Why did you leave your last job?") "My boss was terrible and the company was a mess."
✅ Answer

1. It's generic ("any role") and made of empty adjectives. Fix: name the role and give evidence — "I'm applying for the Marketing Analyst role because my experience growing leads 30% at Meridian matches your need for data-led growth." · 2. Badmouthing. It makes the candidate look like the problem. Fix: stay positive and forward-looking — "I'd achieved what I could there and was looking for a bigger, more data-driven challenge, which is what drew me to this role."

🎯 Your Task: Application Sprint + Interview Role-Play

Pick a real or realistic job you'd apply for. Then:

  1. (10 min) Draft or upgrade your professional summary (2–3 lines: who you are + strengths + what you offer) and three achievement bullets — each an action verb + a quantified result. Tailor the wording to the job's keywords.
  2. (10 min) Prepare STAR answers to two common interview questions — one "tell me about a time…" behavioural question, and one of: "tell me about yourself," "why this role?", or "what's your weakness?"
  3. (3 min) Write two questions to ask them at the end.
  4. (10 min) Role-play the interview. Solo: record yourself and play it back. With a partner/colleague: they ask, you answer with STAR, then swap and give each other feedback.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My summary has all three elements and no empty adjectives
  • ☐ All three bullets start with an action verb and contain a number
  • ☐ My wording reflects keywords from the job advert
  • ☐ Both interview answers follow a clear structure (STAR for the behavioural one)
  • ☐ I have two thoughtful questions ready to ask
  • ☐ I said my answers aloud and they sound natural, not memorised

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Quantify everything you can — numbers, %, time frames, scale. They make achievements believable.
  • Tailor the CV and cover letter to each job, mirroring its keywords (for the ATS and the human).
  • Bank STAR stories in advance — 3–4 flexible ones cover most behavioural questions.
  • Always prepare questions to ask, and send a short thank-you email within a day.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "Responsible for…" bullets: duties, not achievements. Lead with a verb and a result.
  • Generic cover letters: if any company's name would fit, it's not targeted. Name their role and their needs.
  • Rambling interview answers: structure with STAR; keep "tell me about yourself" under a minute.
  • Badmouthing past employers or having no questions — both quietly sink strong candidates.
  • Ignoring local norms: photo/age/length and interview formality vary by country — research and adapt.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A CV/résumé is a scannable 1–2 page summary of your value — contact, summary, reverse-chronological experience, education, skills — tailored to the job's keywords.
  • Powerful bullets = action verb + quantified result. Replace "Responsible for…" with achievements; numbers make them believable.
  • The cover letter is targeted, not generic: opening + why you + why them + call to action. Don't just restate the CV.
  • Prepare common questions (tell me about yourself, strengths/weakness, why us) and use the STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — for behavioural questions.
  • Always ask thoughtful questions, never badmouth past employers, and present with confident body language — CV and interview norms vary by market, so adapt.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now build a CV that recruiters actually read, write a cover letter that fits the role, and walk into an interview with structured answers and good questions ready. Most importantly, you've internalised the one idea behind all of it: prove results, don't list duties. That single shift puts you ahead of most applicants. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What if I don't have impressive numbers to quantify?

Almost everything can be measured. If not a %, use scale ("a team of 6," "40+ tickets a week"), frequency, time saved, or a before→after. Even "reduced errors" becomes "cut reporting errors to near zero." Estimate honestly if you must — the habit of quantifying is what matters.

How many STAR stories do I really need?

Three or four flexible ones usually cover most interviews — a leadership story, a conflict/teamwork story, a deadline/pressure story, and a failure/learning story. The same story can often answer several different questions, so prepare versatile ones.

Is it dishonest to tailor my CV for each job?

Not at all — tailoring means emphasising the true experience most relevant to each role and using the employer's own words, not inventing anything. It's the difference between a highlight reel and a full archive. Never fabricate; always foreground your most relevant real achievements.
