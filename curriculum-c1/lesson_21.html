🗣️ Lesson 7.3: Seminar & Discussion Skills

You can already agree and disagree. But a real seminar, a working meeting, a graduate discussion — these ask for far more: coming into a fast conversation without interrupting rudely, holding the floor long enough to finish your thought, building on what someone else said, checking you've understood, disagreeing without a rupture, and nudging a rambling discussion back on track. These are the moves of the confident participant, and at C1 they're what let your ideas actually land in a group.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Take turns gracefully — come in politely, hold the floor, and hand over to others
  • Build on others' ideas and connect your contribution to what's already been said
  • Ask for and give clarification, and paraphrase others to check understanding
  • Manage disagreement constructively, find common ground, and steer or summarise a discussion

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Take part in (or simulate) a short group discussion, using at least four different discussion-management moves.

Beyond Agreeing and Disagreeing

At lower levels, "discussion skills" mostly means expressing opinions: I think…, I agree…, I don't agree… Useful, but a genuine group conversation is a piece of social machinery, and opinions are only its raw material. What makes a discussion work — and what makes you a valued participant in it — is the invisible layer of moves that manage the conversation itself: who speaks when, how contributions connect, how misunderstandings get repaired, how conflict stays productive, and how the whole thing moves toward a conclusion.

These are sometimes called interactional or discussion-management skills, and they're precisely what C1 speaking exams and real academic and professional settings reward. Crucially, they're not about dominating — the strongest participants often say relatively little but say it at the right moment, connect it to others, and help the group think together. This lesson gives you the phrasebook and the judgement to do that.

🧠 Mindset

Many advanced learners have plenty to say and still go quiet in group discussions — not from lack of ideas, but from not knowing how to get in without seeming rude, or how to disagree without seeming aggressive. That's a skills gap, not a personality flaw, and it's very fixable. The phrases in this lesson are like door-handles: once you have a few automatic ways to enter, hold, and hand over a turn, the anxiety drops and your ideas finally get heard. Start with two or three and build from there. 🌱

Turn-taking: In, Hold, Over

The core mechanic of any discussion is turn-taking — the constant, mostly-unspoken negotiation of who has the floor. In a lively conversation there are rarely neat gaps to wait for, so you need explicit ways to claim a turn, protect it while you finish, and pass it on. Master these three and you'll never again sit silent because you "couldn't find a gap."

MoveWhat it doesPhrases
Coming inclaims a turn politely, even mid-flowCould I just come in here? · Can I add something? · If I could just say…· Sorry to interrupt, but…
Holding the floorsignals you're not finished, fends off interruptionIf I could just finish my point… · Just to finish my thought… · Hold on, there's one more thing… · Let me just get to the point.
Handing overinvites a specific person or the group to speakWhat are your thoughts, Priya? · I'd love to hear what you think. · Does anyone see it differently? · Over to you.

Two subtleties raise these from functional to fluent. First, coming in politely usually pairs a softener ("Could I just…", "Sorry to jump in…") with the word just, which shrinks the intrusion. Second, handing over by name ("What do you think, Priya?") is one of the most generous and impressive moves in group talk — it draws in quieter members, shows you're tracking everyone, and marks you as someone who builds discussions rather than merely competing in them.

⚠️ Interrupting vs coming in

There's a real difference between interrupting (cutting someone off to take the floor for yourself) and coming in (signalling and asking for a turn, often to add to what they're saying). The words "Could I just come in here?" are a request — they leave the current speaker a moment to yield gracefully. Simply talking over someone, even with good ideas, reads as rude in most academic and professional settings and can cost you the room's goodwill. When in doubt, signal and pause a beat before launching in.

Building on Others' Ideas

A discussion isn't a series of separate speeches — it's a shared structure people build together. The move that creates that structure is building on: explicitly linking your contribution to what someone just said, so the conversation accumulates rather than scattering. This is the single move that most makes you sound like a collaborative, high-level participant rather than someone waiting to deliver their own point.

FunctionPhrases
Adding to a pointBuilding on what you said… · To add to that… · Following on from that… · That's a good point, and I'd go further…
Giving an example for someone's pointThat connects to something I saw… · A good example of that is… · Which is exactly what happens when…
Linking two people's ideasThat ties in with Tom's earlier point… · So both of you are really saying… · There's a thread here between…
Extending or reframingI'd take that one step further… · Another way of looking at that… · That raises an interesting question…

The magic word is a genuine reference to the previous speaker — naming them, echoing their phrase, or crediting their idea before you extend it. Compare "I think we should focus on training" (a fresh, disconnected point) with "Building on Anna's point about skills, I think training is where we should focus" (the same idea, now woven into the discussion). The second doesn't just sound more polished — it makes Anna feel heard and moves the group's thinking forward together.

💡 Listen actively so you have something to build on

You can only build on ideas you've actually heard and held onto — which means the best discussants are, first, the best listeners. Track not just what people say but the thread between contributions. A quick mental (or literal) note of each person's key point gives you the raw material for "That ties in with what Tom said…" — and referencing someone's point from two turns ago is quietly impressive, because it shows you've been genuinely following, not just rehearsing your own next line.

Clarifying & Paraphrasing

Misunderstandings are normal in fast, multi-voice discussion — and repairing them smoothly is a mark of skill, not weakness. Two related moves do this work: asking for clarification when you're unsure, and paraphrasing someone's point back to check you've got it. Both keep the discussion accurate, and paraphrasing in particular is a powerful, generous move that shows you're listening and gives the speaker a chance to refine.

FunctionPhrases
Asking for clarificationSorry, could you clarify what you mean by…? · I'm not quite sure I follow. · Could you give an example? · When you say X, do you mean…?
Paraphrasing to checkSo if I understand you correctly, you're saying… · So, in other words… · Let me see if I've got this right… · So your point is that…?
Confirming / correctingYes, exactly. · Not quite — what I meant was… · That's part of it, but also…

Paraphrasing — "So if I understand you correctly, you're saying that remote work saves money but costs collaboration?" — does three things at once: it confirms you've understood, it gives the speaker a chance to correct you, and it consolidates the point for the whole group. It's also a brilliant way to slow down a heated or fast-moving discussion and buy yourself thinking time. Far from sounding uncertain, a well-placed paraphrase sounds attentive and precise.

💡 Asking for clarification is a strength, not a gap

Learners sometimes stay silent when they don't follow, fearing that admitting confusion looks weak. In reality, "Sorry, could you clarify what you mean by scalable?" is exactly what confident native speakers say — and it often turns out that half the room was unsure too. Seeking clarification keeps everyone aligned and signals that you care about getting it right. The genuinely weak move is nodding along to something you haven't understood and then contributing something off-target.

Managing Disagreement

Disagreement is the point of most good discussions — it's how groups test ideas. The C1 skill isn't avoiding it; it's disagreeing constructively: challenging the idea while respecting the person, acknowledging what's valid, and looking for common ground rather than winning. Blunt "No, you're wrong" closes minds; skilful disagreement keeps them open.

TechniqueHow it worksPhrases
Acknowledge firstcredit the valid part before you push backI take your point, but… · I see where you're coming from, though… · That's fair, and yet…
Soften the challengehedge so it lands as a view, not a verdictI'm not sure I'd go that far. · I'd question whether… · Isn't there a risk that…?
Partial agreementagree in part, then divergeUp to a point, yes, but… · I agree in principle, however… · That's true for X, but not for Y.
Find common groundname what you share to keep it collaborativeI think we both want the same outcome… · We agree on the goal; it's the method I'd question. · Where we do agree is…

The reliable structure is acknowledge → soften → challenge: "I take your point about speed (acknowledge), but I'm not sure (soften) we've thought through the cost (challenge)." This lets you be as firm as you like on the substance while keeping the relationship warm. And when things get stuck, steering back to common ground ("We all want this project to succeed — the question is just how") is what turns an argument back into a discussion.

⚠️ Bluntness travels badly

Direct contradiction that feels normal in some cultures — "No." "That's wrong." "I completely disagree." — can sound aggressive or dismissive in English-language academic and professional discussion, even when you don't mean it to. The issue isn't the strength of your view; it's the absence of the softening and acknowledging layer that English speakers expect around disagreement. You can hold your ground completely and still say "I see the appeal of that, but I'd be cautious…" — same firmness, very different temperature.

Steering & Summarising

Discussions drift — down tangents, into circles, past the time. The participant who can gently steer and periodically summarise is invaluable, and you don't need to be the official chair to do it. These moves manage the discussion's direction and keep it moving toward something.

FunctionPhrases
Refocusing / getting back on trackMaybe we could come back to…? · That's interesting, but to return to the main question… · I think we're getting a bit off-topic.
Moving onShall we move on? · I think we've covered this — shall we turn to…? · Are we ready to look at the next point?
Checking the state of playSo where does that leave us? · Where have we got to? · So what are we actually deciding?
Summarising / concludingSo, to sum up where we are… · It sounds like we broadly agree that… · The main points seem to be…

Two questions in particular are worth memorising: "So where does that leave us?" pulls a scattered discussion back to a decision, and "Shall we move on?" closes a topic politely and opens the next. And a periodic summary — "So it sounds like we broadly agree on the goal, but we're split on the timing" — is a gift to any group: it consolidates progress, surfaces the real remaining question, and quietly marks you as the person keeping everyone oriented.

Phrase bank — discussion-management functions

A Seminar in Action

Here four people discuss whether their team should switch to a four-day week. Watch every discussion-management move at work — coming in, holding the floor, handing over, building, clarifying, paraphrasing, disagreeing constructively, and steering. The moves are in bold.

Dana: I think a four-day week would boost productivity — people work better when they're rested. What are your thoughts, Ben?

Ben: Building on what you said, there's real evidence for that — several trials showed output holding steady on four days. To add to that, it's a huge draw for hiring.

Priya: Could I just come in here? I take your point about productivity, but I'd question whether it works for a client-facing team. We can't just close on Fridays.

Dana: So if I understand you correctly, your worry is coverage — clients expecting us five days a week?

Priya: Exactly. That's my main concern.

Marco: If I could just add something — we could stagger the days off, so someone's always in. That ties in with Priya's point: it keeps coverage and gives everyone the shorter week.

Priya: Up to a point, yes, but staggering means we're never all in on the same day. Isn't there a risk that collaboration suffers?

Dana: That's fair. Where we do agree, though, is that the shorter week is worth trying — it's the logistics we're split on.

Marco: So where does that leave us? Maybe we trial it for two months and measure both output and coverage?

Dana: So, to sum up, we broadly agree on trialling a four-day week, staggered, with a review after two months. Shall we move on to who'd own the trial?

Notice how much smoother this is than a series of raw opinions. Priya disagrees firmly — twice — but the conversation never sours, because every challenge is acknowledged and softened, every new point is built onto the last, and Dana keeps steering toward a decision. That's a discussion doing its job.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These five sentences drill the core discussion-management moves — say them until they're automatic enough to reach for mid-conversation.

  • Could I just come in here? I'd like to add something.
  • Building on what you said, I'd take that one step further.
  • So if I understand you correctly, your main concern is the cost.
  • I take your point, but I'm not sure I'd go quite that far.
  • So where does that leave us? Shall we move on to the next point?

🎯 Your Project: Run a Real Discussion

Put the moves to work in an actual conversation — with a group, a partner, or, solo, by scripting and voicing multiple roles.

  1. (3 min) Pick a debatable topic your group can genuinely argue ("Should our city ban cars from the centre?", "Is remote work better for teams?", "Should university be free?"). Agree on 10–15 minutes.
  2. (2 min) Before you start, each choose four discussion-management moves you'll consciously use — e.g. handing over by name, building on an idea, paraphrasing, and steering with "where does that leave us?"
  3. (10–15 min) Hold the discussion. Aim to come in at least twice, build on someone else's point at least once, paraphrase to check understanding once, and disagree constructively at least once.
  4. (3 min) Appoint (or become) a summariser to close: "So, to sum up, we broadly agree that…, but we're split on…"
  5. (3 min) Debrief: which moves felt natural, which felt forced, and where did the discussion flow or stall? If solo, record your multi-role script and review whether the moves connect the turns.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Signal before you take a turn ("Could I just come in?") and hand over by name ("What do you think, Priya?") to draw others in.
  • Build explicitly on others' ideas — reference the previous speaker before you extend their point.
  • Paraphrase to check understanding ("So if I understand you correctly…") — it's attentive, precise, and buys thinking time.
  • Disagree with the structure acknowledge → soften → challenge, and steer back to common ground when things stall.
  • Summarise and steer ("So where does that leave us?", "Shall we move on?") to keep the discussion moving toward a decision.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Waiting for a gap that never comes: lively discussions rarely leave neat pauses. Use a coming-in phrase to claim your turn.
  • Interrupting vs coming in: talking over someone reads as rude; signalling and pausing a beat reads as engaged.
  • Disconnected contributions: delivering your point with no link to what came before makes the discussion scatter. Build on the last speaker.
  • Blunt disagreement: "No, you're wrong" sounds aggressive in English even when you don't mean it to. Acknowledge and soften first.
  • Nodding along when confused: pretending to follow leads to off-target contributions. Ask for clarification — it's a strength.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Turn-taking has three moves: coming in politely ("Could I just come in?"), holding the floor ("If I could just finish…"), and handing over ("What do you think, X?").
  • Building on others' ideas ("Building on what you said…", "To add to that…") weaves the discussion together and marks you as collaborative.
  • Clarifying and paraphrasing ("So if I understand you correctly…") keep the discussion accurate and show active listening — asking is a strength, not a gap.
  • Constructive disagreement follows acknowledge → soften → challenge, and steers to common ground; blunt contradiction closes minds.
  • Steering and summarising ("So where does that leave us?", "Shall we move on?", "To sum up…") keep the discussion on track and moving to a decision.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've moved beyond stating opinions to managing the conversation those opinions live in — claiming and yielding turns gracefully, connecting your ideas to others', repairing misunderstandings, disagreeing without friction, and guiding a group toward a conclusion. These interactional skills are what let a strong thinker become a strong participant, in a seminar room or a meeting or anywhere ideas are worked out together. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Won't all these phrases sound formulaic if I use them?

At first they may feel scripted — that's the rehearsal stage of any skill. But these are the exact phrases fluent speakers use constantly; you simply haven't noticed because they're so natural in the flow. As you use them, you'll start varying and shortening them ("Building on that…", "Just to add…") and they'll blend into your own voice. Formulaic-but-used beats fluent-but-silent every time.

How do I contribute in a fast discussion where everyone talks over each other?

Two tactics. First, use a coming-in phrase with a little volume and a hand gesture — "Sorry, could I just come in here?" — which most people will yield to. Second, get in early, right as someone finishes a thought, rather than waiting for a perfect silent gap that never arrives. And if you're consistently talked over, handing over by name ("I'd love to hear from someone who hasn't spoken") both helps others and models the courtesy back to the group.

Is it my job to steer and summarise if I'm not the chair?

Not your job — but very much your opportunity. Any participant can offer a summary ("So it sounds like we agree on X but not Y") or a gentle refocus ("Could we come back to the main question?"), and doing so is genuinely valued: it helps the whole group and marks you as a mature, aware discussant. Just do it as an offer, not a command — "Shall we move on?" rather than "We're moving on."
