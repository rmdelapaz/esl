📄 Lesson 6.3: Professional & Formal Writing — Reports, Emails & Applications

In professional life, your writing often arrives before you do. A crisp email, a clear report, a confident cover letter — these shape how competent, considerate, and credible you seem to people you may never meet. At B2 you learned to write a basic formal email; at C1 the standard rises. Now the goal is prose that is not merely correct but effective: purpose-first, register-perfect, and built entirely around the reader's needs. This lesson gives you the genres, the moves, and the phrase set to write like a professional.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write a professional email that leads with its purpose, makes polite-but-direct requests, and closes with the right register
  • Structure a short report with headings, an objective tone, and a clear move from findings to recommendations
  • Draft a cover letter / statement of interest that matches you to a role — confident without being boastful
  • Apply the shared principles of professional writing: clarity, concision, appropriate register, and reader-focus

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Write one real professional text — a purpose-first email, a short report section, or a cover-letter paragraph — and read it aloud to test its tone.

The Principles Behind It All

Before the genres, the shared foundations. Every effective professional text — email, report, or application — rests on four principles. Master these and the individual formats become variations on a theme rather than separate rules to memorise.

PrincipleWhat it meansIn practice
Claritythe reader grasps your meaning on the first passplain words, one idea per sentence, no ambiguity
Concisionevery word earns its place; the reader's time is respectedcut throat-clearing, redundancy, and filler
Registerthe tone matches the reader, purpose, and relationshipformal for a stranger or superior; warmer with a known colleague
Reader-focusthe text is organised around what the reader needs to know and dopurpose first; the request or action made easy to find

Notice that all four point the same way: away from the writer's convenience and toward the reader's experience. Professional writing is a service. The most common failing at this level is not grammar but self-centred structure — burying the point in paragraph three because that is the order the writer thought of things.

🧠 Mindset

You may worry that formal writing means sounding stiff, distant, or old-fashioned — piling on long words to seem serious. The opposite is true. Modern professional English prizes clear, confident, human prose. The register is respectful and appropriately formal, but it should still sound like a competent person talking, not a legal disclaimer. If you can be both correct and easy to read, you are writing above your level. Aim for polish, not stiffness. 🌱

The Professional Email

The workplace email is the workhorse of professional writing, and the single most important skill is leading with your purpose. A busy reader should know, within the first two sentences, why you have written and what you need. The reliable structure:

graph LR
    A["Clear subject line"] --> B["Greeting + purpose stated up front"]
    B --> C["Detail / request (polite but direct)"]
    C --> D["Action + appropriate sign-off"]
  • Subject line — specific and informative: "Meeting rescheduled to Thursday 3pm," not "Quick question."
  • Purpose first — "I am writing to request…" / "I am following up on…" — before the background.
  • Polite but direct requests — softened, but never so buried that the ask disappears: "I would be grateful if you could send the figures by Friday."
  • Sign-off matched to formality — "Yours sincerely" (very formal, named recipient), "Kind regards" / "Best regards" (standard professional), "Best wishes" (warmer, known colleague).

📄 Model professional email

Subject: Request for updated budget figures — Q3 report

Dear Ms Okafor,

I am writing to request the updated departmental budget figures for the third quarter, which I need in order to finalise the report due on Friday.

If possible, I would be grateful if you could send the revised spreadsheet by end of day Wednesday. Please do let me know if that timeline poses any difficulty, and I will happily adjust.

Thank you very much for your help with this.

Kind regards,
Daniel Reyes

💡 Direct is not rude; vague is not polite

Learners often over-soften requests until the actual ask vanishes ("I was just wondering if perhaps it might possibly be convenient…"). A professional reader prefers a clear, courteously framed request. "Could you please confirm the date by Thursday?" is both polite and direct. Politeness lives in the framing (please, would you, I'd be grateful); clarity lives in stating exactly what you need and by when. You want both.

The Short Report

A report presents information objectively so that a reader can understand a situation and decide what to do. Unlike an essay, it is designed to be navigated, not read straight through — so it uses headings, and it moves in a clear logical order, typically from findings to recommendations. Its tone is impersonal and neutral, and its style leans on the passive and nominalisation you built in Module 3.

SectionPurposeTypical language
Introduction / Purposestates the aim and scope of the report"This report examines… / sets out to assess…"
Findingspresents the evidence neutrally, under headings"It was found that… / The data indicate…"
Conclusionsinterprets what the findings mean"These results suggest that…"
Recommendationsproposes clear, actionable next steps"It is recommended that… / The department should…"

📄 Model report section (Findings & Recommendations)

3. Findings

A survey of staff was carried out over a two-week period, with a response rate of 78%. The findings indicate a marked decline in satisfaction with the current booking system: 64% of respondents reported difficulty reserving meeting rooms, and average booking time was found to have risen by nearly half since the previous review.

4. Recommendations

In light of these findings, it is recommended that the existing system be replaced with a centralised booking platform. A phased rollout is advised, beginning with a single department, in order to minimise disruption and allow for adjustment before wider adoption.

💡 Why reports love the passive

Notice "a survey was carried out," "it was found," "it is recommended." The passive keeps the focus on the process and findings rather than on who did what, which reads as objective and impersonal — exactly the register a report needs. This is the academic-style toolkit from Lesson 3.1 applied to the workplace. Just keep the recommendations themselves crisp and specific: objective in tone does not mean vague in content.

The Cover Letter

A cover letter (or statement of interest) has one job: to persuade a reader that you are the right person for a specific role. It is not a prose version of your CV; it is an argument — a small, targeted case that connects your evidence to their needs. The tone is the trickiest register in this lesson: confident but not boastful, warm but professional.

ParagraphWhat it doesThe move
Openingstates the role and a crisp reason you are a strong fitname the position; one-line hook of relevance
Body (1–2)matches your experience to their specific requirementsevidence → relevance to the role
Closingexpresses enthusiasm and invites the next stepforward-looking, confident, courteous

📄 Model cover-letter paragraph (body)

"In my current role as project coordinator at Meridian Logistics, I manage a portfolio of six concurrent client accounts and reduced average delivery delays by 22% over the past year by redesigning our scheduling process. I am particularly drawn to this position because it would allow me to apply that same analytical, process-focused approach to the larger and more complex operations your team is scaling — a challenge I would welcome."

Study the balance. It states an achievement with a concrete figure ("reduced delays by 22%") — that is confident and evidence-based, not boastful, because it is specific and verifiable rather than a vague self-compliment. Then it turns immediately to their needs ("apply that same approach to… your team"), keeping the reader-focus. The formula is evidence + relevance: never a claim about yourself without a connection to what the employer wants.

⚠️ The confidence trap — both directions

Two failure modes sit either side of the right tone. Boastful: "I am an exceptional communicator and a natural leader with unrivalled talent." — empty, unverifiable, and off-putting. Over-humble: "I don't have much experience, but I hope I might possibly be suitable." — undersells and plants doubt. The confident middle states what you have done and lets the achievement speak: "I led a team of five and delivered the project two weeks ahead of schedule." Evidence is quietly confident; adjectives about yourself are not.

Professional Phrase Bank

Professional writing runs on a set of reliable formulae — established phrases that signal register and do a specific job. Bank these and you will always have the right words to open, request, refer, attach, and close.

Phrase bank — professional writing formulae

I am writing to… I am writing with reference to… Further to our conversation… Please find attached… I would be grateful if you could… I would appreciate it if… Could you please confirm… Please do not hesitate to contact me… I look forward to hearing from you. Thank you for your time and consideration. This report sets out to… It was found that… It is recommended that… In light of these findings… I am particularly drawn to this role because… Yours sincerely / Kind regards

💡 Match the opener to the situation

"With reference to…" and "Further to…" refer back to a previous message or conversation and are ideal for a follow-up. "I am writing to…" is your all-purpose first-contact opener. "Please find attached…" always accompanies a document — and remember to actually attach it. Small precision in these formulae signals a writer who knows the conventions, which quietly builds trust.

Register in Action

The same message can be pitched at very different registers. Watch how one request shifts as the relationship and formality change — and notice that the clarity of the ask never wavers, only the framing around it.

Very formal (to a senior stranger): "Dear Professor Lin, I am writing to enquire whether it might be possible to arrange a brief meeting to discuss the research proposal. I would be most grateful for any time you could spare."

Standard professional (to a colleague you know): "Hi Sara, could we find twenty minutes this week to go over the proposal? I'd really value your input before I submit it."

Too informal (wrong for either): "hey can we chat about that proposal thing sometime? cheers"

Too stiff (dated, over-formal): "I hereby humbly beseech you to grant me the honour of your esteemed attention at your earliest possible convenience."

The first two are both correct — each is tuned to its reader and relationship. The third is too casual for a professional request; the fourth is so overwrought it sounds absurd. C1 register control means landing between those extremes, and choosing which of the two good versions fits your reader. When in doubt with someone you don't know, lean toward the more formal of your correct options.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These five sentences are professional-writing formulae you can lift straight into an email, report, or application.

  • I am writing to enquire about the current status of my application.
  • I would be grateful if you could confirm the details by the end of the week.
  • Please find attached the report, along with a short summary of its key recommendations.
  • In light of these findings, it is recommended that the current system be reviewed.
  • Thank you for your time and consideration; I look forward to hearing from you.

🎯 Your Project: Write One Real Professional Text

Choose one genre and write a genuine, usable text — something you could actually send.

  • An email (roughly 80–120 words): request information, follow up on something, or make a proposal to a colleague or organisation.
  • A short report section (roughly 100–150 words): a Findings + Recommendations block on a real situation (your workplace, studies, or community).
  • A cover-letter paragraph (roughly 80–120 words): a body paragraph matching your experience to a real advertised role.
  1. (3 min) Identify your reader and your purpose in one sentence each. Decide the register.
  2. (10 min) Draft the text, leading with the purpose and using at least two phrases from the professional phrase bank.
  3. (4 min) Edit for the four principles: cut anything that isn't clear, concise, correctly pitched, or reader-focused.
  4. (3 min) — Read it aloud. Does it sound like a confident, considerate professional — neither too casual nor absurdly stiff? Adjust any sentence that trips your ear.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I identified my reader and stated my purpose up front
  • ☐ My register matched the reader and relationship
  • ☐ Any request was polite but clearly stated, with a deadline where relevant
  • ☐ I used at least two professional phrase-bank formulae correctly
  • ☐ I cut filler so every sentence earns its place
  • ☐ I read it aloud and it sounded confident, clear, and appropriately formal

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Lead with your purpose. Tell the reader why you've written within the first two sentences — never bury the point.
  • Make requests polite and direct: frame courteously, but state exactly what you need and by when.
  • In reports, use headings, an objective tone, and the passive to move cleanly from findings to recommendations.
  • In cover letters, pair every claim with evidence + relevance — a concrete achievement tied to the reader's needs.
  • Read it aloud before sending. Your ear catches stiffness, curtness, and rambling that your eye misses.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Buried purpose: making the reader hunt through three paragraphs for the actual point or request. Front-load it.
  • Over-softening: "I was just wondering if perhaps possibly…" until the request disappears. Polite framing, clear ask.
  • Register mismatch: "hey!" to a stranger, or archaic over-formality ("I humbly beseech you") to a colleague. Match the reader.
  • Boasting instead of evidencing: "I am an exceptional communicator" persuades no one; "I trained twelve new staff" does.
  • Vague subject lines & missing attachments: "Quick question" tells the reader nothing; "Please find attached" with nothing attached undermines trust.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How formal should a work email really be these days?

It depends on the reader and the culture. A first email to a stranger, a client, or a senior person should be fully formal ("Dear…," "Kind regards"). With colleagues you know, a warmer register is normal and expected ("Hi…," "Best,"). The safe default when unsure is to start more formal and relax as the relationship — and their replies — signal it's welcome. Mirroring the other person's register is a reliable guide.

Is the passive voice always right for reports?

It's characteristic, not compulsory. The passive suits neutral findings ("a survey was conducted," "it was found") because it keeps the focus off individuals. But an over-passive report becomes heavy and evasive. Use the active for clear recommendations and actions ("The department should adopt…") — the mix reads as both objective and decisive, just as you learned to balance nouns and verbs in Lesson 3.1.

Won't using set phrases make my writing sound generic?

The formulae ("Please find attached," "I would be grateful if…") are conventions, like a handshake — using them signals competence, not laziness. What makes writing generic is empty content: vague requests, filler openings, self-praise with no evidence. Keep the conventional frame, and make the substance specific and reader-focused. That combination reads as polished and professional.
