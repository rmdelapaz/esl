✂️ Lesson 8.2: Precision & Concision — Editing Your English

"I didn't have time to write a short letter, so I wrote a long one." The joke lands because everyone knows the truth in it: concise writing is harder than long-winded writing, and it almost never comes out right on the first try. At C1 you can already produce fluent, correct English. The next craft is editing it — going back over your own words to cut the padding, drop the empty intensifiers, and let strong verbs carry the weight, so that what you say is clearer and lands harder. This is a process skill, a habit of revision, and it is one of the most valuable you'll ever build.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Cut redundancy and wordiness — replace bloated phrases (in order to → to, due to the fact that → because) with lean ones
  • Remove empty intensifiers and qualifiers (very, really, quite, actually) where they weaken rather than strengthen
  • Prefer strong verbs over noun-plus-weak-verb strings (make a decision → decide) — the reverse move to nominalisation
  • Adopt the redraft mindset: write freely, then edit deliberately — "write, then cut ten percent"

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Take a paragraph of your own writing and edit it down by about 25% without losing any meaning.

Why Edit at All?

First drafts are almost always too long. When we write — and even more when we speak — we reach for the first phrasing that comes, and the first phrasing is padded: we hedge, we repeat, we spell out what the reader already grasps. That's normal and fine. The mark of an advanced writer is not producing a perfect first draft but knowing how to trim the draft you have. Editing for concision is a distinct craft, separate from choosing the right word for its shade of meaning — here the target is clarity, achieved by removing what doesn't earn its place.

See what a single editing pass does:

First draft (39 words): "Due to the fact that we did not have a sufficient amount of time, we made the decision to postpone the meeting until a later date in order to be able to prepare more fully."

Edited (17 words): "Because we lacked time, we decided to postpone the meeting so we could prepare more fully."

More than half the words are gone, and not one idea is lost. In fact the edited version is clearer: the reader gets the point faster and with less effort. Concision is a courtesy to your reader and a signal of control. That's the promise of this lesson — say the same thing in fewer, stronger words.

🧠 Mindset

Editing your own writing can feel like an admission that the first attempt wasn't good enough. Reframe it: the first draft's job is to exist; the second draft's job is to shine. Every professional writer redrafts, often many times, and cutting your own words is a sign of skill, not failure. It's also strangely satisfying — few things feel as clean as deleting a phrase and watching the sentence get sharper. Learn to enjoy the cut. 🌱

Cutting Redundancy & Wordiness

English is full of stock phrases that use several words to do the work of one. They feel formal and safe, so they slip into writing unnoticed — but they add bulk without adding meaning. The fastest win in any edit is to swap these bloated phrases for their lean equivalents.

Wordy (cut this)Concise (use this)
in order toto
due to the fact that / owing to the fact thatbecause / since
at this point in time / at the present timenow / currently
in the event thatif
a large number of / a great deal ofmany / much
in spite of the fact thatalthough
has the ability tocan

A related trap is the redundant pair — two words that mean the same thing, where one is doing all the work: completely eliminate (elimination is already complete → eliminate), end result (→ result), free gift (→ gift), past history (→ history), advance planning (→ planning), each and every (→ each or every). The extra word doesn't intensify; it just repeats.

⚠️ Redundancy in slow motion — a before → after

Before (wordy, 24 words): "In order to completely eliminate the problem, we will, in the near future, make a number of changes to each and every process."

After (concise, 13 words): "To eliminate the problem, we will soon change every process."

Four wordy phrases fell away — in order to → to, completely eliminate → eliminate, in the near future → soon, each and every → every — and the sentence lost nothing but weight.

Removing Empty Intensifiers

We reach for very, really, quite, so, actually, just, basically to add force — but they usually drain it. An intensifier propped in front of a weak word is a confession that the word isn't strong enough. "Very tired" is limp; "exhausted" is vivid. "Really important" pleads; "crucial" states. The concise move is often to delete the intensifier and upgrade the word.

Weak: intensifier + adjectiveStrong: one precise word
very tiredexhausted
very big / really largehuge / vast
really importantcrucial / vital
very goodexcellent / superb
very badterrible / dire
very surprisedastonished

Some qualifiers add nothing at all and can simply go. Actually, basically, really, just, quite, in a sense often survive from speech into writing as filler. Read the sentence with the word removed: if the meaning is unchanged, the word was dead weight. "It's basically just a really simple idea, actually" says no more than "It's a simple idea" — and the shorter version sounds far more confident.

💡 Not every intensifier is empty

Concision is a tool, not a ban. Sometimes an intensifier earns its place — for genuine emphasis ("I'm absolutely certain") or for a precise degree ("slightly undercooked", "remarkably calm"). The target is the reflexive, meaningless intensifier that props up a weak word, not the deliberate one that adds real force or precision. Cut the crutches; keep the choices.

Strong Verbs Over Weak Nouns

One of the biggest sources of wordiness is turning a perfectly good verb into a noun and then propping it up with an empty "light" verb. Decide becomes make a decision; conclude becomes reach a conclusion; discuss becomes have a discussion about. The verb has been buried inside a noun, and a hollow verb (make, reach, have, give, carry out) is doing the grammar. For concision and punch, dig the verb back out.

Noun + weak verb (wordy)Strong verb (concise)
make a decisiondecide
reach a conclusionconclude
carry out an investigation ofinvestigate
give consideration toconsider
have a discussion aboutdiscuss
provide assistance tohelp / assist
make an improvement toimprove
take into considerationconsider

💡 This is the reverse of nominalisation — and that's the point

In Lesson 3.1 you learned nominalisation: turning verbs into nouns for a dense, formal, academic register (decide → the decision). Here you're moving the other way, un-nominalising for clarity and force. Neither is "correct" in the abstract — match the tool to the goal. Reach for nominalisation when you want formal, impersonal density (a research report, a legal clause); reach for the strong verb when you want clear, direct, energetic prose (an email, a summary, a persuasive argument). Concision serves clarity; nominalisation serves formal abstraction. An advanced writer chooses knowingly between them.

Noun-heavy: "The committee will carry out a review of the proposal and make a recommendation."

Verb-driven: "The committee will review the proposal and recommend."

Eleven words become eight, and the sentence gains momentum.

Double Negatives & Tangled Clauses

Concision isn't only about word count — it's about the reader's effort. Two constructions quietly force a reader to work harder than they should: buried double negatives and long tangled clauses. Untangling them is a core editing move.

Double negatives (of the "not un-" kind) make the reader flip two switches to reach a simple meaning. "It is not uncommon" means "it is common"; "not without merit" means "has some merit." These have their place for deliberate understatement, but as a default they slow the reader down for no gain.

Tangled (double negative): "It is not impossible that the results were not entirely inaccurate."

Clear: "The results may be roughly accurate."

Tangled clauses pile subordinate ideas on top of each other until the main point is lost. The cure is often to break one long sentence into two, or to move the main idea to the front. If you can't say a sentence aloud in one breath and still remember how it started, it's a candidate for splitting.

Overloaded (one 34-word sentence): "The report, which was written by the team that had been assembled in order to look into the matter that had been raised at the previous meeting, was well received."

Untangled (two clean sentences): "A team was assembled to look into the matter raised at the last meeting. Its report was well received."

The Redraft Mindset

All of the above depends on one habit: treating writing as a two-stage process. Stage one, you draft — get the ideas down without judging the wording. Stage two, you edit — go back cold and cut. Trying to do both at once is what makes writing painful and slow; separating them is what makes it good. A famous rule of thumb captures it: "Write, then cut ten percent." Whatever length you produce, you can almost always lose a tenth of it and improve the whole.

Editing is not about deleting your ideas — it's about deleting everything that gets between your ideas and your reader.

A practical editing routine for any piece — an email, an essay, a report:

Phrase bank — the editor's checklist (say these to yourself)

"Can I say this in fewer words?" "Which phrase is padding?" "Is this intensifier earning its place?" "Is there a verb hiding in that noun?" "Would a full stop be clearer than this comma?" "What can I cut and lose nothing?" "Does the reader already know this?" "Read it aloud — where do I run out of breath?"

Reading your work aloud is the single best editing test. Padding, tangles, and empty intensifiers reveal themselves the moment you have to speak them — you'll stumble exactly where the reader would.

In the Editing Room

Two colleagues tighten a draft together. Notice how each cut is named — redundancy, intensifier, buried verb, tangle — and how the sentence sharpens with every pass.

Editor: Read me your opening line as it stands.

Writer: "Due to the fact that we are currently at a point where costs are very high, we have made the decision to carry out a review of our spending."

Editor: Right. First, "due to the fact that" is just "because". And "at a point where" — cut it.

Writer: So: "Because costs are very high, we have made the decision to carry out a review of our spending."

Editor: Better. Now, "very high" — is there one word for that? And there are two buried verbs in there.

Writer: "Very high" could be "soaring". And "made the decision to" is just "decided", "carry out a review of" is "review".

Editor: Say the whole thing now.

Writer: "Because costs are soaring, we have decided to review our spending." Twelve words instead of twenty-four — and it actually hits harder.

Editor: That's the craft. You didn't lose a single idea; you lost the padding around them.

Every cut was deliberate and named. That's what editing is — not vague "tidying", but a series of specific, learnable moves you can run down like a checklist.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Tighten the wordy phrase

Objective: Replace each wordy phrase with its concise equivalent — one word each unless noted.

  1. in order to →
  2. due to the fact that →
  3. at this point in time →
  4. completely eliminate →
  5. make a decision →
  6. has the ability to →
  7. very important → (one strong adjective)
  8. reach a conclusion →
✅ Answer

1. to · 2. because · 3. now · 4. eliminate · 5. decide · 6. can · 7. crucial (or vital / essential) · 8. conclude

🏋️ Exercise 2: Cut the redundant word

Objective: Each phrase has one unnecessary word. Type the phrase without it (keep the word that carries the meaning).

  1. free gift →
  2. end result →
  3. past history →
  4. advance planning →
✅ Answer

1. gift (a gift is already free) · 2. result (a result is already the end) · 3. history (history is already past) · 4. planning (planning is already in advance)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Choose the cleaner sentence

Objective: For each pair, decide which version is clearer and more concise — and be ready to say why.

  1. (a) "It is not uncommon for delays to occur in the event that staff are absent." vs (b) "Delays are common when staff are absent."
  2. (a) "We will give consideration to your proposal." vs (b) "We will consider your proposal."
  3. (a) "Basically, it's just a really simple change, actually." vs (b) "It's a simple change."
✅ Answer

In every pair, (b) is cleaner. 1(b) removes a double negative ("not uncommon" → "common") and "in the event that" → "when". 2(b) digs the verb out of "give consideration to" → "consider". 3(b) deletes three empty qualifiers ("basically", "just", "really", "actually") that add nothing. Same meaning, far more confidence.

🎯 Your Project: Cut It by 25%

The editor's core workout: take a chunk of your own writing and edit it down by roughly a quarter, losing no meaning at all.

  1. (2 min) Find a paragraph you wrote recently — an email, an essay section, a report, a forum post — of about 120–180 words. Count the words and note the target: cut about 25% (so ~150 words → ~112).
  2. (5 min) Run the checklist: swap wordy phrases for lean ones (in order to → to), cut redundant pairs, delete empty intensifiers and upgrade weak adjectives, and dig verbs out of nouns (make a decision → decide).
  3. (3 min) Untangle any overloaded sentence — split it in two, or front the main idea. Read the whole thing aloud and cut wherever you stumble.
  4. (2 min) Count again. Did you hit ~25%? Reread the trimmed version against the original: confirm that every idea survived. If something got lost, restore just enough to carry it.
  5. (3 min) Write one line reflecting: which single cut improved the paragraph most, and which type of padding you use most often?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I cut the paragraph by roughly 25% (I counted before and after)
  • ☐ I replaced at least three wordy phrases with concise ones
  • ☐ I removed at least two empty intensifiers or qualifiers
  • ☐ I turned at least one noun-plus-weak-verb into a strong verb
  • ☐ I untangled or split at least one overloaded sentence
  • ☐ I confirmed no meaning was lost, reading the edited version aloud

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Draft first, edit second — get the ideas down, then go back cold to cut. Never try to do both at once.
  • Swap wordy stock phrases for lean ones on sight (due to the fact that → because, in order to → to).
  • Delete a weak intensifier and upgrade the word (very tired → exhausted) rather than propping it up.
  • Dig the verb out of the noun when you want clarity and force (make a decision → decide).
  • Read it aloud and apply "cut ten percent" — you'll hear the padding and the tangles instantly.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Cutting so hard you lose meaning: concision serves clarity, not word-count for its own sake. If a cut removes an idea, restore it.
  • Treating nominalisation as an error: it isn't — it's the right tool for formal, dense, impersonal genres. Match the tool to the goal.
  • Editing while drafting: stopping to polish every sentence as you write kills flow and rarely produces the best cut anyway.
  • Keeping every intensifier: "very, really, quite, just, basically, actually" stacked together sap authority. Question each one.
  • Marathon sentences: if you can't say it in one breath, split it. Clarity beats a clever, tangled structure every time.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Editing is a craft distinct from word choice: the target is clarity, reached by removing what doesn't earn its place.
  • Cut redundancy: swap wordy phrases (due to the fact that → because, in order to → to) and drop redundant pairs (completely eliminate → eliminate).
  • Remove empty intensifiers (very, really, quite, actually) — delete them and upgrade the word (very tired → exhausted).
  • Prefer strong verbs over noun-plus-weak-verb (make a decision → decide) — the reverse of nominalisation, used when clarity beats formality.
  • Match the tool to the goal: concision for clarity and directness; nominalisation for formal, impersonal density. And write, then cut ten percent.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've added the editor's eye to the writer's hand. You can now look at your own English and see the padding — the bloated phrases, the propped-up adjectives, the verbs buried in nouns, the tangled clauses — and cut it away without losing a single idea. Just as importantly, you know that concision is a choice, weighed against formality and register, not a blanket rule. That judgement is exactly the polish that separates competent writing from writing that lands. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't shorter always better?

No. Concision serves clarity, and clarity is the real goal. Sometimes a longer phrasing is clearer, gentler, or more precise, and sometimes a formal, nominalised style is exactly right for the genre. The skill is cutting what doesn't earn its place — not slashing word count for its own sake.

This contradicts Lesson 3.1 on nominalisation — which one is right?

Both — they're tools for different jobs. Nominalisation gives you formal, dense, impersonal prose (reports, legal writing, academic abstraction). Digging out strong verbs gives you clear, direct, energetic prose (emails, summaries, persuasion). An advanced writer owns both dials and turns the right one for the audience and purpose. That conscious choice is the C1 skill.

How do I edit my own writing when I can't see its faults?

Two reliable tricks: leave a gap (edit after a break, even ten minutes, so you read it fresher and colder), and read it aloud. Speaking the sentences exposes padding and tangles instantly — you'll stumble exactly where a reader would. Running a fixed checklist ("any wordy phrase? any empty intensifier? any buried verb?") also catches faults you'd otherwise skim past.
