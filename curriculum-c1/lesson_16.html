🔍 Lesson 6.1: Critical Reading — Evaluating Arguments & Bias

At B2, comprehension meant understanding what a text says. At C1, reading means interrogating what a text is doing: what the writer wants you to believe, how hard they are working to persuade you, and whether the case actually holds up. A critical reader does not swallow prose whole — they weigh it. In this lesson you'll build the toolkit for that weighing: telling fact from opinion, tracing an argument's skeleton, hearing bias in the word choice, and reading the meaning that lives between the lines.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish fact from opinion and separate a writer's claim from the evidence that supports it
  • Identify an author's purpose, stance and tone, and recognise bias and loaded, emotive language
  • Map an argument's structure (claim → evidence → warrant) and name its common weaknesses — unsupported generalisation, false cause, cherry-picking
  • Read between the lines — draw inferences and detect implication — and talk about texts with precise evaluative verbs (the author asserts / concedes / downplays)

⏱️ Estimated Time: 60–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Read a short opinion article of your choice and write a brief critical evaluation — its central claim, the quality of its evidence, any bias, and your verdict.

What Critical Reading Is

Critical reading is not fault-finding, and it is not cynicism. It is the disciplined habit of reading a text as an argument made by a person with a purpose — and then asking whether that argument earns your agreement. Where a passive reader absorbs, a critical reader evaluates: What exactly is being claimed? On what grounds? Whose interests does this serve? What is left unsaid?

This matters far beyond the classroom. The opinion columns, product reviews, policy briefs, and social-media threads you read every day are all trying to move you toward a conclusion. Reading them critically is how you keep your own judgement rather than borrowing theirs.

🧠 Mindset

Being critical is an act of respect, not hostility. Taking a text seriously enough to test it — rather than agreeing or dismissing on a first impression — is precisely how thoughtful readers behave. You will find that your first, gut reaction to a piece is often about tone rather than substance; the skill you're building here is the pause between reacting and judging. That pause is the whole of critical reading, and it gets faster with practice. 🌱

Fact, Opinion, Claim & Evidence

The first cut a critical reader makes is between what can be verified and what is being asserted. A fact is a statement that can, in principle, be checked against evidence and found true or false. An opinion is a judgement, preference, or interpretation — it can be reasonable or unreasonable, but it is not simply "true." The trap is that skilled writers dress opinions in the grammar of fact.

StatementFact or opinion?Why
"The city's population grew by 12% between 2010 and 2020."FactVerifiable against census data
"The city has grown far too quickly."Opinion"too quickly" is a judgement, not a measurement
"Remote work reduces commuting time."Fact (checkable)Can be measured and confirmed
"Remote work is destroying company culture."OpinionAn interpretation stated as if self-evident

Once you can spot an opinion, the next move is to separate the writer's claim (the point they want you to accept) from the evidence (the facts, examples, data, or testimony offered to support it). A claim without evidence is merely an assertion; evidence without a clear claim is just information. Good arguments join the two — and part of critical reading is checking whether the join actually holds.

💡 The three questions that never fail

Facing any persuasive paragraph, ask: (1) What is the claim? — the sentence the writer would defend if challenged. (2) What is the evidence? — what they offer to back it. (3) Does the evidence actually support this claim? — or a smaller, different, or unrelated one? The third question is where most weak arguments quietly fall apart.

Purpose, Stance & Tone

Behind every text is a writer who wants something. Purpose is the goal — to inform, persuade, entertain, warn, sell, provoke, or reassure. Stance is the writer's position on the issue — for, against, sceptical, enthusiastic, ambivalent. Tone is the emotional colouring of the language — measured, indignant, ironic, alarmed, dismissive. Reading these three well tells you how to weight everything else.

DimensionQuestion to askClues in the text
PurposeWhy was this written? What does the writer want me to do or think?Genre, headline, call to action, where it was published
StanceWhere does the writer stand on the issue?Which side gets more space; which is dismissed; the conclusion
ToneWhat is the writer's attitude, and how do they feel about it?Adjectives, adverbs, punctuation, irony, rhetorical questions

Tone is often carried by tiny signals. A writer who calls a policy "so-called reform" has told you their stance in two words. Scare quotes, an incredulous rhetorical question, a sarcastic aside — these leak attitude even when the sentence pretends to be neutral. Learning to notice them is half the battle.

💡 Language for describing what a writer does

C1 readers need precise verbs to talk about texts, not just react to them. A writer may assert (state firmly, without proof), argue / contend (make a reasoned case), imply / suggest (hint without stating), concede / acknowledge (admit a point against their own side), downplay / dismiss (minimise something inconvenient), exaggerate / overstate (inflate for effect), or refute / rebut (argue against). Naming the move sharpens the reading.

Bias & Loaded Language

Bias is a systematic leaning that shapes what a writer includes, omits, and emphasises. It is not always dishonest — everyone writes from somewhere — but an undeclared bias can quietly steer you. The most common vehicle is loaded or emotive language: word choices that carry a judgement smuggled inside them.

Neutral wordingLoaded / emotive wordingWhat the loading does
a group of protestersa mob of protesterscasts them as violent and irrational
the government's spending planthe government's reckless spending spreecondemns before any argument is made
the company reduced its workforcethe company slashed jobsdramatises and assigns blame
people who disagreeso-called experts who disagreeundermines credibility by innuendo

Watch, too, for what is missing. Bias hides not only in the words chosen but in the facts left out, the counter-argument never mentioned, the source conveniently unnamed. Ask whose voice is absent and whose interests the framing serves. A piece that presents only one side, however elegantly, is arguing — not reporting.

⚠️ Emotive language is a signal, not a verdict

Spotting loaded language tells you the writer is trying to move you — it does not by itself prove them wrong. A passionate, clearly biased piece can still be correct; a coolly neutral one can still be misleading. Use tone as a flag that says "check the evidence more carefully here," not as a reason to accept or reject the claim outright. The point is to separate how something is said from whether it is true.

Argument Structure & Its Weaknesses

Most arguments, stripped down, share a skeleton: a claim (the conclusion), the evidence (the grounds), and a usually-unstated warrant — the assumption that links the evidence to the claim. Making the warrant visible is a powerful critical move, because a shaky warrant sinks an argument even when the facts are true.

Claim: "The new cycle lanes should be removed."

Evidence: "Traffic congestion has risen since they were installed."

Warrant (unstated): "The cycle lanes are the cause of the congestion, and reducing congestion outweighs any benefit of the lanes."

The evidence may be perfectly accurate — yet the whole case rests on that hidden warrant, which is exactly where it can be challenged.

graph LR
    A["Evidence (the grounds / data)"] -->|"warrant (linking assumption)"| B["Claim (the conclusion)"]
    B -->|"critical reader asks"| C["Is the warrant sound / is the evidence enough"]

Once you can see the parts, you can name the classic weaknesses that recur again and again:

WeaknessWhat it isExample
Unsupported generalisationa sweeping claim from little or no evidence"Young people today simply won't work hard."
False causeassuming that because B followed A, A caused B"Sales fell after the rebrand, so the rebrand ruined sales."
Cherry-pickingciting only the data that fits, ignoring the restquoting the one good year and skipping five bad ones
False dilemmapresenting two options as if no others exist"Either we cut taxes or the economy collapses."
Appeal to emotionsubstituting feeling for evidence"Think of the children" in place of an argument

Finally, remember that arguments also work by implication — meaning the writer wants you to infer without committing to in print. Reading between the lines means drawing the reasonable conclusion the text points toward. "The minister was, of course, unavailable for comment" states a fact; it implies evasion. Naming that gap between what is said and what is meant is advanced reading at its sharpest.

A Text Under the Microscope

Let's put the whole toolkit to work on a short opinion excerpt. Read it once for sense, then read it again with the questions from this lesson in hand.

📰 Sample opinion excerpt

"Every sensible person can see that these so-called 'smart' parking meters are a disaster. Since they were installed downtown, shopkeepers have complained bitterly, and footfall has plainly collapsed. The council, predictably, refuses to admit its blunder. If we truly cared about local business, we would rip the machines out tomorrow. The choice could not be clearer: save our high street, or let the bureaucrats finish killing it."

🔎 Guided questions — try these before reading the analysis

  • What is the writer's central claim, and where is it stated most directly?
  • Which phrases are loaded or emotive? What judgement does each smuggle in?
  • What evidence is actually offered — and how strong is it?
  • Where does the writer commit a false cause or a false dilemma?
  • What is the tone, and how does it try to position anyone who disagrees?

✅ A critical reading of the excerpt

Claim: the parking meters should be removed. Loaded language does most of the work: "so-called 'smart'" and "disaster" condemn before any case is made; "predictably" and "bureaucrats" paint the council as arrogant and out-of-touch. Evidence is thin — shopkeepers "complained" (testimony, not data) and footfall "plainly collapsed" (asserted, not measured). The writer commits a false cause (footfall fell after the meters, so the meters caused it — ignoring the economy, weather, online shopping) and closes on a stark false dilemma ("save our high street, or let the bureaucrats finish killing it"), as if no middle option exists. The tone is indignant and recruits the reader with "every sensible person," implying that disagreement is foolish. The claim might be right — but this passage argues by feeling, not by proof.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These five sentences are the critical-reading questions you can ask of any text — bank them and use them as you read.

  • What exactly is the writer claiming, and what evidence do they offer for it?
  • Is this a statement of fact that could be checked, or an opinion dressed up as one?
  • The author concedes one small point but downplays the evidence against their case.
  • Notice the loaded language here — it tells us how the writer wants us to feel.
  • The evidence suggests a correlation, but the writer treats it as proof of cause.

🎯 Your Project: A Critical Evaluation

Read a short opinion article of your choice — a newspaper column, a blog post, an editorial, a product or film review (roughly 400–800 words) — and write a brief critical evaluation of it (150–250 words).

  1. (10 min) Read the article twice: once for sense, once with a pen. Underline the central claim and mark each piece of evidence.
  2. (5 min) Note the writer's purpose, stance and tone, and circle any loaded or emotive language.
  3. (5 min) Test the argument: is the evidence strong and relevant? Do you spot a false cause, cherry-picking, generalisation, or false dilemma? What has been left out?
  4. (10 min) Write your evaluation in four short moves: (a) state the claim, (b) assess the evidence, (c) identify any bias or weakness, using precise verbs (the author asserts / concedes / downplays), and (d) give your verdict — is the case persuasive, and why?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I identified the article's central claim in one sentence
  • ☐ I distinguished the evidence from the claim and judged its strength
  • ☐ I named the writer's purpose, stance and tone
  • ☐ I pointed to at least one instance of bias, loaded language, or a specific weakness
  • ☐ I used precise evaluative verbs to describe the writer's moves
  • ☐ I gave a clear, reasoned verdict rather than just agreeing or disagreeing

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Separate the claim from the evidence first — then test whether the evidence really supports that claim.
  • Read the word choice, not just the content: loaded adjectives, scare quotes, and rhetorical questions leak the writer's stance.
  • Make the hidden warrant visible — the unstated assumption linking evidence to claim is often the weakest link.
  • Ask what is missing: the absent counter-argument, the unnamed source, the data that didn't make the cut.
  • Use precise evaluative verbs (asserts, concedes, implies, downplays, refutes) to describe — and think about — what a text is doing.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Confusing tone with truth: a passionate, biased piece can still be right; a calm one can still mislead. Loaded language is a flag to check evidence, not a verdict.
  • Treating any confident sentence as fact: "It is obvious that…" and "Everyone knows…" are opinion markers, not proof.
  • Agreeing because you already agree: we scrutinise arguments we dislike and wave through the ones we like. Apply the same tests to both.
  • Stopping at "I disagree": a critical reading names why — which claim, which weak evidence, which flawed step — not just a reaction.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Fact vs opinion: a fact can be checked; an opinion is a judgement — and skilled writers dress opinions in the grammar of fact.
  • Separate the claim (the point) from the evidence (the support), then ask whether the evidence truly supports that claim.
  • Read a text's purpose, stance and tone, and spot bias through loaded / emotive language and what is left unsaid.
  • Arguments run evidence → warrant → claim; common weaknesses are unsupported generalisation, false cause, cherry-picking, false dilemma, and appeals to emotion.
  • Read between the lines — draw inferences from implication — and describe texts with precise verbs (asserts, concedes, downplays, refutes).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've moved from reading for information to reading for evaluation — the ability to take any persuasive text apart, see its machinery, and decide for yourself whether it earns your agreement. That is the reading half of advanced literacy, and it protects your judgement in a world full of confident, well-crafted persuasion. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't it exhausting to read everything this critically?

You don't have to — and shouldn't. Critical reading is a gear you shift into when the stakes matter: a decision, a purchase, a claim that will shape what you believe. For a novel or a friend's message, read for pleasure and connection. The skill is knowing when to switch the gear on. With practice, the basic checks become almost automatic and cost you very little time.

If a writer is clearly biased, can I just dismiss what they say?

No. Bias tells you to check the evidence more carefully, not to reject the conclusion. Even a strongly one-sided piece can contain true facts and a sound point. Separate how the case is argued from whether it's correct — that separation is the whole discipline.

How is a "warrant" different from evidence?

Evidence is the material offered — the data, the example, the quotation. The warrant is the usually-unstated assumption that connects that evidence to the conclusion. "Sales fell (evidence), so the rebrand failed (claim)" relies on the warrant "the rebrand, and nothing else, caused the fall." Naming that hidden bridge is often how you locate an argument's real weak point.
