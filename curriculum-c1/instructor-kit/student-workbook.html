Precision in Time & Modality

Three lessons about saying familiar things more exactly. Place an event precisely in the future, or in a future seen from the past. Set your certainty on a dial instead of a switch. And use the subjunctive to make formal recommendations that sound measured and authoritative.

How this workbook works

Each lesson has new language, a grammar box, exercises, a model dialogue, a main task, a self-check and space for your own notes. Instructions tell you whether to read, listen, write, speak or work with a partner. Answers are not printed here; your teacher has them.

Whenever a task touches on your own life, you decide how much to share. Use your real experience, or a new identity card, or something you invent: all three are equally valid. In role-plays and debates you argue the position on the card, not your personal view, and you can always say "Pass."

Grammar box

Three vantage points. Stand at a future moment and look around.

In progress at that momentwill be + -ingThis time tomorrow I'll be flying over the Atlantic.
Completed by that momentwill have + past participleBy 2030 the company will have opened ten offices.
Duration up to that momentwill have been + -ingBy June I'll have been working here for a decade.

The time clause stays in the present: By the time you arrive, we'll have finished. Stative verbs stay simple: By July I'll have known her for ten years. The future continuous also makes an inquiry tactful: Will you be using the car tonight?

The future seen from the past

was / were going toan earlier plan (often not carried out)We were going to fly, but the strike changed everything.
woulda future seen from the past, in narrativeShe left in 2001; she would never return.
was / were about toon the point of doing somethingI was about to leave when the phone rang.
was to · was to have + ppdestiny / a formal plan · a plan that did not happenThe decision was to have far-reaching consequences. · The launch was to have taken place in March.

Formal and imminent futures

be toan official arrangement · an instruction · a conditionThe mayor is to open the library on May 12. · You are to report to reception. · If we are to meet the deadline, we need help.
be about toany moment now (no time phrase)We're about to sign the contract.
be on the verge / point of+ -ing or a noun: very closeThey're on the verge of reaching an agreement.
be due toa timetable or scheduleThe fix is due to arrive on the 20th.
be set toexpected, arranged (news style)The firm is set to double its staff.

Certainty as a Dial — Speculation & Nuance

Class C2
Read and say — New words: weighing the evidence
a hypothesisa huncha leada red herringto rule outto point toconclusivecircumstantialon the evidencein all likelihoodto jump to conclusionsa verdict

a red herring = a clue that looks important but leads you away from the real explanation · circumstantial = suggesting something without proving it

Grammar box

The certainty dial. Choose exactly how much you are willing to commit to.

Near-certainmust · is bound to (future)The lights are off; they must have gone to bed. · Prices are bound to rise.
Expectedshould / ought toThey left at noon, so they should be home by now.
Quite likelymay / might / could wellGiven the polls, the incumbent may well lose.
Possiblemay / might / couldPrices might fall next quarter.
A slim chancemight just · could conceivablyThis might just work. · It could conceivably be the software.
Ruled outcan't · can't possiblyHe can't be serious. · They can't possibly have known.

False twins. may well = it's quite likely (probability): The train may well be late. · may / might as well = there's no reason not to (a decision): The train's late, so we may as well get a coffee.

The hidden jobs of will and would

will · present deductionThere's the doorbell; that'll be the courier.
won't · refusal, persistent failureHe simply won't listen. · The car won't start.
will · typical behaviorShe'll sit at that window for hours. · She will keep interrupting! (stressed)
would · past habit (actions only)Every summer we'd drive to the coast.
wouldn't · past refusalI asked her to stay, but she wouldn't. · The engine wouldn't start.
would · criticism"I didn't tell him." "You wouldn't, would you." · You would say that!
would · politenessI'd suggest a short break. · I wouldn't worry too much.

Speculating about now and about the past

Now, in progressmodal + be + -ingHe must be joking. · They might be waiting outside.
The pastmodal + have + ppThey must have left. · It can't have been easy.
The past, ongoingmodal + have been + -ingShe must have been waiting for hours.

It Is Essential That… — The Subjunctive & Unreal Uses

Class C3
Read and say — New words: policies and recommendations
a memoa policya proposala requirementa rationaleto implementto comply withto waive (a fee)to pilotwith immediate effectin the interimon a trial basis

Fixed phrases (formal; use sparingly):

be that as it may (= even so)suffice it to say (= I'll say only this)if need be (= if necessary)come what may (= whatever happens)far be it from me to… (= I wouldn't presume to…)
Grammar box

The mandative subjunctive: after words of demand, recommendation and importance, use the base form of the verb for every person: no -s, no do, no to.

Verbsrecommend, insist, propose, request, require, demand, urge, suggest, askThe board demanded that he resign.
AdjectivesIt is essential / vital / imperative / crucial / important / advisable that…It is vital that the report reach them by Friday.
Nounsrecommendation, proposal, requirement, demand, request, insistencea requirement that each applicant submit references
Negativenot + base formWe ask that he not resign before the vote.
Passivebe + past participleWe propose that the rule be abolished.
bealways beIt's vital that they be ready.

Three registers. I suggest that she be promoted (most formal; standard in American English) · …that she should be promoted (typically British, a little softer) · …that she is promoted (informal). Beware the lookalike: The evidence suggests that he is guilty (= shows: a fact) · She insisted that she was innocent (= asserted a fact).

Unreal forms

if … wereIf I were you, I'd accept. · If it were possible, we would.
Were + subject (+ to)Were I in charge, things would change. · Were we to delay, we'd lose the client.
wish … wereI wish it were simpler.
suppose / supposingSuppose she were to resign; what then? · Supposing the budget was / were cut?
it's (high) timeIt's high time we reviewed this policy. (past simple: it's overdue)
would ratherI'd rather you didn't mention this just yet.

As if / as though: the tense signals how real the comparison is.

+ presentpossibly trueHe talks as if he knows what he's doing.
+ past / werecontrary to fact (now)He talks as if he owned the place. · She treats me as though I were a stranger.
+ past perfectcontrary to fact (earlier)He looked as if he had seen a ghost.

The mechanism: front a negative or restrictive adverbial → switch to question word order (auxiliary + subject) → no auxiliary? Borrow do / does / did, and the main verb goes bare.

Conditional inversion: drop if. Only three auxiliaries: had, were, should. Negatives stay uncontracted.

If I had known…Had I known…

Focus and cohesion frames

It was… that / who…It wasn't until… that…What really matters is…What happened was (that)…All I'm saying is…The reason… is that…The thing that… is…Even more… is…It's a pity (that)…
Grammar box
It-cleftIt + be + focus + that / who…It was her attitude that impressed me. (spotlight up front; often "…not something else")
Corrective it-cleftIt was X, not Y, that…It was the timing, not the price, that worried us.
Time frameIt wasn't until… that…It wasn't until later that I realized.
What-cleftWhat + clause + be + focusWhat impressed me was her attitude. (focus saved for the end)
Other framesAll / The thing / The reason / The place / The way / The one who… + beAll I want is a quiet evening. · The reason I left is that I was bored.

Extrapositionlight it first, heavy clause lastThat you missed it is a pity. → It's a pity (that) you missed it.

Agreement: What I need is a break. · What I need are some answers. · What surprised us was the silence (past). · Formal: The reason is that…, not The reason is because…

Fronting ≠ inversion. That book, I have never read. ✅ · That book have I never read. ❌ (Inversion needs a negative or restrictive trigger.)

Grammar box

A participle clause drops the conjunction and the repeated subject: When she realized the risk, she paused → Realizing the risk, she paused. Choose the participle by voice, not by tense.

FormMeaningExample
-ingactive · same time, reason or resultKnowing the road well, he took a shortcut. · The storm hit the coast, flattening dozens of homes.
having + past participleactive · clearly earlierHaving signed the contract, they celebrated. · Having been warned twice, the driver slowed down.
past participlepassive meaningWritten in 1920, the novel still speaks to readers. · Given more time, we could have finished.
reduced relativenoun + -ing / past participlemembers wishing to attend · any items damaged in transit
absolute clauseits own subject + participle (being often dropped)The weather being fine, we set off. · The meeting over, the staff went home. · She waited, her heart pounding.
The one-question testAfter a leading participle, ask: Who or what is doing this -ing / being this -ed? That exact answer must be the subject of the next clause.
Dangling❌ Walking into the room, the noise was overwhelming. (The noise was walking?)
Repair ①Change the main subject: Walking into the room, I found the noise overwhelming.
Repair ②Give the participle its own subject, or restore a full clause: The exam finished, the results came quickly. · As I walked into the room, …
RegisterFull participle and absolute clauses are formal and written. Fixed phrases (having said that, generally speaking) work in speech too.

What a position paper is — six parts

A position paper takes a clear stand on a debatable question and argues for it with reasons and evidence. It is not a neutral survey of views, and it is not a personal outburst. Its signature move is the counter-argument: you state the strongest objection fairly, then answer it.

Later Bells, Better Learning: Why High Schools Should Start at 9 a.m.

Across much of the country, the high-school day begins at around 7:30 a.m., a timetable designed more for adult convenience than for adolescent biology. This paper argues that high schools should start no earlier than 9 a.m. Not only would a later start protect students' health, but it would also improve the quality of their learning.

The primary reason is biological. Sleep researchers broadly agree that during adolescence the body clock shifts: teenagers begin to feel sleepy up to two hours later than they did as children, yet they still need eight to ten hours of sleep. Given this shift, a student who must wake at 6 a.m. is, in effect, being asked to function in the middle of their biological night. The consequences of chronic sleep loss are well documented: lower mood, weaker concentration and, among newly licensed drivers, a higher risk of accidents on the way to school. It is for this reason that leading pediatric organizations have recommended that no middle or high school begin before 8:30 a.m.; this paper simply goes half an hour further.

A related consideration is academic performance. Schools that have moved their start time later have generally reported better attendance and punctuality, and some studies suggest modest gains in grades, particularly in first-period classes. This is hardly surprising, since a rested brain retains new information far more effectively than an exhausted one. Were the school day aligned with students' sleep patterns, teachers would spend less time repeating material and more time extending it. Nor would the benefit be evenly spread: it is typically students with the longest commutes, often those from lower-income families, who lose the most sleep and would gain the most.

Admittedly, it might be objected that a later start would disrupt family routines. Parents who begin work early may be unable to drop their children off, bus companies would have to redesign their routes, and after-school commitments, from sports practice to part-time jobs, would finish later in the evening.

These concerns, while valid, are practical rather than fundamental, and practical problems invite practical solutions. Schools could open their doors at 7:30 a.m. for a supervised breakfast and quiet study, so that no student is left waiting outside, and several districts that have made the change have reorganized their bus schedules within a single year. Moreover, the cost of adjusting a timetable is paid once, whereas the benefits of better-rested students recur every school day.

On balance, then, the weight of evidence points in one direction. A modest change to the timetable, one that asks adults to adapt rather than asking teenagers to fight their own biology, would appear to offer a rare combination of low cost and high return. Rarely does a single decision promise to improve health, safety and learning at the same time. High schools should seize that opportunity and move the first bell to 9 a.m.
