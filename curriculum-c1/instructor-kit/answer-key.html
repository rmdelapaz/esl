Module 1 — Precision in Time & Modality: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the project briefing, the evidence-ladder verdicts and case solutions, the policy memo), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. A sentence is correct when the form is right (by the time + present, have + pp after a modal, the base form after a mandative trigger, were after inversion) and it expresses the exact meaning intended (in progress vs completed; may well vs may as well; real vs unreal as if) and it suits the situation. Be generous with legitimate variants: contractions; might / could for may in the "well" and "as well" structures; should + base as well as the bare subjunctive (typically British, fully correct); as though for as if; was after suppose / supposing and if I in informal speech (but only were after inversion); British spellings (analysed, apologise, realised). When a learner produces a correct sentence with a different structure from the target, accept it and point to the target: "Correct! Can you also say it with be to?"

1.1 · Advanced Aspect — The Future in Detail (C1)

First-day writing sample

No "answers." Score with the four-row rubric in the materials (Set B): task & register · range · accuracy · organization & cohesion, 0–3 each (max 12). Guide: 9–12 comfortable at C1 · 6–8 typical C1 entry · 5 or less: check placement privately. Give each learner a one-line private note (one strength, one focus); never a score.

Exercise 1 — The Monday stand-up (Script 1.1-A)
1were going to reopen
2will be working
3will have finished
4will have been running
5are about to sign ('re about to sign)
6is to visit
7Will … be needing

The project is behind schedule: item 1 (the April reopening was a plan that changed; it's now the end of May). Item 4 is the one to discuss: will have been running because the program is an ongoing activity measured by its duration up to the reopening. Item 7 is the tactful future continuous: the lead is asking about plans, not demanding a decision.

Exercise 2 — The most precise future form
  1. will be flying (in progress at that moment)
  2. will have completed (finished by the end of the year)
  3. will have been running (duration up to August)
  4. will be driving (in progress at six)
  5. arrive · will have checked (time clause in the present; completed before)
  6. will have known (know is stative: no continuous)
  7. Will you be presenting (a tactful inquiry about plans)
  8. will have taught (a number = a completed quantity = simple)

Wrong: 3 "will run / will have run" (loses the ongoing duration; will have run is marginal and implies it stops) · 5 "will arrive" in the time clause · 6 "will have been knowing" · 8 "will have been teaching more than three thousand students" (continuous + number). Accept: 7 "Are you presenting…?" (present continuous for an arrangement: correct, a little more direct).

Exercise 3 — The future seen from the past; formal futures
  1. We were going to sign the deal on Friday, but… (also: were to have signed, more formal)
  2. I was about to object when she cut me off. (was on the point of objecting means the same, but doesn't fit the frame)
  3. that meeting would change his life (also: was to change, more formal / literary)
  4. The bridge was to have opened in 2024. (also: was going to open / was supposed to open, less formal)
  5. The CEO is to visit the plant on Friday.
  6. The two companies are on the verge of reaching an agreement. (also: on the point of reaching / on the brink of)
  7. If we are to meet the deadline, we need two more testers.

Wrong: 4 "was to open" alone (this doesn't tell us it didn't happen; was to have opened does) · 6 "on the verge to reach" · 7 "If we will meet the deadline." Item 7 is useful in real briefings; praise it.

Exercise 4 — Precision and register
  1. Will you be using: more tactful to a senior colleague; asks about the plan, not a decision.
  2. arrive: present in a time clause.
  3. due to: a scheduled date; about to can't take a future time phrase.
  4. have known: stative verb.
  5. I'll be there: casual register; "I'll have been arriving" is also ungrammatical in meaning.
  6. is to: an official arrangement (formal); about to clashes with "next month."
  7. will have left: completed before the moment of reading. (will be leaving is possible only if the writer is leaving at that exact moment: unlikely.)
Exercise 5 — A future-point paragraph (model)

The new clinic opens on September 1. On opening day, the reception team will be welcoming the first patients while the IT team will still be testing the booking system. By then, we will have moved all the patient records and trained every nurse on the new software, and the project team will have been working on the move for almost a year. We were to have opened in June, but the building inspection was delayed. The final inspection is due to take place next week, and we are on the verge of signing the cleaning contract.

Check: one form per job, a by-anchor for each perfect, no future in time clauses. Praise a sentence that uses If we are to….

Dialogue — a project check-in

Future forms: meets (timetable present) · will it be finished · we'll have been running · we'll have collected · were to have launched · were about to go live · is due to arrive · we'll still be testing · is on the verge of signing · will you be joining · is to attend · is bound to have · If we're to get. A plan that didn't happen: "We were to have launched it in May." Tactful question: "Will you be joining the meeting?" It asks about Daniel's plans without asking him to decide on the spot or implying he must come.

Project Briefing — model (Project 2, Northgate Health Clinic)

Thanks for your time. At the steering meeting on June 30, the movers will be transferring the last of the equipment to the new building. By then, we'll have moved all the patient records and trained every receptionist on the new check-in screens, and the IT team will have been testing the phone system for six weeks. We were going to open on June 2, but the elevator inspection was postponed. The inspection is due to take place on July 3, and we're on the verge of signing the parking agreement with the city. The mayor is to open the building on July 15. If we are to keep that date, we'll need two extra IT staff in the first week.

Stakeholder questions (model): Will you be needing volunteers on moving day? · Will patients still be using the old entrance in July? · Will the pharmacy be opening at the same time?

Self-check
Q1
"By the time you arrive, we ___ for hours." Which form best expresses duration up to that future point?
A) will be waiting
B) have been waiting
C) will have been waiting
Why: the future perfect continuous measures an ongoing activity up to a future point (by the time you arrive). A shows only that we'll be waiting at that moment, not the duration; B is present perfect, anchored to now.
Q2
"We ___ take the earlier train, but it was fully booked." Choose the natural "future in the past" form.
A) will
B) were going to
C) would have taken
Why: was / were going to reports an earlier plan that wasn't carried out. A is present-future; C is a third-conditional result and would need a condition (would have taken it if…).
Q3 · new
"According to the press release, the director ___ visit the site on Friday."
A) is to
B) is about to
C) would
Why: be to reports an official arrangement, the language of press releases and announcements. B means "any moment now" and can't take a future time phrase like "on Friday"; C is a future seen from the past and doesn't fit a present report.
Errors you will hear most (1.1)

"By the time we will finish…" · "When the client will arrive, …" · "By June I will work here for ten years." (present or simple future for a duration) · "…since ten years" · "will have been knowing / owning" · "We're about to sign next week." · "we will finished" / "I will been working" (dropped auxiliary) · "You are to report" read as a suggestion. Appropriacy: future perfects in casual chat ("I'll have been eating by then, lol") and blunt "Will you use…?" to a senior colleague. For range, praise was to have + pp, If we are to…, and a well-placed on the verge of.

1.2 · Advanced Modality — Speculation & Nuance (C2)

Exercise 1 — The Tuesday slump (Script 1.2-A)
1can't beR ruled out
2might beP possible
3can't possibly have startedR ruled out
4may well have switchedL quite likely
5would always orderH past habit
6must beC near-certain
7are bound to beC near-certain (a prediction)
8might as well tryD a decision

Extra modals in the script to notice: she won't be the reason (deduction), It should bring some people in (expectation), It could conceivably work (a slim chance). Follow-up: "Is may well have switched a guess or a fact?" (A well-founded guess: the evidence is circumstantial.)

Exercise 2 — Rank by certainty

a. Least → most certain that it will rain: c, b, d, a (c rules it out · b bare possibility · d quite likely · a near-certain deduction).

b. Weakest → strongest claim: g, e, i, h, f, where "weakest" means the lowest position on the dial. g = ruled out (can't possibly have) · e = a slim chance (could conceivably) · i = possible (might) · h = quite likely (may well have) · f = near-certain prediction (is bound to).

Accept e and i in either order if the learner explains the difference well: could conceivably stresses "only just possible," bare might is neutral. Some learners argue that g is the "strongest" claim (it is very certain, but of a negative). Agree, and point out that the question is about the dial position.

Exercise 3 — Choose the nuance
  1. may as well (no reason not to; the best option left)
  2. must (near-certain deduction)
  3. may well (quite likely)
  4. can't (ruled out on the evidence)
  5. may as well (nothing stops us; a decision)
  6. may well (a strong but hedged prediction)

Accept might as well in 1 and 5 and could well / might well in 3 and 6 if learners change the word. Wrong: 1 "may well" (turns a decision into a probability) · 6 "may as well drop" (means "they might as well drop": nonsense here).

Exercise 4 — The job of will / would
  1. habitual past (a repeated routine)
  2. criticism / characteristic behavior ("that's so typical of him")
  3. politeness: softened advice (also a conditional frame, "if I were you": accept either with a reason)
  4. past refusal (an object's persistent failure)
  5. narrative future in the past
  6. present deduction (a confident guess about now)
  7. refusal now / persistent failure
  8. typical behavior (what she habitually does; her nature)
Exercise 5 — Now or past?
  1. He must have been joking.
  2. They can't have known about the changes. (also: can't possibly have known · couldn't have known)
  3. She might be waiting outside (right now). (also: may / could be waiting)
  4. They should have arrived by now. (also: ought to have arrived)
  5. The email may well have gone to spam. (also: might / could well have gone)
  6. She must have been working all night.

Wrong: 1 "must be joking" (present) · 2 "mustn't have known" (L1 transfer; the negative of deduction is can't have) · 4 "should arrive by now" · 6 "must have worked all night" (acceptable, but loses the ongoing activity) · any "must have went / gone to spam" confusion: "must have gone."

Dialogue — the dark office

must have left (near-certain, past) · may well have (quite likely, ellipsis of "left") · might just be (a slim chance, now) · won't answer (typical behavior / refusal) · might as well email (a decision) · should reply (expectation) · would be very her (characteristic behavior) · It would, wouldn't it (characteristic, echoed) · could have been planning (possible, past ongoing).

The Evidence Ladder — case solutions (read aloud at the reveal)
Case 1 · The Vanishing DeliveriesThe tenant on the 4th floor changed its company name in February (Brightline Design → Kestrel Studio), but the lobby directory and the couriers' address database still show the old name. Couriers who can't match the name leave packages at the 2nd-floor reception, where they sit until someone recognizes them. Red herrings: the new mail-room temp (started after the problem began) and the broken elevator (fixed in a day). Model verdict: It can't possibly have been the temp; the problem started two weeks before she arrived. The name change must have confused the couriers, and packages are bound to keep going astray until the directory is updated. I'd suggest we update the directory and email every supplier this week.
Case 2 · The 3 a.m. AlertThe new cloud backup (set up in October to run every Thursday at 3 a.m.) clashes with the automatic software update that the old IT contractor scheduled for the same night. Both jobs use the server at full capacity. Red herrings: the cleaning crew (they have no passwords and the server room is locked) and the vending-machine Wi-Fi. Model verdict: The cleaners can't possibly have caused it. The backup and the update may well be running at the same time; the timing points to that. It's bound to happen again next Thursday unless one of them is moved. We might as well move the backup to Sunday.
Case 3 · The Empty Training RoomSeveral causes together, which is the point: the course was advertised only online (the website and social media), the few printed flyers showed the wrong day (Tuesday instead of Thursday), and the sign-up form required an online account, which many of the people who asked for the course don't have. The date also falls in a school-holiday week. Model verdict: No single cause explains it. The wrong day on the flyer must have cost us some people, and the online-only form may well have put off many more. The holiday week could conceivably be a factor too. I'd suggest paper sign-up sheets at the front desk and a new date.
Case 4 · The Missing Saturday VolunteersIn March the city changed the Saturday bus schedule: the first Route 12 bus now arrives at 9:40, but the garden's volunteer shift still starts at 9:00. Most regular volunteers don't drive. Red herrings: the new coordinator (volunteers like her; weekday numbers are up) and the move to a group-chat app (Saturday messages are read). Model verdict: It can't be the new coordinator; weekday numbers have gone up since she started. The bus change must be the main reason: the first bus doesn't arrive until 9:40. People are bound to keep missing the start. We might as well move the shift to 10:00.

Groups rarely match the solution exactly. Mark the reasoning: did each hypothesis sit on the right rung for the evidence? Did it move when the evidence changed? Did the group avoid blaming a person without evidence? A group that keeps a hypothesis at could conceivably and says why has done excellent C1 work, even if it's not the answer.

Self-check
Q1
"The shops are shut, so we ___ order in." Which fits, meaning there's no better option?
A) may as well
B) may well
C) can't
Why: may / might as well = there's no better option, a decision with a shrug. B is about probability ("we'll quite likely order in"), which doesn't fit "so"; C contradicts the logic.
Q2
In "Every winter we'd skate on the frozen pond," what job is would doing?
A) Conditional (result of a condition)
B) Habitual past (a repeated routine)
C) Politeness / softening
Why: an established past setting ("every winter") with no condition, and a repeated action: habitual would, like used to for actions.
Q3 · new
"The lights are on and her car's in the driveway. She ___ gone away for the weekend."
A) can't possibly have
B) may well have
C) must have
Why: the evidence (lights on, car still there) rules the idea out, so the speaker needs the bottom of the dial, in the past: can't (possibly) have + pp. B and C point the wrong way on the evidence.
Errors you will hear most (1.2)

"It may as well rain" (meaning likely) · "He must have went / must of gone" · "He mustn't have known" (for impossibility) · "He must be lying yesterday" · "When I was a child I'd have a bike" (habitual would with a state) · "He should be at home: the lights are on" (where must is meant) · "It is bound to rain yesterday." · stacked hedges ("it may possibly perhaps be…"). Appropriacy: over-certainty about people ("It's definitely the temp's fault") and the criticism would ("You would say that") used to a manager. For range, praise could conceivably, may well have, is bound to and a well-timed "That'll be…".

1.3 · The Subjunctive & Unreal Uses (C3)

Exercise 1 — The committee's recommendations (Script 1.3-A)
1be updated5were
2attend6were
3not be asked7took
4have

Items 1–4 are mandative (base form: passive, active, negative passive, have not has). Items 5–6 are unreal were. Item 7: it's high time + past simple. Learners often hear "attends" or "has": replay the line and ask "Is it a fact or a recommendation?"

Exercise 2 — The mandative subjunctive
  1. be extended
  2. be
  3. not answer
  4. be retained
  5. have
  6. submit
  7. be reviewed · not be required

Also correct: should + base in every item (should be extended, should not answer). Wrong: 1 "is extended / extends" · 3 "doesn't answer / not to answer / didn't answer" · 5 "has" · 6 "submits" · 7 "is reviewed," "are not required," "don't be required."

Exercise 3 — Register and meaning
  1. should apply
  2. be waived
  3. should arrive
  4. not sign
  5. works (suggest = show: a fact, indicative)
  6. be tested (suggest = propose: subjunctive; also should be tested)
  7. had sent (insist = assert a fact; backshifted; also sent)
  8. send (insist = demand; also should send)

Items 1–4: both versions are correct; the subjunctive is more formal and the standard American choice, should the characteristically British one. Item 5 "work" (subjunctive) is the classic over-correction: praise the awareness, then show the lookalike.

Exercise 4 — Unreal uses
  1. is (real: the speaker thinks it genuinely might snow)
  2. owned (unreal: he doesn't own it)
  3. were (unreal were: we aren't old friends; accept was in informal use)
  4. knows (real: treated as plausibly true)
  5. updated (it's high time + past simple; also should update, less emphatic)
  6. Were (inversion: only were)
  7. rejected / were to reject (also was to reject informally)
  8. didn't forward (would rather + past for another person's action)

Wrong: 2 "owns" (treats it as possibly true) · 5 "update / will update" · 6 "Was" · 8 "don't forward / not forward / wouldn't forward."

Exercise 5 — Edit the memo
  1. We recommend that each manager send the report by Friday.
  2. It is vital that the kitchen not be left dirty overnight.
  3. They demanded that he apologize in writing. (also: They demanded an apology in writing.)
  4. Were I in charge, I would change the schedule. (also: If I were / was in charge, …)
  5. We ask that visitors not park in the loading area.
  6. He acts as if he owned the place. (he doesn't: unreal)
  7. The grammar is right but the register is wrong: a mandative subjunctive in a friendly text sounds absurdly official (or deliberately comic). → Don't forget the snacks on Saturday! / Can you bring snacks on Saturday?
Dialogue — preparing the recommendation

Mandative subjunctives (3): (I recommend) that every meeting have · that Wednesdays be kept · that managers not invite. Other unreal forms (4): It's high time we did · Were we to do nothing · Suppose the directors refused · I'd rather you did. As if: as if it were the end of the world. Fixed phrases (2): Be that as it may · If need be. Note the second conditional in "we'd lose" and "we'd pilot" as results of the unreal forms.

Policy Memo — model (Brief 1, meeting overload · Position A)

To: Senior Leadership Team · From: Operations Working Group · Date: March 14 · Re: Reducing meeting time

Background. Our staff survey shows that employees spend an average of 14 hours a week in meetings, and many report attending meetings as though attendance itself were the goal. Recommendations. 1. We recommend that every meeting have a written agenda circulated 24 hours in advance. 2. It is essential that Wednesdays be kept free of internal meetings, on a trial basis for one quarter. 3. Our proposal is that managers not invite more than six people unless a decision requires it. Rationale. Were we to recover even a quarter of this time, each employee would gain three and a half hours a week for focused work. Next steps. It is high time we measured the cost of meetings. If need be, the working group will pilot these changes in the Logistics department first. (132 words)

Check: three trigger types (verb, adjective, noun) ✓ · negative ✓ · passive ✓ · unreal form ✓ · as though ✓ · formal, consistent tone ✓.

Self-check
Q1
"The regulations require that each application ___ signed by a supervisor."
A) be
B) is
C) has to be
Why: require that is a mandative trigger: the base form (here a passive, be signed) for every person. B is the informal indicative, heard but not appropriate in regulations; C repeats the obligation already in require.
Q2
"He strode past us without a word, as if he ___ us at all." The speaker means he did recognize them but pretended not to.
A) doesn't know
B) wouldn't have known
C) didn't know
Why: the comparison is contrary to fact (he did know them), so as if takes a past form. A is present and treats it as possibly true; B is a conditional perfect that doesn't fit an unreal comparison.
Q3 · new
"For security reasons, we ask that staff ___ their passwords with anyone."
A) not share
B) don't share
C) not to share
Why: the negative mandative subjunctive is not + base form, with no do and no to. B is the indicative (informal, and odd after ask that); C mixes in the infinitive of "ask staff not to share."
Errors you will hear most (1.3)

"We recommend that he goes" · "that she doesn't attend" · "that he to leave" · "I want that you come" (want is not a trigger) · "It is possible that he be late" (overextension from a Romance L1) · "Was I in charge…" · "It's time that we go" · "as if he owns the place" (for unreal) · "The data suggests that the system be working" (lookalike over-correction). Appropriacy: subjunctives and fossil phrases in casual messages ("Suffice it to say the pizza was good"), and like for as if in a formal memo ("It feels like we are ignoring the problem"). For range, praise Were we to…, a well-placed passive subjunctive, and Be that as it may used once, not three times.

Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. A sentence is right when the structure is correct (the time logic of each conditional clause, auxiliary + subject after a fronted trigger, be agreeing with the focus in a cleft) and the choice suits the meaning and the occasion. An inversion in a text message, or three clefts in a row, can be grammatical and still be marked down for appropriacy: say so, and say why. Be generous with genuine options: was / were after if I / she (prefer were in formal writing), that / who / zero in clefts, provided / providing (that), so long as / as long as, must / should after On no account. American English is the model; accept British spellings and forms (realise, apologise, cancelled, got for gotten). When a learner produces a correct alternative to the target, accept it and nudge: "Correct. Now try it with but for."

2.1 · Mixed Conditionals & Alternatives to "if" (C4)

Exercise 1 — "The Hinge" podcast (Script 2.1-A)
1234567
had accepted · would beweren't · would have takenBut forprovided thatin casewere toUnless

Accept 'd be in 1, wasn't in 2 (but point out that the script uses weren't), providing (that) in 4. The hinge factor: Brightwell turned down the Swift Deliveries contract in 2022, largely because of the founder's cautious character; that caution later helped it survive the battery shortage. Items 1 and 2 are the two directions of mixed conditional: ask "When is the condition? When is the result?" for each.

Exercise 2 — Diagnose the time
  1. hadn't signed · wouldn't be paying (also wouldn't pay) · P→Pr
  2. were (also was) · wouldn't have resigned · Pr→P (a permanent trait explains a past action)
  3. had tested · would still trust · P→Pr
  4. spoke · would have gone · Pr→P
  5. had listened · might be · P→Pr
  6. weren't (also wasn't) · would never have offered · Pr→P

Wrong: 1 "wouldn't have paid … now" (habitual symmetry) · 2 "hadn't been … wouldn't have resigned" is a correct third conditional but loses the "he is impatient by nature" meaning: accept it grammatically, then ask "Is he still impatient?" · 3 "would have still trusted us today" · 4 "If I had spoken" (third conditional: possible only if the learner means a one-off past failure to speak it, not a general inability) · 5 "might have been in profit by now" (acceptable only as speculation about the past; by now signals the present).

Exercise 3 — The exact alternative
  1. on condition that (a formal, explicit stipulation from a bank)
  2. in case (a precaution, taken now)
  3. Supposing
  4. Even if (the result holds regardless)
  5. provided that or as long as
  6. unless (= except if)
  7. otherwise
  8. as long as or provided that (whichever was not used in 5)

5 and 8 are interchangeable; provided that sounds slightly more transactional, as long as slightly more conversational. In 1, provided that is also grammatical, but on condition that is the most formal fit for a bank's stipulation. Wrong: 2 "if sales drop" (then the money would be set aside only after the drop, which misses the precaution) · 6 "if the tests reveal" reverses the meaning.

Exercise 4 — Isolate the decisive factor
  1. But for the engineers' quick action, the data would have been lost. / If it hadn't been for the engineers' quick action, …
  2. If it weren't for the noise from the street, the office would be perfect. / But for the noise from the street, …
  3. If it hadn't been for a single investor (who believed in us), we would never have launched. / But for one investor's faith in us, …
  4. If it weren't for our volunteers, the festival would stop. / But for our volunteers, …
  5. But for a chance meeting at a trade fair, the partnership would never have happened. / If it hadn't been for a chance meeting…

Present reality (2, 4) → if it weren't for; past reality (1, 3, 5) → if it hadn't been for; but for works for both. Accept Were it not for / Had it not been for (previewed; taught in 2.2). Wrong: "But for the engineers acted quickly" (a clause after but for) · "If it wasn't for… would have been lost" (time mismatch in 1) · "If it hadn't been for the noise, the office would be perfect" in 2 (the noise is still there).

Exercise 5 — Right or wrong?
  1. ✘ …I would be an analyst now. (past condition → present result)
  2. ✘ I'd be amazed if she didn't get the promotion. (unless can't express an unreal negative emotion; test: "except if" doesn't fit)
  3. ✔ (a genuine precaution)
  4. ✘ If the price fell (or were to fall), we would buy more stock. (no would in the if-clause)
  5. ✘ But for your help, I would have missed the deadline. (but for + noun)
  6. ✘ Call me if you need anything, and I'll come straight over. (the call depends on the need: a condition, not a precaution)
  7. ✔ (tentative should, inverted; a preview of 2.2)
Dialogue — two co-founders look back, and ahead

Mixed conditionals: "If we hadn't opened five cafés in one year, we'd still have money in the bank" (P→Pr) · "if we weren't such an impatient pair, we'd never have gotten the first one off the ground" (Pr→P). But for: "but for Maria's weekend markets, Elm Street would have closed last spring." Alternatives: provided that · supposing · as long as · in case · even if · unless · on condition that (+ tentative if the board should ask). The precaution: "we hold three months' rent in reserve in case a renewal falls through."

Speaking — The Case Study Panel (model analysis, Case 1: Harbor Fresh)

"Harbor Fresh's turning point came in 2022, when it decided not to build its own warehouse. If it had built that warehouse, it would be the market leader today: GreenCart made exactly that investment and now holds that position. Why did Harbor Fresh hesitate? If its CEO weren't so wary of debt, he would have borrowed the $400,000 without a second thought. When its only wholesaler went bankrupt in 2023, the shelves were empty for three weeks, and but for the Riverside Farmers' Co-op, which supplied it free for two weeks, the company would have closed. Our recommendation is to accept the co-op's partnership offer, provided that the minimum order is reviewed every six months, and to keep a second wholesaler on standby in case the harvest fails. Even if costs rise slightly, the security is worth it. Should a competitor try to buy the co-op, Harbor Fresh would need the right to match the offer. And if the market were to recover strongly next year, a small warehouse of its own would finally make sense." (Tick: P→Pr, Pr→P, but for, even if + provided that + in case, should, were to.)

Accept any analysis that is faithful to the case facts and meets the checklist. Mark down for appropriacy if but for is used with a clause, or if in case is used for a condition ("we'll buy a warehouse in case the market recovers").

Self-check
Q1

A) didn't send
B) wouldn't have sent

Why: the condition is an unreal past action (yesterday), so it needs the past perfect; the result is present (now). A is a second-conditional form (present unreal); B is a result form, never used in the if-clause.
Q2

A) if

Why: in case = I take it now because the battery might die later. With if, you would only take the charger at the moment the battery died. Unless reverses the sense.
Q3 · new

Errors you will hear most (2.1)

"If I had studied medicine, I would have been a doctor now." · "If it would rain, we'd cancel." · "I'll bring a jacket if it gets cold" when a precaution is meant, and the reverse, "Call me in case you need help" for a condition · "Unless she came, I'd be surprised." · "But for you helped me…" · "If it wasn't for the grant, we would have closed" (time mismatch: needed hadn't been) · "provide that" / "provided if" · "in the case that" for in case. Praise range: were to, should, but for, even if, on condition that, and modal variety in the result clause (might have caught, could be).

Exercise 2 — Front and invert
  1. Seldom have I read such a persuasive report.
  2. At no time was the public in danger.
  3. Only when the audit was complete did we understand the scale of the problem.
  4. Not only did she meet the deadline, but she (also) came in under budget.
  5. Hardly had the meeting begun when the power went out.
  6. Under no circumstances should you leave the lab unlocked.
  7. Not until the following morning did the truth emerge.

In 3, the inversion happens in the main clause, not after when: "Only when the audit was complete did we understand." Wrong: "Only when was the audit complete…" · 4 "Not only she met" / "Not only did she met" · 5 "Hardly the meeting had begun" · 7 "Not until the following morning the truth emerged."

Exercise 3 — Drop "if"
  1. Had we known about the delay, we would have rebooked.
  2. Should you require further information, please contact our office.
  3. Were the factory to close, the town would lose its largest employer.
  4. Had it not been for the volunteers, the event would have been canceled.
  5. Were I in your position, I would accept the offer.
  6. Should you not receive a confirmation within 48 hours, please call us.

6 needs should because the original is a real (first-conditional) possibility; the inverted form adds politeness. Wrong: 4 "Hadn't it been for" (a question form) · 6 "Shouldn't you receive" · "Did we know about the delay" (only had, were, should invert) · 3 "Would the factory close".

Exercise 4 — Faulty or fine?
  1. Never have I seen such a turnout.
  2. Rarely does the director admit a mistake. (tense lives on does only)
  3. Had we not left early, we would have missed the train.
  4. ✔ Correct: Only Sarah is the subject, not a fronted adverbial.
  5. Not until the end of the meeting did she mention the budget.
  6. Look, here it comes! (no place inversion with a pronoun)
  7. So impressive were the results that the board approved the plan.
Exercise 5 — Weight or pomp?
  1. ✘ (unless the learner means it as a joke, which is a legitimate use; accept with that explanation) → "I've never eaten such an enormous burrito!"
  2. ✔ A tribute is exactly the occasion for weight.
  3. ✔ Safety notices use emphatic prohibition; this is standard.
  4. ✘ Too formal for a quick lunch email (shall adds to the stiffness) → "If you'd like to join us for lunch, we'll be at the café at noon."
  5. ✔ Reviews reward emphatic openings.
  6. ✘ Four inversions crowd four sentences: they stop emphasizing and start sounding pompous. → e.g. "Never have we grown so fast. We hired more people than ever, and both profits and morale rose. Now we can see the results."
Dialogue — the speechwriter's edit

"Never have I worked with anyone quite like Grace" (Never) · "Only when the water went down did we realize…" (Only when) · "So calm was she that nobody panicked" (So + adjective): the writer advised against it because it would be the third inversion in a row, and density turns weight into pomp · "Should you ever need a book found…" (conditional inversion, warm and slightly playful in a closing line).

Speaking — The Speechwriter (model, Brief 1: tribute to Walter Chen)

Should you ever need a door fixed, please don't call Walter: he's retired. Please raise your glasses: to Walter."

Five inversions, four different types, and the plain sentences between them ("He never missed a graduation…") are deliberate. A sixth ("Not until he took a vacation did we understand how much he did") is exactly what the editor should cut. Exercise 1 — The radio interview (Script 2.3-A)

Item 6 is fronting, not a cleft: ask learners to spot the difference.

Exercise 2 — Rewrite for focus
  1. It was the new manager who (that) changed the schedule.
  2. It was the weather, not the budget, that made us cancel the event. / It was because of the weather, not the budget, that we canceled the event.
  3. What I need is more time.
  4. What happened was (that) the server (simply) overheated.
  5. All I want is a straight answer.
  6. The reason (why) I'm calling is that the invoice is wrong.
  7. It wasn't until Monday that we noticed the error.

Wrong: 1 "It was the new manager which changed" · 3 "What I need it is more time" · 6 "The reason I'm calling is because…" (accept in speech, correct in writing) · 7 "It wasn't until Monday when we noticed" (common; prefer that) · "It was on Monday that we only noticed" (keeps only and the cleft).

Exercise 3 — Front the linking element
  1. The second (one), it accepted immediately. / The second it accepted immediately.
  2. The first one, we will never use again.
  3. Even more serious is the cost of further delay. (fronted comparative; be inverts)
  4. Of the three, training was the most popular request.

Only 3 inverts, because Even more serious is a fronted complement of be. Wrong: "The first one we will never use it again" (resumptive pronoun) · "The second one did it accept" (inversion without a trigger).

Exercise 4 — Move the weight to the end
  1. It surprised nobody that the project was late.
  2. It is (It's) still unclear whether the client will renew.
  3. It is impossible to finish the whole report by Friday.
  4. It is no surprise that she was offered the job.

Wrong: "Is still unclear whether…" (missing dummy it) · "It surprised nobody the project was late" (acceptable in speech; keep that in writing).

Exercise 5 — Which continuation flows better?

1 (a) · 2 (b) · 3 (b) · 4 (a)

1: a corrective it-cleft affirms the real cause and denies the assumed one in one move; (b) is clumsy. 2: the first is given (one of the two options), so fronting it links back and leaves the new verdict for the end; (a) front-loads a heavy clause. 3: end-weight: the heavy that-clause belongs at the end. 4: staff and money are given; time is the new, contrasting point, so the what-cleft saves it for the end; (b) is possible but clumsy, and "have not got" is British and informal.

Dialogue — setting the record straight

It-clefts: "it wasn't the money that closed it" (corrective) · "It was space" (a shortened cleft: It was space [that closed it]) · "It was in March that we first consulted members." What / all: "What the figures show is a return rate of ninety-six percent" · "All we're doing is moving them." Reason frame: "The reason it felt sudden is that the notice went up late." Fronting: "That part, I fully accept." The corrective cleft is the first one: it denies the money explanation and puts space in its place.

Speaking — The Editing Desk (model, Article A)

"Tallis Print Works, a small printing company in Riverside with 40 employees, moved to a four-day week last year. Many people expected productivity to fall. What actually happened was that it rose by eight percent. It wasn't the shorter week on its own that caused the rise, though; it was better scheduling. Every job is now planned on Monday morning. Even more surprising is the fall in sick days, which nobody predicted. The change was a risk, says the owner, Ruth Tallis, but it is one she is glad she took."

Two clefts, one fronted comparative, a passive that keeps "every job" (given) up front, and plain order everywhere else. Models for Articles B and C:

B (Eastgate nap pods): "Eastgate Distribution Center, a warehouse near the airport that runs around the clock, installed two nap pods for night-shift workers last year. What also mattered was a new rule, a twenty-minute break after every four hours, and it was the workers, not management, who suggested it. Other warehouses are now asking Eastgate for advice. The vegetables they grow, the cafeteria now serves, and students are eating more salad than ever. Summer is harder. It is neighborhood volunteers who water the garden while the school is closed."

Accept any version that keeps every fact, moves given information forward and new information to the end, and uses no more than three clefts. Mark down a version that clefts given information ("It was the company that moved to a four-day week").

A further benefit would be improved staff retention, since employees are more likely to remain with an organization that invests in their development. It was argued during the discussion that small businesses may be unable to release staff for a full day. In response, it is proposed that learning days be staggered across each team, so that no more than one employee is absent at any time. A second concern, that the day might be treated as additional leave, could be addressed by requiring a brief written record of what was learned. On balance, the introduction of the scheme represents a modest cost with a potentially substantial return."

Check (W1–W4): proposal in sentence 1 · two supported reasons · objection reported fairly with a reporting verb (It was argued…, One participant questioned whether…) · a response · consistently formal (nominalization: the introduction of the scheme; hedging: would appear to, potentially; subjunctive: be staggered) · no idioms, contractions or rhetorical questions.

Self-check
Q1
A speaker wants to draw listeners in and make them supply the answer themselves. Which device fits?
A) Nominalization ("the implementation of the plan")
B) Ellipsis ("some agreed, others didn't")
C) A rhetorical question ("Can we really afford to wait?")
Why: a rhetorical question invites the listener to answer silently ("No, we can't"), so they arrive at the speaker's point themselves. A is a formal-register tool; B is a cohesion tool.
Q2
Which sentence best combines a formal register with the subjunctive to express urgent necessity?
A) We really need to do something about this soon.
B) It is imperative that action be taken at once.
C) It's imperative that action is taken pronto.
Why: be taken is the (passive) subjunctive after it is imperative that, and every other feature is formal. A is informal and has no subjunctive; C uses the indicative and clashes (it's, pronto with imperative).
Q3 · new
Which sentence keeps a consistent formal register?
A) The committee will sort out the discrepancies.
B) The committee will resolve the discrepancies.
C) The committee's gonna resolve the discrepancies.
Why: committee and discrepancies set a formal register; resolve matches it. A drops an informal phrasal verb into it; C clashes with gonna and a contraction.
Errors you will hear and read most (4.3)

Register clash inside one sentence ("The committee will sort out…", "Hey team, pursuant to…") · the whole talk in the formal register with no warmth · idioms and contractions in the written summary · rhetoric overload (three rhetorical questions in a row) · "Not only it is…" (no inversion) · "It is essential that he is told" (acceptable, but not the C1 target) · "Resuming,…" / "According to me,…" · countering without conceding in the Q&A · signpost-free rambling. At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. Q1
Which sentence contains a dangling participle?
A) Having missed the bus, she walked to work.
B) Rushing to the station, the train had already left.
C) Built in 1850, the house needs repairs.
Why: the person rushing is implied, but the main subject is the train, which can't rush to catch itself. A attaches correctly to she; C attaches correctly to the house, which was built.
Q2
You want to compress "Because the road was blocked, the drivers turned back," but the two clauses have different subjects.
A) The road blocked, the drivers turned back.
B) Blocking the road, the drivers turned back.
C) Being blocked the road, the drivers turned back.
Why: an absolute clause gives the participle its own subject (the road), so nothing dangles. B dangles and reverses the meaning (the drivers blocked the road); C is not grammatical.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is both correct and properly anchored?
A) Damaging in transit, the parcel was returned to the sender.
B) Damaged in transit, the sender returned the parcel.
C) Damaged in transit, the parcel was returned to the sender.
Why: the parcel was damaged (passive → past participle) and the parcel is the next subject. A uses -ing for a passive meaning; B has the right participle but dangles: it says the sender was damaged.
Errors you will see most (5.1)

Dangling participles, especially with -ing + a non-human subject ("Walking home, the rain started") and after / while + -ing ("After finishing, the results…") · -ing for passive ("Building in 1968, the center…") · having for simultaneous actions ("Having walked home, I met a friend") · absolutes without a subject ("Being fine, we set off") or in L1 order ("Finished the meeting, we left") · over-compression: three participles in one sentence with the logic lost · full absolutes in casual messages. Abstract 1 (87 words): A recent pilot study examined the effect of short afternoon walking breaks on the concentration of 62 municipal office workers. Over four weeks, employees who took three five-minute walks each afternoon reported approximately 18% higher concentration than a control group; their accuracy on a data-entry task also improved slightly, although this difference was not statistically significant. 6.2 · Academic & Argument Writing — Structuring an Essay (C18)

Exercise 1 — The introduction (Script 6.2-A)
  1. Hook: an observation / striking fact: in the center of many mid-sized American cities, one of the most common land uses is parking. (Accept "a surprising observation," "a vivid scene.")
  2. Context: lots are half-empty much of the week, but councils are reluctant to remove them, fearing fewer shoppers.
  3. Thesis: "Although drivers' needs cannot simply be ignored, converting underused lots into public squares would bring more people downtown, not fewer, and would give struggling town centers a reason to exist beyond the car." (Accept close paraphrase that keeps the concession and both reasons.)
  4. Predicted body paragraphs: (1) foot traffic / business for shops; (2) community life / a reason to visit downtown; (3) counter-argument: drivers, access, disabled parking, rebutted.
  5. Although (the concession: drivers' needs).
Exercise 2 — Thesis clinic

1 T · 2 O · 3 F · 4 Q (salaries, not training time) · 5 S

Model rewrites: (1/2) "Employers should give staff paid time for training, because a workforce that keeps learning adapts faster to new technology and is less likely to leave." · (3) "While paid training time is costly for small businesses, the case for it is stronger than the case against: the cost of skills gaps and staff turnover is higher still." · (4) "Paid training time is a better investment for employers than across-the-board pay raises, because it increases both productivity and retention." Accept the opposite position if it is specific and arguable ("Paid training time should be optional, not required, because…").

Exercise 3 — Signposting that matches the logic

1 Consequently (result) · 2 By contrast (two things compared) · 3 Nevertheless (concession, then a turn) · 4 Furthermore (another point on the same side) · 5 Admittedly (opens a concession) · 6 For instance (example)

7 "…saved the company money. Nevertheless / However / Even so, some staff disliked it." Disliking it is not a result of saving money; it is a contrast.

Item 2 vs 3 is the key C1 distinction: By contrast compares two different things; Nevertheless accepts a point and then turns against its expected consequence. However would fit both, which is why it is overused.

Exercise 4 — Concede and rebut, fairly

The straw man is B, because nobody opposing paid training argues that workers "should never learn anything new"; the real objection is cost (as in A). B also rebuts with a truism ("the world is changing"), not a reason.

Model (phones collected: for): "Admittedly, collecting phones may seem to treat adults like schoolchildren, and some staff need to remain reachable for urgent calls. Yet this overlooks the fact that a short, device-free meeting respects everyone's time more than a longer, distracted one, and a single designated contact can handle genuine emergencies." (against): "Admittedly, phones on the table can distract a meeting. However, this overlooks the fact that many staff use their phones for the agenda, notes and calendars; a clear norm (screens face-down unless needed) addresses the distraction without removing the tools."

Exercise 5 — Rebuild a PEEL paragraph

B (Point) → D (Evidence) → A (Explanation) → C (Link)

Clues: B is the only sentence that works without context (topic sentence; also links to a previous paragraph); D has for instance; A has This suggests (referencing back to the evidence); C has It follows that and widens to the thesis.

Dialogue — a peer review

Softening phrases: I wasn't entirely sure how… · You might consider adding… · I'd question the rebuttal · That reads a little like… (also it's fair, but). She checks the thesis first so that all her feedback is measured against what the writer is trying to do, and so the writer knows the main message has landed.

The strongest letters quote or mirror two or three phrases from the job posting and answer each with evidence. Exercise 5 — The Listening Lab (Script 7.1-A)

Part A — model notes (the shape matters, not the exact words):

TALK: cramming = inefficient; 2 changes → big diff (thesis) 3 parts announced 1. forgetting - Ebbinghaus, 1880s, tested himself - (aside: nonsense syllables DAX, BOK – skip) - > ½ lost in 1 day; then slower decline = "forgetting curve" ⭐ curve not fixed: each review → forget more slowly 2. spacing - belief: more hrs = more memory (partly true) BUT when ≈ how long - e.g. 3 hrs Sun night vs 1 hr M/W/F → spaced student wins, wk later - spacing effect = v. reliable finding - why? (hedged: "one explanation") some forgetting → effort to rebuild → stronger 3. testing yourself ⭐⭐ (most impt) - test ≠ just measuring; = learning - Roediger & Karpicke 2006: reread vs recall on blank page - 5 min: rereaders slightly better; 1 wk: testers remember much more - rereading feels productive (familiar) ≠ learning ⭐ "feeling of fluency ≠ learning" RECAP: forget fast, review slows it · spacing > cramming · retrieval > rereading ∴ best learners: right intervals + test selves (not longest hours) ⭐ "close notes, see what you remember"

Cue column examples: Thesis? · What is the forgetting curve? · What flattens it? · Spacing: claim vs example? · Why does spacing work (how sure)? · 2006 study: 5 min vs 1 wk? · Why does rereading mislead? · Upshot? Summary strip example: "Cramming fails because we forget fast; spacing study and testing yourself (not rereading) make memories last."

Part B — Comprehension

  1. Cramming is one of the least efficient ways to learn, and two simple changes to how you study (spacing and testing yourself) make a remarkable difference.
  2. (1) How quickly we forget · (2) why spacing out study works better than cramming · (3) why testing yourself beats rereading.
  3. Within a day he had lost well over half of what he had learned; the decline then continued more slowly for weeks. Each time he reviewed, the curve flattened: he forgot more slowly the next time.
  4. Both study for three hours in total: one does all three hours on Sunday night; the other does one hour on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. A week later, the second (spaced) student remembers more, often by a wide margin.
  5. Some students reread a passage several times; others read it once and then tried to recall it on a blank page. After five minutes the rereaders did slightly better; after a week the students who had tested themselves remembered considerably more.
  6. "Don't just review your notes: close them, and see what you can remember."

Part B — Inference

  1. The third part, testing yourself. Evidence: "the part I care about most" (preview) and "the one I'd most like you to take away" (point 3); she also flags it with "I can't stress this enough." Accept either two.
  2. It's a digression with a touch of humor and personal stance (the speaker finds the methods boring). It signals "don't worry about this." It does not belong in the notes, or at most as "(aside – skip)." It's framed by "As an aside" and "I won't dwell on his methods."
  3. She concedes part of a common belief before overturning it (concession → counter-argument). After "that's true" a listener should expect "But…": here, "the research shows that when you study matters at least as much as how long."
  4. (a) Very certain: "one of the most reliable findings in the whole of psychology" (a booster). (b) Tentative: "One explanation is that…" (a hedge: there may be others).
  5. The ease of recognizing familiar material makes us feel we have learned it, but recognizing is not the same as being able to recall it later. Rereading makes the text look familiar, so we overestimate what we know; retrieval is harder, and the effort is what builds memory.
  6. Probably: "Five rereads the night before will feel productive, but you'll forget most of it. Spread the review over several days and, instead of rereading, close the notes and test yourself." Accept any answer that includes spacing and retrieval, and mentions the illusion of fluency.

Part C — model summary (4 sentences):

The speaker argues that cramming is an inefficient way to learn and that two changes to how we study make a large difference. She explains that we forget quickly but that each review slows forgetting, and that spacing study over several sessions is more effective than cramming the same hours into one. Her central point is that testing yourself works better than rereading, because retrieving information strengthens memory while rereading only feels productive. She concludes that effective learners study at intervals and test themselves along the way.

Check against the peer-review list: the claim comes first; the three points are in order; no names (Ebbinghaus, Roediger), dates or the two-student example; the conclusion is the speaker's. Common problems: a summary that retells the examples in order ("First she talked about a German psychologist…"); a summary that adds the writer's opinion ("I think this is true because…"); "The lecture is about the cramming."

Dialogue — after the lecture

Signposts quoted: This talk falls into three parts (preview) · as an aside and I won't dwell on his methods (digression) · I can't stress this enough (emphasis). Lena also uses the upshot (recap). Main idea vs example: Omar's line 6 ("That's just the example. The point is that when you study…") and line 8 ("The study's the evidence. The main idea is…").   2. When the MEETing FINishes, / I'll SEND you the NOTES. · weak: the, you (also I'll)

Watch word stress inside the stressed words: reSULTS, exPECTed, rePORT, proPOSal (not "REsults," "PROposal"). Accept a light stress on When in 2 or Could in 4 in slow speech. Any two correct weak forms per sentence earn the point.

Exercise 2 — Choose the stressed word

1 red · 2 table · 3 I · 4 today · 5 say · 6 Marco

In each case the tonic lands on the new or contrasted information: the word that carries the point of the sentence. In 4 the stress is on the second syllable: toDAY.

Exercise 3 — Thought groups
  1. If you finish early / we can grab a coffee / before the train leaves.
  2. Honestly / I'm not sure / but I think / it's worth a try.
  3. If we can't agree on the budget by Friday / we'll have to postpone the launch. (Also fine: …on the budget / by Friday / we'll have to…)
  4. 4a (non-defining): The report / which I sent yesterday / covers all three regions. 4b (defining): The report which I sent yesterday / covers all three regions.

4a vs 4b. In 4a there is only one report, and "which I sent yesterday" is extra information: the voice pauses before and after it, often on a slightly lower, quicker pitch, like a spoken pair of commas. In 4b the clause identifies which report (the one from yesterday, not another one), so there is no pause before which: the clause belongs in the same group as the report.

Exercise 4 — Stress the new

1 at SEVen · 2 Room FIVE · 3 the FIRST half · 4 toMORrow · 5 DALlas · 6 THAT's my coffee

In 2, 4 and 5 the tonic is a correction (contrast with the speaker's wrong information). In 3 first is the contrast (and implies "but not the second half": a fall-rise works well). In 6 the contrast is this vs that, so the tonic moves right to the front. Wrong: 1 "IT leaves at seven" · 4 "I'm WORKing late tomorrow" (that information is given).

Exercise 5 — Intonation for attitude

Part A: 1 falling · 2 rising · 3 fall-rise · 4 falling · 5 rising. Note the trap in 4: many learners raise wh-questions, but English normally lets them fall.

Part B: 6 ↘ (a clear fall, often from a high start: enthusiasm) · 7 ↘↗ (reservation; a "but" is implied) · 8 ↘↗ (clean… but not quiet) · 9 ↘ (definite; a fall-rise would suggest a condition: "…but only in the morning")

Dialogue — one sentence, many meanings

Alex stresses I to deny being the decision-maker and MARcus to name who was: contrastive stress shifts responsibility ("not me, him"). The fall-rise on "FRIday ↘↗… in theory" signals doubt: officially Friday, but Alex doesn't expect it to hold; a plain fall would sound like a firm yes. Sam's rise, rise, fall is the English list shape: the rises say "more is coming," and the fall says "that's the end, and I'm sure."

Speaking — the Delivery Clinic

No single answer. When learners mark up their own summary, check that: the tonic is usually the last content word of each group unless something is contrasted; pauses come between clauses, before but / and / because and around inserted phrases, never after the or between a verb and its object; statements end with a fall. In the model recap, the contrast OUT vs IN and the fall-rise on "LONGest" (…but…) are the two moves learners most often miss.

Self-check
Q1
In the sentence "I'd like to book a table for two," which words should carry the main stress?
A) to, a, for
B) like, book, table, two
C) every word equally
Why: content words (verbs, nouns, numbers) carry the stress; function words (to, a, for) are weak. Equal stress (C) is syllable-timing, which makes English harder to follow.
Q2
You say "It's a great plan…" with a fall-rise (↘↗) on "plan." What are you signaling?
A) Total, enthusiastic agreement with no reservations
B) That you're asking a yes/no question
C) Reservation: an implied "but I have doubts"
Why: the fall-rise carries an unspoken "but." Enthusiasm would be a clear (often high) fall; a yes/no question would rise.
Q3 · new
"Did you send the report on Monday?" You sent it on Tuesday. Which reply puts the tonic in the right place?
A) "No, I SENT it on Tuesday."
B) "No, I sent the REPORT on Tuesday."
C) "No, I sent it on TUESday."
Why: the sending and the report are given information; the new, corrected information is the day. A suggests you did something else to it (not sent); B suggests you sent something else.
Errors you will hear most (7.2)

Equal stress on every syllable · strong, long TO / OF / FOR / AND · stress on the wrong syllable of a stressed word (REsults, PROposal, DEvelopment for reSULTS, proPOSal, deVELopment) · pauses after the or in the middle of a phrase · rising statements, especially at the end of a presentation point · wh-questions with a rise · the fall-rise heard as sarcasm (usually a pitch range that's too wide or too slow) · can and can't both with a full vowel · no linking ("turn / it / off"). Praise range: a well-placed contrastive stress, a clean rise-rise-fall list, a fall-rise used diplomatically.

7.3 · Seminar & Discussion Skills (C22)

Exercise 1 — Complete the phrase

1 in · 2 finish · 3 Building · 4 understand · 5 point · 6 leave

Also accept 3 Following ("Following on from what you said…"). In 4, understood is wrong: the phrase is fixed as "if I understand you correctly."

Exercise 2 — Complete the exchange

1 finish (holding the floor) · 2 come in (claiming a turn) · 3 thoughts (handing over) · 4 correctly (paraphrasing) · 5 agree (common ground) · 6 move (moving on)

Exercise 3 — Soften the blunt disagreement

Model rewrites (accept any version that acknowledges something specific, softens, and still makes the challenge clear):

  1. I see the appeal of remote work, but I'd question whether productivity really holds up. Isn't there a risk that it drops over time?
  2. I take your point, and I like the ambition, but I'm not sure how it would work in practice. Could we think through the logistics?
  3. I agree we need to control costs, but I'd be cautious about cutting this particular budget. Up to a point, yes, but not here.
  4. That's an interesting reading, but I'm not sure the text goes quite that far. Could we look at the second paragraph again?
  5. You may well be right that most people type their notes now, but I'd question "nobody." Isn't there some evidence that handwriting helps some learners remember?

Not acceptable: rewrites that give up the view ("You're probably right, sorry") or a generic acknowledgment followed by the same bluntness ("Good point, but that's wrong"). The acknowledgment must name something.

Exercise 4 — Name the move
1 Danagives a view, then hands over by name ("What are your thoughts, Ben?")
2 Benbuilds on Dana's point ("Building on what you said"), adds evidence, and adds a new point ("To add to that")
3 Priyacomes in ("Could I just come in here?") + acknowledges and challenges ("I take your point about productivity, but I'd question whether…")
4 Danaparaphrases to check ("So if I understand you correctly…")
5 Priyaconfirms ("Exactly.")
6 Marcocomes in ("If I could just add something") + proposes + links two ideas ("That ties in with Priya's point")
7 Priyapartial agreement ("Up to a point, yes, but…") + soft challenge ("Isn't there a risk that…?")
8 Danaacknowledges ("That's fair") + common ground ("Where we do agree, though, is…") + names the split
9 Marcosteers ("So where does that leave us?") + proposes a next step
10 Danasummarizes ("So, to sum up…") + moves on ("Shall we move on to…?")

Why it never turns sour: every challenge is acknowledged and softened; each contribution builds on or links to the previous one; Dana paraphrases Priya's concern, which shows she has been heard; and Dana and Marco steer toward common ground and a decision instead of a winner.

Exercise 5 — Paraphrase to check
  1. So if I understand you correctly, you think phones are a problem in meetings, but a ban isn't the right solution ↗?
  2. So your point is that portfolios are a fairer way to assess, but the grading workload is a real cost ↗?
  3. So you're saying a car-free Main Street sounds attractive, but it could hurt the businesses that rely on passing drivers ↗?

A good paraphrase keeps both halves of the view and uses different words. Weak: repeating the sentence word for word; keeping only the half you disagree with ("So you're saying we should ban phones?"), which sounds like a trap. The rise matters: a falling paraphrase sounds like an accusation.

Speaking — the Seminar

No single answer. Check the observer sheets for the four target moves (come in, build on, paraphrase, respectful challenge) and the summarizer's two summaries. A good final summary has three parts: what the group broadly agrees on, where it is split, and a next step or open question ("So we broadly agree that…, but we're split on…; the open question is…").

Self-check
Q1
A colleague is mid-sentence and you have something to add. What's the most appropriate way to take a turn?
A) "Sorry, could I just come in here?"
B) Start talking loudly over them until they stop.
C) Say nothing and let the moment pass.
Why: a coming-in phrase is a request that leaves the speaker a moment to yield. B is interrupting; C means your idea is never heard.
Q2
You strongly disagree with someone's proposal. Which opening keeps the disagreement constructive?
A) "No, that's completely wrong."
B) "I take your point, but I'm not sure we've thought through the cost…"
C) "Sure, whatever you think is best."
Why: B follows acknowledge → soften → challenge: firm on the idea, respectful to the person. A closes the discussion; C gives up the view entirely.
Q3 · new
You are the facilitator. Omar has spoken for three minutes; two people haven't spoken yet. What do you say?
A) "Omar, please stop talking now."
B) "Thanks, Omar, that's really useful. I'd love to hear from someone who hasn't come in yet. Lena?"
C) Nothing. The facilitator shouldn't interrupt.
Why: B thanks and credits Omar, then hands over by name, which is the facilitator's main job. A is accurate but face-threatening; C lets the discussion become a monologue.
Errors you will hear most (7.3)

"I am agree / I'm not agree" · "discuss about" · "According to me…" · "explain me" · "Can I say something?" used as a real stop sign in mid-flow without a softener · "I understand you correctly" (for if I understand you correctly) · a paraphrase that changes the speaker's view · generic acknowledgments ("Good point, but…" every time) · challengers who challenge only one side · a facilitator who never hands over · talking over others. Appropriacy matters most here: praise the moves that kept the room warm (That ties in with…, Up to a point, yes, but…, Where we do agree is…).

Marking principle

At C1, mark range, accuracy and appropriacy. In this module many answers are open: a circumlocution, a repair or an edit is correct when it works for the listener or reader: it is clear, grammatical, keeps every idea, and suits the register. Accept any version that does this, even if it differs from the key, and use the key's version to show one more option ("Good. Another way is…"). For editing tasks, the three tests are always the same: (1) no idea, number, name or source lost; (2) still grammatical (no headline-style dropping of articles or auxiliaries); (3) the register unchanged. Contractions are fine. American English is the model; British spellings are acceptable when used consistently.

Dialogue — Maya's strategies

T (buying time): that's a really good question · let me think for a second · well, it depends on how you look at it · how can I put this · bear with me a second · the way I see it. C (circumlocution): the whole system that checks whether information is accurate · the process you use to verify a source · a kind of… trust gap · what's the word (flagging the gap). R (repair): or rather, the lack of it · let me rephrase that · to put it another way. M (markers with a job): hmm · you know (shared understanding) · I mean (clarifying) · sort of a mixed picture (an honest hedge) · essentially. The online version says "the thing you use to verify a source"; the workbook uses "the process," which is more accurate for fact-checking: either is fine.

A) speaking as fast as possible without pausing
B) keeping going coherently, even while you think
C) never pausing at any point
Why: fluency is continuity, not velocity. Filled pauses are normal; silent, frozen pauses and restarts break fluency. A and C describe the anxious "speed" goal the lesson replaces.
Q2

B) go silent until it comes to you
C) describe it: "the thing you use to open a wine bottle"
Why: circumlocution keeps the sentence moving and often prompts the listener to supply the word. A and B stop the conversation.
Q3 · new
Which is the smoothest self-correction?

B) "The meeting is on Thursday. Sorry, sorry, no. Friday. Sorry."
C) "The meeting is on… I don't know how to say. Friday, I think."

Exercise 3 — Upgrade, cut, or keep?

1 exhausted (also worn out, drained) · 2 huge (also vast, enormous) · 3 astonished (also amazed, stunned) · 4 terrible (also severe, devastating) · 5 starving (also famished, ravenous) · 6 spotless (also immaculate)

In 4, devastating is stronger than really bad; accept it if the context supports it. In 10, "quite really" is also ungrammatical stacking: point it out.

Exercise 4 — Dig out the verb

  2. We discussed the budget on Monday.
  3. The manager explained the new policy.
  4. The volunteers helped (assisted) the new families.
  5. The council has improved the bus service.

Exercise 5 — Untangle it
  1. Delays are common when staff are absent. (online lesson answer)
  2. Her proposal is similar to (like) the one we rejected last year.
  3. The results may be roughly accurate. (online lesson answer; also: The results may be broadly accurate.)

Dialogue — In the Editing Room

The four cuts: (1) a wordy phrase: due to the fact that → because; (2) padding removed: we are currently at a point where; (3) an intensifier upgraded: very high → soaring; (4) buried verbs dug out: made the decision to → decided to, carry out a review of → review. Word count: 29 → 11.

Q1
Which is the most concise rewrite of "We will make a decision at this point in time"?
A) We will decide now.
B) We will make a decision at the present time.
C) We will proceed in order to arrive at a decision now.
Why: A digs out the verb (make a decision → decide) and replaces at this point in time with now. B swaps one wordy phrase for another; C adds padding.
Q2
When is nominalization the right move rather than strong verbs?
A) When the genre calls for formal, impersonal abstraction: match the tool to the goal
B) Never: strong verbs are always better than nouns
C) Always: nominalization is more correct than plain verbs
Why: neither style is "correct" in the abstract. Nominalization (C1 3.1) suits reports and legal texts; strong verbs suit emails, summaries and arguments. The writer chooses for the reader and purpose.
Q3 · new
Best edit of "Due to the fact that a large number of staff were absent, we made the decision to postpone the review"?
A) Due to many staff being absent, a decision was made to postpone.
B) Staff absent, review postponed.
C) Because many staff were absent, we postponed the review.

8.3 · A Position Paper & Its Defense (C25)

Exercise 1 — Listen to a defense (Script 8.3-A)
  1. That city centers should be closed to private cars.
  2. That such a ban would hurt local businesses. She answers with evidence: foot traffic on car-free streets tends to rise, not fall.
  3. She concedes that a blanket ban would be unfair, then clarifies that her proposal exempts accessible transportation and permit holders, so the principle stands if the exemptions are well designed.
  4. Whether the rise in foot traffic only happens in wealthy cities that could already afford good public transportation.
  5. She hedges it: the effect is clearest where transportation is strong, the causation may run both ways, and the ban works best alongside investment in transportation, not instead of it.
  6. She checks the question ("If I've understood your question, you're asking…"), concedes she can't be certain, and proposes a trial period with a review after a year.
Exercise 2 — The model paper

(a) Parts: ¶1 = 1 thesis · ¶2 = 2 argument A (health) · ¶3 = 3 argument B (learning, + equity) · ¶4 = 4 counter-argument · ¶5 = 5 rebuttal · ¶6 = 6 conclusion.

(b) Hedges (any three): broadly agree · up to two hours · generally reported · some studies suggest modest gains · hardly surprising · typically · often · may be unable · would appear to offer.

(c) Tools: participle clause: Given this shift, … · inversion: Not only would a later start protect… · Rarely does a single decision promise… · Nor would the benefit be evenly spread · conditional: Were the school day aligned…, teachers would spend… (inverted conditional) · cleft / fronting: It is for this reason that… · it is typically students with the longest commutes… who lose the most sleep · academic collocations: chronic sleep loss · well documented · modest gains · a related consideration · practical rather than fundamental · the weight of evidence · a rare combination of low cost and high return. Also: nominalization and reference (These concerns, … the change) and concession (Admittedly, it might be objected that…). About 480 words + title.

Examiner questions (good examples): "You say some studies suggest gains in grades. How strong and how consistent is that evidence?" · "Wouldn't later finishing times simply move the problem to the evening, when students do homework and jobs?" · "Why 9 a.m. rather than the 8:30 the pediatric organizations recommend?" · "What would change your mind?"

Planning frame, checklist, defense prep, rubric

Open tasks: no key. When you check planning frames (C24) and papers (after C25), use the paper rubric (materials set J). The two most common planning problems: a thesis that is a survey ("There are advantages and disadvantages…") and an objection that is a straw man ("Some people think children should never sleep"). Ask one question for each rather than rewriting it: "Would someone on the other side really say that? What would they actually say?"

Self-check
Q1
What single element most distinguishes a position paper from a simple opinion essay?
A) It is much longer and more detailed.
B) It stays neutral and presents every view equally.
C) It acknowledges a counter-argument and rebuts it.
Why: the concede-then-rebut move shows the writer has weighed the question. Length isn't the point (position papers are often short), and neutrality makes it a survey, not a position.
Q2
Someone raises a genuinely fair objection to your case. The most advanced response is to:
A) refuse to concede anything and restate your thesis more loudly
B) concede the fair point, then explain why your position still holds
C) immediately abandon your position and agree
Why: honest concession plus a reasoned "and yet" is the poised middle. A stonewalls; C collapses.
Q3 · new
You're not sure what exactly an examiner means. Which is the best start?
A) "Sorry, I don't understand. Next question, please."
B) "If I've understood your question, you're asking whether the evidence applies to smaller cities. To some extent, yes; however…"
C) Answer a different question that you prepared at home.
Why: B checks the question, buys a few seconds, and lets the examiner correct you before you answer. A refuses the question; C ignores it (examiners notice immediately).
Errors you will hear and see most (8.3)

In papers: a survey instead of a thesis · the thesis in the last paragraph · a straw-man objection · overclaiming ("This proves that…", "Everyone agrees…") · "According to me" / "In my opinion, I think" · "discuss about," "emphasize on," "explain me" · toolkit items forced in where they don't fit (three inversions in one paragraph) · mixed American and British spelling. In the defense: reading the paper aloud · answering the question they prepared instead of the one asked · collapsing ("You're right, my idea is bad") or stonewalling ("No, that's wrong") · "I am agree with you" · long silences before a hard answer. For range, praise genuine concessions, refined claims (So I'd put it more cautiously…), checking questions, and examiners' precise follow-ups.
