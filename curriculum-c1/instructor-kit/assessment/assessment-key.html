Scoring principles

Score meaning, not spelling, in Listening and Reading: an answer that shows understanding gets the point, even if it is misspelled ("the survay results"). In the one-word Use of English items and in Words & Phrases, the word must be right, because the word is what's being tested: deteriorrate earns the point (a spelling slip), but get bad does not. Circle items are right or wrong. Rewrites (Progress Test 3B) earn the point only when the target structure is correct; ignore small spelling slips elsewhere in the sentence. Writing and speaking use the checklists and rubrics below. Half points are not used. The tests use American English; accept British forms everywhere (the online lessons use many: nominalisation, analyse, organise, colour, centre, programme, pavement, flat, enquire, Ms without a period, Yours sincerely).

Three US/UK and register points to know. ① After a mandative trigger (insist, recommend, It is essential that…), Lesson 1.3 accepts three options: the subjunctive (that he attend, standard in American English), should + base (that he should attend, common in British English), and the indicative (that he attends, informal British). The tests reward the first two; the indicative is not an error in speech, but it earns no point in a rewrite that targets the subjunctive. ② If I was you is common in speech; don't penalize it in the interview, but were is required after inversion (Were I…). ③ Lesson 6.3 teaches British sign-offs; in American English, Sincerely, is standard. Accept Sincerely, Yours sincerely, Kind regards, Best regards.

Part 5 · Writing /10
  • Task & content 0–3. 3 = purpose stated first, a clear recommendation, the other side acknowledged and answered, a formal close, 130–160 words; 2 = one element weak or missing (usually the answer to the other side); 1 = the recommendation is unclear or the text is under about 100 words; 0 = off task.
  • Organization & register 0–2. 2 = logical paragraphs and a consistently formal register (no contractions, slang, or chatty asides); 1 = mostly formal with one or two slips (Hi all, gonna, lots of) or weak paragraphing; 0 = informal throughout.
  • Range 0–2. 2 = at least three Modules 1–4 structures used naturally (a mandative subjunctive, a hedged claim, a mixed or inverted conditional or provided that / unless / in case, a cleft, a nominal phrase, diplomatic distancing); 1 = one or two; 0 = none.
  • Accuracy 0–3. 3 = errors are rare and minor; 2 = occasional errors that never cause misunderstanding; 1 = frequent errors, but the recommendation is clear; 0 = meaning often lost.

Model answer (10/10, 152 words):

Dear Members of the Board,

I am writing to recommend that the monthly newsletter move to an email-only format from January.

The strongest argument is cost. Printing and postage currently account for almost a fifth of our communications budget, and an email version may well reach more members, since most now register online. It is essential, however, that no member be left out. I would therefore propose that printed copies remain available at the front desk for anyone who requests one.

Admittedly, some older members may find the change difficult. That said, were we to offer a short workshop on reading the newsletter by email, much of this concern would, I suspect, disappear. Provided that the front-desk copies continue, the risk of excluding anyone appears to be small.

Should you require any further information, I would be happy to attend the next meeting.

Sincerely,
Jordan Lee

Errors to expect (Progress Test 1)
  • The false twins may well / may as well (Listening 1, item 2) and habitual would with states ("I would have a dog", item 3). Both survive into C1 because they rarely block meaning. Recycle Lesson 1.2's "probability or decision?" test in warmers.
  • Third-person -s in the subjunctive ("require that every visitor wears", items 4 and 18). Speakers of languages with a rich subjunctive (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian) often grasp the idea at once but still add the -s, because English marks the mood with an absence.
  • Symmetrical tenses in mixed conditionals ("If I took… I'd be living", item 6; "I would have been a doctor now") and in case used as if (item 7). Recycle Lesson 2.1's "time each clause separately" routine.
  • Inversion slips: double tense marking ("Rarely does she makes", item 8), a missing auxiliary ("Never I had seen", item 11), and contracted negatives in inverted conditionals ("Hadn't I known…"). Learners who produce inversion freely in speech often lose it on paper under time pressure, and vice versa.
  • Frozen is in what-clefts ("What surprised everyone is how quickly…", item 9) and over-nominalization in rewrite 15 ("The expansion of the company's reduction…"). Praise the attempt; the fix is to put one working verb back.
  • Writing: a recommendation buried in the last paragraph; an acknowledgment of the other side with no answer; and register drift, where the text opens formally and ends "Thanks a lot, see you!" At C1, a register slip costs more than a grammar slip, so name it clearly and kindly.

Part 4 · Writing /30

Score the essay or report with the writing rubric below: five criteria × 0–3 = /15, then double it (/30). Before scoring, check the length: under about 180 words caps Task & content at 1; over about 330 words, score only the first 300 or so and note it as a concision next step. For Option B, the report must use the survey figures; a report that invents data, or ignores the table, is capped at 1 for Task & content.

Model answer, Option A (C1+, 30/30, about 270 words)

Few workplace technologies have spread as quietly as monitoring software. Programs that log keystrokes, emails, and websites visited are now marketed as tools for "productivity insight," and many employers have adopted them without consulting their staff. While some oversight of company equipment is clearly legitimate, this essay will argue that continuous, detailed monitoring is justified only in narrow circumstances and that, as a general practice, it does more harm than good.

The strongest case for monitoring is security. Firms that handle medical or financial data have a duty to detect leaks, and in such settings limited, clearly disclosed monitoring may well be necessary. Few employees would object to measures that protect clients' information.

The difficulty arises when monitoring shifts from protecting data to measuring people. Keystroke counts reward visible activity rather than thoughtful work: an analyst who spends an hour thinking through a problem appears idle, while a colleague who sends thirty trivial emails appears productive. There is also some evidence that constant surveillance erodes trust, and staff who feel distrusted are, unsurprisingly, less likely to go beyond the minimum.

Admittedly, managers of remote teams argue that without such tools they cannot tell who is working. This concern is understandable, yet it overlooks a simpler solution: judging staff by the results they deliver rather than by the minutes they log.

On balance, then, monitoring can be justified where the risk is specific and the rules are transparent, but not as a substitute for management. Were employers to measure outcomes instead of keystrokes, both productivity and trust would be likely to benefit.

Model answer, Option B (C1+, 30/30, about 235 words)

Report on Falling Attendance in Evening Classes

Introduction
This report sets out to identify the reasons for the recent fall in evening-class attendance at the Riverside Adult Learning Center and to recommend appropriate action. It is based on a survey of 120 learners carried out in October.

Findings
Average attendance has fallen from 85% last year to 64% this year. The most frequently reported reason was a clash with work: 58% of respondents stated that the 6–8 p.m. schedule conflicts with their shifts. In addition, 35% reported difficulty finding parking. By contrast, satisfaction with the teaching remains high, with 72% rating it good or excellent. Notably, 41% indicated that they would attend a Saturday-morning option.

Conclusions
The findings suggest that the decline is largely due to scheduling and access rather than to the quality of instruction. It would appear that many learners remain motivated but are unable to attend at the current times.

Recommendations
In light of these findings, it is recommended that a Saturday-morning section be introduced on a trial basis next term. It is also recommended that the center explore a parking arrangement with the neighboring library or provide clear information on public transportation. Finally, attendance should be reviewed at the end of the trial so that the effectiveness of these measures can be assessed.

Errors to expect (Final Test)
  • Listening A: sentences instead of keywords, so learners fall behind and miss items 5–7; and the example remembered instead of the point (writing "suburb" or "history" into the notes). Treat misses as a note-taking next step (Lesson 7.1), not a vocabulary problem.
  • Reading: treating every biased-sounding sentence as "opinion" (item 4), naming the topic instead of the claim (item 1: "laptops in class"), and confusing concede with refute. Learners who agree with the writer often score the weaknesses lower: Lesson 6.1's "test the arguments you like" is the next step.
  • Use of English: The reason is because (6), dangling participles (8; "Walking into the room, the noise…"), and the preposition near-misses comply to, aware about, an increase of (11, 13). Prepositions are the slowest C1 errors to fade; point learners to their Lesson 5.2 chunk list rather than to rules.
  • Over-formalizing in Part C (utilize for use elsewhere in the paper) and, in the writing, over-nominalized sentences that lose the agent ("The implementation of the utilization of…"). Lesson 8.2's "is there a verb hiding in that noun?" is the fix.
  • Essay: a thesis that announces a topic but takes no position; evidence dropped without explanation; a straw-man counter-argument; and new points in the conclusion. Report: "I think" and personal narrative instead of an objective tone, findings and conclusions mixed together, and vague recommendations ("the center should do something about parking").
  • Register drift in both options: sort of, a lot of, kind of in formal writing, or a chatty rhetorical question in a report. Hedging that tips into mush ("it could possibly perhaps be argued that it may…") is the C1 version of the same problem.

Speaking Rubric · Both Tests

Five criteria, each 0–3 (total /15). 0 = no response, or only L1. The bands follow the CEFR qualitative aspects of spoken language (range, accuracy, fluency, interaction, coherence, phonological control) and sociolinguistic appropriateness (register). The C1 column paraphrases the Council of Europe's C1 descriptors (CEFR Companion Volume, 2020). For the progress test, judge against Modules 1–4 language only.

CriterionNot yet C1 (1)C1 (2)C1+ strong (3)
Task & coherence /3 Solid B2: clear descriptions and a viewpoint with reasons, but a long turn is a sequence of points rather than a structure; conclusions are restated rather than drawn; the argument stiffens or collapses under push-back. Gives clear, detailed descriptions of complex subjects, integrating sub-themes, developing particular points, and rounding off with an appropriate conclusion. Produces clear, smoothly flowing, well-structured speech with controlled use of organizational patterns, connectors, and cohesive devices. A long turn that previews, develops, and lands its point with authority; qualifies and refines a position under challenge without losing its thread; the diplomatic message is gentle and unmistakably clear.
Range & register /3 A good B2 range, but relies on a few safe structures and general words (good, big, important); hedges are all-or-nothing (maybe / definitely); register shifts only in vocabulary, or not at all. A broad range of language that lets them select a formulation to express themselves clearly in an appropriate style on general, academic, and professional topics without having to restrict what they want to say. Adjusts the level of formality appropriately; uses hedging and distancing to suit the situation. Precise, flexible choice of structure and word for effect (inversion or a cleft at the right moment, calibrated modality, strong collocations and connotation); the whole register shifts as a set, including relaxed idiom and vague language where it fits.
Accuracy /3 Good control of B2 structures, but C1 forms (aspect, modal perfects, mixed conditionals, inversion) often break down in real time; errors are noticeable and not usually self-corrected. Consistently maintains a high degree of grammatical accuracy; errors are rare, difficult to spot, and generally corrected when they do occur. Complex structures are handled with ease even when the speaker's attention is on the argument; the few slips are corrected smoothly (or rather…) without breaking the flow.
Interaction /3 Takes part and responds, but pushes back bluntly or concedes too readily; struggles to get the floor, keep it, or repair a misunderstanding without help. Selects a suitable phrase from a readily available range of discourse functions to preface remarks, get the floor, or gain time and keep it while thinking. Relates their contribution skillfully to others'; acknowledges before challenging; paraphrases to check understanding. Handles push-back with poise: concedes the fair point, holds the sound one, and steers toward a solution; asks probing follow-up questions; negotiates an alternative in the diplomatic task.
Pronunciation & fluency /3 Generally fluent on familiar topics, but long silent pauses when searching; flat or equal stress hides the key point; rising statements make claims sound unsure. Expresses themselves fluently and spontaneously, almost effortlessly; only a conceptually difficult subject can hinder a natural, smooth flow. Varies intonation and places sentence stress correctly to express finer shades of meaning; lapses in stress or rhythm don't affect intelligibility. Uses prosody deliberately: contrastive stress to correct or highlight, the fall-rise for polite reservation, thought groups that make a long turn easy to follow; fills any pause with a phrase, not silence. Accent has no effect on clarity.

Writing Rubric · Final Test Part 4

Five criteria, each 0–3 (/15), then × 2 = /30. 0 = nothing written, or nothing in English. At C1 the learner can write clear, well-structured texts on complex subjects, underlining the relevant salient issues, expanding and supporting points of view at some length with subsidiary points, reasons, and relevant examples, and rounding off with an appropriate conclusion; they can also write clear, well-structured reports and select a style appropriate to the reader in mind (paraphrased from the CEFR). An assigned side is fine: score the argument, never the opinion.

CriterionNot yet C1 (1)C1 (2)C1+ strong (3)
Task & content /3 Under about 180 words, or a topic described rather than argued; the report ignores or invents data; points asserted without support. Essay: a specific, arguable thesis; each body paragraph develops one point with evidence and explanation; a real counter-argument acknowledged and rebutted. Report: the key figures selected and interpreted accurately; conclusions follow from findings; recommendations are specific and actionable. The strongest objection steel-manned and answered convincingly (essay); patterns and the most important contrast in the data highlighted, not just listed (report); the conclusion or recommendations leave the reader with a clear, earned "so what".
Organization & cohesion /3 Paragraphs without clear topic sentences; connectors repeated or mismatched to the logic; the report lacks headings or mixes findings with opinions. Clear architecture (hook → context → thesis; PEEL paragraphs; restate → synthesize → implication, or report headings in order); signposting matched to the logic; reference and summary nouns (this trend, such measures) link ideas across paragraphs. Topic sentences alone would summarize the argument; cohesion is achieved as much through reference, substitution, and given-before-new ordering as through connectors; nothing is signposted that doesn't need it.
Range /3 General vocabulary (good, big, a lot of, show) and a narrow set of B2 structures; phrasal verbs where the genre wants a formal equivalent. Precise academic and formal vocabulary (obtain, significant, deteriorate), accurate collocations and dependent prepositions, and several C1 structures (nominal phrases, participle clauses, a mixed or inverted conditional, a cleft, the subjunctive in recommendations). Structures and words chosen for effect and used sparingly (one well-placed inversion, not three); nominalization balanced with strong verbs, so the prose stays clear.
Accuracy /3 Frequent errors that make the reader stop and reread; dangling participles or preposition errors in several places. A high degree of grammatical accuracy; errors are occasional, minor, and never cause misunderstanding; punctuation of complex sentences mostly correct. Errors are rare and hard to spot; long sentences and punctuation are fully under control.
Register & tone /3 Conversational features in a formal text (sort of, gonna, chatty questions), or stiff over-formality (utilize, endeavor, commence everywhere); claims either over-stated ("this proves") or hedged into mush. A consistent academic (essay) or objective, impersonal (report) register; claims hedged to match the evidence; concise, without padding or empty intensifiers. Confident and measured: strong where the evidence is strong, cautious where it isn't; the writer's stance is clear without being loaded; every sentence earns its place.

A note on IELTS equivalence (approximate only)

Learners will ask, "What IELTS band is this?" Answer honestly: as a rough guide only, a secure C1 corresponds to about IELTS 7.0–8.0, and C2 to about 8.5–9 (published tables differ slightly at the boundaries; the B2 kit gives about 5.5–6.5 for a secure B2, and Lesson 4.3 of the online course gives the same C1 range). These are broad, published alignments, not a conversion of this test's score. No classroom test can predict an official band, and IELTS rewards exam technique and timing that this course doesn't train. A learner who needs a specific band, especially 7.5+ in Writing, should take the IELTS track and at least one full practice test before booking the real exam.

These are guidelines. Your thirteen weeks of observation count as much as one test on one day, especially the Class 12 and Class 25 review projects: a learner who wrote and defended a strong position paper yesterday has shown you exactly what C1 looks like. A learner who was ill, anxious, or grieving on test day deserves a re-test or a quick re-check with the shared placement test before any "repeat" recommendation. Record the outcome and a one-line reason for your program.

Giving Feedback Kindly

Principles
  • Private, always. Never read scores aloud or return papers in score order.
  • Strength first, then one next step. One concrete step, not a list of errors.
  • Name the progress. Compare with Week 1, not with classmates.
  • Accuracy, range, and appropriacy. At C1, the most useful feedback is often about choice, not correctness: "That was grammatical, but commence sounded stiff in a message to a friend. Would start fit better?"
  • Respect the professional. Many C1 learners are experts in their own fields. Frame feedback as one expert to another, and ask what they noticed first.
  • Written note to take home, with the lesson number and URL.
  • Adults decide. Offer the recommendation and the reason, and let the learner choose when the program allows it.
Phrases that work at C1
You did an excellent job. The way you conceded that point and then held your ground was genuinely impressive. Your thesis was sharp: I knew exactly where you stood by the second sentence. Your hedging was spot on: strong where the evidence was strong. Next step: watch the dangling participles. Ask "Who is doing this?" after every opening -ing. Do Lesson 5.2 again at home and keep adding to your preposition chunk list. Your next class: Mastery English (C2). One more lap of C1. This time it'll feel like refining, not learning. I've really enjoyed teaching you.
Thirteen weeks ago we were placing events in the future. Yesterday you defended a position paper under questioning.
Your speaking is ready for C2. Your writing needs a little more time, so let's find a writing partner or a writing center to go with it.
Avoid

Red ink everywhere, percentages without meaning ("68%"), the word "fail", comparisons ("Ana got 92"), and correcting a learner's accent as though it were an error: at C1, clarity comes from prosody, not from sounding "native". Don't grade opinions: a learner who argues the opposite of your view, clearly and politely, or who argued an assigned side, has done exactly what C1 asks. High-achieving adults can take a "repeat" recommendation hard; if a learner goes quiet or gets upset, pause the conversation and come back to it. The number can wait.
