Progress Test 1 — Modules 1–4

A halfway check after Lesson 4.3. It covers the advanced future forms (future continuous, future perfect simple and continuous, the future in the past), degrees of certainty (may well / might as well, the jobs of will and would, continuous and perfect modals), the mandative subjunctive and unreal were, as if + past, mixed conditionals and the alternatives to if, inversion, cleft sentences and fronting, nominalization, reference, substitution and ellipsis, hedging and diplomatic softening, phrasal verbs, collocation, idiom and connotation, and the register shift of the Lesson 4.3 project. It tells you what to recycle in Weeks 7–13. It does not rank anyone.

⏱ ~58 min written + 8 min pair speaking Written /60 · Speaking /15 · Total /75 CEFR C1
Why a progress test, and how to frame it

C1 learners are usually experienced test-takers, and many are sitting this one with a university application, a professional license, or a promotion in mind. That can make them more anxious, not less: a high-achieving adult often hears a score as a verdict. Say it plainly: "This is a check, not an exam. It tells me which parts of Modules 1–4 are secure and which ones we should revisit, and it shows you how much more precise your English is than it was in Week 1." Remind the class to skim the questions before they read or listen, and do every example item together. Read the listening at a natural pace with natural contractions and weak forms (I'd've, may as well, it was the price that): at C1, hearing them in connected speech is part of the test, so don't over-articulate.

What each part checks
  • Listening /10: hearing fine contrasts in connected speech (modality, inversion, clefts, the tactful future continuous), a short briefing, and diplomatic disagreement with understatement
  • Words & phrases /10: collocation and phrasal verbs (4.1–4.2), then connotation and register (4.1–4.2)
  • Grammar /20: 10 circle items + 10 rewrites (inversion, clefts, nominalization, hedging, the subjunctive, but for, diplomatic distancing)
  • Reading /10: a magazine column, "Quiet Mornings", read for detail, reference, hedging, understatement, and the writer's position
  • Writing /10: a short formal recommendation to a board (130–160 words)
  • Speaking /15: projections and what-ifs, a discussion that reaches a compromise, and a quick register shift (pairs)
Literacy-adapted version (learners whose reading and writing lag behind their speaking)

At C1 this is less common, but you may have a learner whose English was built largely by ear at work, or who has a diagnosed reading difficulty such as dyslexia. Use the same paper and change how it is done, not what it tests. Read every instruction and item aloud, one at a time, and enlarge the paper if you can. In Parts 2–3, read the choices aloud and accept a spoken answer; for the rewrites (11–20), let the learner say the new sentence and score what they say. Do Part 4 orally: read the column aloud twice and ask the questions. In Part 5, the learner may dictate the recommendation while you scribe; score its content, organization, register, and language, not the handwriting or spelling. Give extra time, or split the paper over two sittings. Write "Adapted" at the top and note what changed. Report these learners' listening and speaking as the main evidence. A literacy gap is not a language gap, and the key explains how to act on it.

Personal information and opinions are always optional

The speaking task asks about plans, regrets, and turning points; the discussion and the writing ask for a position. Learners can answer with a "new identity" (the cards on the teacher pages) or an invented story, and in the discussion and the recommendation they may argue an assigned side that isn't their own view. Say this before the test starts: "Real or imagined, your side or the other side: it makes no difference. I'm scoring the English, not your life or your opinions." Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status.

📖Skim the questions first. Then read the column.
Quiet Mornings: Should the Office Go Silent Until Noon?

Last spring, a mid-sized software company in Portland did something many of its employees had long wished for: it banned internal meetings before noon. Staff were told that mornings would be reserved for "deep work" (writing, coding, planning) and that anything urgent should be handled by message. Twelve months on, the results are, by most accounts, encouraging.

According to the company's own survey, 71 percent of employees say they now finish complex tasks faster, and the number of evening emails has fallen by almost a third. This, the company argues, is evidence that the policy has reduced stress as well as saved time. It may well have done so; however, a survey of a single company, carried out by that company, can hardly be considered conclusive.

Not everyone has welcomed the change. Customer-facing teams, in particular, report that clients who call in the morning are often left waiting. "I'm not entirely convinced it works for us," one account manager admitted, choosing her words carefully. Others worry that the rule simply pushes meetings into the afternoon, where they now crowd together. The complaints so far may be only the tip of the iceberg: it is too early to know whether collaboration will suffer in the long run.

What the experiment does suggest is that many meetings were never necessary in the first place. Had managers asked "Does this need to be a meeting?" more often, perhaps no rule would have been needed at all. Quiet mornings are unlikely to suit every organization, but they do raise a question worth asking in any workplace: how much of our working day do we actually control?

Part 5 · Writing

___ / 10
✏️Write a formal recommendation. (130–160 words)

Your community center's board is deciding whether to replace its printed monthly newsletter with an email-only version. Write to the board and recommend one option. You may recommend either option. It doesn't have to be your personal view.

  • State your purpose in the first sentence.
  • Make your recommendation with a formal structure such as I recommend that… / It is essential that…
  • Include at least one hedged claim and one conditional (a mixed or inverted conditional, or provided that / unless / in case).
  • Acknowledge the other side diplomatically, then answer it. Close formally.
I am writing to recommend that … It is essential that … be … This may well … There is some evidence that … Admittedly, … That said, … Provided that … Were the board to …, … Should you require further information, … Sincerely,

Finished. Well done! Now reread your answers, especially the rewrites.

Part 1 · Listening Scripts

Give 30 seconds to read the questions for each section. Read each script twice at a natural pace, with a pause of about 10 seconds between readings. Say the item number first ("Number one"). Don't mouth the answer or point. In Part A, say the sentence once per reading, with natural weak forms and contractions: may as well runs together as /meɪəzwel/, and Had I known keeps a full, stressed Had. The contrast is the test. For Part C, change your voice or position for each speaker, or read it with a colleague.

Good evening, everyone, and thank you for coming. As many of you know, by next spring this clinic will have been serving the east side for ten years, and I'd like us to mark that by trying something new. Pair Speaking Task · 8 minutes

Two learners sit with you. Give Card A to one and Card B to the other. Give them one minute to read the cards silently, then check the instructions:

In Part 1, do you just answer, or do you ask a follow-up question too? (Both.) Short answers or developed ones? (Developed: reasons, detail, and how sure you are.) In Part 2, do you have to argue your real opinion? (No. You can take the side on the card.) What do you need to reach by the end? (A compromise.) Real life or imagined? (Either is fine.)

Part 1 (about 3 minutes): they ask and answer the card questions, which cover projections, past plans, habits, and what-ifs. Encourage one real follow-up each. Part 2 (about 4 minutes): give them the discussion card. They discuss the proposal, hedge their claims, disagree diplomatically (acknowledge → soften → challenge), and work toward a compromise. If a pair doesn't want to choose sides, assign them: Student A argues for, Student B against. Part 3 (about 1 minute): ask each learner to give their conclusion twice, once "as if to a committee" and once "as if to a friend over coffee." Listen for vocabulary, grammar, and structure shifting together (I would suggest that we trial… → Let's just try it for a month). Step in only to restart a stuck pair ("Now ask your next question"). Don't correct during the task. Score each learner on the grid, using the rubric in the key. Mixed-level pairs: score each person on what they did, not on the pair's result.

Discussion card · both students

💬 Part 2 · The proposal

"Our workplace should replace the weekly all-staff meeting with a short written update." Discuss it for about 4 minutes.

  • Give at least two reasons for your side, and hedge at least one claim: This may well…, There's some evidence that…
  • Disagree diplomatically: I take your point, but I'm not sure… · I see it slightly differently.
  • Use at least one conditional: If we'd…, we'd be… · Provided that… · Were we to…
  • Reach a compromise at the end. No preference? Student A argues for, Student B against.

After the test: close the loop in Week 7

Score the papers before Class 14 with the key. Add up each section across the class, because the weakest section tells you what to recycle. Warmers in Classes 14–19 are a good place for it: for example, a 5-minute "Front it and flip it" relay (neutral → inversion) if rewrites 11–12 and 17 were weak, or a "Decide the time of each half" board race if items 6 and 16 were weak. Nominalization (rewrite 15) links naturally to Module 5's participle clauses and academic vocabulary, so recycle it there. Give each learner their paper back with one thing they did well and one thing to refine, plus the online lesson that practices it. The key has a class analysis and feedback phrases.
