Final Test — C1 Exit Check

The end-of-course check on all eight modules, taken on the last day, which is also a celebration. It shows each learner what they can now do in English: follow a lecture and capture its structure in notes, read an argument critically and name its weaknesses, write a structured essay or report in a controlled academic or professional register, use the precision grammar and vocabulary of C1, and hold their own in an individual interview with a long turn, push-back, and a diplomatic task. It also gives you the evidence for the next step: Mastery English (C2), C2 with a review plan, another round of C1 with more support, or a specialist track (IELTS Academic or Business English). It is never a pass/fail verdict on a person.

⏱ ~60 min written + ~10 min interview each Written /85 · Speaking /15 · Total /100 Covers Modules 1–8
Frame it as "show what you can do"

By Class 26 your learners have met you 25 times, and yesterday they presented and defended a position paper. Use that: "Thirteen weeks ago we were placing events in the future with the future perfect. Yesterday you wrote a position paper, conceded a fair point under questioning, and still held your ground, all in English." Do every example item together, keep the room calm and unhurried, and plan the celebration so it happens whatever the scores are. Every learner gets a certificate of attendance. The test decides the next class, not who gets to celebrate.

Literacy-adapted version

Same paper, different delivery. Read all instructions and items aloud, enlarge the paper, and give extra time or split it over two sittings. Part 1: the learner may say the missing keywords while you write them. Parts 2–3: read the column and the choices aloud and accept a spoken answer (for the one-word items, the learner says the word and you check the form). Part 4: the learner dictates the essay or report while you scribe, and you score it with the same rubric. Mark the paper "Adapted". For these learners, the interview and listening are the main evidence for placement. The key explains how to recommend C2 together with literacy support instead of holding someone back for reading and writing alone.

Real or imagined, your side or an assigned side

The interview asks about change, decisions, and opinions. For some learners, questions about work and the past touch on a lost profession, a hard journey, or a qualification that isn't recognized here yet. Say before you start: "Real or imagined, no problem. And in the discussion, I'll argue against you on purpose. That's my job today, not my real opinion. You can take any side you like." Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status. If a learner says "I'd rather not talk about that," thank them and move to the next stage.

☐ Option A · An argument essay

"Some employers now use software that records how much time staff spend typing, reading email, and visiting websites. To what extent is this kind of monitoring justified?"

Write four or five paragraphs: an introduction (hook → context → thesis), body paragraphs that each develop one point with evidence or an example, a paragraph that acknowledges a counter-argument and rebuts it, and a conclusion that restates, synthesizes, and points to an implication, with no new points. You may argue either side. It doesn't have to be your personal view.

Part 1 · Listening Scripts

Give 60 seconds to read the notes frame in Part A and 30 seconds for each later section. Read each script twice at a natural pace, and pause about 10 seconds between readings. Read Part A as a real lecture: slow down very slightly and stress each signpost (first… that brings me to… as an aside… finally… to recap… the upshot is…), exactly as Lesson 7.1 taught learners to expect. Don't pause at the gaps or repeat the numbers. For Part B, change your voice or position for each speaker, or read it with colleagues, and say the item number before each numbered speaker ("Ten.") so learners can keep their place. A · Mini-lecture: "Urban heat" (read the whole lecture twice, then give 1 minute)
Good afternoon. Today I'd like to talk about something you've almost certainly felt, even if you've never had a name for it: the urban heat island. I'll argue that heat in cities is not just a matter of weather; it's largely a matter of design. I'll make three points: first, why cities get hotter than the countryside around them; second, who pays the price; and third, the one I care about most, what can realistically be done.
So, first, the cause. Dark surfaces (asphalt, roofs, parking lots) absorb the sun's heat during the day and release it slowly at night. On a summer evening, a city center can be up to seven degrees Celsius warmer than nearby farmland. Crucially, the difference is often greatest at night, which is exactly when our bodies need to cool down.
That brings me to my second point: who pays the price. As an aside, I won't dwell on the history of air conditioning, fascinating as it is. The key thing is that heat is not shared equally. Neighborhoods with few trees are, on average, noticeably hotter than leafy ones, and they are often the areas where people can least afford to run air conditioning. A case in point is heat-related illness among older residents living alone, which rises sharply during long hot spells.
Finally, and most importantly, what can we do? The answers are not new: plant trees, paint roofs a lighter color, and replace some paved areas with green space. Street trees, in particular, can lower the temperature of the sidewalk beneath them by as much as twenty degrees. None of this is expensive compared with the cost of doing nothing.
So, to recap: cities trap heat because of the materials we build with; the burden falls hardest on those with the fewest resources; and the solutions are already within reach. The upshot is that urban heat is a design problem, which means it is also a problem we can design our way out of.

Individual Speaking Interview · about 10 minutes

Sit beside the learner, not across a desk, with the grid on your lap. Speak at a natural pace, with natural contractions. Ask the core question first. If there's no answer after about 5 seconds, use the support version (rephrase, narrow the question, or give a model). If the answer comes easily, add the stretch question. At C1, listen for precision and control: a long turn that is organized and signposted, claims calibrated to the evidence, an argument that bends without breaking under push-back, and a register that shifts on purpose. Don't correct during the interview. Note one strength and one next step instead. Before stage 1, let the learner choose a long-turn card and think about it quietly (about 1 minute) while you do the warm-up.

1

Warm-up: work, study & what's next

~1 min
Hello! How are you today? So, tell me a little about what you've been working on or studying this term. (real or imagined)
And what do you think you'll be doing this time next year?

Support: "Is that at work, in your studies, or somewhere else?" · Stretch: "What will you have achieved by then, do you think, and how sure are you?" (future perfect + the certainty dial)

2

Long turn (monologue)

~3 min
Now please talk about the card you chose, for about two minutes. I'll just listen.

Don't interrupt. Time about 2 minutes; stop kindly at 2½. Then ask one follow-up question about the content. Support: if they freeze, ask "What's your main point?" then "Can you give me an example?" · Stretch: "What's the strongest objection someone could raise to that?" or "How confident are you in that, and why?" Listen for a preview and a recap, signposting, calibrated certainty (may well, it would appear that), and at least one structure used for effect (an inversion, a cleft, a mixed conditional).

3

Discussion with push-back

~3 min

Read one discussion statement below. Whatever the learner says, take the other side and push back twice, politely but firmly, so they must defend, concede, or refine their view. The second push-back should target their evidence ("How do you know that?") or expose a hidden assumption. Your push-back is a role, not your opinion.

Here's a statement: [statement]. Where do you stand on that? … Why? … I'm not sure I agree. Surely [counter-argument]? … But how do you know that [their claim] is actually true? … So, on balance, where does that leave you?

Support: "Do you agree or disagree?" then "Why?" and one push-back only · Stretch: "What would it take to change your mind?" · "Can you put the other side's case as fairly as you can?" Listen for acknowledge → soften → challenge (I take your point, but…), hedging that matches the evidence, a concession that doesn't collapse the argument, and a refined final position.

4

Diplomatic task + register shift

~2½ min

The learner chooses one task card. Part 1: you play the other person (client, manager, or colleague) and react naturally, including one mild objection. Part 2: switch. You are now the learner's friend, and they tell you what happened, informally.

(Client) I'm calling about our order. Is everything on schedule? … Two weeks? That's a real problem for us. … What can you offer?
(Manager) I've decided to move our team meeting to 7:30 on Monday mornings. It'll get things started early. What do you think? … But everyone's fresh at that time, surely?
(Colleague) Could you take over the Henderson project next week? I'm completely swamped. … Are you sure? It would really help me out.
(Switch) OK, now I'm your friend. Hey! So how did that go? What did they say?

Listen for preface → soften → reason (I'm afraid… · the thing is… · because…), distancing (I was wondering whether… · Would it be possible to…?), an alternative offered, and a message that stays clear under the cushioning. In Part 2, listen for the whole register shifting together: contractions, phrasal verbs, vague language, reported speech. Support: model the first line ("I'm afraid I have some difficult news…"). Stretch: push back a second time and see whether they hold a polite line.

5

Closing + your question

~½ min
Thank you, that's everything. Before you go, is there anything you'd like to ask me? About the course, what comes next, anything at all.
