Module 3 — Cohesion, Register & Style: Answers

Answers to every Module 3 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the two-register rewrite, the repaired reports, the difficult conversations), and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 3.1–3.3Classes C7–C99 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. Most items in this module are rewrites with many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps the original meaning and every fact, (2) is grammatically accurate (the real noun, the right preposition after it, so / not, the anchor word in ellipsis), and (3) fits the reader and the room: nominal and impersonal for a report, plain and direct for a notice, softened but clear for a difficult conversation. When a learner's version is correct but differs from the target structure, accept it and point to the target: "Good. Can you also do it with a light verb?" American English is the model; accept British spellings (nominalisation, behaviour, analyse, favour). Contractions are fine in speech and in the plain-English notices, not in the formal summaries.

3.1 · Nominalization & Academic Style (C7)

Exercise 1 — The update and the summary (Script 3.1-A)
1234567
introductionassessmentincreasefailurereductionapprovalimplementation

Spoken verb → noun: brought in → introduction · looked at how they did → assessment · went up → increase · failed → failure · dropped → reduction · said yes → approval · roll out → implementation. Accept evaluation for 2 and rise for 3 if the learner hears them that way; they fit. Spelling errors to expect: assesment, implemantation, faillure.

Exercise 2 — Form the noun
  1. development
  2. awareness
  3. arrival
  4. ability
  5. failure
  6. analysis
  7. strength
  8. (an) increase (zero-derivation)
  9. belief
  10. refusal
  11. complexity
  12. dependence (also dependency)
  13. choice
  14. growth

Stress: 4 Able → aBIlity (moves) · 8 to inCREASE → an INcrease (moves) · 11 comPLEX / COMplex → comPLEXity (the stress lands just before -ity). Wrong: "the arrivement," "ableness," "the failment," "analyst" (a person), "strongness," "the choosing," "believe" as a noun.

Exercise 3 — Light verbs and prepositions
  1. carried out / conducted · into (also of with conducted)
  2. reached / made / came to
  3. poses · to
  4. had · on
  5. places / placed / puts · on
  6. provided / offered / given · for
  7. in
  8. draw / reach

Wrong: "made an investigation," "did a decision," "has an impact in," "an increase of demand," "an explanation of the delay" (possible, but for is the natural choice after provide an explanation). take a decision is British; accept it.

Exercise 4 — From verbal to academic (models; wording may vary)
  1. An investigation was carried out into the failure of the system. / The causes of the system failure were investigated.
  2. The company's rapid expansion led to a reduction in its unit costs.
  3. No explanation was provided for the difference in the results.
  4. Population aging is driving a rise in healthcare costs. / The aging of the population is leading to an increase in healthcare costs.
  5. A cut in the training budget would lead to a decline in staff motivation. / Cuts to the training budget would result in lower staff morale.
  6. Growing awareness of the sugar content of drinks has led to a decline in their consumption. / Increased public awareness of sugar levels in drinks has resulted in a fall in sales.

Check: the main action is a noun, the agent (we, they, people) is gone, the preposition after the noun is right (a reduction in, a rise in, an explanation for). In 5, the conditional becomes would lead to: a nice C1 move. Appropriacy flag: a version with four or more stacked nouns (the reduction of the allocation of the budget for training…) is over-nominalized: send it to Ex 5.

Exercise 5 — Back to plain English (models)
  1. The reorganization aims to improve communication.
  2. The committee considered postponing the vote.
  3. Using the software improved the accuracy of the measurements. / The software made the measurements more accurate. (accuracy can sensibly stay: it names a quality.)
  4. We'll finish repairing the elevator as soon as the new parts arrive.
  5. Please confirm that your child will attend before the program starts.

Accept any version with working verbs and a clear agent. Items 4 and 5 should address the reader (we, you, please). Not yet: versions that change only one noun and keep "prior to the commencement of."

Dialogue — two readers, one fact

Nominalizations: summary · introduction · rise · (visitor) numbers · extension · necessity · recruitment · (the) cost. The over-nominalized sentence is Nadia's "The extension of hours resulted in the necessity of the recruitment of additional staff." Tom fixes it first with plain verbs ("meant we had to hire"), then with a middle version: one working noun (recruitment) and one clear verb (required). Rooms: Nadia's first sentence = a chat; "The introduction of extended opening hours led to a rise in visitor numbers" = the report; "We're now open until 9 p.m.!" = the poster.

Speaking — Register Workshop

Model, Round 1 (Source 1, the four-day week): "A three-month trial of a four-day working week was carried out. The results showed a 12% increase in closed support tickets and a reduction in sick leave. However, there were complaints from customers about the lack of availability on Fridays. A solution to this problem will be required before a final decision is reached." Models for all six source cards are printed small on each card (classroom materials, set C). Check the tick-boxes: 4+ nominalizations, 2 light verbs, 1 passive, 1 there is / are, no I / we / they, facts unchanged.

Model, Round 2 (Notice 1, elevator repairs): "We're still repairing the elevator. We'll finish as soon as the new parts arrive, and we'll let you know when it's working again. If you need help carrying anything heavy before then, please call the management office." All four models are on the notice cards. The "Who does what?" test should pass instantly for every plain-English sentence.

Self-check
Q1
Which is the correct noun formed from the adjective able?
A) ability
B) ableness
C) ablement
Why: adjectives often take -ity (stable → stability), and able changes its spelling: ability. B and C are invented forms.
Q2
A friendly email says: "The facilitation of the enhancement of team communication is the objective of the reorganization." Best advice?
A) Add more nominalizations.
B) It's over-nominalized: rebalance with plain verbs.
C) Leave it: it's grammatical.
Why: four abstract nouns hide who does what, and the register is wrong for a friendly email. Better: "We're reorganizing to help the team communicate better." Grammatical (C) is not the same as appropriate.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct and suits a formal report?
A) …a sharp increase of demand…
B) …demand kind of went up a lot.
C) The survey showed a sharp increase in demand for evening classes.
Why: the noun increase takes in (A has the wrong preposition, a common transfer from aumento de / augmentation de); B uses vague spoken language in a report.
Errors you will meet most (3.1)

Invented nouns ("arrivement," "failment," "the deciding") · wrong preposition after the noun ("an increase of," "an impact in," "an investigation about") · process vs person nouns ("the analyst of the data") · missing or extra articles with abstract nouns ("Decision to expand led to reduction…" / "The education is important") · wrong stress on derived nouns (STA-bi-li-ty) · over-nominalization presented as good style · "did / made an investigation" for carried out / conducted. For range, praise well-controlled noun phrases (a gradual but measurable improvement in…) and light verbs with adjectives (a thorough investigation). For appropriacy, praise the learner who chose plain English for the notice.

3.2 · Cohesion — Reference, Substitution & Ellipsis (C8)

Exercise 1 — The handover call (Script 3.2-A)
  1. it = the supplier's invoice
  2. do it = check the invoice
  3. ones = chairs (the new chairs)
  4. have = (have) sent the figures to finance
  5. so = (that) the budget will be approved on Monday
  6. not = (that) the delivery date will move again → "I hope it won't"
  7. the former = the meeting-room order
  8. the latter = the training-room order

Priya still has to check the invoice (tomorrow) and deal with the meeting-room order (urgent). The training-room order can wait. Accept any wording that shows the right target.

Exercise 2 — Replace the repetition
  1. "Do you want the big suitcase or the small one?"
  2. "You should apologize. If you fail to do so, she'll never forgive you." (also: If you don't (do so)…)
  3. "I believe so."
  4. "He was rude to a customer in public. Such behavior is unacceptable."
  5. "We considered Chicago and Denver; we chose the latter."
  6. "I'm afraid not."
  7. "Energy prices doubled last winter. This increase / This rise hit small businesses hardest."

Wrong: 3 "I believe yes" · 5 "the former" (= Chicago: wrong meaning) · 6 "I'm afraid no" / "I'm not afraid so" · 7 "This hit…" (acceptable but vague; the exercise asks for a summary noun). Note in 2 that do so sounds formal; in speech "If you don't, …" is more natural.

Exercise 3 — Drop it (ellipsis)
  1. She wanted to come, but she couldn't.
  2. I haven't seen the movie, but my brother has.
  3. "I'd love to."
  4. He was tired but happy.
  5. The trip cost far more than we had expected. (also: …than we had expected it to.)
  6. The first group finished early; the second (group) did not. / …the second didn't.

Wrong: 1 "but she couldn't come it" · 2 "but my brother has seen" (anchor + part of the verb) or "but my brother" (no anchor) · 3 "I'd love." · 6 "the second not." Point out that 6 is fine in formal writing: ordinary ellipsis works in every register.

Exercise 4 — Fix the faulty reference
  1. "The teacher praised the student's essay, and the praise / this recognition made her proud."
  2. "There's still some milk if you want some."
  3. "I hope not."
  4. "He resigned suddenly, which everyone found strange. The resignation came in March." (also: He resigned suddenly in March, which everyone found strange.)
  5. "I think so." (negative: "I don't think so")
  6. "I'd love to, but I'm working."

In 1, it could point at the essay or the praise; a summary noun removes the doubt. In 4, This could point at "everyone found it strange" rather than the resignation. Accept restructured sentences that remove the ambiguity.

Exercise 5 — A short repair (model, 44 words from 84)

"The office is moving to the third floor on Friday, so please pack your desk by Thursday. If you can't, tell Maria. She has boxes: large ones for files and small ones for personal items. The IT team will move the computers on Saturday."

Devices: reference (She), substitution (ones ×2), ellipsis (If you can't). Also fine: "…This move means…", "…and small ones for personal things." Check that no fact is lost (third floor, Friday, Thursday, Maria, two kinds of box, IT, Saturday).

Dialogue — two reports

Reference: it (the long report) · they (the client) · it (the summary). Substitution: the short one / the long one · I think so · I'll do so · I'll write one · Did they? (= Did they ask for a summary?) · Such requests (reference + summary). Ellipsis: the long one, not yet · I didn't get to · I'll try to · I can. The slightly formal devices for a chat between colleagues: I'll do so and Such requests (Ben is being a little playful; "I'll send them this afternoon" is more usual).

Speaking — Repair the Report (models, 90 words or fewer)

Report 1, the office kitchen (77 words): "The office kitchen, designed for 20 staff, is now used by 45. At lunchtime, staff wait up to 15 minutes for the two old microwaves, one of which often breaks. The fridge is always full and rarely cleaned. To solve these problems, staff have suggested buying two new microwaves and cleaning the fridge every Friday, and we recommend doing so. Both changes would make staff happier. The former would cost about $400, which we consider reasonable."

Report 2, the composting pilot (76 words): "In April, the tenants' association started a three-month composting pilot. Thirty households put food waste in two compost bins in the back yard, producing 900 pounds of compost for the community garden. In hot weather in June, some tenants complained about the smell. Once the bins were moved away from the windows, these complaints stopped. The association wants to continue the pilot and add two more bins. If the landlord agrees, it will do so in September."

Report 3, the customer survey (87 words): "In May, Spoke & Chain Bike Repair surveyed 120 customers about prices, speed and service. Most were happy with the first and the last, but many were not with the speed: repairs took an average of six days, while customers want them to take two or three. Some suggested an online booking system, which would let them book a repair time; others, longer opening hours. The owner likes the former but not the latter, which would cost too much, and will introduce online booking in the summer."

"Point at it" check for Report 3: the former = online booking, the latter = longer hours, which = longer hours. If a group writes "It would cost too much" after mentioning both options, flag the it. Also accept participle clauses and other C1 compression (producing 900 pounds) even though they aren't cohesion devices.

Self-check
Q1
"Tenants must vacate by June. Those who fail to ___ will face penalties." Which fits a formal notice best?
A) do it
B) make so
C) do so
Why: do so replaces a stated verb phrase (vacate by June) in formal writing. do it is concrete and conversational; make so doesn't exist.
Q2
Which sentence uses ellipsis correctly and naturally?
A) "I haven't read the memo, but Sara has."
B) "Would you like to stay?" — "I'd love."
C) "She can swim, and she can also swim well."
Why: the auxiliary has stays as the anchor. B drops the anchor to; C has no ellipsis and sounds odd (She can swim, and well, too).
Q3 · new
"Will the shipment be delayed again?" You don't want a delay.
A) I don't hope so.
B) I hope not.
C) I hope no.
Why: hope and be afraid make the negative with not after the verb. think, believe, expect usually negate the verb (I don't think so), but I don't hope so is unnatural. C is a transfer from espero que no.
Errors you will meet most (3.2)

"I think yes / I hope no / I don't hope so" · "a coffee one / a milk one / the advices ones" · "I'd love." / "but Sara." (no anchor) · vague it / this pointing at two possible ideas · gendered he / she for things ("the proposal… she is too expensive") · dropped subjects and objects ("Is very expensive." "I bought the report and read.") · the former / the latter in casual speech · "I did so" in a chat where "I did" is natural. For range, praise this / such + summary noun, those who fail to do so in writing, and fluent spoken ellipsis (I meant to, but I didn't get to).

3.3 · Hedging, Vague Language & Diplomatic English (C9)

Exercise 1 — Feedback on a draft (Script 3.3-A)
1234567
a couple of thoughtsreally solidmaybe a bit longI wonder ifnot entirely convincedmight be worthWould it be possible

Dev must: cut the events section to five, rethink the headline (ask the volunteers), and send a new version by Thursday. This is the point of the exercise: the feedback is soft but completely clear.

Exercise 2 — Soften the blunt statements (models; many answers possible)
  1. "There are just a few small things in the report I think we could tighten up."
  2. "I'm afraid I won't be able to help with this one. The thing is, I'm really stretched this week. Could you ask Maya?"
  3. "I see what you mean, though I'm not entirely convinced. Could it also be that…?"
  4. "Would it be possible to send me the file when you get a moment?"
  5. "I'm a little concerned the deadline might be tight. Would it be possible to push it back a week, or to reduce the scope?"
  6. "Unfortunately, we're not able to offer a discount on this order. The thing is, our prices already include delivery. We could perhaps look at a discount on your next order, though."

Check: at least one softener (distancing, minimizer, preface) and the message is still there. Not yet: "Please give me the file now" (still an order) · "Maybe the report has some little problems, possibly" (vague and over-hedged) · 5 "You must change the deadline" (too strong to a manager).

Exercise 3 — Hedge the over-strong claims (models)
  1. "This study suggests that remote work may increase productivity, at least in some contexts."
  2. "Many learners seem to benefit from visual aids, to some extent." / "Visual aids appear to help a large number of learners."
  3. "There is some evidence that online courses may be less effective than classroom teaching for some learners, although results vary considerably."
  4. "Moderate coffee consumption appears to improve concentration in the short term for many people."

Look for two devices from different families (modal + verb, verb + adverbial, frame + modal). Over-hedged (flag it): "It could perhaps possibly be argued that coffee might arguably sometimes help some people to some extent." Wrong register: "Coffee sort of helps you concentrate."

Exercise 4 — Which toolkit?

1 H (report, essay) · 2 D (spoken or written request) · 3 V (casual conversation) · 4 H (formal writing) · 5 D (neutral–formal request) · 6 V (casual; like and or so) · 7 D (meeting: polite disagreement) · 8 H (academic writing)

Exercise 5 — What do they really mean?

1 b · 2 d · 3 a · 4 e · 5 c

5 is the one learners underestimate: "a slight issue" can signal a serious problem, especially in British English. Tone and context decide.

Dialogue — a request, a refusal, an alternative

Omar's preface: "Ah. As much as I'd like to, I'm afraid…" · soften: "Thursday's not going to work" · reason: "The thing is, I'm with a client all afternoon." · alternative: "Perhaps we could ask Jin instead? … Or couldn't we record a short video?" and "I'm happy to help you record it on Wednesday." Distancing: "I was wondering if you could do me a favor" · "Would it be possible for you to…?" Negative questions: "couldn't we record a short video?" · "Wouldn't it be worth asking?" Also: minimizer "a bit of a problem" and litotes-style "I'm not entirely sure."

Speaking — Diplomatic Role-Play Series (model turns for A)

① Bad news (v1): "I'm afraid I have some difficult news about the brochures. Unfortunately, our paper supplier is running late, so the full order won't be ready until the 18th, not the 4th. I know that's not what you were hoping to hear, especially with the event on the 10th. What we can do is have 500 copies printed locally by the 4th, at no extra cost, and deliver the rest on the 18th. Would that work for you?"

② Disagreeing with a manager (v1): "I can see why next week is attractive, especially with the competitor's launch. With respect, I'm not entirely convinced the app is ready yet. In testing we saw double bookings, and I'd be worried about the effect on customers. Would it be possible to test it with fifty real customers for two more weeks? Or couldn't we launch to a small group first?"

③ Declining (v2): "Thanks for thinking of me. I'd really like to help, but I'm afraid I can't take on a third project right now. The thing is, the Harris report is due on Friday. I could start in two weeks, or, if another deadline can move, I could take on the data cleaning. Would either of those work?"

④ Feedback (v1): "Can I share a couple of thoughts on the slides? The content's really strong, and the chart on slide 3 is excellent. One thing I'd suggest is that some slides are a bit crowded, so the audience might struggle to follow. I wonder if we could keep each slide to about thirty words and move the detail into a handout? I'm happy to look at it with you tomorrow."

"What must change?" test, target sentences for B: ① "I'll get 500 on the 4th and the rest on the 18th." · ② "We'll test for two more weeks / launch to a small group first." · ③ "You can't start this week, but you can start in two weeks or do the data cleaning." · ④ "Thirty words or fewer per slide and a handout." If B's sentence doesn't match, discuss what was missing, usually the message or the alternative.

Self-check
Q1
A colleague proposes a risky plan. Which reply is the most diplomatically softened?
A) "That won't work. It's too risky."
B) "It's sort of risky and stuff."
C) "I wonder if it might be a bit risky — it might be worth thinking through the downside first?"
Why: C uses a tentative frame (I wonder if), a modal (might), a minimizer (a bit) and a constructive suggestion. A is blunt; B is vague casual language, not diplomacy, and gives no direction.
Q2
Which sentence belongs in a formal academic essay?
A) "It kind of helped, twenty or so people improved and stuff."
B) "The data suggest that the intervention may improve outcomes to some extent."
C) "I was wondering if you could re-run the numbers?"
Why: B hedges with a lexical verb, a modal and an adverbial. A is vague language (casual speech); C is diplomatic distancing (a polite request), not an academic claim.
Q3 · new
Your manager asks you to take on a third project, but you're overloaded. Which reply protects the relationship and gives a clear answer?
A) "No. I have too much work."
B) "I'd really like to help, but I'm afraid I can't take on another project right now. The thing is, the Harris report is due on Friday. Could we look at it again next month?"
C) "Hmm, that might be a little bit tricky, maybe, possibly… we'll see."
Why: B follows preface → soften → reason → alternative. A is clear but blunt; C is so soft that the manager may think the answer is yes.
Errors you will meet most (3.3)

"I want that you send it" · "Please give me the file" as a polite request · "Can you to check…?" · "You must change this" to a manager · "You're coming, isn't it?" · "maybe" as the only hedge · over-hedging until the claim disappears · vague language in writing ("The results were sort of significant") · refusals so soft the listener hears a yes · stressing the minimizer sarcastically ("It's a BIT late") · flat intonation that undoes polite words. For range, praise I'd have thought…, not exactly ideal, Would it be at all possible…, With respect used sincerely, and stacked hedges that stay clear (appears to… to some extent).
