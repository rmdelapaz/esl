Advanced English Workbook · Module 3

Cohesion, Register & Style

Three lessons about how you package what you mean. Turn actions into nouns for a compact, impersonal academic style, and turn them back into verbs when your reader needs plain English. Stitch sentences together without repeating yourself. And soften your language on purpose: to keep a claim defensible, to deliver a hard message kindly, or simply to sound relaxed.

How this module works

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Write, Listen, Say, Read or With a partner. Most tasks in this module have more than one good answer; what matters is that your choice fits the reader and keeps the meaning.

When a task draws on your own experience, you decide what to share. Use your real life, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good. In role-plays you play the card, not yourself, and you can always say "Pass."

3.1

Actions into Things — Nominalization & Academic Style

Class C7
Read and say — New words and chunks
nominalizationregisteragentimpersonalabstractconcisea noun phraseplain Englishappropriacyto rebalance

Light verb + noun collocations (the meaning lives in the noun):

carry out an investigation intomake an assessment ofreach a conclusionprovide an explanation forpose a threat tobring about a change inhave an impact onplace emphasis onlead to a reduction ingive rise to

agent = the person or thing that does the action · register = the level of formality and style that fits a situation · appropriacy = choosing language that suits the reader, the purpose and the setting

Grammar box

Same fact, two packages.

Verbal (spoken, agent visible)They decided to expand, which reduced their costs.
Nominal (written, agent hidden)The decision to expand led to a reduction in costs.

Forming the noun. Check the real noun: many are irregular.

-tion / -siondecide → decision · expand → expansion · introduce → introduction
-ment · -ance / -enceassess → assessment · perform → performance · depend → dependence
-ity · -nessstable → stability · complex → complexity · aware → awareness
-al · -ure · -thapprove → approval · fail → failure · grow → growth · strong → strength
no suffixincrease → an increase · change → a change · rise → a rise · use → a use
irregularbelieve → belief · choose → choice · analyze → analysis

The nominal style travels with the passive, there is / there are, and long noun phrases. Watch the preposition after the noun: an increase in · an impact on · an investigation into · a threat to · a response to.

Don't overdo it. ❌ The implementation of the utilization of renewable resources is a requirement for the achievement of a reduction in emissions. ✅ To reduce emissions, we must use renewable resources. If you can't tell who does what, turn a noun back into a verb.

Read and say — Dialogue: two readers, one fact

Nadia: Can you look at my summary for the quarterly report? I'm not sure it sounds formal enough.

Tom: Sure. "We tried longer opening hours, and more people came in." It's clear, but it reads like a chat.

Nadia: So how would you package it?

Tom: Something like "The introduction of extended opening hours led to a rise in visitor numbers."

Nadia: Nice. And the part about the cost? "The extension of hours resulted in the necessity of the recruitment of additional staff."

Tom: Hmm. Now I've lost track of who's doing what. Try "The longer hours meant we had to hire two more staff."

Nadia: In a report? Isn't that too plain?

Tom: Then meet in the middle: "The extended hours required the recruitment of two additional staff." One noun doing real work, one clear verb.

Nadia: Got it. And for the poster in the lobby?

Tom: "We're now open until 9 p.m.!" Nobody wants a nominalization on a poster.

Underline every nominalization. Which sentence was over-nominalized, and how did Tom fix it? Which version belongs in which "room"?

The Thread Between Sentences — Reference, Substitution & Ellipsis

Class C8
Read and say — New words and chunks
cohesionreferencesubstitutionellipsisan antecedentambiguousa summary nounrecoverablerepetitivestilted
this trend / these findingssuch a movethe former … the latterfor the above reasonsas followsthe red one / the old onesif you fail to do soI think so / I hope notI'm afraid soI'd love to

antecedent = the word or idea a pronoun refers back to · ambiguous = could mean two different things · stilted = stiff and unnatural

Grammar box

When you are about to repeat something, ask three questions: Can I point to it? Swap it? Drop it?

Point to it (reference)it · they · this / that + noun · such (a) + noun · the former / the latter · the above · as followsHouse prices rose sharply. This increase surprised economists.
Swap it (substitution)one / ones for count nouns · do / do so / do it for verb phrases · so / not for clausesI'll take the red one. · Those who fail to do so… · I hope not.
Drop it (ellipsis)after and / but / or, auxiliaries, to, comparatives: keep the anchorI haven't finished, but Sam has. · I'd love to.
so / notI think so · I don't think so · I believe so · I expect so · I hope so · I hope not · I'm afraid so · I'm afraid not   ❌ I don't hope so
one / onescount nouns only: the small one · the new ones   ❌ a coffee one → some
do so vs do itdo so = formal, a general or stated action · do it / do that = concrete, spoken

Keep it unambiguous. ❌ Sales fell and the manager resigned. It was unexpected. ✅ The resignation was unexpected. Add a summary noun after this / that whenever it points at a whole idea.

Register. the former / the latter, such, the above, as follows, do so → writing. one, do it, I think so and ellipsis → everywhere, especially speech.

Taking the Edge Off — Hedging, Vague Language & Diplomatic English

Class C9
Read and say — New words and chunks
to hedgedefensibleto save facetactfulbluntunderstatementa minimizerto softenrapportto decline (a request)

Academic hedging:

The evidence suggests that…This may indicate that…It could be argued that…There is some evidence that……appears to……tends to…arguablyto some extent

Diplomatic English:

I was wondering if…Would it be possible to…?I'm not entirely convinced.I take your point, but…I see it slightly differently.It might be worth…I'm afraid…The thing is…As much as I'd like to…Perhaps we could… instead.

Vague language (casual speech only): eightish · twenty or so · sort of / kind of · a couple of · and that sort of thing · roughly

Grammar box

Three reasons to soften, three toolkits.

H · Academic hedgingprotects your claim · formal, writtenThe data suggest that the program may improve outcomes to some extent.
D · Diplomatic softeningprotects the person · spoken or writtenI was wondering if you could take another look.
V · Vague languagebuilds rapport · casual, spokenLet's meet at eightish. There'll be ten or so of us.

The distance ladder: past, continuous and would make a request softer.

DirectCan you send it today?
SofterCould you send it today?
Softer stillWould it be possible to send it today?
Most distantI was wondering if you could send it today.

Minimizers and understatement: a slight problem · a bit of an issue · not exactly ideal · not the cheapest option (= expensive) · I'm not entirely convinced (= I disagree). Negative questions: Wouldn't it be safer to wait?

Bad news and refusals: preface (I'm afraid… / Unfortunately…) → soften (it's not going to be possible to…) → reason (the thing is…) → alternative (Could we perhaps…?).

❌ The results were sort of significant. (vague language in an essay) · ❌ over-softening: if the listener can't say what must change, you haven't delivered the message.

Read and say — Dialogue: a request, a refusal, an alternative

Carla: Have you got a minute? I was wondering if you could do me a favor.

Omar: Sure, what's up?

Carla: Would it be possible for you to present our results at Thursday's meeting? I'm going to be at the training day.

Omar: Ah. As much as I'd like to, I'm afraid Thursday's not going to work. The thing is, I'm with a client all afternoon.

Carla: Oh, I see. That's a bit of a problem.

Omar: Perhaps we could ask Jin instead? She knows the figures. Or couldn't we record a short video?

Carla: A video might actually work. I'm not entirely sure the manager will like it, though.

Omar: Wouldn't it be worth asking? I'm happy to help you record it on Wednesday.

Carla: That's really kind. Let's do that.

Find Omar's preface, softener, reason and alternative. Which two lines use distancing? Which line is a negative question?
