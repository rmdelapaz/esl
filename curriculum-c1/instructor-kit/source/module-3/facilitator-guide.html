Module 3 — Cohesion, Register & Style

Three classes about the packaging of meaning rather than the meaning itself. First, nominalization: turning actions into nouns (they decided to expand → the decision to expand) to write in a compact, impersonal academic register, and, just as important, turning them back into verbs when a plain-English reader needs clarity. Then cohesion: reference, substitution and ellipsis (this trend · the latter · do so · I hope not · I'd love to), the quiet devices that stop a text repeating itself. Last, softening: academic hedging (the data suggest… to some extent), diplomatic English for bad news, disagreement, refusals and feedback (I was wondering if… · I'm afraid… the thing is…), and casual vague language (eightish, or so).

3 lessons Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C7–C9 Weeks 4–5 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding freer task (a workshop, not a quick chat) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-cohesion-register.html. Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C7. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Register Workshop source texts (verbal → academic) and Plain English notices (academic → plain), the Repair the Report drafts and talk-topic cards, the Diplomatic Role-Play Series A/B cards with observer sheets, the Soften It! board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • These are skills classes, so run them as workshops. Every class spends roughly a quarter of its time on one extended product: a rewritten text in two registers (C7), a repaired report plus a two-minute talk (C8), a series of four difficult workplace conversations (C9). Protect that time. If you're running late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. At B2 learners met the passive and impersonal reporting (B2 3.1), reduced clauses (B2 3.2), linking and formal/informal style (B2 Module 4) and polite requests. Don't re-teach any of it. The C1 step is control of register: choosing, sentence by sentence, the packaging that fits this reader, and being able to justify the choice.
  • Feedback on three things. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: accuracy (errors to fix: the arrivement, an increase of prices, I don't hope so), range (ambitious, well-chosen language to praise and spread: pose a threat to, such a move, the latter, I'd have thought…) and appropriacy (grammatical but wrong for the room: a nominalized friendly email, the former / the latter in a chat, sort of in a report, a "diplomatic" refusal so soft nobody noticed it was a refusal). At C1 the appropriacy column is often the most useful. In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured peer-review step, and each has its own test: the "Who does what?" test (C7: can a reader say who did what in each sentence?), the "Point at it" test (C8: draw an arrow from every it / this / one to what it refers to) and the "What must change?" test (C9: the listener says back what they understood they must do or accept). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote the text instead of summarizing it ("In line 3, this could point at two things").
  • Dominant speakers. C1 classes often include confident professionals who are used to running meetings. Use the built-in roles: give them the jobs that need listening, not talking. In C7 they are the plain-English reader (who must honestly say where they get lost); in C8 the tally-keeper in the two-minute talk; in C9 the observer with the checklist. Say it kindly: "Your ear is excellent. I want you on the checklist this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first" before opening the floor.
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is deliberate effect: nominalizing to hide an agent on purpose and noticing when a text does it to you (C7), cohesion across paragraphs with summary nouns and cataphoric reference (The problem is this: …) (C8), and calibrated understatement that a listener has to read between the lines (C9). Ask strong learners to explain the effect of a choice, not just make it.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (nominalization, utilization, analyze, behavior, favor, criticize, color, apartment, bucks, movie). Accept British forms (nominalisation, favour, colour, flat, quid, film) whenever a learner uses them. Lesson 3.3 deliberately notes that British English uses more understatement than American English; point that out in C9 rather than asking learners to copy it.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Package it → connect it → soften it. In C7 learners choose how to package an idea: as a verb (we cut the budget, so staff were unhappy) or as a noun (budget cuts led to a decline in morale), and learn that the best writers move in both directions. In C8 they connect ideas across sentences without repeating them (this decline · such a move · the latter · I hope so · I'd love to). In C9 they control force: hedging a claim so it is defensible and softening a message so the relationship survives it, without losing the message. Every class produces something another learner reads or hears: a two-register rewrite, a repaired report, a set of difficult conversations.

Trauma-informed: realistic workplaces, low personal stakes

Diplomatic English lives next to difficult moments: criticism, refusals, conflict with a boss, bad news. Keep every scenario workplace-realistic but low-stakes and invented: a delayed project, a rejected budget request, a vacation that can't be approved this month, a report that needs another draft, a colleague asking for too much help. Never role-play a firing or layoff, a disciplinary hearing, a medical diagnosis, a landlord eviction, a police or immigration interview, or a family conflict. The online lesson's journal asks learners to recall a real difficult conversation: always offer "a real one, an invented one, or one from a new identity card." Every role-play position is assigned on the card; learners play the role, not themselves, and anyone can say "Pass." Some learners come from workplaces where disagreeing with a manager was genuinely unsafe. Make it explicit that the C9 scenarios practice language options, not a rule that they must disagree with anyone in real life.

Four picture "register rooms" cards (book, email, coffee, notebook) are used in all three classes. Cut them before C7.
  • For C7: one set of Register Workshop source cards (6 chatty texts, one per group of 3) and Plain English notices (4 over-nominalized texts, one per pair), plus one two-register worksheet per group.
  • For C8: one Repair the Report draft per group of 3 (3 different reports), a device checklist per group, a set of two-minute talk topic cards and a phone timer.
  • For C9: the Diplomatic Role-Play Series (4 scenarios × 2 versions, each an A/B pair) and one observer checklist per group of 3. The Soften It! board game (1 board per group, 1 die, counters) is for early finishers in C9 and the C10 warmer.

  • Optional but powerful: bring two real texts on the same light topic in two registers, for example a city's recycling page written for residents and the council report behind it, or a clinic's patient leaflet and the study it cites. Learners love finding the same fact packaged twice.

3.1 · C7

Nominalization & Academic Style

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the noun from a verb or adjective with the right suffix, including irregulars (believe → belief, choose → choice, grow → growth) and zero-derivation (increase → an increase), and choose the process noun over the person noun (the analysis, not the analyst, of the data)
  • rewrite a verb-heavy, agent-visible sentence in a compact, impersonal, noun-based style, using a light verb (carry out, make, reach, pose, provide, bring about) and the right preposition after the noun (a reduction in, the impact on, an investigation into)
  • explain the three effects of nominal style (concise, impersonal, abstract) and recognize its partners: the passive, there is / there are, long noun phrases
  • diagnose over-nominalization with the "Who does what?" test and rebalance the sentence with working verbs, producing plain English that is still serious
  • choose the packaging for a given reader and genre, and justify the choice

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: nominalization. The information stays the same; the grammatical packaging changes. An action or quality becomes a noun, so it can be a subject or object, take adjectives, be counted and be linked to other nouns. The doer usually disappears.

"The decision to expand led to a reduction in costs." Who decided? (We don't know: the company, probably.) Did anyone reduce anything? (Yes, costs went down.) Is it a story about people or about processes? (Processes.) Where would you read it? (A report, an article, a company update.)
"The analysis of the data" vs "the analyst of the data." Which is a process? (Analysis.) Which is a person? (Analyst.) Which one do we want in a report about results? (Usually the process: the analysis.)

Meaning: light verbs. In carry out an investigation, the meaning is in the noun. The verb just makes the grammar work.

"We carried out an investigation into the cause." Did anybody carry anything? (No.) Where is the real meaning? (In investigation.) Why not just "We investigated"? (Both are fine; the noun lets us add a thorough / an independent investigation.) · "Poor lighting poses a threat to cyclists." Is lighting a person? (No: an abstract noun can be the subject now.)

Meaning: over-nominalization. Too many abstract nouns and the reader can't find who did what. Dense is not the same as intelligent.

"The facilitation of the improvement of communication is an objective of the reorganization." Who is doing something? (Hard to say!) What is the real message? (The reorganization aims to improve communication.) Is the short version less serious? (No, just clearer.) · Would you send the long version to a friend? (No.) To tenants of a building? (Also no: they need to understand it quickly.)

Form.

Verb → noun-tion / -sion · -ment · -ance / -ence · -al · -ure · zerodecide → decision · assess → assessment · perform → performance · arrive → arrival · fail → failure · increase → an increase
Adjective → noun-ity · -ness · -th · -ance / -encestable → stability · aware → awareness · strong → strength · important → importance
Light verb + nounmake · carry out · conduct · reach · draw · provide · pose · bring about · have · placereach a conclusion · pose a threat to · place emphasis on
Noun + prepositionthe preposition often differs from the verbincrease → an increase in · impact → an impact on · investigate → an investigation into · respond → a response to
Agent after the noun's or of / bythe company's rapid expansion · the approval of the proposal by the committee
Partnerspassive · there is / are · long noun phraseNo explanation was provided. · There is growing recognition that… · a significant reduction in urban air pollution levels

Pronunciation. Suffixes move the stress, and learners who know the verb often stress the noun wrongly. -tion / -sion / -ity put the stress on the syllable just before them: deCIDE → deCIsion, eDUcate → eduCAtion, STAble → staBIlity, COMplex → comPLEXity. -ment, -ness, -al, -ure don't move it: deVElop → deVElopment, aWARE → aWAREness. Zero-derivation often shifts stress to the first syllable for the noun: to inCREASE → an INcrease, to reCORD → a REcord. For formal read-aloud, long noun phrases need chunking: "The committee's approval of the proposal | ensured its swift implementation."

Drill script
  1. Ping-pong (fast, choral then individual): T: "decide" → S: "decision." T: "aware" → "awareness." T: "fail" → "failure." T: "grow" → "growth." T: "believe" → "belief." T: "increase" → "an INcrease" (exaggerate the stress). T: "analyze" → "analysis." T: "complex" → "complexity."
  2. Light verb, please: T: "an investigation" → S: "carry out / conduct an investigation into." T: "a conclusion" → "reach / draw a conclusion." T: "a threat" → "pose a threat to." T: "emphasis" → "place emphasis on." T: "a contribution" → "make a contribution to."
  3. Package it: T: "They decided to close the branch." → S: "The decision was made to close the branch. / The decision to close the branch…" T: "Prices went up because more people were buying." → S: "Rising demand led to a price increase." T: "The new law really helped small businesses grow." → S: "The legislation contributed significantly to the growth of small businesses."
  4. Unpack it (the reverse): T: "The utilization of the software resulted in an enhancement of accuracy." → S: "Using the software made the measurements more accurate." T: "There was a consideration of the postponement of the vote." → S: "They considered postponing the vote."
Register Workshop — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 with three roles: writer (holds the pen), editor (checks the checklist) and plain-English reader (in Round 2, reads the rewrite as if they were the tenant, patient or parent, and must honestly say where they got lost). Rotate the pen between rounds. Give the plain-English reader job to a dominant speaker.
  • Round 1 (10 min): each group gets one source card, a chatty spoken update (topics: a team trial of a four-day work pattern, a clinic's walking-group pilot, a library's longer opening hours, a bike-lane trial, an office move to shared desks, a school's phone-free lunch trial). They write a 4–5 sentence formal summary for a report. Checklist on the worksheet: ☐ 4+ nominalizations ☐ 2 light-verb collocations ☐ 1 passive ☐ 1 there is / are ☐ no I / we / they ☐ facts unchanged.
  • Round 2 (8 min): each group gets one Plain English notice (a building-management letter about elevator repairs, a clinic letter about appointment changes, a school letter about pickup times, an office memo about a new expense form). They rewrite it for its real reader: short sentences, active verbs, you / we, and no loss of politeness or facts. They may keep a nominalization when it names a real thing the reader knows (the repairs, your appointment).
  • Round 3, peer review (7 min): swap with the next group. Run the "Who does what?" test on both texts: for every sentence, can you say in three seconds who does what? In the academic text, the answer may properly be "it's hidden," but the relationship must be clear (X led to Y). In the plain text, the answer must be obvious. Reviewers write one strength and one suggestion on a sticky note, quoting the text.
  • Share: two groups read both versions aloud. Ask the class: "Which reader is each version for? Would either one work in the other room?"
  • Keep it honest: there is no single right answer. Praise any version that fits its reader and keeps the facts.
ICQs before the Register Workshop
In Round 1, who's the reader? (A manager reading a report.) Can you use "we"? (No.) How many nominalizations at least? (Four.) Can you change the facts? (No.) · In Round 2, who's the reader? (The tenant / patient / parent.) More nouns or more verbs? (More verbs.) Can it be rude or chatty? (No: polite and clear.) · In the peer review, what's the question for every sentence? (Who does what?)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C1 speakers are much stronger orally than in writing (often learners with interrupted schooling who have lived in English for years). Give them the suffix table on a strip and a set of four noun + light-verb "chunks" to copy into Round 1 (led to a reduction in · carried out an assessment of · there was an increase in · had a positive impact on). In Round 2 they're the ideal plain-English reader: their honest "I got lost here" is exactly what the group needs. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–8, Ex 4 items 1–3. Target: one clean nominal sentence and one clean plain-English sentence, read aloud.

Stretch (toward C2)

Nominalization as strategy. Give a sentence like Mistakes were made in the processing of your application: who is being protected by the grammar? Ask strong learners to write two versions of one bad-news sentence: one that hides responsibility and one that takes it (We made a mistake when we processed your application), and to say which a customer would trust. Then ask them to build one long, well-controlled noun phrase (a gradual but measurable improvement in patient satisfaction scores) and a sentence where a nominalization refers back to a whole earlier paragraph (This reorganization…), which leads straight into C8.

L1 watch-outs
  • Over-nominalizing by default: academic writing in Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Arabic, Russian and German (Nominalstil) often prizes long noun chains, so these learners may produce "the realization of the implementation of…" with pride. Frame it as a genre difference, not an error: English academic style is noun-based too, but it expects a working verb in every sentence.
  • False-friend nouns: Spanish realización and French réalisation → "the realization of a meeting" (= holding); actualización → "actualization" (= update); formación → "formation" (= training). Portuguese efetivação, Italian attuazione produce similar coinages. Ask "Is that a real English noun?" and check a learner's dictionary together.
  • Invented suffixes: "the arrivement," "the deciding," "the failment," "ableness." Everyone does this; treat each noun as vocabulary to learn, not assemble.
  • Articles with abstract nouns: Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese speakers may drop them ("Decision to expand led to reduction in costs"); Spanish, French, Portuguese and Arabic speakers may add them to general statements ("The education is important"). Rule of thumb: general and uncountable → no article (Education matters); specific, with an of-phrase → the (the education of refugees' children), and a count noun with an adjective → a (a sharp increase).
  • Wrong preposition after the noun: "an increase of prices" (Spanish un aumento de, French une augmentation des), "an impact in," "an investigation about," "a solution of." Teach noun + preposition as one chunk.
  • Stress on derived nouns: learners who read far more English than they hear often say de-CI-sion correctly but STA-bi-li-ty, e-CO-no-my / e-co-NO-mic, PHO-to-graph / pho-TO-gra-phy wrongly. Use the Pronunciation note above.

3.2 · C8

Cohesion — Reference, Substitution & Ellipsis

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • refer back cleanly with pronouns, this / that + a summary noun (this decline, these findings, such a move), the former / the latter and formal the above / as follows, and repair an ambiguous it or this
  • substitute with one / ones (countable nouns only), do / do so / do it (verb phrases) and so / not (clauses after think, hope, be afraid, etc.), including I don't think so vs I hope not
  • leave out recoverable words (ellipsis) after and / but, after auxiliaries, after to and in comparatives, keeping the anchor word (I'd love to. · but Sara has.)
  • choose devices that fit the register: do so, such, the latter, the above in writing; one, do it, I think so and ellipsis in speech
  • repair a repetitive text so it is much shorter and still completely clear, and sustain cohesion in two minutes of unscripted speech
Materials
  • Workbook 3.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Repair the Report frame, self-check)
  • Slides 8–12 · Cohesion device flashcards · Repair the Report drafts (3) + device checklists · Two-minute talk topic cards · phone timer · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 3.2-A (the handover call) and 3.2-B (dictation)

Left: point back (reference). it · they · this / that + noun (this trend, such a move, these findings) · the former / the latter · the above / as follows (formal). ✘ Sales fell and the CEO resigned. It was unexpected. → ✔ The resignation was unexpected.

Center: swap it (substitution). the red one / the old ones (count only! coffee → some) · She works harder than I do. · If you fail to do so… (formal) · I did it the next day (concrete) · I think so / I don't think so · I hope not / I'm afraid not (✘ I don't hope so).

Right: drop it (ellipsis). He was poor but he was honest. · I haven't finished, but Sam has finished. · I'd love to come. · more than I expected it to cost. Keep the anchor: to / the auxiliary. Three questions: point to it? swap it? drop it?

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: reference. A reference word sends the reader to find something elsewhere in the text, usually earlier. It must be impossible to point at the wrong thing.

"House prices rose sharply. This surprised economists." What does this point at? (The whole idea: the sharp rise.) A single noun or a whole sentence? (A whole sentence.) How can we make it even clearer? (This rise / this increase surprised…) · "We can fly or take the train; the latter is cheaper." Which is cheaper? (The train, the second one.) Would you say "the latter" to a friend over coffee? (Probably not: "the train's cheaper.")

Meaning: substitution. A short stand-in replaces a repeated word of the same kind: one for a count noun, do (so) for a verb phrase, so / not for a whole clause.

"I'll take the red one." One what? (The red jacket: something countable we already mentioned.) Can I say "Is there coffee? — Yes, there's a hot one"? (No, coffee is uncountable here: "Yes, there's some.") · "Applicants must register by Friday; those who fail to do so will lose their place." Do so = ? (Register by Friday.) Formal or chatty? (Formal: a notice.) · "Will he pass?" — "I hope not." Do I want him to pass? (No!) Can I say "I don't hope so"? (No: hope and be afraid take not.) · "I don't think so" vs "I think not": which is normal in conversation? (I don't think so. "I think not" sounds formal or old-fashioned.)

Meaning: ellipsis. We leave out words the listener can recover. It's the natural rhythm of English, not laziness; repeating everything sounds robotic or even aggressive.

"I haven't read the memo, but Sara has." Has Sara read what? (The memo.) Which word must stay? (has: it holds the meaning.) · "Are you coming?" — "I'd love to." Can I say "I'd love"? (No: keep to.) · "The results were significant; the controls were not." Is ellipsis OK in a formal report? (Yes: ordinary ellipsis works in every register.) · "Seen it?" "Told you." Formal or informal? (Very informal: speech only.)

Form.

Referenceit · they · this / that / these / those (+ noun) · such (a) + noun · the former / the latter · the above · as followsThese findings… · Such a move… · For the above reasons…
one / onesreplaces a count noun (keep the adjective or this / which)the small one · the new ones · Which one?

Ellipsisafter and / but / or · after an auxiliary · after to · in comparativespoor but honest · but Sam has · I'd love to · more than we expected

Pronunciation. Ellipsis moves the stress onto the anchor, which becomes strong: "I haven't finished, but Sam has" /bət ˈsæm ˈhæz/, not a weak /əz/. The same with to: "I'd love to" /aɪd ˈlʌv tuː/, with a full vowel, never /tə/ at the end. In I think so / I hope not, the stress normally falls on the verb or not: "I think so," "I hope not." Contrastive one: stress the adjective, not one: "not the big one, the small one."

Drill script
  1. So or not? T asks, S answers with the cue: T: "Is it going to rain? (think, yes)" → S: "I think so." T: "(think, no)" → "I don't think so." T: "Will the train be late? (hope, no)" → "I hope not." T: "Is the meeting canceled? (afraid, yes)" → "I'm afraid so." T: "(afraid, no)" → "I'm afraid not."
  2. Keep the anchor: T: "Have you sent the report?" (no / Kim yes) → S: "No, but Kim has." T: "Can you come on Friday?" (want, but can't) → "I'd like to, but I can't." T: "Did you finish on time?" (should have, didn't) → "I should have, but I didn't." Stress the anchor.
  3. One or some? T: "Do you need a pen?" → S: "No thanks, I've got one." T: "Do you need some paper?" → "No thanks, I've got some." T: "Which chairs do you want?" → "The black ones."
  4. Summary noun: T reads a sentence, S starts the next with This + noun: T: "Sales doubled in March." → S: "This increase…" T: "The manager canceled the trip." → "This decision…" T: "Three staff members left in one month." → "These departures…"
Repair the Report + the two-minute talk — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Each gets one Repair the Report draft and a device checklist. The drafts are deliberately terrible: every noun repeated, every verb phrase in full. Tell learners that's the joke; they may laugh.
  • Repair (10 min): rewrite in 90 words or fewer (the original is about 140). Every fact must survive. Tick the checklist as they go: ☐ 2+ reference (at least one this / these + summary noun) ☐ 2+ substitution ☐ 2+ ellipsis ☐ no ambiguous it / this ☐ written-report register (so do so, such, the latter are welcome; and stuff is not). The word-counter counts.
  • Peer review (5 min): swap with another group and run the "Point at it" test: the reviewers draw an arrow in pencil from every reference and substitution word to its target. An arrow that could land in two places gets a "?" flag. Then one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text. Return the drafts; groups fix any flags.
  • Two-minute talk (6 min): the speaker picks a topic card (a place they know well, how their workplace or school has changed, a skill they learned, a tool or app they rely on; a new identity card or an invented answer is always fine). The tally-keeper makes two columns: repeats (a full noun or verb phrase repeated when it could have been replaced) and devices (it, this, one, so, I did, the latter…). The third learner gives the strength-and-suggestion feedback. Give the tally-keeper job to your most dominant speaker first.
  • Share: one group reads its repaired report; the class checks it with the "Point at it" test live on the board.
ICQs before Repair the Report
How many words at the end? (90 or fewer.) Can you delete a fact? (No.) Is this a chat or a written report? (A written report.) So the latter and do so are…? (Fine, good.) What do reviewers draw? (Arrows from each it / this / one to what it means.) What does a "?" mean? (It could point at two things: fix it.) · In the talk, what does the tally-keeper count? (Repeats and devices.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the report on a sheet with the repeated nouns already highlighted, and a strip of six ready-made devices to try (it · they · this problem · the latter · one · did). The learner's job is to cross out and replace, not to rewrite from scratch. Speaking is often the stronger skill: in the two-minute talk they can go first, with a 30-second planning time and the phrase strip on the table. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–5, Ex 3 items 1–4. Target: five repetitions replaced, one "I hope not / I'm afraid so" used in speech.

Stretch (toward C2)

Cohesion across paragraphs. Ask strong learners to add a two-sentence closing paragraph to their repaired report that opens with a summary noun referring to the whole report (These problems…, Such an arrangement…). Introduce cataphoric reference (pointing forward): The problem is this: nobody owns the kitchen. · Here's what I'd suggest: … and lexical cohesion (synonyms and general nouns instead of pronouns: the scheme… the initiative… the pilot). In the talk, the target is zero repeats and at least one forward reference.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I think yes" / "I think that yes" / "I hope no": Spanish creo que sí / espero que no, Portuguese acho que sim, French je pense que oui, Italian credo di sì. Very common even at C1 in speech. Drill so / not until it's automatic; keep "I think yes ✘" on the board.
  • Dropped subjects and objects: pro-drop languages (Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Arabic) drop the subject ("Is very expensive"); Chinese, Japanese and Korean often drop the object as well ("I bought the report and read"). These learners may over-elide in English. Remind them: English ellipsis needs an anchor.
  • Gendered it: French, Spanish, Portuguese, German, Arabic and Russian speakers may refer to a thing as he / she ("The proposal… she is too expensive"). Recast gently to it.
  • Repetition as good style: in some languages and school traditions (for example, Chinese, Korean, Japanese and some formal Arabic writing), repeating the full noun is clearer and more respectful than a pronoun. Learners don't hear it as clumsy. Explain that English readers do.
  • "one" for everything: "a coffee one," "an information one," "the advices ones." Tie it to the countable / uncountable distinction learners already know.
  • Formal devices in speech: learners from exam-focused systems use the former / the latter and aforementioned in conversation. Praise the range, then point out the register: "Perfect in your report. At the coffee machine: the first one / the second one."

Hedging, Vague Language & Diplomatic English

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • tell the two reasons to soften apart: protecting a claim (academic caution) and protecting a person (diplomacy), plus a third, casual kind: vague language for rapport
  • hedge a claim to the strength of the evidence with modals (may, might, could, tend to), lexical verbs (suggest, appear, indicate), adverbials (arguably, largely, to some extent) and impersonal frames (It would appear that…, There is some evidence that…), without hedging it into nothing
  • soften requests and opinions with distancing (I was wondering if…, Would it be possible…), minimizers (a slight problem, not exactly ideal), litotes (not the cheapest), negative questions (Wouldn't it be safer…?) and deference markers (With respect…)
  • deliver bad news and refusals with preface → soften → reason (+ an alternative), and give critical feedback that the listener can act on
  • handle four workplace-realistic difficult conversations so that the relationship survives and the message still lands

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: academic hedging. You calibrate the claim to the evidence. A hedged claim is stronger in academic terms, because it is harder to attack.

"These results suggest that caffeine may improve memory in some conditions." Did the study prove it? (No.) Is the writer weak or careful? (Careful.) Which is easier for a critic to attack: this or "proves"? ("Proves.") · "It would seem, arguably, that it may possibly tend to help to some extent in some cases." What is the claim? (We can't tell: too many hedges.)

Meaning: distancing. Past tense, continuous and would move a request "further away," so it sounds less demanding. The time is still now.

"I was wondering if you could do me a favor." Past or present request? (Present: I'm asking now.) Why the past tense, then? (It's softer, less direct.) · "Wouldn't it be safer to wait?" Is the speaker asking for information? (No: suggesting we wait.) Is the speaker giving an order? (No: inviting agreement.)

Meaning: minimizers and litotes.

"There's a slight problem with the figures." Could it be a big problem? (Yes! Listen to the tone and the context.) · "It's not the cheapest option." Cheap or expensive? (Expensive.) · "I'm not entirely convinced this will work." Does the speaker agree? (No, they think it won't work.)

Meaning: preface → soften → reason.

"I'm afraid that's not going to be possible this time. The thing is, the budget's already committed. Could we perhaps revisit it in the spring?" Is it a yes or a no? (A no.) Is it clear? (Yes.) Is there a reason? (The budget.) What does the listener get instead? (A possible alternative: spring.) · If I only say "Hmm, that might be a bit tricky…" and stop, what's the risk? (The listener might think it's a yes.)

Pronunciation. Softening is half intonation. A fall-rise on the key word signals "there's a but coming" or "I'm not saying everything": "The opening's ˇstrong…" (…but the rest isn't). A flat, falling tone on a softened sentence can undo all the words ("I was wondering if you could send it" said flatly sounds like an order). Requests with Would it be possible…? rise gently at the end. Keep just, a bit, slightly unstressed; stressing them sounds sarcastic ("It's a BIT late"). Tag questions that seek agreement fall: "It's a bit risky, ↘isn't it?"

Drill script
  1. The distance ladder (choral, then individual): T: "Send me the file." → S: "Can you send me the file?" → "Could you send me the file?" → "Would it be possible to send me the file?" → "I was wondering if you could send me the file." Then with a new request: "Move the meeting."
  2. Soften the blunt: T: "That's wrong." → S: "I'm not sure that's quite right." T: "That's a bad idea." → "I'm not entirely convinced that's the best idea." T: "It's expensive." → "It's not the cheapest option." T: "It's late." → "It's a little behind schedule."
  3. Hedge the claim: T: "Exercise improves mood." → S: "Regular exercise appears to improve mood in many people." T: "Remote work increases productivity." → "There is some evidence that remote work may increase productivity, at least in some roles."
  4. Preface, please: T gives the bad news bluntly, S adds the preface and reason: T: "No vacation in August." → S: "I'm afraid we can't approve August this year. The thing is, half the team is already away. Would September work instead?"
  5. Intonation: say "The opening's strong…" with a fall-rise and a pause; learners finish the "but." Then say "I was wondering if you could help" flat, then warm; learners judge which one they'd say yes to.
Diplomatic Role-Play Series — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Four scenarios, each with two versions (1 and 2) so neighboring groups aren't identical. Every card gives the setting, the relationship (peer, manager, client, team member), the message A must deliver, and how B feels and what B wants. Positions are assigned: nobody is expressing their own view of their own boss.
  • ① Giving bad news: a project lead tells a client the delivery will be two weeks late (v1); an office manager tells a team their requested standing desks aren't in this year's budget (v2).
  • ② Disagreeing with a manager: a manager wants to launch a new booking app to customers next week without testing (v1); a manager proposes moving the team's weekly meeting to 7:30 a.m. (v2). A disagrees respectfully and proposes an alternative; B (the manager) is attached to the idea but open to a good argument.
  • ③ Declining a request: a colleague asks A to cover their Saturday shift, but A has a family commitment (v1); a manager asks A to take on a third project while A is already overloaded (v2). A must say no (or "not now") and protect the relationship.
  • ④ Softening criticism in feedback: A gives feedback on a colleague's presentation slides that are too crowded (v1), or on a new team member's customer emails that are too informal (v2). A must start with a real strength and name one specific change; B is proud of the work.
  • Each round (5 min): 1 min plan with the workbook grid (preface, softeners, reason, alternative) · 2½ min conversation · 1½ min observer feedback. The observer ticks: ☐ preface ☐ 3+ different softeners ☐ reason ☐ alternative or next step ☐ message clear. Then the "What must change?" test: B says in one sentence what they now understand ("So the delivery will be on the 18th, not the 4th"). If B can't, the message was over-softened.
  • Rotate roles after every round so each learner plays A, B and Observer at least once. With pairs only, drop the observer and B fills in the checklist after the conversation.
  • Whole-class share (last minute): ask one group to replay their best 30 seconds of scenario ②, the hardest one.
ICQs before the role-play series
Are you playing yourself? (No, the card.) How many scenarios? (Four.) Do roles change? (Yes, every round.) What does the observer count? (The preface, softeners, the reason, the alternative.) After the conversation, what does B say? (What they understood: what must change.) If B can't say it? (It was too soft.) Can you say "Pass"? (Yes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a four-line frame card for A: "I'm afraid… / The thing is… / I know that… / Could we perhaps…?" and let the learner say it from the card. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 4 items 1–5. Target: one complete preface → soften → reason turn, said aloud with a warm tone.

Stretch (toward C2)

Calibrated understatement. Ask strong learners to play scenario ② three times at three strengths (mild concern, clear disagreement, firm opposition) using only understatement: "I might have a slight concern…" · "I'm not entirely comfortable with…" · "I'd have real reservations about…". The group guesses which level was intended. Discuss cross-cultural misreading: the same "slight concern" might be read as nothing by one listener and as a strong objection by another. On the academic side: write a hedged abstract sentence with exactly two hedges and justify each one against the evidence.

L1 watch-outs
  • Directness norms: learners from cultures where directness is honest and efficient (for example, German, Dutch, Hebrew, Russian, Polish, and many Eastern European workplaces) may sound blunt or even rude in English with perfect grammar ("You must change this," "This is wrong"). Present softening as a tool for being heard, not as dishonesty.
  • Over-softening: learners from high-context communication cultures (for example, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Indonesian, Filipino and Vietnamese workplaces) may soften so much that the message disappears. The "What must change?" test is designed for them: gentle and clear.
  • "I want…" and "Please + imperative": Spanish quiero, Portuguese quero, Russian я хочу, Arabic ureed make "I want that you send the file" feel neutral. And please doesn't soften an imperative much in English ("Please send it today" is still an order). Teach the distance ladder.
  • Modal strength: German müssen, Russian нужно / надо and French il faut often come out as "must" or "have to," which sound far stronger in English. Offer it might be worth…, we'd probably need to…
  • One-size tag "isn't it?": speakers of Chinese, Malay, Hindi-Urdu, Tagalog and others (and of some world Englishes) use "isn't it?" everywhere ("You're coming, isn't it?"). At C1, match the auxiliary: aren't you? · won't it? · didn't we?
  • "Maybe" as the only hedge: Chinese 可能 kěnéng, Spanish quizás, Arabic rubbamā. Push for range: may, tends to, appears to, to some extent.

Watch for in Module 4: phrasal verbs in 4.1 are the verbal, spoken side of the register dial learners met in C7 (look into vs investigate vs carry out an investigation into): make that three-way link out loud. Collocation and connotation in 4.2 build directly on light verbs (pose a threat, reach a conclusion) and on the "hidden meaning" of litotes. The 4.3 review project is the natural place to demand all three skills in one performance: a nominal, hedged written summary and a diplomatic spoken exchange.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it tests: forming nouns (including irregulars and zero-derivation), light-verb collocations and noun + preposition, rewriting in both directions (verbal → nominal, over-nominalized → plain), reference with a summary noun, one / ones, do so, so / not (especially I hope not), ellipsis with the anchor word, hedging a claim, softening a blunt request or refusal, and labeling H / D / V register.
