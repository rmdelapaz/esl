Module 4 — Lexical Range & Fluency

Three classes that move the spotlight from grammar to word choice, and then bring the whole first half of C1 together. First, phrasal and multi-word verbs: the four types, the pronoun rule, polysemy, and the C1 skill of the register swap (look into ↔ investigate, put off ↔ postpone) and knowing when each one fits. Then collocation, idiom and connotation: strong vs weak collocations, collocation grids, connotation clines (slim · thin · skinny · scrawny) and the appropriacy of idioms. Last, the halfway review, The Advanced Communicator: a short persuasive presentation, a Q&A and a written summary that draw on Modules 1–4, scored with a speaking and writing checklist that feeds Progress Test 1 in C13.

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a demanding communicative task) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C12 is a task-based review: the language work comes from the tasks.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-lexical-range.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C10. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Register Swap A/B cards and "Which fits?" context cards, the connotation cline cards, the idiom and "Use it or lose it" cards, the chunk-bingo listener cards, the Advanced Communicator topic, persona and challenge cards, the C12 speaking & writing checklist, the Register Ladder review game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • This module is lexical, not grammatical. C1 learners already understand hundreds of phrasal verbs and collocations; what they lack is controlled production and judgment. Don't teach long lists. Teach the system (the four types, the pronoun rule, the collocation types, the cline) and then spend the time on choice: which verb, which partner, which shade, which register, and why. The question you'll ask most in this module is "Would you say that here, to these people?"
  • Fluency before accuracy, then range and appropriacy. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: accuracy (errors to fix: turn off it, make a photo), range (ambitious language to praise and spread: it paid off handsomely, a pressing concern) and appropriacy (register slips: commence over coffee, sort out in a formal report, an idiom in a written summary). At C1 the third column matters most. In feedback, board 2–3 items from each column without names.
  • Dominant speakers. Every task has a listening role. Give it to the learner who talks most: the register judge in the Register Swap (decides, doesn't narrate), the chunk counter in the Precise Talk (ticks the bingo card), the timekeeper and last questioner in the C12 circles. Use talking tokens in C12. Say it kindly: "Great point, Viktor. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • Peer review, every class. C1 learners can and should give each other precise feedback. Use one protocol all module so it becomes routine: Praise – Question – Polish. Praise one exact word choice ("tolerate was perfect there"); ask one question ("Why did you keep carry out?"); polish one item ("very worried could be deeply concerned"). Model it once with your own text before learners try it. Feedback is on the language, never the person.
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is finer lexical judgment: the particle's own meaning (up = completion: use up, eat up), phrasal verbs that are fully at home in formal prose (carry out, point out, bring about), semantic prosody (cause and rife with attract negative partners), playful variation of an idiom in a talk (the tip of a very large iceberg), and holding one register while shifting another feature. Don't add new lists; ask strong learners to justify choices aloud.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and a few British informal words (nominalisation, practise, recognise, colour, favour, programme, grey, travelling, centres, apologise; reckon, well dodgy, Shall we…?). Teach nominalization, practice (verb), recognize, color, favor, program, gray, traveling, centers, apologize; for the informal register, I think / I figure (for reckon), pretty sketchy (for well dodgy), Should we…? / Want to…?. Get away means a short vacation, not "holiday." Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Choose the verb → choose the partner → choose the voice. In C10 learners choose between a phrasal verb and its single-word synonym, and learn that neither is "better": We'll look into it belongs in the team meeting, We will investigate the matter in the formal reply. In C11 they choose the word's partner (heavy rain, meet a deadline, deeply concerned) and its shade (slender, thin, skinny, scrawny), and they learn when an idiom sparkles and when it clangs. In C12 they orchestrate all of it: one argument, delivered aloud to a small audience, defended in Q&A, and then written up as a formal summary. The same message, in the voice the moment calls for, is the whole module in miniature.

Trauma-informed: stories, bodies and opinions

Three places in this module need care. (1) Stories in C10. The online project suggests "a trip" or "a career move": for some learners, the biggest trip of their life was forced migration, and a career move was a job lost. Offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card, or a Register Swap story card (all workplace and community stories). Keep phrasal verbs such as lay off and come down with in third-person, invented contexts. (2) Connotation in C11. Slim / skinny / scrawny and stingy / nosy / pig-headed are judgments about people. Never ask learners to describe classmates, family members or real people with these words. Use the invented characters on the cards, objects (a slender vase, a scrawny tree) and animals (a scrawny cat). Say it plainly: "We're describing invented people today. Nobody in this room." Some learners carry pain about weight or food scarcity; if the body cline seems unwelcome, do the money cline first and treat the body cline as reading only. (3) Opinions in C12. Topics stay in workplace, education, technology, health habits, community and everyday science. No party politics, religion or immigration policy. Every learner may argue an assigned side from a card, and the persona cards let anyone speak from a point of view that is not their own.

  • For C11: one envelope of cline cards per pair (6 sets), one idiom card set and one "Use it or lose it" discussion card per group, and one chunk-bingo listener card per learner.

4.1 · C10

Phrasal Verbs & Multi-word Verbs

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • sort a multi-word verb into one of four types (intransitive set off · separable turn off · inseparable look after · three-part put up with) and predict where its object goes
  • apply the pronoun rule: a pronoun object must split a separable verb (turn it off) and must follow an inseparable one (look after it); keep long objects at the end (turned off every light in the house)
  • work out the sense of a polysemous phrasal verb from its collocates (the plane took off / sales took off / take off your coat)
  • perform a register swap in both directions (look into ↔ investigate, put off ↔ postpone, find out ↔ discover, get rid of ↔ eliminate) and justify which one fits a given reader, including when to keep the phrasal verb
  • use and pronounce the nominalized forms with first-syllable stress (a BREAKdown, an OUTcome, a TAKEover)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: what counts as a multi-word verb. A verb plus one or two particles that behave as one unit, often with a meaning you can't build from the parts. Whether the particle is an adverb (off, up, out, away, back) or a preposition (after, with, for, into) decides where the object can go.

"She looked after her neighbor's cat." Is she looking behind the cat? (No. She took care of it.) Can I say "She looked the cat after"? (No: inseparable.) · "He turned the offer down." Did he turn anything physically? (No: he refused it.) Can I move "the offer"? (Yes: turn down the offer / turn the offer down.) With "it"? (Only "turn it down.")

Meaning: the pronoun rule.

"Can you fill in the form?" Two positions possible? (Yes.) "Can you fill in it?" (No: "fill it in.") · "Can you deal with the form?" "Deal the form with"? (No: inseparable.) "Deal with it"? (Yes: the pronoun stays after.) · How do I test a new verb? (Try it with "it.")

Meaning: polysemy.

"Business is picking up." Is someone lifting something? (No: it's improving.) · "He picked up Portuguese while traveling." Did he take a class? (No: he learned informally.) · "I could just make out a signature." Was it easy to see? (No, only just.) · "He made out that he'd done all the work." Is that true? (Probably not: he claimed it.)

Meaning: the register swap. Phrasal verbs lean spoken and informal; their single-word synonyms (often from Latin or French) lean written and formal. Neither is better. The skill is choosing by reader, setting and purpose.

"We'll look into the complaint." / "We will investigate the complaint." Same meaning? (Yes.) Which one in a meeting with your team? (look into.) Which one in a formal reply to a customer? (investigate.) Is "look into" wrong in the reply? (Not wrong, just less formal.) · "I shall relinquish caffeine," said at lunch. Correct grammar? (Yes.) Good English? (No: absurdly formal. "I'm giving up coffee.") · "The team carried out a survey." In a formal report, must I change it to "conducted"? (No: "carry out" is fine in formal writing.)

① Intransitiveverb + particle, no objectThe car broke down. · We set off at dawn.

Heavy objecta long object goes after the particleShe turned off every light in the house before she left.
Noun formsfused spelling, first-syllable stressa breakdown · an outbreak · a takeover · an outcome · input · a setback

Pronunciation. In phrasal verbs (types ①, ②, ④) the particle usually takes the main stress: "set OFF", "turn it OFF", "put up WITH it" (with a noun object, the noun takes it: "put up with the NOISE"). In prepositional verbs (type ③) the verb is stressed and the preposition is weak: "LOOK after them" /ˈlʊk æftər ðəm/, "DEAL with it". The nouns flip to the first syllable: "The car broke DOWN" → "a BREAKdown"; "they took OVER" → "a TAKEover". Linking makes pronoun splits smooth: "turn it off" /tɜrnɪˈtɔf/, "pick it up" /pɪkɪˈtʌp/; in American English the t flaps: "put it off" /pʊɾɪˈɔf/, "set it up" /sɛɾɪˈɾʌp/.

Drill script
  1. Pronoun please (point at box ② or ③): T: "Turn off the heater." → S: "Turn it off." T: "Look after the plants." → S: "Look after them." T: "Throw away the old files." → S: "Throw them away." T: "Put up with the delays." → S: "Put up with them." T: "Pick up the kids at six." → S: "Pick them up at six." Fast, choral then individual.
  2. Swap it up / swap it down (flashcards): T holds up postpone: S: "put off." T: "find out": S: "discover." T: "tolerate": S: "put up with." Then full sentences: T: "We'll look into it. Formal!" → S: "We will investigate the matter." T: "The price has increased. Spoken!" → S: "The price has gone up."
  3. Stress flip: T: "The system broke down." → S: "There was a breakdown." T: "A competitor took them over." → S: "It was a takeover." T: "Things turned out well." → S: "It was a good outcome." Clap the stress each time.
  4. Which one here? T gives a context and a pair: "A text to your brother: put off or postpone the dinner?" → S: "Let's put it off." T: "A letter from a hospital: find out or discover?" → S: "…discovered." T: "A safety report: carry out or conduct?" → S: "Either. Both are fine in formal writing."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 says something they need to do this week, with a phrasal verb and a noun object ("I need to fill in my tax form."); S2 repeats it with a pronoun ("You need to fill it in.") and then formally ("You are required to complete it."). Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Register Swap — how to run it
  • Pairs. Give each pair one Register Swap story (Set C): six workplace and community stories (a community center leak, a library app pilot, a clinic's new booking system, a school fundraiser, a delivery company schedule change, a food bank move). Each story has an A card (an informal voice note, dense with phrasal verbs) and a B card (a formal memo about a later stage of the same story, dense with Latinate verbs).
  • Round 1: tell it (4 min). A reads their voice note silently, turns it over and tells it to B in their own words, like a coworker at the coffee machine. B jots down every phrasal verb heard (a tally on the back of the card).
  • Round 2: formalize it (6 min). Together they write a 70–90-word formal update (for the manager, the board or the parents' committee) on the lines in the workbook. Rules: swap wherever the register calls for it; keep at least one phrasal verb on purpose (e.g., carry out, set up, point out) and write K above it; no idioms. Check every pronoun object.
  • Round 3: reverse it (3 min). B reads the formal memo on their card, turns it over, and gives A a 30-second spoken update in natural spoken English: "OK, so basically, they've put off the…". A listens for any "stiff" word that survived and says "Too formal!" when they hear one.
  • Round 4: peer review (2 min). Pairs join another pair and swap written updates. Each pair gives Praise – Question – Polish: one swap that lands, one question about a choice ("Why did you keep set up?"), one word to polish.
  • Roles: in a group of 3, the third learner is the register judge: holds the "Which fits?" context cards, decides disputes, and reads the final version aloud. Give this job to a dominant speaker.
  • Early finishers: deal three "Which fits?" context cards (a text to a friend, a job interview, a complaint letter, a team stand-up, a university essay, a podcast, a safety report, a wedding toast) and say the same sentence in the right register for each.
ICQs before the Register Swap

Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on six register pairs only (put off / postpone, find out / discover, look into / investigate, go up / increase, set up / establish, put up with / tolerate) and the pronoun rule with it. Give a color-coded card: blue = spoken, red = written. In the Register Swap, the learner tells the story (Round 1) and underlines the phrasal verbs on the partner's draft instead of writing the whole update; accept a 40-word update. Workbook: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 3a only. Target: 4 correct swaps and 4 correct pronoun splits, said aloud.

Stretch (toward C2)

Work on the particle's own meaning: up = completion (use up, eat up, drink up), out = to the end / thoroughly (sort out, work out, figure out), off = separation (call off, cut off). Add less common three-part verbs (come up against, get round to, face up to, own up to). In the formal update, use one nominalized phrasal noun (the breakdown of the talks, a positive outcome) and write a one-line justification for every phrasal verb kept. Early finishers write a third version for a different reader (a press release) and explain which features changed.

L1 watch-outs
  • Latinate by default: Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian and Romanian speakers find investigate, postpone, tolerate easy because of cognates (investigar, postergar, tolerar), and may sound stiff in speech ("I will investigate where my keys are"). Praise the accuracy, then offer the spoken twin. The C1 target for them is the phrasal verb in speech.
  • Pronoun after the particle: "switch on it," "pick up her." Very common for all L1s, including learners whose own language has particle verbs. Always test with it.
  • German and Dutch separable verbs (anrufen → ruft … an, opbellen) help with the idea of splitting but can push the particle to the very end of a long clause ("She turned every light in the house she had lived in for years off"). Teach the heavy-object rule.
  • Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese and Arabic speakers may avoid phrasal verbs altogether and use the single-word verb everywhere; their writing is accurate but their speech sounds formal. Praise every attempt at a phrasal verb in speech.
  • Extra or missing prepositions: "discuss about," "enter into the room" (Arabic, Spanish, Russian, Persian), but also "look forward to see you" (to is a preposition: look forward to seeing). Link to the three-part box.
  • Verb vs noun spelling: "There was a break down." "The out come was positive." Some Tagalog and Spanish speakers also stress both parts equally. Drill the noun stress.

4.2 · C11

Collocation, Idiom & Connotation

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use natural collocations of five types (heavy traffic · make a decision · deeply concerned · apologize profusely · a round of applause) and avoid translated ones (make a photo, strong rain, do a mistake)
  • tell strong collocations (fixed, predictable: bitterly cold, meet a deadline, pay attention) from weak ones (open, combinable: very cold, a good idea) and upgrade a weak one when precision matters
  • place near-synonyms on a connotation cline (slender · slim · thin · skinny · scrawny; thrifty · economical · stingy) and choose the word that praises, stays neutral or criticizes
  • use an idiom accurately and appropriately, and rephrase it plainly for formal writing (the tip of the iceberg → only a small fraction of the problem)
  • discuss how vocabulary is kept alive ("use it or lose it") and plan to use new chunks in real life
Materials
  • Workbook 4.2 (New words, Lexis box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, the Precise Talk planner)
  • Slides 9–13 · Collocation picture cards (coffee, umbrella, winter, worried, nervous, angry, sad, mobile phone) · Cline cards (6 envelopes) · Idiom cards + "Use it or lose it" discussion cards · Chunk-bingo listener cards · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 4.2-A (the pilot program meeting) and 4.2-B (dictation)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: collocation. Words that habitually occur together and sound right to an expert ear, often for no logical reason. The alternative is usually grammatical and understandable; it just isn't what people say.

"There was strong rain." Can you understand it? (Yes.) Is it English? (Not quite: we say heavy rain.) Is there a rule? (No, it's convention: learn the pair.) · "She made a decision." "She did a decision"? (No.) · "I need to take a photo." Why not "make"? (Convention again. Many languages use "make" here.)

Meaning: strong vs weak collocations.

"It was bitterly cold." If I say "bitterly," can you guess the next word? (Yes: cold, disappointed.) Strong or weak? (Strong: a fixed pair.) · "It was very cold." Is "very" wrong? (No, but it goes with everything: weak.) Which is more precise and vivid? (bitterly cold.) · "bitterly hot"? (No: "bitterly" goes with cold and disappointment, not heat.)

Meaning: connotation. Same denotation (dictionary meaning), different feeling. The word you choose shows your judgment.

"She's slender." Compliment or criticism? (Compliment.) · "She's skinny." Same body? (Yes.) Same message? (No: critical, maybe unhealthy.) · "He's thrifty." Is that good? (Yes: sensible with money.) "He's stingy." (Bad: he won't share.) · "The town is notorious for its traffic." Famous for something good? (No, for something bad.) · "a childlike sense of wonder" vs "Stop being so childish." Which one is positive? (childlike.)

Meaning: idiom appropriacy.

"We finally bit the bullet and booked the repairs." Did anyone bite anything? (No: they did something unpleasant but necessary.) Spoken or written English? (Mostly spoken.) · In a formal report, "This is just the tip of the iceberg." OK? (No. "This represents only a small fraction of the problem.") · "He hit the nail on its head." Nearly right. Is nearly right OK for an idiom? (No: idioms are fixed. A broken idiom is worse than none.)

Form.

Pronunciation. Say a collocation as one chunk, with no pause inside it: "heavy RAIN," "make a deCIsion," "DEEPly conCERNED." In adverb + adjective pairs both words are stressed, and the adverb often carries the emphasis ("It's HIGHly unlikely"). Idioms are also chunks; the last content word carries the stress: "bite the BULlet," "the tip of the ICEberg," "food for THOUGHT." Connotation lives in the voice too: a warm fall on "She's SLENder ↘" vs a flat, clipped "He's STINgy." American English: gray area; the flap in "a lot of" /əˈlɑɾəv/.

Drill script
  1. Finish the chunk (fast): T: "heavy…" → S: "traffic / rain." T: "meet a…" → S: "deadline." T: "pay…" → S: "attention." T: "deeply…" → S: "concerned." T: "a round of…" → S: "applause." T: "bitterly…" → S: "cold / disappointed."
  2. Upgrade it: T: "It's very unlikely." → S: "It's highly unlikely." T: "I'm very worried." → S: "I'm deeply concerned." T: "He said sorry a lot." → S: "He apologized profusely." T: "It was a big disappointment." → S: "It was a bitter disappointment."
  3. Fix the translation: T: "Can you make a photo?" → S: "Can you take a photo?" T: "I did a mistake." → S: "I made a mistake." T: "There was strong rain." → S: "There was heavy rain."
  4. Praise it / criticize it (cline cards): T: "He's careful with money. Praise!" → S: "He's thrifty." T: "Criticize!" → S: "He's stingy." T: "She's sure of herself. Praise!" → S: "She's self-assured." T: "Criticize!" → S: "She's arrogant."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 describes an invented person or an object with a neutral word ("My neighbor's dog is thin."); S2 says it twice, once kindly and once critically ("It's slender. / It's scrawny."). Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Precise Talk and "Use it or lose it" — how to run them
  • 90 seconds, not three minutes. The online project asks for 2–3 minutes; in class, 90 seconds per speaker in groups of 3 keeps it moving. The full version is homework. Three minutes of planning plus three speakers × (90 seconds of talk + 90 seconds of shades and feedback) fill the 12 minutes exactly.
  • Chunk bingo. Each listener has a 4 × 4 card (Set E) with collocations from the lesson, an "idiom used well" square and three free squares ("a strong collocation not on the card," "a precise near-synonym"). They tick what they hear. One listener also has a register-slip box. The bingo is feedback, not a competition: after the talk, the listener reads out the two best chunks.
  • Don't force the third idiom. Say it before planning: "One or two idioms that genuinely fit. If it sounds bolted on, cut it." Praise the learner who didn't use an idiom because it didn't fit.
  • Shades. After each talk, the speaker explains one near-synonym set as it applies to their topic: "If I were writing about my town for a tourist brochure, I'd say it's renowned for its market; in a complaint to the council, I'd say it's notorious for parking." Push for register and connotation, both.
  • "Use it or lose it" (5 min, before the talk). Groups of 3 take one discussion card. Questions: Is "use it or lose it" true of vocabulary? Have you ever "lost" words in your first language or in English? Are idioms more fragile than collocations? What's a realistic way to use three new chunks this week? Each learner writes three chunks and one place they'll use them (an email at work, a conversation with a neighbor, a message to a friend). This becomes the C12 warmer report. Keep it light: nobody is asked about losing their first language unless they choose to talk about it.
  • Roles: the chunk counter (a dominant speaker) holds the bingo card and gives feedback last.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on verb + noun and adverb + adjective only, with the picture cards: heavy rain, strong coffee, bitterly cold, deeply concerned, take a photo, make a decision. In the grid, give only the top half (make / do / take × 6 nouns). For the clines, give the money set and the confidence set (4 cards each) with 🙂 😐 🙁 faces on the strip. In the talk, a 45-second version with 3 collocations and no idiom is a complete task. Target: 6 collocations and one cline said aloud correctly.

Stretch (toward C2)

Introduce semantic prosody: some words attract negative partners (cause problems / damage / delays, not cause success; rife with corruption / rumors; set in: the rain / boredom set in). Ask them to find two more. Add finer clines (frugal vs thrifty: frugal is closer to austere; slender vs slim: slender is more elegant and literary). In the talk, vary one idiom deliberately for effect ("the tip of a very large iceberg") and explain why it works. Write a formal paragraph on the same topic with no idioms and compare.

L1 watch-outs
  • make / do / take: "make a photo" (German ein Foto machen, Russian sdelat' foto, Spanish hacer una foto, Dutch een foto maken); "do a mistake" (Arabic, Hindi, Greek and others use one "do/make" verb); "make research," "do a decision." The priority collocations to correct every time.
  • "strong rain" / "big rain": Spanish lluvia fuerte, Italian pioggia forte, Chinese dà yǔ (big rain), Japanese ōame (big rain). Teach heavy rain / traffic / snow as a family.
  • "win money" for a salary (Spanish ganar, Portuguese ganhar, French gagner): earn / make money; you win a prize or the lottery.
  • Translated idioms: learners may translate a colorful idiom from their own language word for word. Enjoy it (it's often a great cultural moment), then give the English equivalent if there is one. Keep a class "idiom exchange" corner on the board.
  • False-friend connotations: Spanish flaco is often affectionate (even a nickname), but skinny is usually critical in English; Portuguese and Spanish ambicioso, French ambitieux can be negative, while ambitious is usually positive in American English. Ask "praising or criticizing?" every time.
  • Idiom density: learners who have studied idioms from lists (common in exam-prep backgrounds everywhere) may overload a talk with them. Praise accuracy, then apply the "a pinch, not a plateful" rule.

Review Project — The Advanced Communicator

90 min
A task-based review, not a new-language class

There is no new grammar today. The new language is the register spectrum (shifting vocabulary, grammar and structure as a set), a handful of rhetorical devices used sparingly, signposting for extended talk, and diplomatic Q&A. The class is built around one combined task in three parts: a three-minute persuasive presentation to a small group, two minutes of questions and challenges, and a 150-word formal written summary of the argument that includes the strongest objection and the response. You listen and read with the speaking & writing checklist (Set G). Language work comes from the tasks: a short focus-on-form clinic between them. The checklist results feed C13 (Progress Test 1): they are your spoken and written evidence for Modules 1–4, and they tell you who goes to which catch-up station.

Objectives — learners can…
  • shift the register of one message (formal ↔ neutral ↔ informal), changing vocabulary, grammar and structure together, and repair a register clash
  • structure and deliver a three-minute persuasive talk with a compelling opening, two or three developed points and a memorable close, signposted at every join
  • use one or two rhetorical devices at the hinges of the argument (a rhetorical question, a rule of three, contrast, a cleft or inversion)
  • field questions and challenges diplomatically, conceding before countering (I take your point, but I'd argue… · That's fair, though I'd push back on one thing…)
  • write a 150-word formal summary of their argument (nominalized, hedged, no idioms) that fairly reports the strongest objection and their response
  • deploy structures and lexis from all four modules (aspect and modality, the subjunctive, mixed conditionals, inversion and clefts, nominalization, cohesion, hedging, phrasal verbs, collocation) in extended, controlled output

Meaning: register shifts as a set.

Pronunciation. Signposts are chunks followed by a clear pause: "This brings me to | my second point." Slow down for the preview and the close. T: "We'll check it. M4, formal." → S: "We will investigate the matter."
The Advanced Communicator — how to run it
  • Three minutes, not five. The online capstone asks for 4–5 minutes; in class, three minutes plus two minutes of Q&A per speaker keeps four speakers inside 20 minutes and still shows structure and range. The full version is homework (a phone recording, with the register shift).
  • Topics. Everyday proposals and trade-offs in workplace, education, technology, health habits and community life (Set F: a paid learning day, a four-day week for office jobs, phones locked away in school for under-16s, apprenticeships vs degrees, car-free town centers on Sundays, AI tools for homework, pricier sugary drinks in workplace cafeterias, public libraries as community hubs, remote work as the default for office jobs, first-aid training for every adult). A learner's own topic is welcome if it stays in this territory. The side is on the card or chosen freely; it is not the speaker's personal opinion unless they want it to be.
  • Q&A. Every listener asks one question or challenge with a starter (Set F: "Isn't there a risk that…?" · "How would that work for…?" · "You said…, but surely…?" · "What would you say to someone who…?"). The speaker concedes before countering at least once. The timekeeper stops the Q&A after two minutes, kindly. A speaker may say "That's beyond the scope of this proposal, but…" once: a legitimate C1 move.
  • Challenge notes feed the summary. After each talk, listeners write the speaker's main claim in one sentence and the best challenge on the back of their card, and hand them over. The speaker now has an outsider's version of their argument and the strongest objection, ready for the written summary.
  • The written summary is a register shift of the talk: the same argument, formal and concise, for readers who weren't there. 150 words (130–170 is fine). It must include the strongest objection, reported fairly with a reporting verb (One participant questioned whether…) and hedged (It could be argued that…), and the response. No idioms, no contractions, no rhetorical questions. The page has a peer-review checklist.
  • Talking tokens in the Q&A if one listener dominates: two each; one token per question. Give the timekeeper and last questioner roles to the most dominant speakers in each group.
  • Persona cards (a hospital night-shift nurse, a small-business owner, a university student, a parent of three, a retired teacher, a city bus driver) give learners a point of view that isn't theirs: "Speaking as a small-business owner, I'd be deeply concerned about…". Great for quieter learners and for anyone who doesn't want to speak for themselves.
  • If a Q&A gets heated, the timekeeper (or you) says: "Great challenge. One sentence to answer, then we move on." Challenge the idea, never the person.
The speaking & writing checklist — how to score it (feeds C13)

Speaking: five criteria, 0–3 points each, /15. Writing: four criteria, 0–3 points each, /12. 0 = no response · 1 = not yet C1 · 2 = C1 · 3 = strong C1 (moving toward C2). Score the whole spoken performance across the talk and the Q&A, and the written summary separately. Tick the module boxes (M1–M4) when you hear or read a structure from that module used correctly. The full descriptors, the class grid and the score slips are in the classroom materials (Set G).

CriterionWhat 3 points looks like
S1 · Task & organizationA compelling opening, a preview, 2–3 developed points each with evidence or an example, a memorable close; signposted at every join; within time.
S2 · Range (Modules 1–4)Structures from at least 3 of the 4 modules used naturally and without prompting; precise, strong collocations; one or two devices well placed.
S3 · AccuracyConsistent control of complex grammar; errors are rare, minor and often self-corrected.
S4 · Register & interactionRegister consistent and appropriate to the audience, shifted on purpose; Q&A handled diplomatically (concedes before countering, clarifies the question, stays calm).
S5 · Fluency & deliveryEven pace with pauses at signposts; stress highlights contrast and key points; little searching for words; easy to follow for three minutes.
W1 · ContentAccurately summarizes the proposal and main reasons; reports the strongest objection fairly and responds to it; 130–170 words.
W2 · Organization & cohesionLogical order; cohesive devices (reference, linkers, the former / the latter) make it read as one text.
W3 · Register & lexisConsistently formal: nominalization, hedging, precise collocations, Latinate verbs where they fit; no idioms, contractions or clashes.
W4 · AccuracyComplex sentences controlled; errors rare and do not affect meaning; punctuation supports the structure.

Feeding C13. Record each learner's scores and module ticks on the class grid. In C13, after Progress Test 1, send learners to a catch-up station for any module with no tick and for their lowest criterion: no M1 tick → time & modality station (future perfect/continuous, may well / might as well / needn't have, the subjunctive); no M2 tick → conditionals & emphasis station (mixed conditionals, Had I… / But for…, inversion, clefts); no M3 tick → cohesion & hedging station (nominalization rewrites, reference and ellipsis, softening); no M4 tick → lexis station (Register Swap cards and cline cards from Sets C and D); S4 or W3 ≤ 1 → register-shift station (Workbook 4.3 Ex 2 and 4 + one "three rungs" round); S5 ≤ 1 → shadowing station (the 🔊 model sentences in online lessons 4.1–4.3); W4 ≤ 1 → editing station (rewrite two sentences of your own summary with the teacher's codes). If Progress Test 1 has its own speaking and writing tasks, keep this grid anyway: it is your evidence of extended output for Modules 1–4, and it lets you give each learner one kind, specific next step.

ICQs before the presentations and the summary
Presentations: How long do you speak? (Up to three minutes.) · Can you read your notes? (Only a quick look.) · What does each listener do? (Asks one question or challenge, then writes the main claim and the best challenge on the card.) · How do you answer a challenge? (Concede, then counter.) · Do you have to present to the group? (No, you can present only to the teacher.)
Summary: Who is the reader? (A committee that wasn't there.) · How many words? (About 150.) · Formal or informal? (Formal.) · Idioms or contractions? (No.) · What must you include besides your reasons? (The strongest objection and your response.) · What do you do in the last four minutes? (Peer review with the checklist.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a talk strip: "I'd like to make the case that… · There are two points I want to raise. · Let me begin by… because… · This brings me to… For example,… · In essence,…" and a reply strip: "I take your point, but… · That's a fair challenge. What I'd say is…". A 90-second talk with two points is a complete task. For the summary, give a frame with the first words of each sentence ("This proposal argues that… · The principal reason is… · A further benefit would be… · It was argued, however, that… · In response,…") and accept 80–100 words. Score on the same checklist: a clear, well-signposted short talk can earn full marks on S1 and S5.

Stretch (toward C2)

Use one structure from each module and one device at each hinge (opening, turn, close), no more. In the Q&A, paraphrase the challenge before answering ("If I understand you correctly, your concern is…"). Shift register within the talk on purpose: open formally, land the key point with one well-judged informal line, and explain the choice afterward. In the summary, use a concessive structure (Whilst / While it is true that…, …) and one nominalized phrasal noun (the likely outcome). Homework: the 4–5 minute version with no notes, plus the formal and informal restatements.

L1 watch-outs
  • Over-formal by default: speakers of Romance languages, Greek and Russian (where academic speech is highly nominal) may deliver the whole talk in the formal register. Praise the control, then ask for one neutral or informal moment to create warmth.
  • Contractions and idioms in the summary: learners who have worked hard on idioms may carry them into writing. Link to C11: "Spoken: yes. Written summary: rephrase it."
  • False friends in signposts: "Resuming…" (Spanish resumiendo, French en résumé) → In essence / To sum up; "Actually…" meaning "currently" (Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, German aktuell); "Eventually…" meaning "possibly" (Spanish eventualmente, French éventuellement, German eventuell).
  • "According to me…" (French selon moi, Spanish según yo, Indian English): according to is for other sources. Offer "In my view…" or "I'd argue that…".
  • The subjunctive: "It is essential that he is told" is common in British English and acceptable, but the C1 target (Lesson 1.3) is that he be told; the mandative subjunctive is standard in formal American English. Don't mark the indicative wrong; tick M1 only for the subjunctive.
  • Directness differs. Some learners counter without conceding ("No, this is not correct."); others concede without countering, so their view disappears. Teach the English middle path, concede and then counter, as a technique, not a personality change.

Closing the Module

End of C12: Module 4 wrap-up and the halfway point

Recap on slide 18, "choose the verb, the partner, the voice": We'll look into it. / We will investigate the matter. (the register swap) → heavy traffic · a bitter disappointment · slender, not skinny · thrifty, not stingy (the partner and the shade) → one argument, three voices: the talk, the Q&A, the formal summary. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Use those notes, the Register Ladder observations and your checklist to plan the C13 catch-up stations.

Celebrate the halfway point. Learners have finished half of C1. Ask each person to say one thing they can do now that they couldn't do in Week 1, in any register they like ("What I'm proudest of is…", "Never have I spoken for three minutes without notes before!"). Keep it short and warm.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it can test: the four multi-word verb types and pronoun placement (turn it off / look after it / put up with it), the register pairs in both directions and choosing by context, polysemous phrasal verbs in context, nominalized forms (breakdown, outcome), collocation types and strong collocations (heavy, deeply, highly, meet, make / do / take), connotation (slender / skinny, thrifty / stingy, renowned / notorious, childlike / childish), idiom meaning and appropriacy, register shifting and clash repair, rhetorical devices and signposting. The C12 speaking and writing checklist gives you the extended-output evidence.

Watch for in Module 5: participle and absolute clauses (5.1) build on the nominal, compressed style of the written summary (Having weighed the evidence, the committee… · The budget approved, work began.). Advanced prepositions (5.2) connect directly to the inseparable and three-part verbs of 4.1 (in response to, with regard to, on behalf of), and academic vocabulary (5.3) extends the formal column of the register swap. Keep a two-minute "register tennis" or "praise it / criticize it" warmer in C14–C16: it is quick, and it keeps Module 4 lexis alive in speech. Use it or lose it.
