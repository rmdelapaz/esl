Grammar box

Four types. Ask two questions: Does it take an object? and Can the object go in the middle? The quickest test is to try it with it.

① Intransitiveno objectThe car broke down. · We set off at dawn.
② Separablenoun object: either position · pronoun: in the middle onlyturn off the light · turn the light off · turn it off
③ Inseparablethe object always follows, even a pronounlook after the kids · look after them
④ Three-partnever splitput up with the noise · put up with it

✅ Turn it off. · Pick her up at six. · Deal with it.   ❌ Turn off it. · Pick up her. · Deal it with.

A long object goes at the end: She turned off every light in the house before she left.

The register swap. Phrasal verbs lean spoken; their single-word twins lean written and formal. Neither is better: choose by reader, setting and purpose. Some phrasal verbs are perfectly at home in formal writing: carry out, point out, set up, bring about.

Noun forms fuse the two parts and move the stress to the front: break DOWN → a BREAKdown · take Over → a TAKEover · turn OUT → an OUTcome · break out → an OUTbreak.

Dialogue — the launch, in two registers

Leah: So how did the launch go? I heard it nearly fell through.

Omar: It almost did. Two suppliers pulled out at the last minute, so we had to put it off by a week and set up new contracts from scratch.

Leah: Ouch. How did the team hold up?

Omar: Honestly, they put up with a lot. A couple of them nearly burned out, but once things picked up, morale came back. Sales have taken off since.

Leah: Nice. Did you ever find out why the suppliers backed out?

Omar: We looked into it. Turns out a competitor had taken them over. We've since cut down on single-source deals so we don't get caught out again.

The formal written version: The launch was nearly abandoned. Two suppliers withdrew at short notice, obliging us to postpone it by a week and establish new contracts from scratch. The team tolerated considerable pressure, but as performance recovered, morale returned, and sales have since increased sharply. On investigating the suppliers' withdrawal, we discovered that a competitor had acquired them; we have consequently reduced our reliance on single-source agreements.

Underline every phrasal verb in the dialogue (there are more than twelve). Then find the formal twin of fell through, pulled out, put off, set up, put up with, looked into, found out, taken over in the written version. Which phrasal verb from the dialogue could have stayed in the written version?

Lexis box

Collocation types. Store the whole partnership, not the single word.

adjective + nounheavy rain · a valid point · a bitter disappointment
verb + nounmake a decision · do research · take a risk · pay attention · meet a deadline
adverb + adjectivedeeply concerned · highly unlikely · bitterly cold · fully aware
verb + adverbapologize profusely · argue convincingly · fail miserably
noun + nouna round of applause · a surge of anger · a flurry of activity

Strong and weak. A strong collocation is fixed and predictable (bitterly → cold). A weak one combines with almost anything (very, good, big). Weak is never wrong, but strong is precise.

Weakvery cold · very unlikely · very worried · a big problem · said sorry a lot
Strongbitterly cold · highly unlikely · deeply concerned · a pressing problem · apologized profusely

Connotation: same dictionary meaning, different feeling. Ask: Am I praising, staying neutral, or criticizing?

Idioms: three rules. Mostly spoken (rephrase them in formal writing) · a pinch, not a plateful (one or two per talk) · exact form (a broken idiom is worse than none).

Dialogue — the community garden fence

Rosa: Have you seen the quote for the new fence? I'm deeply concerned about the cost.

Ken: Me too. It's highly unlikely the city will cover all of it.

Rosa: We could do some research on cheaper materials. Or build it ourselves.

Ken: Ourselves? That's a calculated risk. None of us are carpenters.

Rosa: True, but Mr. Ali is. He's famously frugal. He'll find us the cheapest wood in town.

Ken: Frugal? He's the stingiest man on the block!

Rosa: Let's not be unkind. He's thrifty, and that's exactly what we need right now.

Ken: Fair enough. Let's raise the issue at Thursday's meeting and make a decision by the end of the month.

Rosa: Agreed. We'll also need to meet the grant deadline.

Ken: Then let's bite the bullet and ask him tonight.

Underline eight strong collocations and circle the idiom. Which three words describe Mr. Ali? How does each speaker feel about him?

Grammar box — the spectrum and the toolkit

Register is a spectrum, and its features shift as a set: vocabulary, grammar and sentence structure together.

FormalThe initiative will commence next quarter and is anticipated to yield significant benefits.nominal · passive · hedged · Latinate · long sentences · no contractions
NeutralThe project starts next quarter and should bring real benefits.balanced · some hedging
InformalWe're kicking this off next quarter, and it should pay off nicely.verbal · active · contracted · phrasal verbs · so, look, honestly

Rhetorical devices: one or two, placed at the hinges (the opening, the turn, the close).

Rhetorical questionCan we really afford to wait another decade?
Rule of threeIt is cheaper, it is cleaner, and it is already here.
RepetitionWe owe it to our patients. We owe it to our staff. We owe it to ourselves.
ContrastThe problem is not a lack of money, but a lack of will.
Cleft / inversionWhat we need is courage. · Never has the case been clearer.
