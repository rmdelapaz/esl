Module 4 — Lexical Range & Fluency: Answers

Answers to every Module 4 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the Register Swap update, the Precise Talk, the Advanced Communicator talk plan and written summary), and the errors you can expect to hear and read most.

Lessons 4.1–4.3Classes C10–C129 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. In a lexical module, many items have more than one good answer: accept any answer that is natural (an expert user would say it), precise (it means exactly what the context needs) and appropriate (the register and connotation fit the reader). Where a learner's answer is correct but a different choice from the key, accept it and ask for the reason: "Good. Why that one here?" A good reason is the C1 skill. American English is the model; British spellings and forms (grey, programme, nominalisation, take a decision) are acceptable when a learner uses them. In the register exercises, mark the whole sentence: a formal verb in an otherwise chatty sentence is a clash, even if every word is correct.

4.1 · Phrasal Verbs & Multi-word Verbs (C10)

Exercise 1 — The voice message (Script 4.1-A)
12345678
fell throughpulled output it offset upheld uppicked uplook intocut down on
①①②②①①③④

5 and 6 are intransitive here (the team held up · things picked up), although both verbs are separable in other senses (hold up a bank / pick up a friend): a good polysemy moment. Retell formally: fell through → was nearly abandoned · pulled out → withdrew · put it off → postponed it · look into → investigate · cut down on → reduce. Extra phrasal verbs to notice in the script: taken off, backed out, caught out.

Exercise 2 — Place the object
  1. Could you turn it down? (turn down it)
  2. She looks after her on weekends. (looks her after)
  3. Please fill it in. (also fill it out, very common in American English)
  4. I can't put up with it any longer.
  5. We need to deal with them today.
  6. He threw them away.
  7. Have you gotten over it yet? (inseparable: gotten it over) · British: got over it
  8. They called it off.

b) A: a long ("heavy") object goes at the end, keeping the verb and particle together; in B the listener waits too long for off. Wrong: any pronoun after a separable particle (1, 3, 6, 8); any pronoun between an inseparable verb and its preposition (2, 5, 7).

Exercise 3 — The register swap

a) 1 e (postpone) · 2 i (discover) · 3 c (investigate) · 4 g (increase) · 5 h (establish) · 6 f (abandon) · 7 a (tolerate) · 8 j (conduct) · 9 d (eliminate) · 10 b (encounter)

b)

  1. look into: a text to a roommate is spoken register; investigate sounds like a police report.
  2. postponed: a formal letter from an institution.
  3. either: carry out is one of the phrasal verbs that is fully at home in formal writing; conducted is slightly more formal. Accept both with this reason; this is the "don't over-correct" item.
  4. give up: relinquish over coffee is absurdly formal.
  5. rose: a single-word verb suits academic writing (increased is also good).
  6. encounter: formal customer-service register. Accept come across if the learner argues for a warmer, neutral email; the reason matters.
  7. get rid of: a quick spoken team meeting.

c) Model: "We have discovered that the heating failed because the annual inspection had not been carried out, and we have therefore established a new maintenance schedule." Also accept had not been conducted, broke down → failed / malfunctioned, a nominalized version ("Investigation revealed that the heating failure resulted from the omission of the annual inspection; a new schedule has consequently been established."). Wrong: leaving found out or set up in an otherwise formal sentence is a mild clash rather than an error; carried out may stay.

Exercise 4 — One verb, many meanings

a iv (leave the ground) · b vi (become successful) · c ii (remove clothing) · d viii (deduct) · e ix (collect someone) · f v (learn informally) · g i (improve) · h vii (manage to see) · i iii (claim, probably falsely)

In d, take 15% off separates around a long object; in i, make out that… introduces a clause and carries doubt. Point out that the collocates decide the meaning: flight, podcast, shoes, price.

Exercise 5 — From verb to noun

1 breakdown · 2 takeover · 3 outbreak · 4 outcome · 5 input · 6 setback · 7 turnout (all first-syllable stress; all one word)

Wrong: "a break down," "the take over," "an out come" (spacing), "a break-out of flu" (the noun flips: outbreak). Turnout is the usual word for attendance at events and elections.

Dialogue — the launch, in two registers

Phrasal verbs (16): fell through · pulled out · put (it) off · set up · hold up · put up with · burned out · picked up · came back · taken off · find out · backed out · looked into · (turns out) · taken over · cut down on · get caught out. Formal twins: fell through → (nearly) abandoned · pulled out → withdrew · put off → postpone · set up → establish · put up with → tolerated · looked into → investigating · found out → discovered · taken over → acquired. Could have stayed: set up (fine in formal business writing) and arguably look into in a neutral report; takeover as a noun is standard business English ("following a takeover by a competitor"). Accept any with a reason.

Speaking — the Register Swap (model update)

Model (Story 1, the community center leak; 84 words): "Following reports of water damage in the main hall, the facilities team investigated the matter on Monday and discovered that a pipe beneath the kitchen had deteriorated. Repairs have been postponed until Thursday so that a licensed plumber can carry out (K) the work safely. In the meantime, the hall will remain closed, and Saturday's classes have been relocated to the library. We apologize for the inconvenience and appreciate the patience of all users. A full report will follow." Check: 70–90 words, at least one kept phrasal verb with a reason (carry out is standard in formal writing), no idioms, pronouns placed correctly, consistent register.

Self-check
Q1
The light is still on. Which is correct?
A) Could you turn off it?
B) Could you turn it off?
C) Could you turn it off from?
Why: turn off is separable, and a pronoun object must go between the verb and the particle. A breaks the pronoun rule; C adds a stray preposition (turn off is not a three-part verb).
Q2
In a formal written report, which best replaces "we'll look into the complaint"?
A) we will look up the complaint
B) we will put up with the complaint
C) we will investigate the complaint
Why: investigate is the formal single-word twin of look into. A changes the meaning (look up = search for information) and is still informal; B means tolerate.
Q3 · new
Which sentence uses the three-part verb correctly?
A) I can't put up it with any longer.
B) I can't put up with it any longer.
C) I can't put it up with any longer.
Why: three-part verbs are never split, even by a pronoun: the object follows both particles. (Put it up exists, but it means "raise / display it," a different verb.)
Errors you will hear and read most (4.1)

"Turn off it." · "Pick up her at six." · "Look the kids after." · "Put up it with." · "a break down" (noun spacing) · "look forward to see you" · "discuss about the plan" · over-formal speech ("I will investigate where I left my keys," "Shall we commence?") and phrasal verbs in formal letters ("We will sort out your refund"). The pronoun error is the priority: correct it every time, in speech too. For range, praise noun forms (the outcome, a setback), polysemy used well (business picked up) and a justified K.

4.2 · Collocation, Idiom & Connotation (C11)

Exercise 1 — The team meeting (Script 4.2-A)

Heard (8): the last straw · cut corners · hit the nail on the head · the tip of the iceberg · a blessing in disguise · on the same page · food for thought · bite the bullet. Not heard: get cold feet · a gray area.

Meanings (accept any accurate paraphrase): the last straw = the final problem that makes a situation unbearable · cut corners = do something cheaply or quickly and lose quality · hit the nail on the head = describe a problem exactly · the tip of the iceberg = a small visible part of a much bigger problem · a blessing in disguise = something that seems bad but turns out well · on the same page = in agreement, sharing an understanding · food for thought = something worth thinking about seriously · bite the bullet = make yourself do something difficult or unpleasant. Decision: tell the board they need another month; Sam will write the email formally, without idioms.

Exercise 3 — From weak to strong
  1. bitterly cold (also freezing cold)
  2. highly unlikely
  3. deeply concerned (also deeply worried, gravely concerned in formal writing)
  4. apologized profusely
  5. heavy traffic
  6. a bitter disappointment
  7. gave a long round of applause (also gave a standing ovation if they stood)
  8. a key / pressing / major issue

Wrong: "strongly cold," "heavily unlikely," "big traffic," "a heavy disappointment," "apologized strongly."

Exercise 4 — Choose the shade and the register

a) 1 slender · slim · thin · skinny · scrawny · 2 thrifty · frugal · economical · tight · stingy · 3 curious · inquisitive · interested · nosy

Accept either order inside the positive pair and inside the negative pair (slender / slim, thrifty / frugal, tight / stingy). Frugal can sound more austere than thrifty; inquisitive can tip toward prying ("inquisitive neighbors"). A learner who notices this deserves praise.

b) 1 self-assured · 2 require · 3 thrifty · 4 notorious · 5 get going · 6 childlike · 7 nosy · 8 determined

Each alternative would jar: arrogant insults, want is too blunt for a formal email, stingy criticizes, renowned celebrates crime, commence is absurd over lunch, childish insults the retiree, curious is too kind for the complaint, stubborn would damage a reference.

Exercise 5 — Idioms: fix them, or rephrase them

a) 1 hit the nail on the head · 2 a blessing in disguise (no the) · 3 on the same page · 4 got cold feet · 5 the tip of the iceberg

b) Models:

  1. The complaints represent only a small fraction of the problem.
  2. The contractor compromised safety standards in order to reduce costs. / The contractor failed to follow the required safety procedures.
  3. The regulations on this matter are unclear. / This remains a legally ambiguous area.
  4. The board eventually took the difficult decision to raise the fees. / The board reluctantly raised the fees.
Dialogue — the community garden fence

Strong collocations: deeply concerned · highly unlikely · cover (all of) it / cover the cost · do (some) research · a calculated risk · raise the issue · make a decision · meet the (grant) deadline. Idiom: bite the bullet. Mr. Ali: Rosa calls him frugal and thrifty (admiring: he spends carefully); Ken calls him the stingiest man on the block (critical, half-joking). Same behavior, opposite judgment: the whole lesson in one exchange.

Speaking — the Precise Talk (model opening)

Model (topic: a place I know well): "The market in my old neighborhood is widely regarded as the best in the city, and on a bitterly cold Saturday morning it's still packed. What struck me, though, when I went back last year, was how many stalls had closed. Rents have risen sharply, and several families took a calculated risk by moving online. For some it paid off handsomely; for others it was a bitter disappointment. Honestly, the empty stalls are just the tip of the iceberg…" Shades: "In a travel article I'd call the market renowned; in a complaint to the city about parking, notorious." Check: 6+ strong collocations, 1–2 idioms that fit, register consistent, a near-synonym set explained with both connotation and register.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence uses the natural English collocations?
A) There was heavy traffic, so we had to make a quick decision.
B) There was strong traffic, so we had to make a quick decision.
C) There was heavy traffic, so we had to do a quick decision.
Why: heavy traffic and make a decision are both fixed partnerships. B uses "strong traffic"; C uses "do a decision."
Q2
You want to compliment a friend who is careful and sensible with money. Which word fits the connotation?
A) thrifty
B) stingy
C) tight
Why: all three describe careful spending, but only thrifty is positive. Stingy and tight criticize someone for being unwilling to spend or share.
Q3 · new
An invented coworker keeps asking about everyone's salary and private life. Which word expresses your critical view?
A) curious
B) inquisitive
C) nosy
Why: nosy is the negative end of the cline: too interested in things that are none of your business. Curious is positive or neutral; inquisitive is mostly positive (an inquisitive mind).
Errors you will hear and read most (4.2)

"make a photo" · "do a mistake" · "make research" · "strong rain" · "win money" (salary) · "strongly unlikely" · "very bitterly cold" (stacked intensifiers) · "hit the nail on its head" · "a blessing in the disguise" · idioms in formal writing · three idioms in one breath · positive intent with a negative word ("My friend is so skinny, she looks great"). make / do is the priority: correct it every time. For range, praise noun + noun chunks (a pang of guilt, a flurry of activity), verb + adverb (apologized profusely) and a well-judged absence of idioms in writing.

4.3 · Review Project — The Advanced Communicator (C12)

Exercise 1 — The model talk and Q&A (Script 4.3-A)

a) Ask yourself this (1) · I'd like to make the case that (2) · There are three points I want to raise (3) · Let me begin by (4) · This brings me to (5) · Turning now to (6) · In essence (7) · I take your point, but I'd argue (8) · That's a fair challenge. What I'd say is (9)

b) Heard:

future perfectthe scheme will have paid for itself
subjunctiveit is essential that the day be protected
mixed conditionalHad we offered something like this five years ago, we would not now be losing…
not only inversionNot only does a learning day show staff that they are valued, but it also…
what-cleftWhat we need is not another annual course, but…
nominalizationThe introduction of a learning day is a small change…
hedgingThe evidence would appear to suggest…
strong collocationa pressing concern (also staff turnover, a large return)

Not heard: needn't have; ellipsis with so / not. Also notice: a contrast (not another annual course, but a steady habit), a rule of three in the preview (skills, loyalty and cost), phrasal verbs kept in a persuasive talk (look into, kept up).

Exercise 2 — Shift the register (models)
  1. "It is important that we address the financial implications at the earliest opportunity."
  2. "Any chance you could send me those documents?"
  3. "There are some real problems here that we'll need to resolve."
  4. "The new schedule will be introduced in March and is expected to reduce overtime."
  5. "Everyone's got to be there, no exceptions, or you could get in trouble."

Answers will vary. Check that vocabulary, voice and sentence shape all match the target: 1 and 4 need nominal or passive structures and no contractions; 2 and 5 need contractions and everyday words; 3 is the middle (we'll is fine, total mess is not). Also accept for 1: The financial aspects will need to be discussed shortly.

Exercise 3 — Name the device

1 rule of three / parallelism · 2 contrast (antithesis) · 3 what-cleft (fronting for emphasis) · 4 rhetorical question · 5 deliberate repetition (anaphora), also a rule of three · 6 negative inversion

Exercise 4 — Repair the register clash (models)
  1. Formal: "The board will convene on Thursday to resolve the outstanding issues." / Informal: "The board's meeting Thursday to sort out the mess."
  2. Formal: "I have serious reservations about the aforementioned proposal." / Informal: "I think that proposal's pretty sketchy."
  3. Neutral: "Hi team, following up on our discussion, should we meet to talk it through?"
  4. "Dear Ms. Patel, Thank you for your application. Unfortunately, we are unable to offer you the position."
  5. Formal: "The quarterly figures indicate a substantial decline in revenue." / Informal: "So basically, we made a lot less money this quarter."

The key is consistency: pick a register and align every feature to it. In 4, the direction is fixed by the genre (a rejection letter must be formal and kind).

Exercise 5 — Upgrade with the toolkit
  1. By the end of 2028, the scheme will have paid for itself.
  2. It is essential that every employee complete the training. (British and acceptable: …completes / should complete; the C1 target is the subjunctive.)
  3. If we had invested in training, we wouldn't be short of skilled staff now. / Had we invested in training, we would not now be short of skilled staff.
  4. Not only is the plan cheap, but it is also effective.
  5. What we need is time, not money. / It is time, not money, that we need.
  6. The introduction of the policy led to a sharp fall in waste. / …resulted in a marked reduction in waste.
  7. This would appear to suggest that the scheme works. / This may indicate that the scheme is effective.
  8. We will investigate the reasons for the sharp increase in costs.

Wrong: 1 "will be paid for itself" · 2 "that every employee completes" is not wrong, but tick M1 only for the subjunctive · 3 "If we hadn't invested… we wouldn't be" (reversed logic), "Had we invested… we wouldn't have been short now" (time mismatch) · 4 "Not only the plan is cheap" (no inversion) · 7 "This proves perhaps…" (hedge in the wrong place).

Dialogue — one point, two voices

Features that shift together (any five): would respectfully suggest ↔ Look, between us · hedged may well prove prudent ↔ blunt there's no way · nominal some compromise to quality ↔ phrasal/idiomatic cutting corners · a modest extension ↔ push it back a week · it is by no means certain ↔ we're (contractions) · quality must remain paramount ↔ a false economy · participle clause Having reviewed… ↔ short simple clauses · no tag ↔ OK?.

Speaking & writing — The Advanced Communicator

Talk planner (model: a paid learning day). Open: Ask yourself this: when did you last learn something new at work because someone gave you the time? · Preview: skills, loyalty, cost · Point 1: tools change faster than training (What we need is…) · Point 2: turnover is a pressing concern (Had we offered…, we would not now be…) · Point 3: the cost is recovered (will have paid for itself; it is essential that the day be protected) · Close: In essence, the choice is a simple one. · Q&A: small businesses can't spare staff → stagger the day; staff will treat it as a day off → a short written note after each day.

Model written summary (155 words): "This proposal argues that employers in the region should offer every member of staff one paid learning day per month. The principal justification is the widening skills gap: as workplace technology evolves, regular, protected learning time would appear to be more effective than an annual training course. A further benefit would be improved staff retention, since employees are more likely to remain with an organization that invests in their development. It was argued during the discussion that small businesses may be unable to release staff for a full day. In response, it is proposed that learning days be staggered across each team, so that no more than one employee is absent at any time. A second concern, that the day might be treated as additional leave, could be addressed by requiring a brief written record of what was learned. On balance, the introduction of the scheme represents a modest cost with a potentially substantial return."

Check (W1–W4): proposal in sentence 1 · two supported reasons · objection reported fairly with a reporting verb (It was argued…, One participant questioned whether…) · a response · consistently formal (nominalization: the introduction of the scheme; hedging: would appear to, potentially; subjunctive: be staggered) · no idioms, contractions or rhetorical questions.

Self-check
Q1
A speaker wants to draw listeners in and make them supply the answer themselves. Which device fits?
A) Nominalization ("the implementation of the plan")
B) Ellipsis ("some agreed, others didn't")
C) A rhetorical question ("Can we really afford to wait?")
Why: a rhetorical question invites the listener to answer silently ("No, we can't"), so they arrive at the speaker's point themselves. A is a formal-register tool; B is a cohesion tool.
Q2
Which sentence best combines a formal register with the subjunctive to express urgent necessity?
A) We really need to do something about this soon.
B) It is imperative that action be taken at once.
C) It's imperative that action is taken pronto.
Why: be taken is the (passive) subjunctive after it is imperative that, and every other feature is formal. A is informal and has no subjunctive; C uses the indicative and clashes (it's, pronto with imperative).
Q3 · new
Which sentence keeps a consistent formal register?
A) The committee will sort out the discrepancies.
B) The committee will resolve the discrepancies.
C) The committee's gonna resolve the discrepancies.
Why: committee and discrepancies set a formal register; resolve matches it. A drops an informal phrasal verb into it; C clashes with gonna and a contraction.
Errors you will hear and read most (4.3)

Register clash inside one sentence ("The committee will sort out…", "Hey team, pursuant to…") · the whole talk in the formal register with no warmth · idioms and contractions in the written summary · rhetoric overload (three rhetorical questions in a row) · "Not only it is…" (no inversion) · "It is essential that he is told" (acceptable, but not the C1 target) · "Resuming,…" / "According to me,…" · countering without conceding in the Q&A · signpost-free rambling. For range, praise a well-placed cleft or inversion at a hinge, a fair and precise report of the objection in writing, and a deliberate, justified register shift.
