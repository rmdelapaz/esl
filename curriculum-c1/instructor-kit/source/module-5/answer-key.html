Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. An answer is correct if the meaning is preserved (no fact lost in compression, no claim stronger than the source), the form is right (participle chosen by voice; the participle's implied subject matches the main subject or has its own; the exact dependent preposition; -ing after a preposition; the academic word in its correct sense), and the register suits the context. Be generous about genuine alternatives: several compressions, several academic equivalents (obtain / acquire, examine / investigate), regarding / with regard to / concerning, the data suggest / suggests, and "being" kept or dropped in an absolute (The meeting (being) over). A compression that is correct but less elegant is still correct: note an upgrade, don't deduct. American English is the model; British forms (realise, analyse, cancelled, programme, enquiry, in the light of, towards) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

5.1 · Participle & Absolute Clauses (C14)

Progress Test 1 return — "Let's fix it!" (board errors)

Not only did she finish the report, but she also presented it. (inversion after a fronted negative: auxiliary before subject) · If I had accepted the offer, I would be living / would live in Boston now. (mixed conditional: past condition → present result; or keep "by now" with would have been living in Boston for a year by now) · It was in 2019 that she moved to Denver. (it-cleft uses that, even for time) · We need to do (some) research before the meeting. (research is uncountable; do / conduct research) · Extras: She must have forgotten the password. · Hardly had I sat down when the phone rang. (or No sooner had I sat down than…) · The manager insisted that every employee attend. (mandative subjunctive; attends and should attend are normal in British English, so accept them)

Exercise 2 — Compress each sentence
  1. Knowing the area well, … (reason, active)
  2. Having locked the door, … (earlier action)
  3. Written in a hurry, the report… (passive → past participle)
  4. The volunteers helping at the fair… (reduced relative)
  5. Not having received a reply, … (also: Having not received, common but less standard; Not receiving is acceptable)
  6. Taken as directed, … (also: If taken as directed)

Wrong: 3 "Writing in a hurry" (the report didn't write) · 6 "Taking as directed" · 2 "Locking the door" is understandable but loses the sequence; accept with an upgrade note.

Exercise 3 — Does the subject do it, or receive it?
  1. Checked twice, the figures… (the figures were checked)
  2. Having interviewed all the candidates, the panel… (the panel interviewed, earlier)
  3. Any passengers traveling without a ticket… (they travel: active reduced relative)
  4. Founded in 1992, the co-op… (someone founded it)
  5. …, destroying three bridges. (result; the flood destroyed them)
  6. Having been told about the delay, the passengers… (they were told: perfect passive)
Exercise 4 — Absolute clauses
  1. The funds having run out, the project was suspended.
  2. The last guest gone / having left, the hosts sank into their chairs.
  3. The speeches over / (being) over, the music began. (also: The speeches having ended, …)
  4. She presented the plan, her voice calm and steady. (also: her voice being calm and steady, heavier; or with her voice calm and steady)

Wrong: "Having run out, the project was suspended" (dangles: the project didn't run out) · "Being over the speeches, …" (word order) · "Gone the last guest, …" (Romance word order).

Exercise 5 — Sound or dangling?
  1. S (she missed the bus; she walked)
  2. D → Rushing to the station, we found the train had already left. / By the time we reached the station, the train had already left.
  3. S (the library was opened)
  4. D → Looking out of the office window, I could clearly see the new park. / From the office window, the new park was clearly visible.
  5. S (fixed phrase, exempt from subject-matching)
  6. D → After signing the lease, we felt at home in the apartment at last. / Once we had signed the lease, the apartment finally felt like home. / The lease signed, the apartment finally felt like home.
Dialogue task

Dangling: "Reading the report, several errors jumped out at me." · Fixed phrase: "Having said that" (also "All things considered") · Absolute: "The budget approved, work began in May."

Strip 5 is the subtle one: a manager can be "delayed by a storm," so some teams will call it sound. Ask: "What did the storm delay, the manager or the delivery?" Accept "sound" only if the team argues convincingly that the manager was delayed, then point out the ambiguity: in formal writing, ambiguity is itself a reason to rewrite.

Workshop, Round 2 — Model condensed reports (for the teacher, not a target to copy)

Report 1 · Library Wi-Fi (124 → 90 words): Meeting on May 3, the library board reviewed the results of the Wi-Fi upgrade. Completed in April and funded by a city grant, the new system, designed by a local firm, supports up to 200 users; the old routers, installed in 2012, handled only 40. Having examined the figures, the board noted a 70 percent fall in complaints. The budget not having been fully spent, it decided to extend the service to the garden terrace from June, weather permitting. It thanked the staff who had worked evenings during the installation.

Report 2 · Night bus (115 → 86 words): Its buses running every 30 minutes, the city's three-month Route 12 night-bus pilot connected the hospital, the university and the industrial park. Many riders working late shifts, the transit office had expected strong demand. The pilot over, it surveyed 400 riders; carried out by university students, the survey found that 82 percent felt safer. Ridership having exceeded the target, the office has recommended a permanent service from September, provided that the council approves the funding. It thanked the drivers who had volunteered for the late shifts. Trap: "Running every 30 minutes, the pilot connected…" dangles (the buses ran, not the pilot).

Report 3 · Clinic text alerts (125 → 88 words): Tested for six weeks in the Hillside Clinic waiting room, a text-alert system let checked-in patients leave the building, texting them when the doctor was ready. The waiting room having often been overcrowded, staff had received many complaints. The trial over, an outside consultant analyzed the clinic's data, finding that average waiting-room time fell from 38 to 11 minutes. Patients having responded positively, the clinic will keep the system and, provided that the software company agrees, extend it to the pharmacy. It thanked the patients who had taken part.

What counts as success: 90 words or fewer, all facts present, four or more constructions (one absolute), one fixed phrase (weather permitting, provided that, given that), no danglers. Keeping a relative clause (who had volunteered) is a sound choice: the past perfect meaning is clearer than in a reduced form. A team at 95 words with a clear text has done well.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence contains a dangling participle?
A) Having missed the bus, she walked to work.
B) Rushing to the station, the train had already left.
C) Built in 1850, the house needs repairs.
Why: the person rushing is implied, but the main subject is the train, which can't rush to catch itself. A attaches correctly to she; C attaches correctly to the house, which was built.
Q2
You want to compress "Because the road was blocked, the drivers turned back," but the two clauses have different subjects.
A) The road blocked, the drivers turned back.
B) Blocking the road, the drivers turned back.
C) Being blocked the road, the drivers turned back.
Why: an absolute clause gives the participle its own subject (the road), so nothing dangles. B dangles and reverses the meaning (the drivers blocked the road); C is not grammatical.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is both correct and properly anchored?
A) Damaging in transit, the package was returned to the sender.
B) Damaged in transit, the sender returned the package.
C) Damaged in transit, the package was returned to the sender.
Why: the package was damaged (passive → past participle) and the package is the next subject. A uses -ing for a passive meaning; B has the right participle but dangles: it says the sender was damaged.
Errors you will see most (5.1)

Dangling participles, especially with -ing + a non-human subject ("Walking home, the rain started") and after / while + -ing ("After finishing, the results…") · -ing for passive ("Building in 1968, the center…") · having for simultaneous actions ("Having walked home, I met a friend") · absolutes without a subject ("Being fine, we set off") or in L1 order ("Finished the meeting, we left") · over-compression: three participles in one sentence with the logic lost · full absolutes in casual messages. Correct danglers and voice errors every time (they change or garble the meaning); treat stiffness and over-compression as Fit or Upgrade comments, not errors.

Exercise 3 — Fixed formal phrases
  1. With regard to your inquiry… (also: Regarding)
  2. In the event of a power outage…
  3. On behalf of the whole team…
  4. In light of the new safety data… (new information → revision)
  5. …chair by virtue of her long experience…
  6. In the wake of the merger…

Text to a friend: any two, e.g. 1 → "About your question, …" · 2 → "If the power goes out, …" · 4 → "Because of the new safety data / Now that we've seen the safety data, …" · 6 → "After the merger, …". Note: on behalf of (3) is fine in a friendly thank-you speech; accept learners who argue it isn't stiff.

Exercise 5 — The clinic email (6 errors)
  1. With regards regard to your recent inquiry (or Regarding)
  2. In accordance to with new county guidelines
  3. aware about of the difficulties
  4. an alternative for to the online system
  5. refrain to call from calling the front desk
  6. discuss about this further → discuss this further

Correct, leave alone: result in shorter waiting times (the change causes them). Learners who change it to result from reverse the meaning.

Dialogue task

Chunks: account for, results from, consistent with, indicative of, reliant on, an alternative to, an impact on. Fixed phrases: With regard to, In the event of, in accordance with. In a hallway chat: "With regard to the quarterly figures" → "About the quarterly figures" (or "In the event of" → "If there's…").

Workshop, Round 2 — Model replies

Card A (81 words, body): Dear Ms. Whitfield, On behalf of the Evening Students' Council, thank you for your notice and for arranging the free shuttle. With regard to the shuttle schedule, many students are reliant on it between 5:30 and 6:30 p.m., so we would be grateful for a bus every ten minutes during that period. We are also concerned about lighting at the Oak Street garage, as some classes end at 9:30 p.m. Finally, in light of the reduction in parking, could March permits be partly refunded? Sincerely, …

Card B (84 words, body): Dear Alex Rivera, Thank you for your message, and please accept our apologies for the short notice. The change resulted from the heavy rain earlier this month. In accordance with college policy, notices are normally sent seven days in advance, and we are fully aware of how disruptive a late notice can be. In light of this, we are offering a 50% reduction in the March permit fee. As an alternative to the north lot, a free shuttle runs from the Oak Street garage every twenty minutes. Sincerely, Facilities Office

Mark for: four or more correct chunks, two fixed phrases used appropriately, a courteous formal tone, and no stacked formality ("In light of the fact that, with regard to the matter of…").

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence uses the dependent preposition correctly?
A) We were not aware about the deadline.
B) Let's discuss about the results.
C) All staff must comply with the dress code.
Why: comply takes with. A should be aware of; B should be discuss the results (transitive, no preposition).
Q2
"There was a significant increase ___ carbon emissions."
A) of
B) on
C) in
Why: in introduces the area that changed. Of is for the size of the change (an increase of 8%); on doesn't follow increase.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct for a formal report?
A) In light of the new figures, the board will revise its forecast.
B) In light with the new figures, the board will revise its forecast.
C) By light of the new figures, the board will revise its forecast.
Why: the fixed phrase is in light of (British: in the light of), used when new information prompts a change. B and C mangle the phrase.
Errors you will see most (5.2)

"comply to" · "aware about" · "depend of" · "married with" · "discuss about / enter into the room / contact with" · "an increase of crime" · "capable to do / refrain to do / object to do" · "an alternative for" · "a solution of" · "with regards to" · "in the event that of…" · formal phrases stacked in one sentence, or used in casual speech ("In the event of you being free, …"). Correct wrong chunks every time in writing (they're the whole point of the lesson); in speech, note them for feedback. Treat an unnecessary formal phrase as a Fit comment, not an error.

Exercise 4 — Stance at the right strength
  1. suggest (the authors say "may": tentative)
  2. highlights (draws attention to)
  3. acknowledge (admits a limitation)
  4. demonstrates (strong, consistent evidence justifies it)
  5. notes (a neutral fact, not a contested position)
  6. maintain (hold a position firmly, despite evidence)
Exercise 5 — Sharper or just formal?
  1. Sharper → "The team investigated the causes of the delay." (investigated is more exact and fits a report)
  2. Just formal → "I'll use the conference room at three." (utilize adds length, not meaning)
  3. Just formal (absurdly so) → "I'll try to leave soon." / "I'm heading off soon."
  4. Sharper → "A substantial proportion of respondents were deterred by the price." (precise and appropriate to a research summary)

Labels are judgments: accept a different label if the learner's reason is sound (e.g. 1 "just formal" if they argue looked into is clear enough in an internal report).

Dialogue task

looked at → an examination of · found out → indicated · a lot of people → a substantial proportion of respondents · got put off → were deterred · went up → increased · cut → reduced · kind of shows → suggests · the big thing → the primary factor · start doing across the board → implement more widely. The hedge: suggests (also would … be advisable).

Workshop — Model summaries (for the teacher, not a target to copy)

Abstract 1 (87 words): A recent pilot study examined the effect of short afternoon walking breaks on the concentration of 62 municipal office workers. Over four weeks, employees who took three five-minute walks each afternoon reported approximately 18% higher concentration than a control group; their accuracy on a data-entry task also improved slightly, although this difference was not statistically significant. The authors suggest that walking breaks may offer a low-cost means of sustaining concentration, but acknowledge that the small sample and reliance on self-report limit the findings. Larger studies are needed.

Abstract 2 (86 words): A twelve-week study in three university cafés evaluated a $1 deposit scheme for reusable cups. Disposable cup use fell by approximately 41%, although total café waste declined by only 6%, partly owing to an increase in food packaging. Staff reported that the scheme was easy to administer, but returns decreased toward the end of the semester. The authors conclude that such schemes can substantially reduce disposable cup use, yet their effect on overall waste appears modest; longer-term research is needed to determine whether participation is sustained.

Fidelity checks: Abstract 1: the accuracy result must be qualified (not statistically significant / did not reach significance); "improved concentration" should be self-reported or reported; no prove / demonstrate / show that walking breaks work. Abstract 2: don't say the scheme "reduced waste substantially" (only cup use fell substantially). Word-family shift examples: significant → significance ("did not reach significance"), analyze → analysis, generalize → generalizability, concentrate → concentration.

Self-check
Q1
In an academic essay, which is the best upgrade of "The results show the plan didn't really work"?
A) The results demonstrate that the plan was largely ineffective.
B) The results kind of show the plan didn't work out.
C) The results evidence that the plan's utilization was suboptimal.
Why: precise academic words, natural structure. B stays casual; C is over-formalized and garbled (evidence as a verb, empty utilization).
Q2
A friend texts to ask when you're leaving. Which reply has the right register?
A) I shall commence my departure imminently.
B) I'll head off in about ten minutes.
C) I anticipate initiating my egress shortly.
Why: plain, friendly words suit a text to a friend. A and C are grammatical but absurdly formal: an error of register.
Q3 · new
An abstract says brief walking breaks "may" improve concentration. Which summary sentence is faithful?
A) The findings prove that brief walking breaks improve concentration.
B) The findings suggest that brief walking breaks may improve concentration.
C) The findings indicate walking breaks is the primary factor of concentration.
Why: B keeps the source's tentative stance. A over-claims (prove); C over-claims (the primary factor) and has an agreement error (breaks is) and a preposition slip (factor in).
Errors you will see most (5.3)

Over-claiming a source ("This study proves…") · wrong-sense upgrades ("a significant building" for a big one, "approximately the budget" for "regarding") · empty formality (utilize, commence, endeavor, in order to everywhere) · Romance false friends (actual for current, realize a study, assist for attend, significative) · word-family slips ("an analyze," "significance improved," "evidently" for "evident") · stress errors on analysis, significant, approximately, deteriorate · copying whole abstract sentences instead of summarizing. Correct over-claiming and wrong-sense words every time (they misinform); treat stiffness as a Fit comment.
