5.1

Compress the Clause — Participle & Absolute Clauses

Class C14
Key phrases
  • a participle clause
  • an absolute clause
  • a dangling (misrelated) participle
  • a reduced relative clause
  • having said that, …
  • all things considered, …
  • …, weather permitting
  • given that …
  • provided that …
  • judging by …
  • barring any delays, …
  • strictly speaking, …
Grammar box

A participle clause drops the conjunction and the repeated subject: When she realized the risk, she paused → Realizing the risk, she paused. Choose the participle by voice, not by tense.

FormMeaningExample
-ingactive · same time, reason or resultKnowing the road well, he took a shortcut. · The storm hit the coast, flattening dozens of homes.
having + past participleactive · clearly earlierHaving signed the contract, they celebrated. · Having been warned twice, the driver slowed down.
past participlepassive meaningWritten in 1920, the novel still speaks to readers. · Given more time, we could have finished.
reduced relativenoun + -ing / past participlemembers wishing to attend · any items damaged in transit
absolute clauseits own subject + participle (being often dropped)The weather being fine, we set off. · The meeting over, the staff went home. · She waited, her heart pounding.
The one-question testAfter a leading participle, ask: Who or what is doing this -ing / being this -ed? That exact answer must be the subject of the next clause.
Dangling❌ Walking into the room, the noise was overwhelming. (The noise was walking?)
Repair ①Change the main subject: Walking into the room, I found the noise overwhelming.
Repair ②Give the participle its own subject, or restore a full clause: The exam finished, the results came quickly. · As I walked into the room, …
RegisterFull participle and absolute clauses are formal and written. Fixed phrases (having said that, generally speaking) work in speech too.

Dialogue — read with a partner

Editor: Your draft is good, but "Reading the report, several errors jumped out at me" dangles. The errors didn't do the reading.

Writer: Ah. So either "Reading the report, I noticed several errors," or I give it its own subject: "Once the report had been read, several errors stood out."

Editor: Exactly. And "Having said that, the second half is much tighter" is fine as it is. It's a fixed phrase, so keep it.

Writer: Good. What about "The budget approved, work began in May"?

Editor: That's an absolute clause. It has its own subject, so it's perfectly sound. All things considered, just the one dangler to fix.

Grammar box

A dependent preposition is fixed by the word in front of it, not by logic. Store the pair as one chunk.

FamilyExamples
of (knowing, lacking)aware of · capable of · devoid of · indicative of · a lack of
with (matching)comply with · consistent with · compatible with · familiar with
to (tending, turning)subject to · prone to · conducive to · object to · resort to · a solution to · an alternative to
on / from / in / fordepend on · reliant on · an impact on · refrain from · benefit from · an increase in · insight into · a reason for · a demand for
Verb → nounThe preposition can switch: increase prices → an increase in prices · solve a problem → a solution to a problem
in vs ofin = the area that changed · of = the size of the change: an increase in unemployment of 3%
Meaning changesresult in (cause) vs result from (be caused by) · agree with a person, to a proposal, on a plan
No prepositiondiscuss the plan · enter the room · contact the office · emphasize the point  ❌ discuss about
After a preposition-ing: capable of managing · refrain from smoking · object to paying
RegisterFixed phrases belong in reports and formal emails. In conversation: with regard to → about · in the event of → if · in the wake of → after

Manager: With regard to the quarterly figures, how do you account for the drop in sales?

The Right Word, the Right Strength — Academic & Formal Vocabulary

Class C16
Key words
  • obtain (get)
  • demonstrate (show)
  • substantial (big: amount)
  • significant (big: importance)
  • examine (look at)
  • sufficient (enough)
  • approximately (about + number)
  • regarding (about + topic)
  • deteriorate (get worse)
  • determine (find out)
  • facilitate (help, make easier)
  • constitute (make up)

Stance phrases:

The findings suggest that…The data indicate that…The authors argue that…This study demonstrates that…The report highlights…The authors acknowledge that…Further research is needed to…

Workshop — Abstract to Summary

Read the abstract. Underline academic words, box every hedge and stance verb, and circle the one result that was not statistically significant.

Short Walking Breaks and Afternoon Concentration in Office Workers: A Pilot Study

Prolonged sitting is widely associated with reduced alertness, yet evidence regarding brief interventions in the workplace remains limited. This pilot study examined whether three five-minute walking breaks per afternoon affect self-reported concentration and task accuracy among office staff. Sixty-two employees at two municipal offices were randomly assigned to a walking-break group or a control group for four weeks. Concentration was assessed through a daily questionnaire, and accuracy was measured using a standardized data-entry task. Participants in the walking-break group reported approximately 18% higher afternoon concentration than the control group, and their error rate declined modestly; however, the difference in accuracy did not reach statistical significance. The findings suggest that brief walking breaks may constitute a low-cost means of sustaining afternoon concentration, although the small sample and reliance on self-report limit the generalizability of the results. Further research involving larger and more diverse workplaces is required.
