Module 5 — Structure & Advanced Vocabulary

Three workshop classes on the quiet machinery of formal English. First, participle and absolute clauses fold a whole clause into the edge of a sentence (Having reviewed the figures, the board… · The meeting over, the staff went home), and learners hunt the dangling participle that betrays a careless draft. Second, dependent prepositions and fixed formal phrases (comply with, aware of, an increase in, in light of, by virtue of, with regard to) are tested in a formal-email correction challenge. Third, academic and formal vocabulary (Academic Word List families, get → obtain, show → demonstrate, stance verbs graded by strength) is put to work summarizing a short research abstract without over-claiming. The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

3 lessons Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (here, a writing or editing workshop in small teams) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-5-structure-vocabulary.html. Print materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html before C14. It holds the phrase and chunk flashcards, the Dangler Hunt strips and the three Condense the Report cards, the formal email for the Correction Challenge with its reply cards and score sheet, the two research abstracts with the summary peer-review card, "The Editor's Path" board game, and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. B2 Lesson 3.2 taught basic participle clauses (Walking home, I saw…; Having finished, she left) and reduced relatives, and B2 learners have used interested in, depend on and a handful of formal phrases for years. Check what they know in each lead-in and move quickly where they are strong. The new C1 work is the voice-driven choice of participle (-ing / having done / past participle), the absolute clause as the fix for different subjects, formal and noun-based preposition chunks (an increase in / of, a solution to, by virtue of), and register judgment: knowing when the formal word is sharper and when it is merely fancy.
  • Workshops, not lectures. All three classes end with a team editing or writing task (a report to condense, an email to correct, an abstract to summarize). Keep the input stages brisk so the workshop gets its full time: that is where the learning becomes visible.
  • Feedback on range, accuracy and appropriacy. In every workshop, circulate and take notes; don't stop a team to fix aware about. At feedback, give three kinds of comment, without names: one accuracy fix ("comply to → comply with"), one range upgrade ("because the funds ran out → the funds having run out"), and one appropriacy note ("commence is right in the report, but in the email to a colleague, start is better"). Write them in three board columns: Fix · Upgrade · Fit.
  • Peer-review protocol (all three classes): Praise · Question · Polish. Reviewers give one specific praise ("Your absolute in line 2 is elegant"), ask one genuine question ("Who is doing the reviewing here?"), and suggest one polish, never more. The writer decides whether to accept it. Reviewers write their three points on the review card, not on the draft. This keeps critique precise and adult, and stops one strong editor rewriting another team's work.
  • Dominant speakers. Every workshop has built-in roles: scribe (holds the pen, writes only what the team agrees), checker (runs the "who is doing this?" test or the preposition-only proofread), and word-counter / timekeeper. Give the most dominant learner the checker role: they may only ask questions, not propose wording. In whole-class discussion, use talking chips (3 per learner). Say it kindly: "Thanks, Omar, that's a sharp point. Let's hear from a team that hasn't reported yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each class has a "Stretch" box with C2-leaning material (with-absolutes and literary absolutes, prepositions fronted in relative clauses such as the policy in accordance with which…, stacking and calibrating hedges, precise academic collocations such as conduct research, draw a conclusion). Offer it; don't require it.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spelling and some British forms throughout this module: realise, practised, cancelled, analyse, emphasise, utilisation, programme, enrolment, enquiry, queue, CV, towards, "we've no buffer". Teach realize, practiced, canceled, analyze, emphasize, utilization, program, enrollment, inquiry, line, résumé, toward, "we have no buffer." Note that American usage prefers in light of; British English often says in the light of. Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Compress → connect → choose. In C14 learners compress clauses so formal prose carries more meaning in fewer words, and check that every participle is anchored to the right subject. In C15 they connect words with the exact small preposition the pattern demands, and the formal phrase that fits the genre. In C16 they choose the precise academic word and the right strength of stance verb, and learn to hear over-formality as an error. Each class ends with a text the team improves together: an over-connected report condensed, a flawed formal email corrected, a research abstract summarized faithfully. These are the editing habits Module 6 (critical reading, essays, professional writing) will rely on.

Trauma-informed: tests, formal letters and research topics

The test return. Some adults, including highly qualified professionals whose credentials were not recognized here, carry painful associations with being graded. Keep the return brief, private and warm (see C14). Never read scores aloud or compare learners.

Formal correspondence. For many newcomers, formal letters mean immigration offices, benefits agencies, courts or landlords. Keep every formal text in this module in neutral territory: a college parking notice, a workplace training update, a clinic's appointment system, a library's Wi-Fi. Don't use immigration, asylum, benefits, court or eviction letters as examples, and don't ask learners to bring in real letters they have received.

Research topics. The C16 abstracts are about walking breaks at work and a reusable-cup scheme on a campus: low-stakes, everyday science. Learners discuss the study, not their own health. Journal prompts always allow an invented or reported example ("something a colleague concluded," "a news item"). As in every module, a new identity or an invented example is always a good answer.

Write a short, positive comment on every paper: one strength + one thing to practice, phrased as an upgrade, not a deficit ("Your conditionals are accurate; next, try mixing time frames"). Collect 4–5 common errors from different papers for the C14 board (no names). Note which learners scored below about 60% so you can offer a quiet 1:1 chat at the break.
  • Print the flashcards (Set A): fixed participial phrases, preposition chunks and academic words, most of them word-only; 6 picture cards for the phrase drill. Card stock helps.
  • For C14: one set of Dangler Hunt strips per team of 3 and one Condense the Report card per team (Set B; three different reports, so neighboring teams differ), plus one peer-review card per team.
  • For C15: one copy of the Riverside College parking email per team, one score sheet, and one reply card (A or B) per team (Set C).
  • For C16: one research abstract per learner (Set D: Abstract 1 for most teams, Abstract 2 for fast or strong teams), an A/B "explain it to a friend" card per pair, and one summary peer-review card per team. One Editor's Path board (Set E) per group of 3–4, a die or coin, and counters.
  • Have a phone ready to "play" the voicemail in C15 (read the script while holding the phone to your ear).

Returning Progress Test 1 (12 min) — keep it warm

1. Celebrate first (3 min). Before any paper comes out, write "We can…" on the board and list three things the whole class did well (for example: place events precisely in the future with be due to / be on the verge of · speculate about the past with must have / can't have · add emphasis with inversion and clefts · hedge a claim diplomatically). Say it out loud.

Four modules of C1! You can speculate precisely, imagine mixed time frames, emphasize with inversion, and soften a claim so it sounds professional. That's advanced English. Give yourselves a hand.

2. Return privately (2 min). Hand papers back face-down or in folders. Never read scores aloud and never compare learners. Everyone gets a written comment: one strength + one upgrade to work on.

3. "Let's fix it!" (5 min). Show 4 anonymous errors from the tests. Use your own learners' errors if you can; typical ones at this point: Not only she finished the report, but she also presented it. · If I had accepted the offer, I would live in Boston by now. · It was in 2019 when she moved to Denver. · We need to make a research before the meeting. (Also common: She must have forgot the password. · Hardly had I sat down than the phone rang. · The manager insisted that every employee attends.) Pairs fix them. Take answers. Praise the fixing, not the error-free papers.

4. One goal (2 min). Each learner writes one goal in the workbook box ("My goal after Progress Test 1"): "I want to refine ___. Every week I will ___." Give two examples: "I want to refine my inversion. Every week I'll rewrite three sentences from a news article with Not only… / Rarely…." / "I want to hedge more naturally. I'll note five hedges from a podcast each week and use two at work."

A test is a map. It tells us where we are. It doesn't tell us who we are.

Some adults carry hard memories of school and tests, and professionals retraining in a new country can feel a test as a judgment on their whole career. Keep your voice light and move on quickly. Offer a 1:1 chat at the break to anyone who struggled (below about 60%), and plan catch-up with the Module 1–4 online lessons. Don't let the test return grow past 12 minutes. The best encouragement is a successful lesson straight after it, and today's grammar rewards what C1 learners already have: a good ear for written English.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: compression, not a new meaning. A participle clause says what a full clause says, minus the conjunction and the repeated subject. The reader supplies the link (time, reason, condition, result).

"Knowing the road well, he took a shortcut." Who knew the road? (He did.) Why did he take the shortcut? (Because he knew the road.) Is because in the sentence? (No, the reader supplies it.) · "The storm hit the coast, flattening dozens of homes." What flattened the homes? (The storm.) Before or after it hit? (As a result of hitting.)

Meaning: choose by voice, not by tense.

"Having signed the contract, they celebrated." Which came first? (Signing.) Who signed? (They did: active.) · "Signed in March, the contract runs for two years." Did the contract sign anything? (No, someone signed it: passive, past participle.) · "Building in 1920, the museum…" Can a museum build? (No! Built in 1920.)
"Members wishing to attend are asked to register." Is this a full clause? (No, a reduced relative.) Full version? (Members who wish to attend…) · "Items damaged in transit will be replaced." Active or passive? (Passive: items that are damaged.)

Meaning: absolute clauses have their own subject.

"The weather being fine, we set off early." Was we fine? (No, the weather was fine.) How many subjects? (Two: the weather, we.) Why can't we write "Being fine, we set off"? (It says we were fine: it dangles.) · "The meeting over, the staff went home." What's missing? (being: the meeting being over.) Formal or casual? (Formal, written.)

Meaning: the dangling participle.

"Walking into the room, the noise was overwhelming." Grammatically, who is walking? (The noise!) Do we understand it anyway? (Yes, but it's a careless error in formal writing.) Two ways to fix it? (Change the subject: …, I found the noise overwhelming. Or give it its own subject / a full clause: As I walked into the room, …)
"Having said that, the second option is cheaper." Who said it? (No one in particular: a fixed phrase.) Is it a dangling participle? (No, fixed phrases are exceptions.)

Pronunciation. A leading participle clause is its own tone unit: a slight rise and a short pause at the comma, then the main clause falls: "Having reviewed the figures ↗ | the board approved the plan ↘." Absolutes need the pause most, or listeners attach the noun to the wrong verb: "The meeting over ↗ | the staff went home ↘." Having is often weak /ˈhævɪŋ/ → /əvɪŋ/ in fast speech but should stay clear when reading aloud. Past participles keep their endings: canceled /ˈkænsəld/, damaged /ˈdæmɪdʒd/, based /beɪst/. Fixed phrases are single chunks with one main stress: "all things considered," "weather permitting," "having said that."

Drill script
  1. Compress it (T says a full sentence; learners say the compressed version): T: "Because he knew the area well, he took a shortcut." → S: "Knowing the area well, he took a shortcut." T: "After she had locked the door, she checked it twice." → S: "Having locked the door, she checked it twice." T: "The report was written in a hurry, and it contained errors." → S: "Written in a hurry, the report contained errors." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Voice switch (T gives a verb + a subject; learners decide): T: "write · the letter" → S: "Written in haste, the letter…" T: "write · the manager" → S: "Having written the letter, the manager…" T: "delay · the train" → "Delayed by snow, the train…" T: "warn · the driver (twice, earlier)" → "Having been warned twice, the driver…"
  3. Absolute or dangle? T: "The road was blocked. The drivers turned back." → S: "The road blocked, the drivers turned back." T: "The meeting had ended. Everyone left." → "The meeting over, everyone left." T: "The weather was fine. We set off." → "The weather being fine, we set off."
  4. Rhythm: choral with a clear rise–pause–fall: "Having reviewed the figures ↗ | the board approved the plan ↘." · "Weather permitting ↗ | we'll eat outside ↘." Then individuals read one line from Script 5.1-A.
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 says a two-clause sentence about something from today's news or workplace (real or invented); S2 compresses it and says which kind it is (present, perfect, past, absolute). 1 minute, then swap.
Condense the Report — how to run it
  • Teams of 3 with roles: scribe (writes only what the team agrees), checker (runs the one-question test on every leading participle and counts constructions), word-counter / timekeeper. Rotate roles in C15 and C16.
  • Round 1 — Dangler Hunt (5 min). Eight strips from "an intern's draft" of a building report. Teams sort them into two piles, sound and dangling. Five dangle (1, 3, 5, 7, 8). The fixed phrase (Having said that) and the absolute (The funding approved) are sound: learners who "fix" them are over-correcting, which is worth a quick word at feedback. Each team repairs two danglers aloud, one each way.
  • Round 2 — Condense (12 min). Three report cards (the library Wi-Fi upgrade, the Route 12 night-bus pilot, the clinic's text-alert trial), each about 125 words and deliberately over-connected with and, because, when, after, which. Target: 90 words or fewer, every fact kept, at least four participle or absolute constructions (one must be an absolute) and one fixed phrase. Model condensed versions are in the answer key.
  • Round 3 — Peer review (5 min). Teams swap drafts with a team that had a different report. On the review card: Praise (one construction that works well) · Question (one "who is doing this?" query) · Polish (one suggestion) · Danglers found: 0 / 1 / 2. Drafts go back; writers decide whether to accept the polish.
  • Judgment, not maximization. Remind teams of the online lesson's advice: if a sentence feels airless after three participles, restore one conjunction. A clear 92-word draft beats a cryptic 70-word one.
  • Dominant speakers make good checkers: they may only ask questions ("Who is doing the examining?"), never dictate wording. Quieter learners often make excellent scribes; give them the pen and the authority to write only agreed wording.
ICQs before the workshop
Round 1: How many strips dangle? (Five.) Is Having said that a dangler? (No, it's a fixed phrase.) · Round 2: Maximum words? (Ninety.) Can you delete a fact? (No!) How many constructions? (At least four, one absolute, plus one fixed phrase.) Who holds the pen? (The scribe.) · Round 3: How many comments do you write? (Three: praise, question, polish.) Do you rewrite their report? (No, you comment on the card.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C1 learners speak far more fluently than they read and write. Give a strip with the three voice boxes and color-code them (-ing blue, having green, p.p. orange); learners underline the participle in each Script 5.1-A chunk in its color. In the Dangler Hunt, read each strip aloud for the team. In Round 2, the learner takes the first two sentences of the report card only and compresses them orally before the scribe writes. Target: 3 constructions, spoken and then copied; Workbook Ex 2 items 1–4 only.

Stretch (toward C2)

Add with-absolutes (With the deadline approaching, the team worked late), literary absolutes without a participle (He entered, hat in hand), and participles after conjunctions (Once completed, the form should be returned; If left untreated, …). Discuss the accepted exceptions: considering, given, assuming, judging by, speaking of are dangling in form but standard. In the workshop, challenge strong teams to 75 words with no loss of clarity, then to rewrite one sentence in a lighter register for a staff newsletter.

L1 watch-outs
  • Dangling participles: speakers of topic-prominent languages (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese) often let the opening phrase set a topic rather than share a subject ("Walking home, the rain started" mirrors "As for walking home, rain started"). Make the one-question test a routine.
  • Positive transfer, different word order: Spanish, Portuguese and Italian have absolutes (Terminada la reunión, salimos; Finita la riunione, …), so the idea is familiar, but learners may keep the L1 order: "Finished the meeting, we left." English puts the subject first: The meeting finished / over, …
  • -ing for passive meaning: "Building in 1920, the museum…" is common across L1s, especially where one participle form covers both voices (German speakers sometimes mix -end / ge-…-t equivalents; Arabic and Turkish speakers transfer active verbal nouns). Point at the voice boxes.
  • Overusing having + p.p.: Russian, Ukrainian and Polish have perfective adverbial participles (прочитав), and French has ayant fini, so learners may put having everywhere, even for simultaneous actions ("Having walked home, I met a friend"). Ask: "Did the walking finish first?"
  • French en + gérondif: "By walking home, I met a friend" (from en rentrant) confuses manner with time. By -ing = method; a bare participle = time or reason.

5.2 · C15

Advanced Prepositions & Prepositional Phrases

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use the fixed preposition after key verbs (comply with, refrain from, account for, resort to, result in / from, object to, consist of) and adjectives (aware of, subject to, prone to, consistent with, reliant on, conducive to)
  • use the preposition an abstract noun requires (an increase in, a solution to, an alternative to, insight into, an impact on), and notice when it differs from the verb's
  • distinguish in (the area that changed) from of (the size of the change): an increase in unemployment of 3%
  • avoid intrusive prepositions after transitive verbs (discuss, enter, contact, emphasize)
  • use fixed formal phrases (with regard to, in light of, by virtue of, in the event of, on behalf of, in accordance with, in the wake of) in formal writing, and plain equivalents in conversation
  • correct a formal email and write a short formal reply with accurate prepositions

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: "dependent" = chosen by the word in front, not by logic.

"All contractors must comply with the regulations." What does comply mean? (Obey, follow.) Could we say "comply to"? (No. But conform to takes to: you can't reason it out.) · "Prices are subject to change." Will prices definitely change? (No, they may: it's a condition.)
"How do you account for the delay?" Am I asking for a number? (No, an explanation.) · "Young people account for 40% of users." Here? (A proportion: they make up 40%.) · "They resorted to legal action." Was it their first choice? (No, a last option.)
"Poor planning resulted in the loss." Cause or effect first? (Cause: planning → loss.) · "The loss resulted from poor planning." (Effect first.) Same facts? (Yes, opposite direction.) · "I agree with you." (a person / opinion) · "I agree to the proposal." (accept it) · "We agreed on a date." (reached a decision together)

Meaning: nouns, and in vs of.

"We need to increase funding." Preposition after increase? (None.) "There's been an increase ___ funding." (in.) "an increase in funding of 12%." What does of tell us? (The size.) · "We solved the problem" → "We found a solution ___ the problem." (to.) Is it of? (No.)
"Let's discuss about the budget." Correct? (No: discuss takes a direct object.) Other verbs like this? (enter the room, contact him, emphasize the point, approach the building, reach an agreement.) But the noun? (a discussion about / of the budget: the noun can take a preposition.)

Meaning: fixed formal phrases.

"In light of the new figures, we'll revise the plan." Did something new happen? (Yes, new information.) Plain version? (Considering / Because of the new figures.) · "She chairs the committee by virtue of her seniority." Why does she chair it? (Because she is the most senior.) Is by virtue of about being virtuous? (No! It means "because of a quality or position.") · "With regard to your inquiry…" Where would you see this? (A formal email or letter.) To a friend? (About your question…)

Verb + prepdepend on · comply with · account for · refrain from · resort to · result in / from · consist of · contribute to · object to · benefit fromPlease refrain from using your phone.

Pronunciation. Dependent prepositions are almost always weak and linked to the word before: aware of /əˈwɛrəv/, comply with /kəmˈplaɪwɪð/, consist of /kənˈsɪstəv/, depend on /dɪˈpɛndɑn/. Because they are unstressed, learners mishear them. Drill them as one word. In fixed phrases the stress falls on the content noun: "with regard to," "in light of," "by virtue of," "on behalf of," "in the event of," "in accordance with." Exception: prepositions become strong at the end of a clause or in contrast: "It's not what you comply to, it's what you comply with."

Drill script
  1. Chunk drill (hold up a chunk card showing the word only): comply → S: "comply with" · aware → "aware of" · subject → "subject to" · an increase → "an increase in" · refrain → "refrain from" · insight → "insight into." Choral, then fast individuals. Then learners add a short object: "comply with the rules."
  2. Family drill: T: "of!" → learners call out as many of-adjectives as they can in 10 seconds (aware, capable, devoid, indicative, conscious). Then with, to, on.
  3. Verb → noun: T: "increase prices" → S: "an increase in prices" · T: "solve the problem" → "a solution to the problem" · T: "demand housing" → "a demand for housing" · T: "affect health" → "an effect on health."
  4. Register flip: T (formal): "In the event of rain…" → S (plain): "If it rains…" · T (plain): "After the decision, he resigned." → S (formal): "In the wake of the decision, he resigned." · T: "about your inquiry" → "with regard to your inquiry."
  5. Weak forms: choral /əˈwɛrəv/ "aware of it" ×3, "comply with the law" ×3, "consists of three parts" ×3, fast, linked.
Formal Email Correction Challenge — how to run it
  • Teams of 3, new roles: scribe, checker, timekeeper. Each team gets one copy of the Riverside College email about resurfacing the north parking lot (about 190 words, from the Facilities Coordinator to evening students) and one score sheet.
  • Round 1 — Correct (8 min). The email contains 12 preposition errors: wrong preposition, missing preposition, intrusive preposition, to instead of from + -ing, and a mangled fixed phrase. It also contains 5 decoys: correct chunks that often look wrong to learners (result in, reliant on, of about 120 spaces, In the event of further delays, agreed to the shuttle proposal). Teams underline each change and write the correction on the score sheet. +1 for each correct fix; −1 for changing a correct chunk. Teams may "bank" a doubtful item by writing "?" (0 points) instead of guessing.
  • Reveal (included). Go through the email line by line on the slide or board. Let teams argue their case: "We think of about 120 spaces is wrong" → "Why is of right here?" (It's the size of the change.) Highest score wins; ties go to the team with fewer −1s.
  • Round 2 — Reply (8 min). Reply card A: you are the elected student representative, writing on behalf of evening students; ask for more frequent shuttles and better lighting at Oak Street. Reply card B: you are the Facilities Office, replying to a student who complained that they were not warned in time. Both: 80–100 words, at least four dependent prepositions and two fixed formal phrases, formal but human.
  • Round 3 — Preposition-only proofread (3 min). Teams swap replies and read only for prepositions: circle every preposition attached to a pattern word and ask, "Is this the chunk?" Then Praise · Question · Polish on the review card.
  • Dominant speakers are good checkers in Round 1 (they must justify every change by naming the chunk), but in Round 2 they may not write: the scribe writes. Quieter learners can take Reply card A, which has a phrase bank.

L1 watch-outs
  • Direct translation of the L1 preposition: Spanish and Portuguese depender de → "depend of"; casado con / casado com → "married with"; consistir en → "consist in" (for "consist of"). French se conformer à → "comply to"; discuter de → "discuss about". German abhängig von → "dependent from"; interessiert an → "interested on". Ask learners to write their own three "L1 traps" at the top of the chunk list.
  • "an increase of": Russian (рост цен), Polish, Arabic (ziyādat al-…) and Chinese learners use a genitive structure, so "an increase of prices / of crime" feels natural. Use the "area vs figure" test every time.
  • Intrusive or missing prepositions: Chinese, Japanese and Korean speakers (postpositions or none) may drop the preposition ("aware the risk," "comply the rules") or add about after discuss, mention, explain. Arabic speakers often add prepositions to transitive verbs ("enter to the room").
  • to + infinitive after a preposition: "capable to do," "refrain to park," "object to pay." Most L1s use an infinitive here. Rule: after a preposition, -ing. The to in object to / look forward to / be committed to is a preposition, not an infinitive marker.
  • "in the light of" / "with regards to": learners from British-English schooling write in the light of (correct British; in light of is the American norm). With regards to is heard everywhere but marked in formal writing; teach with regard to or regarding.

Academic & Formal Vocabulary

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • replace everyday words with academic equivalents that fit the exact sense (get → obtain, show → demonstrate, big → substantial / significant, about → approximately / regarding)
  • move an idea across an Academic Word List family (analyze / analysis / analytical; significant / significance / significantly; evident / evidence / evidently)

  • recognize over-formalization (utilize, commence, endeavor in the wrong place) as an error of register
  • summarize a research abstract in academic register, and explain the same study plainly to a friend

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the formal word must fit the exact sense.

"a substantial increase" vs "a significant discovery." Which one is about size or amount? (substantial.) Which is about importance? (significant.) In a research report, "a significant difference" can mean what else? (Statistically significant: unlikely to be chance.) · "approximately 40%" vs "concerns regarding privacy." Can we say "concerns approximately privacy"? (No: approximately is only for numbers.)
"The researchers obtained the samples." Did they buy them, take them, or receive them? (We don't know: obtain is neutral and formal.) · "Conditions deteriorated." Better or worse? (Worse.) Plain English? (got worse.)

Meaning: stance verbs have strength.

"The data suggest a link." Is the link proven? (No, possible.) · "The trial demonstrates that the drug works." Stronger or weaker? (Much stronger: treated as proven.) · "Critics maintain the model is flawed." Does everyone agree? (No; they hold the position firmly, maybe stubbornly.) · "She concedes that the sample was small." Is she admitting a weakness? (Yes.)
The abstract says "may constitute a low-cost means." A student writes "proves that walking breaks are effective." Is that a style problem or an accuracy problem? (Accuracy: it misrepresents the source.)

Meaning: don't over-formalize.

A text to a friend: "I shall endeavor to commence my journey imminently." Is it grammatically wrong? (No.) What's wrong? (The register: absurdly stiff.) Better? (I'll leave soon.) · Does utilize add meaning to use? (Almost never.) Does demonstrate add meaning to show in a report? (Sometimes: it implies proof.)

Abstract to Summary — how to run it
  • Abstract 1 (about 145 words): "Short Walking Breaks and Afternoon Concentration in Office Workers: A Pilot Study." 62 employees at two municipal offices, four weeks, three five-minute walking breaks each afternoon; self-reported concentration about 18% higher than the control group; error rate on a data-entry task declined modestly but not significantly; the authors hedge ("may constitute") and acknowledge limitations. Abstract 2 (for fast teams): a reusable-cup deposit scheme in three university cafés, with a large fall in disposable cups but a small, uncertain effect on overall waste.
  • Step 1, alone: learners annotate. The key trap is the non-significant accuracy result: a faithful summary must not say accuracy "improved" without qualification.
  • Step 2, pairs: card A: "Explain the study to a friend over coffee. No word from the Academic Word List allowed!" Card B: "You're the friend. Ask two questions, e.g. 'So does it actually work?' 'How many people were in it?'" Swap. This makes the register contrast physical before anyone writes.
  • Step 3, teams: 70–90 words for the staff newsletter's "Research Roundup." Requirements on the frame: six academic items (words or phrases), two stance verbs (one must be suggest / indicate or acknowledge), one word-family shift (e.g. significant → significance), and no claim stronger than the abstract's. Sentence starters are on the frame for teams that want them.
  • Step 4, peer review: the review card asks: Praise · Question · Polish; Faithful? (Does it claim more or less than the abstract? Underline any over-claim.); Sharper or just formal? (label two word choices). The model summary in the answer key is for you, not a target to copy.
  • Dominant speakers take the fidelity checker role: they compare the draft with the abstract sentence by sentence and may only point, not rewrite. In Step 2, pair a dominant speaker with a confident partner and ask B to hold them to the "no academic words" rule.

L1 watch-outs
  • Latinate false friends: Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian speakers have a head start with academic vocabulary but meet false friends: actual (≠ current: actual = real), eventual(ly) (≠ possible: = in the end), assist (≠ attend: French assister à, Spanish asistir a), realize (≠ carry out: Spanish realizar un estudio → conduct a study), sensible (≠ sensitive). Flag these in the vocabulary box.
  • Over-formalizing everything: learners from school systems that reward elaborate written style (many Arabic, Persian, Spanish-speaking and South Asian contexts) may see utilize, commence as "better English." Use the use / utilize test and praise plain precision.
  • Over-claiming: in some academic traditions strong assertion signals confidence, and learners from Chinese, Russian or Arabic academic writing may write "proves" or "it is clear that." Frame hedging as honesty about evidence, not weakness.
  • Stress on AWL words: speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Turkish) and many Asian languages give equal weight to each syllable ("a-na-ly-sis"); French speakers often stress the final syllable ("analysis"). Drill the stress shift in each family.
  • "The data is / are": learners may have been taught either. Accept both; in formal science writing prefer the data suggest.

Workbook 5.3: "My new words" (fifteen everyday → academic pairs, each marked sharper or just formal), the journal, and "Module 5: look back at my goal."

Check — self-check answers

Q1 A The results demonstrate that the plan was largely ineffective. · Q2 B I'll head off in about ten minutes. · Q3 (new) B The findings suggest that brief walking breaks may improve concentration. Radio item (Ex 1), any six: looked at → examined / studied · about → approximately · found out → found / determined · a lot more → considerably / substantially more · went down a bit → decreased / declined slightly · not enough to really prove anything → not statistically significant · kind of shows → suggests / indicates · do a bigger study → conduct a larger study.

Closing the Module

End of C16: Module 5 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "compress, connect, choose": Having reviewed the figures, the board… · The meeting over, … (compress, and keep the subject anchored) → comply with · an increase in · in light of · by virtue of (connect with the exact preposition) → obtain · demonstrate · the data suggest (choose the precise word and the honest strength, and don't over-formalize). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C17 warmer.

Check the C14 goals. At the end of C16, invite learners to read their Progress Test goal again (the "Look back at my goal" box in Workbook 5.3): "Did you practice it? What will you do next?" This is private: no one reports to the class.

Watch for in Module 6: critical reading (6.1) turns today's stance verbs outward: learners will notice when a writer's demonstrates is stronger than the evidence. Essay writing (6.2) and professional writing (6.3) need all three skills at once: a condensed, anchored sentence, the right preposition in with regard to your application, and a register that suits the reader. Keep the Fix · Upgrade · Fit columns: they become the marking categories for Module 6 writing. Learners who have just discovered formal vocabulary may over-formalize their C17–C19 writing; praise precision, and keep asking "sharper, or just formal?"
