Advanced English Workbook · Module 6

Reading & Writing at C1

Three workshops on the texts that shape decisions. First you take an argument apart: what is claimed, on what evidence, and where the reasoning slips. Then you build one that could survive the same scrutiny. Finally, you put that discipline to work in the documents that represent you professionally: the email, the report and the job application.

How this module works

Each class is a workshop: you read, draft, test and revise, usually with a partner. Task labels tell you what to do: Read · Listen · Write · Say · Pair work · Group work. Every writing genre in this module comes with an annotated model and a rubric, so you always know what "good" looks like before you start.

You never have to argue for a view you actually hold. In the debates and essays you may take the side on your card. In the job-application task you may write as yourself or as one of the professional profiles your teacher provides. Both are equally valid.

Language box — the anatomy of an argument
Claimthe point the writer wants you to accept"The pilot should be made permanent."
Evidencewhat is offered in support: data, research, testimony, examples"Sick days fell by 18 percent."
Warrantthe usually unstated assumption that links the evidence to the claim"The four-day week, and nothing else, caused the fall."
Weak pointwhere the evidence is thin, unrepresentative or does not support this claimCompared with which period? Winter, when people are often ill?

Three questions that never fail: What exactly is claimed? What is the evidence? Does the evidence support this claim, or a smaller, different one?

Describing a text preciselyThe author contends that… · concedes that… but downplays… · implies (without stating) that… · overstates the significance of… · dismisses the objection that…
Evaluating evidenceThe evidence is relevant but thin / anecdotal / selective / outdated / compelling. · The figure suggests a correlation but does not establish cause.

Read — Two articles on one decision

Harborview Logistics, a regional delivery company, has just finished a six-month trial of a four-day working week. Management must now decide whether to keep it. Two columnists in Riverside Business Weekly have taken opposite sides. Both writers, the company and the figures are invented. Read each article twice: once for sense, once with a pen. Sentence numbers are for reference.

Article A · Give Us Friday Back — by Dana Whitfield, workplace columnist

1 Six months ago, Harborview Logistics did something its competitors still consider unthinkable: it gave its 240 employees Fridays off, with no cut in pay. 2 The results are in, and they should settle the argument. 3 During the pilot, sick days fell by 18 percent. 4 In May, the company's best month, output per employee actually rose by 9 percent. 5 Anyone who still believes that more hours automatically mean more work has simply not been paying attention.

6 Consider Rosa Delgado, a warehouse supervisor who has worked at Harborview for eleven years. 7 "For the first time, I'm not exhausted on Monday," she told me. 8 "I spend Fridays with my daughter, and I come back ready to work." 9 Stories like hers are everywhere at Harborview. 10 According to Dr. Amara Osei, an organizational psychologist at Lakeside University, rested employees make significantly fewer errors, a finding that matters enormously in a business where a single mislabeled pallet can cost thousands.

11 Admittedly, customer response times slipped during the pilot. 12 But this was a minor teething problem, and it will surely disappear as teams adjust.

13 Meanwhile, forward-thinking companies across the country are making the switch. 14 Harborview can either join them or watch its best people walk out the door to employers who already have. 15 The dinosaurs in the boardroom who want to drag staff back to the grinding five-day treadmill should ask themselves one question: do they want a workforce that is merely present, or one that is genuinely productive? 16 The answer is obvious. 17 Give us Friday back — permanently.

Article B · A Long Weekend Is Not a Business Plan — by Martin Keane, business commentator

1 Harborview Logistics' four-day-week experiment has been a pleasant holiday for some employees. 2 For the company's customers, it has been something else entirely. 3 During the six-month pilot, customer complaints rose by 11 percent. 4 That figure alone should end the discussion.

5 Last month I spoke to the owner of a family bakery that relies on Harborview for its flour deliveries. 6 A Friday order arrived on Monday, and she had to close her shop for a day. 7 Behind every statistic is a small business like hers, struggling to survive. 8 Is a longer weekend for warehouse staff really worth a family's livelihood?

9 The supporters of this fashionable gimmick seem to believe that employees should be paid for doing less. 10 That is not how business works. 11 Profits fell in the second quarter of the pilot, which tells you everything you need to know about where this experiment is heading. 12 Industry consultant Greg Hollis, who has advised logistics firms for thirty years, calls the four-day week "a luxury that only tech companies can afford."

13 Of course, it is true that staff turnover fell during the pilot. 14 But young workers today will always chase the next perk; loyalty bought with long weekends will not last. 15 And if Harborview gives in now, where will it end? 16 Next year it will be a three-day week, and the year after that, the warehouse will be run on goodwill alone. 17 Harborview's management should end the experiment before the damage becomes permanent.

Read · Say — Dialogue: talking about a text, not just reacting to it

Nadia: So, did Whitfield convince you?

Tomás: Emotionally, yes. Intellectually, I'm less sure. She asserts that the results "settle the argument," but the evidence doesn't quite carry that weight.

Nadia: The 18 percent drop in sick days seems solid enough.

Tomás: It's relevant, certainly. But she never says what it's being compared with. If the comparison is with the winter months, the pilot may simply have coincided with fewer colds.

Nadia: Fair point. And the May figure is the best month, isn't it? That's cherry-picking.

Tomás: Exactly. And notice what she does with the customer problem: she concedes it in one sentence and downplays it in the next. "A minor teething problem" is an assertion, not a finding.

Nadia: To be fair, Keane isn't any better. He dismisses the fall in staff turnover with a sweeping remark about young workers.

Tomás: Agreed. Neither of them is arguing in good faith, really. They're both recruiting readers rather than informing them.

Nadia: So where does that leave the decision?

Tomás: Where it should be: with the full figures, not with either columnist.

Underline every verb that describes what a writer does. Which sentence contains Tomás's verdict?

Read — Model: a critical evaluation (annotated)
Model textWhat it is doing
In "A Long Weekend Is Not a Business Plan," Martin Keane contends that Harborview Logistics should end its four-day-week pilot.Claim identified in one sentence, with a precise verb (contends, not says).
His purpose is plainly persuasive, and his tone, scornful from the first sentence, where the pilot becomes "a pleasant holiday," signals his stance before any evidence appears.Purpose, stance and tone, supported with a short quotation.
His strongest evidence is statistical: customer complaints rose by 11 percent. This is relevant, but Keane overstates its weight when he claims it "should end the discussion"; a six-month total can conceal whether a problem is growing or fading.Evidence evaluated fairly: its strength is acknowledged before its limits.
The rest of the case is weaker. A single bakery's late delivery is an anecdote presented as typical, and the question that follows is an appeal to emotion rather than an argument. Keane also attributes the fall in second-quarter profits to the pilot without considering other causes, a classic false cause. He concedes that turnover fell, yet immediately dismisses the point with an unsupported generalization about "young workers," and he closes with a slippery-slope prediction of a three-day week.Weaknesses named precisely, each tied to a specific part of the text.
Keane may well be right that customer service needs protecting. However, because his argument depends more on loaded language and speculation than on evidence, it does not, in my view, justify ending the pilot.Verdict: fair (grants what is right), hedged (may well, in my view), and reasoned (because…).

Notice what the model does not do: it never says whether the four-day week is a good idea. It judges the argument, not the policy.

Phrases for building and defending a case

This essay will argue that…One clear advantage is…A more serious concern, however, is…The evidence strongly suggests that…Admittedly, … Nevertheless, …Critics contend that… Yet…This overlooks the fact that…Taken together, these points indicate…On balance, …The wider implication is that…
Language box — the architecture of an argument essay
IntroductionHook → context → thesis. The thesis usually comes last, where it lands with most force.
Body paragraphPEEL: Point (topic sentence) → Evidence → Explanation (why it supports the point) → Link (back to the thesis). One idea per paragraph.
Counter-argumentConcede → rebut: state the strongest opposing point fairly, then show why your position survives it.
ConclusionRestate → synthesize → implication. No new points.
AddingFurthermore · Moreover · What is moreContrastingHowever · By contrast · On the other hand
ResultConsequently · Therefore · As a resultConcedingAdmittedly · Granted · It is true that
ExemplifyingFor instance · To illustrate · A case in pointTurning backNevertheless · Yet · Even so

A strong thesis is specific, arguable and defensible, and it answers the question actually asked. Test: Could a reasonable person disagree?

Read — Model essay (annotated)

Question: "To what extent should employers limit work email outside working hours?"

Model textWhat it is doing
For many employees, the working day no longer ends when they leave the building; it follows them home in their pocket and chimes at the dinner table. As smartphones have blurred the line between office and home, a growing number of organizations have begun to ask whether after-hours email is a harmless convenience or a hidden cost. While a total ban would be impractical for many businesses, employers should set clear limits on out-of-hours email, because doing so protects employee well-being and, perhaps surprisingly, improves the quality of the work itself.Hook (a vivid image, not "Since the dawn of time…") → context (frames the debate) → thesis: concedes (While a total ban…), commits (should set clear limits) and previews two reasons.
The most compelling reason for limiting after-hours email is its effect on recovery. Studies in occupational health have repeatedly found that employees who cannot mentally detach from work in the evening report poorer sleep and greater exhaustion. This matters because recovery is not a luxury: a worker who never fully switches off arrives each morning with a smaller reserve of energy and patience. Protecting evenings, therefore, is less a perk than a condition of sustainable performance.P topic sentence · E research evidence · E explanation (This matters because…) · L link back to the thesis (therefore).
A second, less obvious benefit is that limits force clearer communication during the working day. Organizations that have introduced "quiet hours" often report that staff plan ahead, write fuller messages and rely less on last-minute requests. In other words, a boundary on time becomes a discipline on thinking: a question that cannot be fired off at 10 p.m. tends to be asked better at 10 a.m. Far from slowing work down, then, sensible limits can make it more deliberate.Second reason, same PEEL rhythm. A second, less obvious benefit signposts and references back. Nominalization (a discipline on thinking) gives an academic register.
Admittedly, critics contend that rigid rules ignore the reality of modern work: teams are spread across time zones, and genuine emergencies do not respect office hours. Yet this objection targets a blanket ban rather than the limits proposed here. A policy that allows delayed sending, defines what counts as urgent and gives staff the right not to reply until morning preserves flexibility while removing the expectation of constant availability. It is that expectation, not the occasional late message, that does the damage.Concede the strongest objection, fairly and in full → rebut it (Yet this objection…) by showing the thesis survives it. No straw man.
Limiting after-hours email, then, is not an attack on flexibility but a defense of the conditions that make good work possible. Better recovery and clearer daytime communication point in the same direction: the costs of constant availability outweigh its convenience. Employers who set sensible boundaries now are likely to find that what they protect is not only their employees' evenings, but their employees' best thinking.Restate in fresh words → synthesize the two reasons into one judgment → implication (the "so what"). No new evidence.

Test the architecture: read only the first sentence of each paragraph. Do they add up to the thesis?

To: Operations Management Team · From: Leila Haddad, Operations Coordinator · Date: October 14		Informative title; memo line tells the reader who, from whom, when.
1. Purpose
This report examines staff satisfaction with the current shift-swap system and recommends improvements.		Aim and scope in one sentence.
2. Method
An online survey was distributed to all 240 warehouse staff over two weeks in September; 172 responses were received (a response rate of 72%). Four team leaders were also interviewed.		Credibility: how the information was gathered. Passive keeps the focus on the process.
3. Findings
3.1 Ease of use. A majority of respondents (58%) described the paper swap form as difficult to complete.
3.2 Approval times. Swaps were found to take an average of four days to approve; in one case in five, approval arrived after the shift had begun.
3.3 Fairness. Almost half of respondents (47%) felt that requests were approved inconsistently across teams.		Numbered subheadings make the report navigable. Neutral, figure-based statements; no opinions yet.
4. Conclusions
These findings suggest that the current system is slow, is perceived as unfair, and places an unnecessary burden on team leaders.		Interprets the findings, with a hedge (suggest).
5. Recommendations
It is recommended that the paper form be replaced with a digital request in the existing scheduling app, that a maximum approval time of 48 hours be introduced, and that the approval criteria be published. A three-month trial in the Receiving team is advised before the system is adopted company-wide.		Specific, actionable steps. Subjunctive after recommended that (be replaced). A realistic next step.

Read · Say — Dialogue: the read-aloud test

Ana: Could you listen to this email before I send it? It's to a supplier I've never dealt with.

Joseph: Sure. Go ahead.

Ana: "Dear Mr. Patel, I hope you are well. We have been customers of your company for some time, and we have generally been satisfied. Recently, however, there have been some issues, and I was wondering whether it might be possible to discuss them."

Joseph: It's very polite. But as the reader, I still don't know what you want. What's the actual problem?

Ana: Our last two deliveries were three days late, and I need to know whether the next one will arrive on the 12th.

Joseph: Then say that first. Something like: "I am writing about our last two deliveries, which both arrived three days late. Could you please confirm that our next order will arrive on June 12?"

Ana: Isn't that a bit blunt for a first email?

Joseph: Not at all. It's courteous; it's just clear. The politeness is in "Could you please," not in making him guess.

Ana: Fair enough. Direct isn't rude, and vague isn't polite.
