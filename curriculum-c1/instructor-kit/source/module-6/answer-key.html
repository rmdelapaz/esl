Module 6 — Reading & Writing at C1: Answers

Answers to every Module 6 workbook exercise and self-check question, a full analysis of the two Harborview articles (with the company's real pilot figures), model answers for the open writing tasks, notes on using the five genre rubrics, and the errors you can expect to see most.

Marking principle

This module is about reasoning and genre, so many answers are open. Accept any analysis that is accurate, anchored in the text and precisely named. A learner who calls sentence A14 a "false dilemma" and another who calls it "an appeal to fear" can both be right if they can say why. What you do not accept: vague labels with no textual anchor ("it's biased"), and verdicts about the topic (whether the four-day week is good) offered in place of verdicts about the argument. For writing, mark with the rubrics in the workbook: four criteria, three bands each, 12 points per genre. American spelling and conventions are the model (organization, analyze, Ms., Sincerely, résumé, inquire); accept British forms (organisation, Yours sincerely, CV, enquire) when used consistently. At C1, appropriacy counts as much as accuracy: a grammatically perfect email that buries its request is not a strong email.

6.1 · Critical Reading — Evaluating Arguments & Bias (C17)

Exercise 1 — The radio commentary (Script 6.1-A)
  1. Claim: supermarkets (in Riverside) should get rid of their self-checkout machines.
  2. Evidence 1: the 81-year-old neighbor who struggled for twenty minutes: an anecdote (one case, presented as typical).
  3. Evidence 2: theft rose by 20 percent at one store on Pine Street, according to its manager: statistics + testimony, but from a single store (unrepresentative / possibly cherry-picked), and the rise may have other causes (false cause).
  4. Weakness: the false dilemma at the end ("either we keep real cashiers, or… nobody talks to anybody"). Also accept: bandwagon ("what everybody is already thinking"), concede and dismiss (shorter lines, waved away), appeal to emotion / rhetorical question ("Is that the kind of town we want…?"), loaded language ("fighting with", "ruining").
  5. Tone: indignant · exasperated · nostalgic · combative. Accept any precise adjective; do not accept "negative" or "angry" without refinement at C1. Push: "Angry how? Contemptuous? Wistful?"
Exercise 2 — Fact, opinion, or opinion in disguise?

1 F · 2 F · 3 D (clearly presents a judgment as self-evident) · 4 F (checkable; whether it is significant is another matter) · 5 D (It is widely accepted borrows the authority of consensus without evidence) · 6 F · 7 D (a causal claim stated as fact; it can be investigated, but here it is asserted) · 8 O (Frankly openly signals a personal judgment).

Item 7 generates the best discussion. Some learners will say "F, because you could check it." Good: a causal claim is testable in principle, but the sentence offers no test. That is exactly the gap a critical reader notices. Accept D or a well-argued O; do not accept F without that nuance.

Exercise 3 — Verbs for what a writer does

1 C (assert) · 2 E (concede) · 3 H (downplay) · 4 F (imply) · 5 D (refute) · 6 B (overstate) · 7 G (dismiss) · 8 A (concede … but downplay)

Watch: refute means to prove wrong; in everyday use people say "he refuted the claim" when he only denied it. At C1, prefer rebut or reject for an attempt, refute for a successful one. Also: imply (the writer hints) vs infer (the reader concludes).

The real pilot figures (Evidence Checker role card · classroom materials, set C)

The Evidence Checker holds these invented "official" figures. They reveal that both columnists select and frame the data:

  • Sick days: −18% compared with the previous six months (July–December, which includes the winter flu season); only −7% compared with the same months last year. Whitfield's figure is real but flattering: a false cause risk (season) and an unstated comparison.
  • Output: +9% per employee in May (the best month); −2% averaged over the six months; output per hour worked +8%. Whitfield cherry-picks May; Keane ignores the per-hour gain.
  • Customer complaints: +11% over six months overall, but +31% in January–February; back to normal by April; −3% in May–June compared with last year. Keane's total hides a problem that had already faded; Whitfield's "teething problem" is closer to the truth, but she asserts it without showing it.
  • Profits: Q1 +1%; Q2 −4%. Industry fuel costs rose 15% in Q2, and competitors' profits fell by about 5%. Keane's false cause.
  • Staff turnover: 6 people left during the pilot, compared with 14 in the same months last year. Staff survey: 81% want to continue; 12% say the workload is harder to manage in four days.

A strong group verdict: "Whitfield makes the stronger case, because the full figures mostly support her, but neither article is well argued: both select data and substitute loaded language for evidence." Also accept a well-reasoned verdict for Keane (e.g. the early complaint spike is a real business risk).

Exercise 5 — Take the loading out
  1. senior managers who oppose the change · smuggled in: they are outdated and doomed
  2. this new scheduling policy / this trial · smuggled in: it is trivial and a passing trend
  3. return staff to a five-day week · smuggled in: force, suffering, pointless repetition
  4. a shorter working week for employees · smuggled in: staff are relaxing, not working
  5. management reduced customer service hours / staffing · smuggled in: violence, carelessness, blame
  6. experts who disagree · smuggled in: their expertise is fake (innuendo)

Accept any genuinely neutral wording. Reject "euphemistic" rewrites that load the other way ("a well-earned rest," "streamlined service"): that is the stretch discussion.

Dialogue — talking about a text

Evaluative verbs: asserts · concedes · downplays · dismisses (also convince, recruiting, informing as describing moves). Tomás's verdict: "Where it should be: with the full figures, not with either columnist." (Also accept "Neither of them is arguing in good faith.")

Model critical evaluation · rubric notes

The model in the workbook (on Article B) would score 12/12. Homework asks learners to write their own on Article A or an article of their choice. A 12/12 answer on Article A names: the claim (A17); purpose/tone with a quotation ("dinosaurs in the boardroom"); the sick-day figure as relevant but uncompared; the May figure as cherry-picked; the concession in A11 downplayed in A12; the false dilemma in A14; a fair, hedged verdict. Typical 6–8/12: accurate on loaded language, but "evaluates" evidence only by repeating it, and ends "I agree with her."

Self-check
Q1
"Since the ban came in, accidents have fallen, so the ban clearly works." What should a critical reader most suspect?
A) That the accident figures must have been invented
B) That the sentence uses loaded language
C) A false cause: the ban preceding the fall does not prove it caused it
Why: sequence is not causation. Other factors (weather, road works, fewer drivers) may explain the fall. A is cynicism, not critical reading; B misreads a mostly neutral sentence (clearly is the only loaded word).
Q2
"The council's reckless plan will devastate our neighborhood." What does the word choice tell a critical reader?
A) The language is loaded: it signals the writer's stance before any evidence is given
B) The plan is definitely dangerous, because the writer says so
C) The sentence is neutral and factual
Why: reckless and devastate carry the judgment. Loaded language is a flag to check the evidence, not proof that the writer is wrong (and not proof that they are right: B).
Q3 · new
"Remote workers answer emails faster, so remote work makes people more productive." Which is the unstated warrant?
A) Remote workers receive more emails than office workers.
B) Answering emails quickly is a good measure of overall productivity.
C) Some people prefer working in an office.
Why: the argument only works if email speed stands for productivity, which is exactly where it can be challenged (fast replies may mean constant interruption). A is a new fact, not the linking assumption; C is irrelevant.
Errors you will see most (6.1)

"There are many evidences." (uncountable: a great deal of evidence, several pieces of evidence) · "The author says that…" everywhere (push for contends, asserts, concedes) · "This is a fallacy" with no name or anchor · confusing imply and infer · "The writer critics the council" (criticizes; critic is a person, critique a noun/formal verb) · "In my opinion the article is bad" (a reaction, not an evaluation) · false friends: actual for "current" (Spanish/Portuguese/French), argument for "plot" or "topic" (Spanish argumento, Italian argomento) · treating every emotional word as a fallacy. For range, praise the evidence is suggestive but not conclusive · conflates correlation with cause · the figure is conspicuously absent · to her credit, she concedes…

6.2 · Academic & Argument Writing — Structuring an Essay (C18)

Exercise 1 — The introduction (Script 6.2-A)
  1. Hook: an observation / striking fact: in the center of many mid-sized American cities, one of the most common land uses is parking. (Accept "a surprising observation," "a vivid scene.")
  2. Context: lots are half-empty much of the week, but councils are reluctant to remove them, fearing fewer shoppers.
  3. Thesis: "Although drivers' needs cannot simply be ignored, converting underused lots into public squares would bring more people downtown, not fewer, and would give struggling town centers a reason to exist beyond the car." (Accept close paraphrase that keeps the concession and both reasons.)
  4. Predicted body paragraphs: (1) foot traffic / business for shops; (2) community life / a reason to visit downtown; (3) counter-argument: drivers, access, disabled parking, rebutted.
  5. Although (the concession: drivers' needs).
Exercise 2 — Thesis clinic

1 T · 2 O · 3 F · 4 Q (salaries, not training time) · 5 S

Model rewrites: (1/2) "Employers should give staff paid time for training, because a workforce that keeps learning adapts faster to new technology and is less likely to leave." · (3) "While paid training time is costly for small businesses, the case for it is stronger than the case against: the cost of skills gaps and staff turnover is higher still." · (4) "Paid training time is a better investment for employers than across-the-board pay raises, because it increases both productivity and retention." Accept the opposite position if it is specific and arguable ("Paid training time should be optional, not required, because…").

Exercise 3 — Signposting that matches the logic

1 Consequently (result) · 2 By contrast (two things compared) · 3 Nevertheless (concession, then a turn) · 4 Furthermore (another point on the same side) · 5 Admittedly (opens a concession) · 6 For instance (example)

7 "…saved the company money. Nevertheless / However / Even so, some staff disliked it." Disliking it is not a result of saving money; it is a contrast.

Item 2 vs 3 is the key C1 distinction: By contrast compares two different things; Nevertheless accepts a point and then turns against its expected consequence. However would fit both, which is why it is overused.

Exercise 4 — Concede and rebut, fairly

The straw man is B, because nobody opposing paid training argues that workers "should never learn anything new"; the real objection is cost (as in A). B also rebuts with a truism ("the world is changing"), not a reason.

Model (phones collected: for): "Admittedly, collecting phones may seem to treat adults like schoolchildren, and some staff need to remain reachable for urgent calls. Yet this overlooks the fact that a short, device-free meeting respects everyone's time more than a longer, distracted one, and a single designated contact can handle genuine emergencies." (against): "Admittedly, phones on the table can distract a meeting. However, this overlooks the fact that many staff use their phones for the agenda, notes and calendars; a clear norm (screens face-down unless needed) addresses the distraction without removing the tools."

Exercise 5 — Rebuild a PEEL paragraph

B (Point) → D (Evidence) → A (Explanation) → C (Link)

Clues: B is the only sentence that works without context (topic sentence; also links to a previous paragraph); D has for instance; A has This suggests (referencing back to the evidence); C has It follows that and widens to the thesis.

Dialogue — a peer review

Softening phrases: I wasn't entirely sure how… · You might consider adding… · I'd question the rebuttal · That reads a little like… (also it's fair, but). She checks the thesis first so that all her feedback is measured against what the writer is trying to do, and so the writer knows the main message has landed.

Workshop — outline, draft, peer review

Model theses for every question card are printed on the teacher strip under the cards (classroom materials, set E). Mark the draft (introduction + one paragraph) against the first three rubric rows only; save the full rubric for the finished essay (homework). Check the peer-review sheets as well as the drafts: a reviewer who ticks every box "Y" and writes "good" in both boxes has not reviewed. Return those with one question: "Which sentence would you cut?"

Self-check
Q1
Which of these is the strongest thesis statement for an argument essay?
A) "This essay will discuss electric cars and their advantages and disadvantages."
B) "Although electric cars are costly upfront, their long-term savings and lower emissions make them the better choice for most drivers."
C) "Electric cars are a type of vehicle that runs on electricity."
Why: B concedes, commits and previews two reasons; a reasonable person could disagree. A announces a topic; C is a definition.
Q2
You are writing your conclusion and think of a strong new piece of evidence. What should you do?
A) Move it into a body paragraph, where it can be developed; the conclusion introduces no new points
B) Add it to the conclusion for a strong finish
C) Leave it out entirely, since the essay is nearly done
Why: new material in a conclusion leaves a loose thread. Good evidence is worth the revision (C wastes it).
Q3 · new
"Critics argue that paid training is expensive." Which is the strongest rebuttal?
A) "But these critics clearly don't care about workers."
B) "Yet the cost of replacing staff who leave for lack of development is often far higher."
C) "However, training is very important."
Why: B answers the actual objection (cost) with a comparison of costs. A attacks the critics, not the argument; C is a truism that ignores the objection.
Errors you will see most (6.2)

Thesis as announcement ("In this essay I will talk about…") · the thesis in the first sentence, with no hook or context · "On the other hand" used to add a point (it must contrast, ideally after On the one hand) · "Besides," / "Moreover," / "Furthermore," opening every sentence · comma splices ("Remote work saves time, it also reduces costs.") · evidence dropped without explanation · In conclusion + a new argument · informal slips (a lot of, stuff, nowadays everybody) · overgeneralizing (always, everyone, never) where a hedge is needed (often, many, tends to) · "Some people say… but I think…" as the entire counter-argument. For range, praise Granted, … · This objection rests on the assumption that… · Far from…, … · It is precisely… that… (cleft from Module 2).

6.3 · Professional & Formal Writing — Reports, Emails & Applications (C19)

Exercise 1 — From voicemail to email (Script 6.3-A)

Notes: To: the four Receiving team leaders (Dev, Maria, Olu, Chris) · Purpose: tell them the digital shift-swap trial starts Monday, March 3, in Receiving only · Action: each sends the names of two volunteers by noon Thursday; confirm attendance at training · Details: training this Wednesday, 2:00–3:00 p.m., Conference Room B; copy (cc) Karen Liu.

Subject: Shift-swap app trial starts March 3 — two volunteers needed by Thursday noon
Hi Dev, Maria, Olu and Chris,
I am writing on Karen Liu's behalf to let you know that the new digital shift-swap trial will begin in Receiving on Monday, March 3.
Could you each please send me the names of two volunteers to test the app by noon this Thursday? There will also be a short training session for team leaders this Wednesday from 2:00 to 3:00 p.m. in Conference Room B; I would be grateful if you could confirm that you can attend.
Please let me know if you have any questions.
Best regards,
[Name]
cc: Karen Liu

Accept Dear all, or Hello everyone,; Hi + names is normal among colleagues. The deadline, the date and cc Karen are the details most often lost. A subject line of "Trial" or "Info" scores 1 on the first rubric row.

Exercise 2 — The right formula

1 I am writing to inquire · 2 Please find attached · 3 Following up on our conversation · 4 I would be grateful if you could · 5 please do not hesitate to contact me · 6 I look forward to hearing from you

Accept enquire (British). In 1, about must follow inquire; learners who write "I am writing to ask" have the right register with a less formal verb: accept. In 5, lowercase please after the comma.

Exercise 3 — Register repair
  1. Problem: far too informal for the reader (and vague: "sort it"). Model: "Dear Mr. Alvarez, I am writing about the quarterly safety report, which is due on Friday, May 16. Could you please confirm who will be completing it this quarter? Thank you. Best regards, …"
  2. Problem: buried purpose; filler. Model: "Dear Ms. Adeyemi, I am writing because we have not yet received the invoice for March. Could you please send it by Friday? Thank you. Kind regards, …"
  3. Problem: over-softened; no specific date. Model: "I would like to request a day off on Friday, May 23. Please let me know whether this can be approved."
  4. Problem: archaic, absurdly stiff for a colleague. Model: "Hi Sara, could we find twenty minutes this week to go over the proposal? I'd really value your input."

Accept any version that leads with the purpose, keeps the request clear, and matches the reader. Invented names and dates are fine; missing dates are not.

Exercise 4 — Notes into report language
  1. A survey was distributed to all staff.
  2. A majority of respondents described the form as difficult to complete. (Also: The majority of respondents reported difficulty completing the form.)
  3. Approval was found to take an average of four days. / Swaps were found to take four days, on average, to approve.
  4. It is recommended that the paper form be replaced / discontinued. (Accept should be replaced; point out the subjunctive be as the more formal choice, from Lesson 1.3.)
  5. Inconsistent approval across teams was a significant source of dissatisfaction. / Dissatisfaction arose from inconsistent approval across teams.

Wrong: "It is recommended that the form is replaced" (acceptable in British English; in American formal writing prefer be) · "It is recommended to replace…" (acceptable but less formal) · keeping "we" and "loads of" · "Majority of respondents" (needs A or The).

Exercise 5 — Confident, not boastful

1 B · 2 C · 3 H · 4 C · 5 B · 6 H

Model rewrites. B → C: "I have presented our team's monthly results to senior management for two years and led the orientation for every new hire." H → C: "Although I am new to this sector, I bring three years of scheduling experience and a certificate in logistics, which I completed while working full-time." For item 6, the best advice is to delete the apology: never draw a reader's attention to your English; let the letter demonstrate it.

Writing lab — model report sections (data card, classroom materials set G)

The data card (Harborview break-room and canteen survey) is deliberately different from the workbook model so that learners cannot copy it. A 12/12 answer: 3. Findings with three subheadings (e.g. Opening hours · Food options · Seating), each reporting the key figure neutrally ("Almost two thirds of night-shift respondents (64%) reported that no hot food was available during their break."); 4. Conclusions hedged ("These findings suggest that night-shift staff are poorly served…"); 5. Recommendations specific and justified ("It is recommended that a vending option for hot meals be installed…; that 20 additional seats be provided…"). Deduct on Use of data if the writer invents figures or reports only the most dramatic ones.

Writing lab — cover letters: what to look for

The strongest letters quote or mirror two or three phrases from the job posting and answer each with evidence. The commonest weakness at C1 is not grammar but generic content: a letter that could be sent to any employer. Ask "Which sentence could only be written to this employer?" Learners writing from profile cards should still target the posting; those writing as themselves may invent details they would rather not share. In the United States, a cover letter does not include age, marital status, nationality, religion or a photo; if learners add these (common in some countries' conventions), explain the convention; don't mark it as a language error.

Self-check
Q1
You need a colleague to send figures by Friday. Which request is best for professional writing?
A) "I was just wondering if maybe it might be possible to perhaps get some figures sometime?"
B) "Send me the figures by Friday."
C) "I would be grateful if you could send the figures by Friday."
Why: polite framing (I would be grateful if) plus a clear request and deadline. A is over-softened and vague; B is clear but curt for most workplace relationships.
Q2
Which sentence strikes the right confident-but-not-boastful tone for a cover letter?
A) "I am an exceptional leader with unrivaled talent and natural brilliance."
B) "I led a team of four and delivered the project two weeks ahead of schedule."
C) "I don't really have much experience, but I hope I might just about be suitable."
Why: B lets a specific, verifiable achievement speak. A is empty self-praise; C undersells and plants doubt.
Q3 · new
You are writing to a hiring manager, Ms. Carter, for the first time. Which opening and closing suit American business English best?
A) "Hi Ms. Carter!" … "Cheers,"
B) "Dear Ms. Carter," … "Sincerely,"
C) "Dear Sir or Madam," … "Yours faithfully,"
Why: you know her name, so use it, with the formal American pair. A is too casual for a first contact. C is the British convention for an unknown reader; in American English, Dear Hiring Manager, is now the usual choice when the name is unknown.
Errors you will see most (6.3)

"Dear Sir/Madam" when the name is on the posting · "Dear Mrs. Carter" (use Ms. unless you know her preference) · "Dear Karen Liu" (full name in the greeting) · missing subject line or "Hello" as subject · "I am writing you to…" (acceptable in informal American English; prefer I am writing to in formal text) · "Please find attached my CV" with no attachment · "Kindly do the needful" / "Please revert back" (regional usage in South Asian English: explain that US readers may not understand it) · "Respected Sir" · "Hope you are fine" (well) · "I am interesting in this position" (interested) · "informations," "advices," "equipments" · date ambiguity (03/04) · reports that mix opinion into findings ("Sadly, 58% of staff…") · "It is recommended that the form is replaced" (accept, but teach be). For range, praise In light of these findings… · a marked decline in… · I would welcome the opportunity to… and a well-judged register switch between the email and the report.
