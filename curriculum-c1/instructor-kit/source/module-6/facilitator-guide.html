Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 6 — Reading & Writing at C1

Three skills workshops rather than three grammar lessons. First, a critical-reading lab: two short, invented opinion columns on the same workplace decision, taken apart for claims, evidence, bias and fallacies (with a fallacy card sort and a structured seminar). Then an essay-structuring workshop: thesis, paragraph architecture, counter-argument and rebuttal, conclusion, moving from outline to draft to structured peer review. Last, a professional-writing lab: the email, the report with headings and the cover letter, all tied to one invented workplace and written for real or fictional job-seeker profiles.

3 lessons Lessons 6.1–6.3 Classes C17–C19 Weeks 9–10 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The shape is the kit's usual one (warmer → lead-in → clarify with CCQs → drill → controlled practice → freer task → feedback → homework), but in this module the "language" being clarified is mostly moves (claim, warrant, concession, rebuttal, purpose-first) and genre conventions, and the freer task is a real reading or writing product. Treat each class as a workshop: you model, learners try, peers test, learners revise.
  • The stage plan is your clock and adds up to 90 minutes. Writing stages are the ones that overrun. Protect them by cutting the controlled practice first (every exercise that is cut is listed as homework).
  • Present from slides/module-6-reading-writing.html. Print materials/module-6/classroom-materials.html before C17. It holds the flashcards, the professional profile cards, the seminar role cards and the pilot fact sheet, the fallacy card sort, the essay question cards with assigned sides, the peer-review sheet, the writing-lab pack (report data card, job postings, reader cards), Fallacy Bingo and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons for the five model sentences; the lesson projects (a critical evaluation, an essay plan, one real professional text) extend the class products.
  • Refinement, not first contact. At B2 learners met opinion essays, formal emails and linking words (B2 Module 4 and 7). Don't re-teach paragraphs or "Dear Sir/Madam." Spend the time on what is new at C1: the warrant, the difference between a strong case and a well-made argument, the fair concede → rebut, the purpose-first reader-focus of professional writing, and register control across genres. Recycle the course's own grammar deliberately: nominalization and academic style (3.1), cohesion (3.2), hedging (3.3), the subjunctive after recommend (1.3), academic vocabulary (5.3).
  • Feedback on range, accuracy and appropriacy. In every freer task, keep three columns: accuracy (errors), range (ambitious, precise language to praise and spread) and appropriacy (right words, wrong register or wrong move: an email that buries its request; a rebuttal that dismisses; a "verdict" that judges the topic, not the argument). At C1 the third column matters most. Board 2–3 items from each, without names.
  • Peer review is taught, not assumed. Adults from many education systems have never been asked to critique a classmate, and some feel it is rude. Model it (the 6.2 dialogue and slide 11), give a fixed protocol (reviewer speaks first, writer listens, writer asks one question, writer decides), and give the reviewer a job (a reverse outline and a checklist) rather than an invitation to "give feedback." Review the reviewers: check the peer-review sheets, not only the drafts.
  • Dominant speakers. The seminar in C17 has fixed roles: give the most talkative learner the Reporter (who must summarize the group, not their own view) or the Evidence Checker (who may speak only when figures are cited). Use a round: everyone speaks once before anyone speaks twice. In peer review, the timer protects the quieter writer: 3 minutes reviewer, 1 minute writer's question, no interruptions. Say it plainly: "Thanks, Omar. I'd like to hear the group's view now, not yours. Reporter?"
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is judgment and style: when a "fallacy" is actually reasonable (a slippery slope with evidence, a relevant authority); how to rebut the rebuttal; synthesizing two sources rather than evaluating one; controlling rhythm and emphasis in prose (periodic sentences, fronting, the well-placed short sentence); switching register fluently between genres for the same content.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and conventions (recognise, sceptical, colouring, generalisation, organise, practise, enquire, favour, judgement, behaviour, neighbourhood, centralised; Ms Okafor without a period, Yours sincerely, CV) and a few British words (high street, footfall, pupils). Teach the American forms and business conventions: Ms. with a period, Sincerely, / Best regards,, résumé, inquire, downtown / main street, foot traffic, students, and dates written as March 3. Accept British forms when learners use them consistently.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs. Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Take it apart → build it → put it to work. One invented company, Harborview Logistics, runs through the module. In C17 learners read two columnists fighting over Harborview's four-day-week pilot and discover that both of them cherry-pick the same company's figures. In C18 they build the kind of argument those columnists failed to write: a thesis, disciplined paragraphs, a fair counter-argument. In C19 they step inside Harborview and write the documents a workplace actually runs on: an email to team leaders, a report with headings and recommendations, and a cover letter for a real-looking job posting. Every class ends with a text another learner reads critically, which is the whole module in one habit.

Trauma-informed and opinion-safe

Argument and application tasks can touch sensitive ground. Topics stay in the workplace, education, technology, health habits and community life: no party politics, religion or immigration policy, and don't bring in real news articles on those topics for the critical-reading lab. Every argument task uses an assigned side (the essay question cards say "Side A" or "Side B"), so nobody has to reveal a personal view; say so before you hand them out. The cover letter is the most personal task in the course: some learners have careers interrupted by migration, credentials that are not recognized here, employment gaps or jobs below their qualifications. Offer three options out loud, as equally valid: your real experience, a professional profile card, or a mix (your real skills in an invented job). Never ask learners to explain a gap, and never read a cover letter aloud to the class without permission. The data and people in every text are invented; say so.

  • For C17: the seminar role cards (set C: one set of four per group, including the Evidence Checker's pilot fact sheet) and one envelope of fallacy card-sort cards per pair (set D: 27 cards). Learners read Articles A and B in the workbook.
  • For C18: the essay question cards (set E: 6 questions × Side A / Side B; cut off the teacher strip of model theses) and one peer-review sheet per learner (set F). Print 4–6 Fallacy Bingo grids (set H) for the warmer.

Lesson Plans

6.1 · C17

Critical Reading — Evaluating Arguments & Bias

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • distinguish a checkable fact from an opinion, including an opinion dressed in the grammar of fact (clearly, it is widely accepted that…)
  • separate a writer's claim from the evidence, name the evidence type, and make the unstated warrant visible
  • identify purpose, stance and tone, and point to the loaded words that reveal them
  • name and locate common fallacies in a real text, and explain why each weakens the case
  • describe what a text does with precise verbs (asserts, contends, concedes, downplays, overstates, dismisses) and give a fair, hedged verdict on the argument rather than the topic
Materials
  • Workbook 6.1 (Key language, Language box, Exercises 1–5, Articles A and B, dialogue, seminar frame, model evaluation, rubric)
  • Slides 2–7 · Flashcards: evaluative verbs, evidence types · Fallacy card sort (1 envelope per pair) · Seminar role cards + pilot fact sheet (1 set per group of 4)
  • Listening scripts 6.1-A (radio commentary, "Second Opinion") and 6.1-B (dictation)

Left: the skeleton. EVIDENCE —(warrant)→ CLAIM. Three questions: What exactly is claimed? On what evidence? Does the evidence support this claim? Evidence types: statistics · research · expert testimony · anecdote · analogy · bare assertion.

Center: the verbs. asserts · contends · implies · concedes / acknowledges · downplays · dismisses · overstates · refutes / rebuts. Frame: "She concedes that…, but downplays…" · "The figure suggests a correlation but does not establish cause."

Right: two questions for the seminar. (1) Which article makes the stronger case? (2) Which article is better argued? → not the same question! Fallacy list grows here as the card sort goes on.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: fact, opinion, opinion in disguise. A fact can in principle be checked; an opinion is a judgment. The C1 skill is spotting opinions wearing the clothes of fact: clearly, obviously, it is widely accepted that, everyone knows, the results speak for themselves.

"Customer complaints rose by 11 percent." Can we check it? (Yes: fact.) Does it tell us whether that's serious? (No: that's interpretation.) · "The four-day week is clearly the future." Can we check "the future"? (No.) Which word makes it sound like fact? (Clearly.)

Meaning: the warrant. The unstated assumption that links evidence to claim. Often the weakest point, and the most powerful one to name.

"Sick days fell by 18 percent, so the pilot should be permanent." Is the 18 percent the claim or the evidence? (Evidence.) What must we assume for the evidence to prove the claim? (That the pilot caused the fall.) Could anything else explain it? (Yes: the season, a mild winter, a flu outbreak the year before.) Is the writer lying? (Not necessarily: the figure can be true and the argument still weak.)

Meaning: evaluative verbs.

"She concedes that response times slipped." Is that a point for her side or against it? (Against: she admits it.) · "…but downplays it as a teething problem." Does she make it bigger or smaller? (Smaller.) · "He dismisses the fall in turnover." Does he discuss it seriously? (No: he waves it away.) · "Refute" or "rebut": which one means he succeeded in proving it wrong? (Refute; rebut is the attempt.) · Writer implies, reader…? (Infers.)

Meaning: a strong case vs a well-made argument. The key conceptual move of the class.

Can an article be right but badly argued? (Yes: true conclusion, weak reasons.) Can an article be well argued and still wrong? (Yes: if its evidence turns out to be incomplete.) So when you judge an article, what are you judging? (The argument: the reasons and evidence, not whether we like the conclusion.)

Form.

Verb + that-clauseasserts / contends / concedes / acknowledges / implies that + clauseShe concedes that response times slipped.
Verb + objectdownplays / dismisses / overstates / exaggerates + noun phraseHe dismisses the fall in turnover. ✘ He dismisses that turnover fell.
Evaluating evidenceThe evidence is + adjective; the figure + suggests / indicates / does not establishThe evidence is anecdotal. The figure suggests a correlation but does not establish cause.
Uncountableevidence, information, research, advice (no plural)several pieces of evidence · a great deal of research

Pronunciation. Word stress on the evaluative verbs: conCEDE, conTEND, asSERT, imPLY, reFUTE /rɪˈfjuːt/, disMISS, overSTATE, downPLAY (the noun DOWNplay is rare). evidence = EVidence (three syllables, not ev-I-dence). In the concession frame, contrastive stress carries the meaning: "She conCEDES it, ↗ | but she DOWNplays it. ↘" Seminar language: a falling tone on a verdict sounds decided ("On balance, Article A is stronger. ↘"); a fall-rise on a concession invites the "but" ("It's a relevant figure… ↘↗").

Drill script
  1. Stress the verb (point at flashcards): T: "con-CEDE" → Ss chorally. Through the eight verbs; then T says a verb with the wrong stress (DIS-miss) and learners correct it.
  2. Name the move: T reads a line, S names it with a verb: T: "'Admittedly, response times slipped.'" → S: "She concedes that response times slipped." T: "'But it was only a minor teething problem.'" → S: "She downplays the problem." T: "'Sick days fell. Case closed.'" → S: "She overstates the evidence." T: "'Some say turnover fell, but young people always chase perks.'" → S: "He dismisses the fall in turnover."
  3. Evidence verdicts: T gives a piece of evidence, S evaluates it with one adjective + reason: T: "One bakery's delivery was late." → S: "That's anecdotal: one case can't show a pattern." T: "The best month's output." → S: "That's selective: it's cherry-picked."
  4. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 invents a one-line argument about an everyday topic ("My gym is the best, because it's always full."); S2 names the warrant ("You're assuming that popular means good."); S3 names the weakness ("That's a bandwagon argument."). Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The critical-reading seminar — how to run it
  • Groups of 4 (3 is possible: the Chair also reports). Roles on the cards. Give the Evidence Checker card to a careful reader, and the Reporter card to the strongest or most dominant speaker: they must summarize the group, not argue.
  • The information gap. Only the Evidence Checker has the pilot fact sheet. The rule: the Checker reveals a figure only when someone makes a claim the figure tests ("Whitfield says sick days fell by 18%." → "Compared with the previous six months, yes. Compared with last year, only 7%."). Groups soon discover that both columnists selected their data.
  • Devil's Advocate must argue the opposite of whatever the group seems to be concluding, at least twice. This keeps the verdict honest and gives confident learners a legitimate way to challenge.
  • Two questions, deliberately separated: "Which makes the stronger case?" (who is closer to right, given all the figures) and "Which is better argued?" (which uses evidence more fairly). Many groups conclude: Whitfield's case is stronger, but neither is well argued. That is the lesson.
  • Verdicts: each Reporter speaks for 60 seconds using the four-move frame. The class may ask one question per group. No vote on the four-day week itself: we judged the articles.
ICQs before the seminar
Who has the full figures? (The Evidence Checker.) · When can the Checker share a figure? (When someone makes a claim the figure tests.) · Is the Devil's Advocate giving their own opinion? (No: they argue the opposite.) · How many questions? (Two: stronger case, better argued.) · Are they the same question? (No!) · Are we deciding whether the four-day week is a good idea? (No: whether the articles argue well.) · Who speaks for the group at the end? (The Reporter, 60 seconds.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C1 learners have strong spoken English but read slowly, or learned to read in a non-Latin script. Let them work on Article A only (read it aloud to them once while they follow), with sentence numbers as anchors: their task is to find the claim (A17), one statistic (A3), one anecdote (A6–8) and two loaded phrases. In the card sort, give them the 5 core fallacies only (the ones from the online lesson). Give the seminar frame with the first words filled in. Make them the Chair (a speaking role) rather than the Evidence Checker (a reading-heavy role).

Stretch (toward C2)

Ask: "When is a 'fallacy' not a fallacy?" A slippery slope with evidence for each step is a reasonable prediction; an appeal to a relevant expert is good evidence; emotion is legitimate when the stakes are genuinely human. Then: write a one-paragraph synthesis of both articles for Harborview's managers, using the full figures, in neutral language, with no verdict (the C2 skill of reporting a debate fairly). Early finishers do Ex 5 and then load the neutral phrases the other way (euphemism is loading too).

L1 watch-outs
  • "Evidences," "informations," "researches": Spanish, French, Portuguese, Russian and Arabic speakers pluralize these (evidencias, informations, informações). Board "a piece of evidence / several pieces of evidence."
  • False friends: actual (= current in Spanish, Portuguese, French: "the actual situation" for the current situation) · argument (Spanish argumento can mean the plot of a story; Italian argomento means the topic; so "the argument of the article" may mean "what it's about") · critic used as a verb (Spanish criticar, Portuguese criticar): teach criticize, critique.
  • Reading "politely": learners from education systems where published texts and authorities are not questioned in class (common in parts of East and South Asia, the Middle East and Eastern Europe; also among learners with little formal schooling) may feel that criticizing a writer is disrespectful. Say it explicitly: "In this class, testing an argument is a sign of respect." The mindset box in the online lesson says the same.
  • Imply vs infer: most languages use one verb for both, or none. CCQ it: "The writer implies; the reader infers."
  • Tone words at C1: learners reach for "negative / positive / aggressive." Push for precision with the flashcard adjectives: scornful, indignant, dismissive, measured, wistful, alarmist, campaigning.

6.2 · C18

Academic & Argument Writing — Structuring an Essay

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • write a thesis that is specific, arguable and answers the exact question, and diagnose weak ones (topic announcement, truism, fence-sitting, off-question)
  • open with hook → context → thesis and build body paragraphs with PEEL (point, evidence, explanation, link)
  • signpost only where the logic needs it, matching the connector to the relationship (Consequently = result; By contrast ≠ Nevertheless)
  • concede the strongest opposing point fairly and rebut it without a straw man, and close with restate → synthesize → implication
  • take part in structured peer review: produce a reverse outline and checklist for a partner, and receive feedback without defending
Materials
  • Workbook 6.2 (Key language, Language box, Exercises 1–5, model essay, dialogue, outline frame, peer-review checklist, rubric)
  • Slides 8–12 · Connector flashcards (color-coded by function) · Fallacy Bingo grids (warmer) · Essay question cards with assigned sides (1 per learner) · Peer-review sheets (1 per learner) · a visible timer
  • Listening scripts 6.2-A (an introduction read aloud) and 6.2-B (dictation)

Left: the architecture. INTRO: hook → context → thesis · BODY ×2–3: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link · COUNTER: concede → rebut · CONCLUSION: restate → synthesize → implication (no new points!).

Center: the thesis test. Could a reasonable person disagree? Does it answer this question? Weak: T (topic) · O (obvious) · F (fence) · Q (off-question). Frame: While / Although X, Y; therefore Z.

Right: connectors by job (colored flashcards). add: Furthermore · result: Consequently · compare: By contrast · concede: Admittedly / Granted · turn back: Nevertheless / Yet · example: For instance. Rebuttal openers: Yet this objection overlooks… · Such a view fails to account for…

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: thesis. The one-sentence position that every paragraph serves. Not a topic, not a fact, not a list of both sides.

"This essay is about training at work." Does it take a position? (No.) · "Training is important." Would anyone disagree? (No: so it's not a thesis.) · "There are arguments on both sides." Where does the writer stand? (Nowhere: fence-sitting.) · "Although paid training carries short-term costs, employers should treat it as an investment." Could a reasonable person disagree? (Yes: good.) What does Although do? (Concedes a point to the other side.)

Meaning: explanation in PEEL. The stage learners skip. Evidence doesn't speak for itself.

"Studies show tired workers make more mistakes." Is that the point, the evidence or the explanation? (Evidence.) Does it tell the reader why this matters for this essay? (No: that's the explanation's job.) Which phrase starts an explanation? (This matters because… · This suggests that…)

Meaning: concede vs rebut vs straw man.

"Admittedly, critics contend that rules ignore emergencies." Is the writer agreeing completely? (No: accepting a point, then answering it.) · "Yet this objection targets a blanket ban, not these limits." Is that a concession or a rebuttal? (Rebuttal.) · "Some people think workers should never learn anything." Does anybody really argue that? (No: it's a straw man, an invented weak version.) Why does a straw man make your essay weaker, not stronger? (A critical reader notices; you lose trust.)

Meaning: connectors.

"The course is expensive. Nevertheless, participants value it." Does "Nevertheless" add a point on the same side? (No: it turns against what we'd expect.) · "Small teams decide quickly. By contrast, large departments…" Are we conceding? (No: comparing two different things.) · "It saved money. Consequently, staff disliked it." Is disliking it a result of saving money? (No: wrong connector.)

Form.

Concessive thesisWhile / Although + concession, + main clause (position) + (because / for + reasons)While a total ban would be impractical, employers should set clear limits, because…
Sentence connectorfull stop / semicolon + connector + comma…the cost. Nevertheless, … · …the cost; nevertheless, …
Conjunctionalthough / whereas / while + clause, within one sentenceAlthough it is expensive, most participants value it.
Concede → rebutAdmittedly / Granted / It is true that … . Yet / However, this overlooks / fails to account for + noun phraseYet this objection overlooks the cost of replacing staff.
✘ Comma splicetwo main clauses joined by a comma only✘ Remote work saves time, it reduces costs. → ✔ …saves time; it also… / …saves time and…

Pronunciation. Reading a draft aloud is a proofreading tool, so teach it: pause at every full stop; if you run out of breath before one, the sentence is probably too long. Sentence connectors take a short pause and a slight rise: "Nevertheless ↗ | most participants value it ↘." Stress contrastive pairs in concessions: "Although it's exPENsive, | it's worth it." Word stress: THEsis (plural THEses /ˈθiːsiːz/), CONtrast (noun) / conTRAST (verb), conSEquently, ne-ver-the-LESS, adMITtedly.

Drill script
  1. Connector by function (hold up a colored flashcard): T: "Result." → S: "Consequently." T: "Concede." → S: "Admittedly." T: "Compare two things." → S: "By contrast." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Finish the logic: T: "Remote work saves commuting time. Furthermore…" → S: "…it widens the pool of applicants." T: "…saves commuting time. Nevertheless…" → S: "…some people feel isolated." T: "…Consequently…" → S: "…staff have more time for family."
  3. Concede → rebut chain: T gives an objection; S1 concedes it fairly, S2 rebuts: T: "Training is expensive." → S1: "Admittedly, training involves real costs." → S2: "Yet this overlooks the higher cost of replacing staff." T: "Phones in meetings help people take notes." → S1: "It is true that some staff use phones for notes." → S2: "However, a paper notebook does that without the notifications."
  4. Thesis upgrade (open pairs): S1 says a weak thesis on an everyday topic ("Libraries are good."); S2 upgrades it with Although / While + a position + a reason. Then closed pairs, 1 minute.
Structured peer review — the protocol
  • Swap across questions, not within them, so the reviewer reads as a genuine outsider (and can't "correct" the argument toward their own).
  • Reviewer's job (6 min, silent): read twice; write the thesis in your own words; write a one-line reverse outline of each paragraph; complete the checklist (Y / P / N); copy the strongest sentence; write one question that would make the draft stronger ("What would a skeptical manager say to paragraph 2?").
  • Round 1 (3 min): reviewer A speaks for 2 minutes in this order: thesis check → strongest sentence → the question. Writer B listens and does not defend or explain. Then B asks one clarifying question (1 min). Round 2: swap.
  • Language (slide 11, dialogue in the workbook): Can I check I've understood your thesis? · The strongest sentence for me is… · I wasn't entirely sure how… connects to… · You might consider… · I'd question… This is Lesson 3.3's hedging in a real job.
  • The writer owns the text. Say it: "Your reviewer gives you information, not instructions. You decide what to change."
  • Review the reviewers. Collect the sheets with the drafts. A sheet with all "Y" and "good" in the boxes goes back with: "Which sentence would you cut, and why?"
ICQs before peer review
Whose draft do you review: someone with the same question or a different one? (Different.) · What do you write for each paragraph? (One line: its point.) · Who speaks first? (The reviewer.) · Can the writer explain or defend while the reviewer is speaking? (No, just listen.) · How many questions can the writer ask? (One.) · Who decides what to change? (The writer.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the thesis frame on a strip ("Although ______, ______ should ______, because ______.") and the PEEL openers (One clear reason is… · For instance, … · This matters because… · This shows that…). Draft target: the introduction only (3–4 sentences), then one body paragraph as notes. Pair the learner with a patient reviewer and let them review orally: the reviewer reads the draft aloud, and the writer hears where it works. Allow learners to plan orally in L1 with a same-language partner before writing in English (strategic L1 use).

Stretch (toward C2)

Write the counter-argument paragraph instead of a body paragraph, and then "rebut the rebuttal": what would the critic say back, and does the thesis still survive? Work on style: vary sentence length deliberately (a short sentence after a long one lands a point), use one cleft or fronted structure for emphasis (It is precisely this expectation that…, from Module 2), and cut every connector the logic doesn't need. Ask: "Could your thesis be more precise: to what extent, for whom, under what conditions?"

L1 watch-outs
  • Where the main point goes. English academic writing is deductive: thesis early, topic sentence first. Many writing traditions build toward the point (e.g. Japanese ki-shō-ten-ketsu, Chinese qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé, Korean essay structure, some Arabic and Persian prose styles), or value elaborate introductions (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian academic prose). Present this as a convention of this genre, not a better way of thinking.
  • Comma splices and run-ons: Spanish, Portuguese, Vietnamese, Arabic and Chinese punctuation allows longer comma chains. Teach the semicolon and the full stop as tools, with the read-aloud test.
  • "On the other hand" to add a point: very common across L1s (it's used like also). It must contrast, and it sounds best after On the one hand. "Besides" is informal in English writing (French d'ailleurs, Spanish además are neutral): prefer Furthermore.
  • Articles in abstract nouns: "The education is important," "Technology is changing the society" (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Greek use the article with general abstract nouns). Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese speakers may drop articles entirely ("Employer should offer training"). Recast in feedback; it is a persistent C1 error in formal writing.
  • "I think" everywhere: some learners were taught never to use "I"; others use "In my opinion, I think" in every paragraph. Point to the online lesson's guidance: lean impersonal in formal essays (The evidence suggests… · This essay argues…).

6.3 · C19

Professional & Formal Writing — Reports, Emails & Applications

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • apply the four principles (clarity, concision, register, reader-focus) to diagnose and repair professional texts
  • write an email with a specific subject line, the purpose in the first sentence, and a polite but direct request with a deadline
  • organize a short report under headings, moving from findings to conclusions to specific recommendations, in an impersonal register (passive, nominalization, It is recommended that… be…)
  • write a cover letter that matches concrete, quantified evidence to an employer's stated needs, confident but not boastful
  • use American business conventions (Ms., Sincerely, résumé, inquire, unambiguous dates) and test tone by hearing the text read aloud
Materials
  • Workbook 6.3 (Key language, Language box, Exercises 1–5, three annotated models, dialogue, writing lab, three rubrics)
  • Slides 13–17 · Formula flashcards · Writing-lab pack: report data card, five job postings, reader cards (A = writer, B = reader) · Professional profile cards on a side table
  • Listening scripts 6.3-A (Karen Liu's voicemail) and 6.3-B (dictation)

Left: four principles. CLARITY · CONCISION · REGISTER · READER-FOCUS → "Direct is not rude; vague is not polite."

Center: three genres. EMAIL: subject → purpose → request + deadline → thanks → sign-off. REPORT: Purpose · Method · Findings · Conclusions · Recommendations (It is recommended that the form be replaced.). COVER LETTER: role + fit → evidence + relevance → confident close.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: reader-focus and purpose-first. The commonest C1 failing is not grammar but self-centered structure: telling the story in the order the writer lived it.

"I hope you are well. We have been customers for some time. Recently there have been some issues…" After three sentences, does the reader know what you want? (No.) Where should the request be? (In the first or second sentence.) Is "Could you please confirm the delivery date?" rude? (No: it's polite and clear.) Where does the politeness live? (In the framing: Could you please.)

Meaning: findings vs conclusions vs recommendations.

"58% of respondents found the form difficult." Finding, conclusion or recommendation? (Finding: data.) · "The current system appears to be slow and unfair." (Conclusion: interpretation.) · "It is recommended that the form be replaced." (Recommendation: action.) Can an opinion go in the Findings section? (No: "Sadly, 58%…" mixes them.)

Meaning: confident vs boastful vs over-humble.

"I am an outstanding communicator." Can the reader check it? (No: empty.) · "I trained twelve new staff, and all passed probation." Can they check it? (Yes: that's why it's confident, not boastful.) · "I'm sorry if my English isn't perfect." What does it make the reader notice? (Your English. Delete it.)

Purpose openersI am writing to + verb · I am writing with regard to + noun · Following up on our conversation, …I am writing to request the Q3 figures.
RequestsCould you please + verb · I would be grateful if you could + verb · I would appreciate it if you could + verbI would be grateful if you could send it by Wednesday.
Report findingspassive (was distributed, were found to) · nominalization (a decline in) · figures in bracketsSwaps were found to take an average of four days.
RecommendationsIt is recommended / advised / proposed that + subject + base verb (subjunctive, Lesson 1.3)It is recommended that the form be replaced. ✘ …that the form is replaced (British, acceptable)

Pronunciation. This class's pronunciation is the read-aloud test: a text that sounds curt, pleading or pompous aloud will read that way too. Model reading an email aloud: a polite request rises gently at the end of Could you please…? and falls on the deadline ("by Wednesday ↘"). Word stress: RÉsumé /ˈrezəmeɪ/, reSPONdent, recommenDAtion, adVERtisement (US also /ˌædvərˈtaɪzmənt/), PURpose (not pur-POSE). Weak forms in formulae: "I'd be grateful if you could /kəd/ send it."

Drill script
  1. Formula match (flashcards): T: "You're sending a document." → S: "Please find attached…" T: "You spoke yesterday." → S: "Following up on our conversation…" T: "First contact, asking for information." → S: "I am writing to inquire about…" Choral, then individuals.
  2. Report register: T reads a chatty note, S gives the report sentence: T: "We asked everyone." → S: "A survey was distributed to all staff." T: "Most people hated the form." → S: "A majority of respondents described the form as difficult." T: "Get rid of the form." → S: "It is recommended that the form be replaced."
  3. Soften, don't bury: T gives a bare command; S makes it polite but keeps the deadline: T: "Send the figures Friday." → S: "Could you please send the figures by Friday?" T: "Call me back." → S: "I would be grateful if you could call me back today."
  4. Evidence, not adjectives (open pairs): S1 says a boastful sentence ("I'm a brilliant organizer."); S2 turns it into evidence ("I organized a conference for 200 people, on budget."). Then closed pairs, 1 minute. Learners may invent.
The reader panel — how to run it
  • Why aloud, and why someone else's voice? Hearing your text in another person's voice exposes tone instantly: curtness, pleading, stiffness, and the moment the reader loses interest.
  • Reader cards (B) are the information gap. Each job posting has a hiring-manager card; the report has a director card. They reveal what that reader really cares about ("I have 80 applications. If the first paragraph doesn't name the role and one concrete reason, I stop."). The writer (A) didn't know this, which is exactly the point about reader-focus.
  • Sequence (per text, 5 min): the reader reads the text aloud (2 min) → reacts in role (2 min: what they noticed first, where they stopped or stumbled, what they would do next) → the writer notes one change (1 min). Swap.
  • Cover letters are personal. The reader reacts only to the letter as a letter. Before the panel, say: "If you wrote as yourself and prefer not to share, swap your email instead." Never ask anyone to read a cover letter to the whole class.
ICQs before the reader panel
Who reads your text aloud: you or your partner? (My partner.) · Is the reader reacting as themselves? (No: as the person on the reader card.) · Can the writer explain what they meant? (No, just listen and take notes.) · How many changes do you note? (At least one.) · If your cover letter is personal, what can you do? (Swap the email instead.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a skeleton for the chosen genre: the email with gaps (Subject: ______ · Dear ______, · I am writing to ______. · Could you please ______ by ______? · Thank you. · Best regards,). For the report, three headings and one sentence frame each ("___% of respondents reported that…" · "These findings suggest that…" · "It is recommended that…"). For the cover letter, the opening and closing are given; the learner writes one evidence + relevance paragraph (3 sentences). The email in Ex 1 is enough as the main product; the report/cover letter can be finished at home.

Stretch (toward C2)

Write both the report and a 60-word email to the director that summarizes it (same content, two registers). Then write a reply to a difficult email: a customer complaint in which the company is partly at fault (acknowledge, apologize precisely, state the remedy, without admitting more than the facts show). For the cover letter: cut it to 180 words without losing a single achievement (preview of Lesson 8.2, precision and concision).

L1 watch-outs
  • Indirect, context-first requests: learners from high-context business cultures (e.g. Japan, Korea, China, parts of South and Southeast Asia, the Middle East) and from very polite letter traditions (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Persian) may place the request after a long courteous preamble. Present purpose-first as a convention of US workplace email, not as more polite or more honest; warmth can still come in the closing.
  • Regional business English: South Asian and West African English use Kindly do the needful, Please revert (back), Respected Sir, Please find the attached herewith; Philippine English often opens with Good day! These are correct in their own contexts. Explain that US readers may find them unfamiliar, and offer equivalents (Could you please…, Please reply, Dear Mr. …).
  • Titles and names: Dear Karen Liu (full name), Dear Mrs. Carter (marital status assumed), Dear Ms. Karen (title + first name: common in Tagalog, Spanish and Arabic usage). Teach Ms. + family name, or first name only once invited.
  • German, Dutch and Hebrew speakers may write requests that sound curt in English ("Send me the file until Friday."). Also until for by (German bis, Spanish hasta, Portuguese até): "by Friday" = no later than; "until Friday" = continuously up to.
  • Cover-letter conventions differ: in some countries a photo, age, marital status or nationality is expected, and modesty formulas are obligatory. In the US these are left out (and some are legally sensitive). Explain the convention; never frame it as a language error.
