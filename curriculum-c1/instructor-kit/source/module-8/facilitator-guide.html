Module 8 — Fluency, Craft & the Final Project

The last three classes of C1 pull in two directions and then bring them together. First, fluency under pressure: buying time, using fillers with control, talking around a missing word and repairing a sentence mid-flight, rehearsed in impromptu one-minute talks. Then craft: editing for precision and concision, cutting wordiness, redundancy, empty intensifiers and buried verbs, with a 30% Cut Challenge. Finally, the review project, A Position Paper & Its Defense: every learner writes a paper of about 500 words on an everyday, workplace, education or technology question and defends it in a short viva-style Q&A with peers.

3 lessons Lessons 8.1–8.3 Classes C23–C25 Weeks 12–13 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The first two follow the usual shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. At C1, 8.1 and 8.2 are really skills workshops: the "language" is a set of strategies and editing moves, and most of the class time goes to doing them. C25 is a project class: short input, then the vivas.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-8-position-paper.html. Print materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html before C23. It holds the Talk Around It picture cards and the phrase cards, the new identity cards, the impromptu topic cards and curveball cards, the strategy spotter sheets, the before/after editing cards, the four 30% Cut Challenge texts, the Keep Talking board game, the position paper kit (topic cards, side cards, viva roles, the rotation sheet and the chair's script), the six question-card sets, the paper rubric and defense rubric, the teacher observation checklist and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home. For this module the online lessons are also where learners record themselves: the two-minute impromptu talk (8.1) and the recorded defense (8.3).
  • The project starts in C23, not C25. A 500-word paper cannot be written in class. Learners choose a topic at the end of C23, fill in the planning frame (Workbook 8.3) for C24, write and edit the paper after C24 and bring it (or email it to their viva group) before C25. C25 itself is the defense. The timeline is in the box "The project timeline" below; put it on the board in C23 and leave it there.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. Learners already hedge (C1 3.3), nominalize (3.1), build cohesion (3.2), structure an argument essay (6.2) and manage a seminar (7.3). Don't re-teach any of it. This module asks them to do it in real time (8.1), economically (8.2) and all at once (8.3). Point back to the earlier lesson whenever a learner reaches for a tool: "That's your 3.3 hedge. Good."
  • Feedback on range, accuracy and appropriacy. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: Fix (errors), Upgrade (correct but could be more precise or more natural) and Wow (ambitious, well-judged language to spread). At C1 the most useful feedback is often appropriacy: the hedge that makes a claim too weak, the formal phrase in a chatty answer, the filler that turns into a tic. Board 2–3 items from each column, without names.
  • Dominant speakers. In C23 the strategy spotter role (listens, tallies, reports) and a strict 60-second timer do most of the work. In C24 give the strongest voice the meaning-keeper job: they may only defend ideas, not add words. In C25 the viva format is naturally fair: each learner has one defense and fixed examiner turns, and the chair keeps them. Say it kindly and early: "Great question, Ana. Let's give Wei the next one."
  • Quiet or anxious learners. Impromptu speaking is the most exposing task of the course. Let learners choose the easy topic pile first, allow a 15-second silent planning time, and treat any visible strategy ("Let me think… Well, it depends…") as a success, even if the content is thin. In C25, a nervous learner may present to their group seated and may keep their paper in front of them.
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is control and invisibility: at C2, strategies stop being noticeable. Fillers become rhetorical pauses, circumlocution becomes precise paraphrase, a repair becomes a deliberate refinement ("or, more precisely…"), editing becomes rhythm and emphasis, and a defense becomes genuine intellectual exchange (reframing the question, identifying the assumption behind it). Ask strong learners to explain why a choice works, and give them the hardest topic cards.
  • Peer review, the C1 way. All three classes use the same protocol, and learners already know it from C17–C18: (1) say what the text or talk does well, specifically; (2) ask a question before you suggest a change ("What did you mean by…?"); (3) suggest one or two changes, not ten; (4) the writer or speaker decides. Post it on the wall. Peer comments are about the work, never the person.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (recognize, apologize, nominalization, practicing, center, organization, pedestrianized, sidewalks, foot traffic, store, period, defense). Accept British forms (pavements, footfall, shop, full stop, defence) when learners use them, but ask for one system per paper.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Keep going → cut back → stand your ground. In C23 learners learn to keep speech moving when a word, an idea or a sentence fails them: That's a good question… It's a kind of… or rather… In C24 they learn the opposite discipline, going back over writing to cut everything that doesn't earn its place: due to the fact that → because, make a decision → decide. In C25 the two come together. The paper is the edited, concise product of C24; the defense is the spontaneous, strategic speaking of C23, with hedging and concession from earlier modules. Every class ends with something real that other learners hear or read: a one-minute talk, an edited text, a defended position.

Trauma-informed: topics, sides and "pass"

Impromptu speaking and a public defense are high-stakes for adults who have had to defend themselves in far more serious settings: interviews with officials, workplace disputes, family conflict. Keep every topic in everyday, workplace, education, technology, health-habit and community territory. The kit's topic cards contain no party politics, religion, immigration or border policy, policing, war or national identity; don't add any, and don't use the policy-style prompts listed in the online lesson 8.3 (on government regulation and public debate) as in-class topics. Learners may choose a topic card or draw one, and they may draw a side card so that the paper argues an assigned position: "You're arguing the card, not confessing your opinion." In C23, personal topics ("a skill I'm glad I learned") can always be answered as a new identity or invented, and any learner may say "Pass, next card." In C25 the viva is never an interrogation: examiners challenge the argument, the chair stops any question that becomes personal, and nobody is asked where they are from or what they personally believe.

Why ~500 words when the online lesson says 250–400? The online capstone is written in one sitting. In class, learners have a week, an editing lesson in between, and a real audience, so the paper can carry two or three developed arguments and a proper rebuttal. Five hundred words is still short enough that every sentence must earn its place, which is the point of C24. If a learner's life is too full for 500 words this week, 350–400 well-edited words are fine: tell them so privately.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: fluency. At C1, fluency means sustaining connected, comprehensible speech while you are still thinking. Pauses are normal; silent, frozen pauses and restarts are what break fluency.

"The reason is… um… [long silence] … sorry, I don't know how to say it." Did the speaker know the answer? (Probably, yes.) What went wrong? (She stopped and apologized; the listener lost the thread.) · "The reason is, well, how can I put this? It's partly about cost." Was this speaker faster? (No.) Why does it sound more fluent? (There was no dead silence; the phrase filled the gap.)

Meaning: time-buyers carry meaning.

"That's a good question…" Is it only stalling? (No, it also acknowledges the person who asked.) · "Well, it depends…" What does the listener expect next? (A balanced answer with conditions.) · "How can I put this?" Is the speaker lost? (No, they're choosing words carefully: it signals precision.) · Could you use "That's a good question" to answer "What time is it?" (No: it only fits a hard or interesting question. Overuse sounds evasive.)

Meaning: fillers, job or tic?

"It was, you know, a difficult decision." Does you know do a job here? (Yes: it invites the listener to share the feeling.) · "I was, you know, walking to, you know, the store, you know." (No job: it's a tic, and we start counting instead of listening.) · "Actually, it worked well." What does actually tell us? (It's a bit surprising, or it corrects an expectation.) · "It's sort of a compromise." Hedge or tic? (A hedge: it's an honest approximation.) · "It's sort of on Tuesday." (A tic: Tuesday is a fact; don't hedge it.)

Meaning: circumlocution and repair.

"It's the thing you use to open a wine bottle." Does the speaker know the word? (No, not right now.) Did the conversation stop? (No.) What often happens next? (The listener says the word: "A corkscrew!") · "We met on Tuesday, or rather, Wednesday." Which day was it? (Wednesday.) Did she apologize? (No.) Does the listener trust her more or less? (More: she's monitoring her own speech.)

Pronunciation. Time-buyers only sound natural with the right tune. That's a good question is said slightly slower, with a fall on question and a short pause after: "That's a good QUEStion ↘ | …". Well, it depends has a fall-rise on well (↘↗ = "it's not simple"). Let me think is often "lemme think." Repairs are said lightly and quickly, a little lower in pitch, as if in brackets: "We met on Tuesday (or rather) WEDnesday." Stress the corrected word, not the repair phrase. After a time-buyer, slow down slightly: a measured pace sounds more fluent than a rushed one. Um and er can often be replaced by a silent beat with eye contact: it sounds confident, not lost.

Drill script
  1. Tune the time-buyers (choral, then individuals, with the intonation): "That's a good QUEStion…" · "Well ↘↗, it dePENDS…" · "How can I PUT this?" · "Let me THINK for a second…"
  2. Hot seat: hard question → time-buyer + one sentence. T: "What's the biggest problem with modern work?" → S: "That's a good question. I'd say it comes down to…" T: "Is it better to rent or buy?" → S: "Well, it depends on…" T: "How would you describe your ideal manager?" → S: "How can I put this? Someone who…"
  3. Talk around it (T says a word only the learner sees on a card; S describes it with the frame T names): T: "Function!" (ladder) → S: "It's the thing you use to reach something high." T: "Role!" (landlord) → S: "It's the person who owns the apartment you rent." T: "Situation!" (traffic jam) → S: "It's when all the cars stop and nobody can move."
  4. Or rather chain: T gives a wrong detail and the right one: "Tuesday → Wednesday." → S: "We met on Tuesday, or rather, Wednesday, and…" T: "twenty people → thirty." → S: "There were about twenty, or rather, thirty people…"
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 asks a hard question from the board; S2 answers for 20 seconds using a time-buyer, one circumlocution (S1 whispers a "forbidden" word S2 may not use) and one repair. Then all pairs, 2 minutes.
Impromptu Talks — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, sitting in a small triangle. Each group has the three card piles face down, the curveball cards, a spotter sheet and one phone timer.
  • One turn = about 2 minutes: draw a card (or choose between two, see below), 15 seconds of silent thinking (no writing, or three key words at most), 60 seconds of talk, then the timekeeper asks one follow-up question and the speaker answers in 20–30 seconds. The spotter reports in 20 seconds. Rotate: every learner speaks once per round.
  • Round 1: Warm-up pile (familiar, personal-ish topics; new identity cards welcome). Round 2: Opinion pile ("Are online reviews trustworthy?"). Round 3: Stretch pile (abstract: "Is boredom useful?") plus a curveball card the timekeeper reveals at 30 seconds ("Now argue the opposite," "Explain it to a 12-year-old," "Your listener interrupts: 'Sorry, what do you mean by that?'").
  • Rules on the slide: no silence longer than about 3 seconds; no restarting; no "sorry." If you dry up, use a time-buyer, repeat your main point in other words, or say "Let me come at this from another angle."
  • The spotter sheet has four columns: time-buyers · talked around a word · smooth repairs · long silences / restarts, plus one line: "a filler you used a lot." The spotter reports numbers and one example, not a judgment. This is the job for a dominant speaker: they must listen, count and report briefly.
  • Choice lowers anxiety. In Rounds 1 and 2 the speaker draws two cards and chooses one. Anyone may say "Pass, next card" once per round. Nobody watches the whole class: all groups talk at the same time.
  • You circulate with a notepad (Fix / Upgrade / Wow). Spend one full turn with each group. Early-finishing groups play the Keep Talking board game.
ICQs before the Impromptu Talks
How long do you talk? (One minute.) · How long can you think first? (Fifteen seconds.) Can you write a script? (No, three words at most.) · What does the timekeeper do at the end? (Asks one follow-up question.) · What does the spotter do? (Counts strategies and silences, then reports numbers.) · If you forget a word, do you stop? (No, talk around it.) · If you make a mistake? (Repair it: "or rather…" and keep going.) · Can you say "Pass"? (Yes, once per round.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the learner four phrase cards only to hold during the talks: That's a good question · Well, it depends · It's a kind of… · or rather. Let them talk for 30–40 seconds and choose from the Warm-up pile in every round. Pair them in the triangle with a patient speaker, and make them the timekeeper first so they watch two turns before speaking. In the workbook, do Ex 2 items 1–4 and Ex 3 with the four picture items. Target: one time-buyer and one circumlocution, used on purpose.

Stretch (toward C2)

Make the strategies invisible: a rhetorical question instead of a stock time-buyer ("So why does this matter? Well…"), a precise paraphrase instead of "the thing" ("a device for measuring air pressure"), a refinement instead of a correction ("or, more precisely…"). Speak for 90 seconds and take the Stretch pile from Round 1. Give the curveball before the talk and ask them to build it in. Ask: "Which of your fillers did a job? Which were habits?" Homework: the online two-minute talk, recorded twice.

L1 watch-outs
  • L1 fillers leaking into English. Under pressure, learners fill pauses with the filler of their first language: Spanish este…, o sea; Japanese ēto, ano; Mandarin nàge, jiùshì; Arabic yaʿni; Tagalog ano…, parang; Russian nu…, tak skazat'; French euh, du coup; Korean eum, geu. Treat it kindly and privately: it's a sign the learner is thinking. Give them one English replacement to practice (Well… / Let me think…).
  • "How do you say…?" in the middle of a turn, often followed by the L1 word. Fine in class, fatal in an interview. Teach the frame that keeps going: "It's a kind of…"
  • Over-apologizing. Learners from cultures where apologies soften any imposition (Japanese sumimasen, Korean, and many others) may say "sorry" at every repair. Repairs in English need no apology.
  • "Actually" as a false friend: Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, Portuguese atualmente, German aktuell, Italian attualmente mean "currently." "Actually, I work in a hotel" (meaning "at the moment") will confuse. Actually = in fact / surprisingly.
  • Over-hedging vs no hedging. Learners whose academic culture values indirectness (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Vietnamese) may put sort of / maybe / a little on everything; learners from more direct traditions (German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew, Polish) may drop even useful markers and sound abrupt. Both are appropriacy issues, not errors: show the middle.
  • Speed as a status symbol. Some learners (often very able ones) believe speed = fluency and rush. Time them and praise the slower, clearer version out loud.

8.2 · C24

Precision & Concision — Editing Your English

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • replace wordy stock phrases with lean ones (in order to → to · due to the fact that → because · at this point in time → now · in the event that → if) and cut redundant pairs (end result, free gift, each and every, completely eliminate)
  • delete empty intensifiers and qualifiers (very, really, quite, basically, just, actually) and upgrade the word instead (very tired → exhausted · really important → crucial), while keeping intensifiers that add genuine emphasis or precise degree
  • dig strong verbs out of noun + light-verb strings (make a decision → decide · carry out a review of → review), and explain when nominalization (C1 3.1) is still the right choice
  • resolve double negatives (not uncommon → common) and untangle an overloaded sentence by splitting it or fronting the main idea
  • cut a text by at least 30% without losing any idea, count before and after, and name each cut

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: concision. Concision means saying the same thing in fewer, stronger words. The goal is clarity, not a low word count: a cut that removes an idea, a source or a necessary nuance is a bad cut.

"Due to the fact that we did not have a sufficient amount of time…" → "Because we lacked time…" Is any meaning lost? (No.) Which is easier to read? (The second.) · "Research by the developers shows…" → "Research shows…" Is anything lost? (Yes! Who did the research. That matters for trust.) So is shorter always better? (No: clarity and accuracy come first.)

Meaning: redundancy and intensifiers.

"a free gift": can a gift cost money? (No: free adds nothing.) · "completely eliminate": can you eliminate something halfway? (No.) · "very tired" vs "exhausted": which is stronger? (Exhausted.) Which uses fewer words? (Also exhausted.) · "I'm absolutely certain." Should we cut absolutely? (Not necessarily: it's genuine emphasis, a choice.) · "slightly undercooked": cut slightly? (No: it gives a precise degree.)

Meaning: strong verbs vs nominalization.

"The committee will carry out a review of the proposal." Where's the real action? (Review.) Which verb is doing the grammar? (carry out: an empty verb.) Shorter version? ("The committee will review the proposal.") · In C1 3.1 we turned verbs into nouns. Was that wrong? (No, it's right for formal, impersonal writing: a report, a legal clause.) So which do you use in an email to your team? (Strong verbs.) In a formal research summary? (Often nominalization.) Who decides? (The writer, for the reader and the purpose.)

Meaning: double negatives and tangles.

"It is not uncommon for delays to occur." Common or uncommon? (Common.) How many switches did your brain flip? (Two.) Is "not uncommon" ever useful? (Yes, for deliberate understatement, but not as a default.) · A 30-word sentence with four clauses: can you say it in one breath and remember how it began? (No.) What can you do? (Split it into two, or put the main idea first.)

Pronunciation. Reading aloud is the editor's best test, so teach learners to read their drafts in thought groups: a pause at each natural break, stress on the new information. Wordiness shows up as too many unstressed syllables in a row ("due to the fact that we are currently at a point where…"): the voice runs out of energy. A concise sentence has a clear rhythm of stressed words: "Because COSTS are SOARing, we've deCIDed to reVIEW our SPENDing." Where a learner runs out of breath, the sentence probably needs to be split.

Drill script
  1. Say it shorter (Script 8.2-B; each sentence once, learners say the concise version). T: "In order to save money, we are going to walk." → S: "To save money, we'll walk." T: "Due to the fact that it was raining, the game was canceled." → S: "Because it was raining, the game was canceled." T: "At this point in time, we have the ability to deliver on Saturdays." → S: "We can now deliver on Saturdays."
  2. One strong word (fast): T: "very tired" → S: "exhausted." T: "really important" → "crucial." T: "very surprised" → "astonished." T: "very big" → "huge." T: "very bad" → "terrible." T: "very clean" → "spotless."
  3. Find the verb: T: "make a decision" → S: "decide." T: "reach a conclusion" → "conclude." T: "give an explanation of" → "explain." T: "have a discussion about" → "discuss." T: "make an improvement to" → "improve."
  4. Cut or keep? T reads a sentence; S says "Cut!" or "Keep!" and why. T: "It's basically just a really simple idea." → S: "Cut! 'It's a simple idea.'" T: "I'm absolutely certain the door was locked." → S: "Keep: real emphasis." T: "The meat was slightly undercooked." → S: "Keep: exact degree."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 reads a sentence from Workbook Ex 4 with its wordy form; S2 says it concisely without looking. Swap. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The 30% Cut Challenge — how to run it
  • Four texts, four genres (materials set F): A an email to front-desk staff about a schedule change (151 words → target 105 or fewer) · B a community center newsletter item (156 → 109) · C an app description (143 → 100) · D a paragraph from a student report on teenage sleep (159 → 111). Give different texts to neighboring groups so they can compare at the end. Text D connects to the model position paper in Workbook 8.3.
  • Roles: the editor holds the pen and marks cuts on the card (cross out, write above); the word-counter counts after each sentence and keeps a running total; the meaning-keeper has a list of the text's ideas (on the back of the card) and ticks each one as it survives. The meaning-keeper can veto any cut: "No, that loses the time." Give this job to a dominant speaker: they defend ideas but can't add words.
  • Sequence: (1) read the text aloud once, together; (2) first pass: wordy phrases and redundant pairs (the fast wins); (3) second pass: intensifiers and buried verbs; (4) third pass: tangles and double negatives; (5) count; (6) read the final version aloud and fix any sentence where the reader stumbles.
  • Honesty rules: no idea may disappear; numbers, names, times, and sources of evidence stay; don't change the register (the email stays an email). If the group reaches 30% quickly, the stretch target is 50%: the model versions in the answer key cut 53–57%.
  • Round 3 read-out: each group reads its version and gives "before / after / percent." Classmates with the same text (if any) compare choices; everyone else listens for lost meaning and asks one question. Vote for "clearest," not "shortest."
  • Keep the cut texts. At the end of the class, show how the same moves apply to the position paper: "Your first draft will be about 650 words. You'll cut it to 500 the same way."

Literacy support

Stretch (toward C2)

Aim for 50% in the Cut Challenge and then do the reverse: rewrite the cut text in a deliberately formal, nominalized register for a different reader, and explain which version suits which purpose. Look at rhythm: vary sentence length on purpose (a long sentence, then a short one for impact). Find one sentence where a double negative is the better choice (polite understatement: "Her proposal is not without merit"). Early finishers edit a paragraph of their own planning notes.

L1 watch-outs
  • "Long = educated." In many academic traditions (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Russian, Arabic, Persian, and German with its Nominalstil), long, nominal, subordinate-rich sentences signal education and respect. Learners may feel that cutting makes them sound simple. Frame concision as an English reader-responsible convention, not "better thinking," and show that the C1 3.1 formal style still has its place.
  • "very" as a default: Mandarin 很 hěn often links a subject and an adjective with little intensifying force ("She is very tall" = "She is tall"), so learners overuse very. The strong-word drill helps.
  • Double negatives from negative concord: Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Russian, Polish and others require multiple negatives ("No sé nada" → "I don't know nothing"). That's a grammar error; "not uncommon" is a style choice. Keep the two separate so learners don't start avoiding all negatives.
  • Comma splices in tangled sentences: Spanish, Russian, Vietnamese and Chinese allow clauses joined by commas where English needs a period, a semicolon or a linker. When learners "split" a sentence, check they really split it.
  • "in order to" everywhere: Romance para / pour / per + infinitive translates naturally as "in order to." Plain to is enough in most cases.
  • Over-cutting (any L1): dropping articles, that or auxiliaries to hit the target ("Committee will review proposal"). That's headline style, not concision. Check grammar after every cut.

Why a peer viva in class, but a recorded solo defense online?

The online capstone lets self-study learners present and then answer their own prepared list of questions. In class the real C1 challenge becomes possible: questions you didn't write, asked by people who have read your paper closely. That's why the Q&A matters more than the opening statement. A learner whose opening is plain but who concedes a fair point gracefully and then holds their position with a hedge has given a strong C1 performance. Note it on the checklist.

Pronunciation for defending. The concession has a fall (it's sincere, not sarcastic): "That's a FAIR point ↘." The hold has a fresh start: a short pause, a slightly higher pitch and stress on the contrast word: "| That SAID, ↗ the PRINciple still stands." Keep the pace calm and unhurried: speed sounds defensive. In the opening statement, pause after each signpost ("My second point | is fairness."). And use 8.1 in real time: a time-buyer before a hard answer is not a weakness; a long silence is.

Drill script (quick, choral → individual)
  1. Concede → hold chain: T gives a challenge: "Isn't that too expensive?" S1: "That's a fair point; it isn't cheap." S2: "That said, the cost is paid once, and the benefit recurs." S3: "So I'd put it slightly more cautiously: it's worth it if it's phased in." New challenge, new chain.
  2. Collapse → poised: T (collapse): "Yes, you're right. Forget my idea." → S: "You're right that it's a risk. Even so, I'd still argue that…" · T (stonewall): "No, that's wrong." → S: "I see why you'd say that. However, …"
  3. Overclaim → hedge: T: "This proves that…" → S: "This strongly suggests that…" T: "Everyone agrees that…" → S: "Most researchers seem to agree that…" T: "It will definitely work." → S: "It's likely to work, provided that…"
  4. Check the question: T asks a long, slightly unclear question. S: "If I've understood your question, you're asking whether… Is that right?" Then S answers in one sentence.

Running a viva — the chair's order (about 9 minutes)
  1. Chair introduces (15 s): "Welcome. Our candidate is Ana. Her paper addresses the question: should high schools start later? Ana, you have two minutes."
  2. Opening statement (2 min): the candidate summarizes thesis, arguments, the objection and the rebuttal from 3–4 key words. The chair shows "30 SECONDS" and "TIME."
  3. Lead examiner (about 2½ min): two questions. After each answer, one short follow-up is allowed ("So would you accept that…?").
  4. Second examiner (about 1½–2 min): one or two questions, preferably of a different type from the lead examiner's.
  5. Chair's question (if time, 30–60 s): often a wildcard ("If you could keep only one argument, which one?").
  6. Close and feedback (1 min): the chair thanks the candidate; each examiner says one star (a specific strength) and one wish, and circles the defense rubric. Then rotate.
The rubrics and the observation checklist → C26

The paper rubric (set J, and the self-check in Workbook 8.3) has six criteria: 1 position & thesis · 2 arguments & support · 3 counter-argument & rebuttal · 4 organization & cohesion · 5 range & precision (the C1 toolkit) · 6 accuracy, register & concision. The defense rubric also has six: 1 opening statement · 2 understanding the questions · 3 concede & hold · 4 hedging & calibration · 5 fluency strategies · 6 delivery & interaction. Three levels: ★★★ strong C1 (reaching toward C2) · ★★ C1 · ★ not yet. Examiners circle the defense rubric for their candidate; learners self-assess both; you use both for written feedback on the paper, returned at C26. Don't grade C25: it's practice. Tell learners so, clearly.

The observation checklist (set K) has one row per learner and 10 can-do items. Three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with support, or with errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You can't sit through every viva: see each learner's whole opening statement or at least two of their answers, and one of their examiner questions. After class, use it to:

  • Plan each learner's C26 assessment. The C1 final assessment is an individual speaking interview (about 10 minutes) plus listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's C1 final assessment. Before C26, read it and match your checklist notes to its speaking criteria: which topics suit each learner, and where to start the interview so that each learner begins with something they did well.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become a short review before the assessment starts.
  • Write one sentence of feedback for each learner on the returned paper: one real strength from the defense, named specifically ("Your concession to Omar's question about cost was excellent, and you held your thesis with a clear reason").
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who froze or collapsed under questioning gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is one-to-one and friendly. No audience. You'll be fine."
Differentiation
Literacy support

Before C25 (in C24 or by message), agree a shorter paper: 250–350 words, with thesis, two arguments, one objection and a rebuttal, using the planning frame's sentence starters. In the viva, allow a 1-minute opening statement from the planning frame, and ask the group's examiners to use clarifying and objection cards (not wildcards). Give the learner the four defense phrase cards to hold: That's a fair point · That said · I'd still argue that… · If I've understood your question…. As an examiner, they may read a question straight from a card. Target: one clear concession and one hold with a reason.

Stretch (toward C2)

Answer the question behind the question: identify the examiner's assumption ("That question assumes that parents' schedules can't change. I'd question that."). Reframe rather than defend ("I'd put the issue differently: it's not about lateness but about alignment"). Use a precise concession frame (Granted, … nevertheless… · Be that as it may, …) and concede a point you didn't anticipate, then refine the thesis in real time. As an examiner, ask the wildcard cards and a genuine follow-up. At home: write a 150-word "response to examiners" that revises the paper's weakest paragraph.

L1 watch-outs
  • Where the thesis goes. Some rhetorical traditions build up to the main point (the Japanese ki-shō-ten-ketsu structure, Korean gi-seung-jeon-gyeol, and some Chinese, Arabic and Persian essay conventions value context before claim). English position papers put the thesis in the first paragraph. Frame it as a genre convention, not a better way of thinking.
  • "According to me" / "In my opinion, I think" (French selon moi, Spanish según yo / en mi opinión, pienso que, Italian secondo me): not used in English. Teach This paper argues that… · I would argue that… · In my view, … (without I think).
  • "I am agree" / "I am not agree" (Spanish, French, Portuguese): still surfaces under pressure at C1. I agree / I don't agree. Board it after the vivas, not during.
  • Challenging a peer. Learners from cultures where questioning a classmate publicly causes loss of face (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Vietnamese, Filipino, and many others) may ask only gentle clarifying questions; learners from more direct traditions may challenge bluntly. The role card and the question cards help both: "The card asks the question, not you." Teach the softener: I wonder whether… · Someone might object that…
  • Memorized openings. Learners from exam systems that reward memorized speeches may recite the paper. Allow a full script at home for confidence; in the viva, key words only. Kindly collect scripts if needed.
  • "Discuss about," "explain me," "emphasize on" (Spanish, Arabic, Hindi, Urdu and others): discuss something · explain it to me · emphasize something. Common in papers: mark them in written feedback.

Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, the C1 final assessment: an individual speaking interview (about 10 minutes) plus listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's C1 final assessment. Read it before C26 and tell learners how the class will be organized (the order of the tasks and when each learner will be interviewed), so there are no surprises. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next steps, the C2 course, Mastery English, or the specialist tracks (IELTS, Business English).

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You'll have a one-to-one conversation with me for about ten minutes, and some listening, reading and writing. It's everything we've practiced. Today you answered questions you didn't expect, about a paper you wrote. That was harder than anything in C26. Then we celebrate."

Reduce anxiety: show C1 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you have written 500 concise words on a debatable question and then defended them against three sharp examiners?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that their recorded defense is the best practice for the interview.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite 2–3 volunteers to give a one-minute impromptu "closing speech" drawn from a card: the C23 skill, with a friendly audience.
