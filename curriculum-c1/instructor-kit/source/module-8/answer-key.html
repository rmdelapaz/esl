Module 8 — Fluency, Craft & the Final Project: Answers

Answers to every Module 8 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the clean filler-free answer, the four 30% Cut Challenge texts, an annotated model position paper, poised defense answers), and the errors you can expect most.

Lessons 8.1–8.3Classes C23–C259 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C1, mark range, accuracy and appropriacy. In this module many answers are open: a circumlocution, a repair or an edit is correct when it works for the listener or reader: it is clear, grammatical, keeps every idea, and suits the register. Accept any version that does this, even if it differs from the key, and use the key's version to show one more option ("Good. Another way is…"). For editing tasks, the three tests are always the same: (1) no idea, number, name or source lost; (2) still grammatical (no headline-style dropping of articles or auxiliaries); (3) the register unchanged. Contractions are fine. American English is the model; British spellings are acceptable when used consistently.

8.1 · Fluency & Spontaneity (C23)

Exercise 1 — Freeze or flow? (Script 8.1-A)
WallWhat Speaker 2 saysStrategy
1 · hard question"That's a good question. Let me think for a second…"buying time
2 · missing word (deadline)"…the, what's the word, the date when everything has to be finished"circumlocution (situation / function)
3 · wrong detail"…three projects, or rather, four, at the same time"smooth repair

Discussion: neither speaker was faster. Speaker 2 sounds more fluent because there is no dead silence and no apology; every wall is filled with a phrase that carries meaning. Accept the gist of each quotation.

Exercise 2 — Choose the strategy phrase
  1. it depends (a nuanced answer)
  2. That's a good question (buying time on a hard one)
  3. the thing you use to (paraphrasing thermometer)
  4. or rather (a smooth correction)
  5. what I mean is (restating more clearly)
  6. Let me think (openly signaling thinking)
  7. the person who (a role: a lawyer / legal counsel)
  8. how can I put this (searching for careful wording)

Items 1–6 come from the online lesson. Accept That's an interesting question in 2 and let me see in 6. In 8, it depends would also be possible if 1 were answered differently; each phrase is used once.

Exercise 3 — Talk around it (model answers)
  1. remote control: "It's the thing you use to change the channel without getting up." (function)
  2. wardrobe: "It's a kind of tall cupboard where you hang your clothes." (category)
  3. mechanic: "It's the person who fixes cars." (role)
  4. umbrella: "It's like a small roof you carry, but it folds up." (comparison)
  5. a receipt: "It's the piece of paper the store gives you to prove you paid." (function / thing)
  6. a commute: "It's when you travel to work and back every day." (situation)
  7. a landlord: "It's the person who owns the apartment you rent." (role)
  8. a deadline: "It's the date when a piece of work has to be finished." (situation)

Accept any clear description; the task asks for a different frame each time, so check variety. Weak answers: a single word in another language; "a thing for clothes" (too vague: add category + function); a definition that uses the word itself ("a person who does mechanics").

Exercise 4 — Clumsy → smooth
  1. "The store opens at nine, or rather, ten."
  2. "There were about fifty people, or should I say, closer to eighty."
  3. "The new system is cheaper, or rather, what I mean is, it's faster."
  4. "She's the manager of the, what's it called, the department that handles the company's money: finance."

Any repair phrase from the box is fine. Three things to check: no "sorry," no restart, and the corrected information stressed. In 4, accept "the finance department," "accounts," or a description alone ("the department that deals with money").

Exercise 5 — The filler audit (Script 8.1-B)

Tally: about 23 fillers: so, um ×4, basically ×2, like ×4, you know ×3, sort of ×2, actually ×2, I mean ×2, and stuff, really, yeah. Accept 18–25; the point is the ratio, not the exact number. Worth keeping (max. two): I mean before "not always" (it introduces a genuine clarification) and actually before "I miss my colleagues" (it signals a contrast with what came before). An opening So is also acceptable as a turn-starter.

Model clean version (about 30 words): "Working from home suits me, because I don't have to commute and I have more free time. But, actually, I miss my colleagues. Not all the time, I mean, but sometimes."

Dialogue — Maya's strategies

T (buying time): that's a really good question · let me think for a second · well, it depends on how you look at it · how can I put this · bear with me a second · the way I see it. C (circumlocution): the whole system that checks whether information is accurate · the process you use to verify a source · a kind of… trust gap · what's the word (flagging the gap). R (repair): or rather, the lack of it · let me rephrase that · to put it another way. M (markers with a job): hmm · you know (shared understanding) · I mean (clarifying) · sort of a mixed picture (an honest hedge) · essentially. The online version says "the thing you use to verify a source"; the workbook uses "the process," which is more accurate for fact-checking: either is fine.

Self-check
Q1
At C1, the best definition of spoken fluency is:
A) speaking as fast as possible without pausing
B) keeping going coherently, even while you think
C) never pausing at any point
Why: fluency is continuity, not velocity. Filled pauses are normal; silent, frozen pauses and restarts break fluency. A and C describe the anxious "speed" goal the lesson replaces.
Q2
You can't remember the word "corkscrew" mid-sentence. The most fluent response is to:
A) stop and say "sorry, I don't know the word"
B) go silent until it comes to you
C) describe it: "the thing you use to open a wine bottle"
Why: circumlocution keeps the sentence moving and often prompts the listener to supply the word. A and B stop the conversation.
Q3 · new
Which is the smoothest self-correction?
A) "The meeting is on Thursday, or rather, Friday, so we have an extra day."
B) "The meeting is on Thursday. Sorry, sorry, no. Friday. Sorry."
C) "The meeting is on… I don't know how to say. Friday, I think."
Why: A repairs with or rather and continues the sentence. B apologizes three times and draws attention to the slip; C breaks the sentence and adds unnecessary uncertainty.
Errors you will hear most (8.1)

"Sorry, sorry…" at every repair · "How do you say…?" + the L1 word · L1 fillers (este, ēto, nàge, yaʿni, ano, nu, euh) · "Actually" meaning "currently" · "That's a good question" before an easy question (sounds evasive) · stacked hedges on facts ("It's sort of, like, on Tuesday, kind of") · restarting the whole sentence after a small slip · speeding up after a time-buyer instead of slowing down. For range, praise How can I put this?, Let me come at this from another angle, precise paraphrases ("a device for measuring air pressure") and refinements (or, more precisely…).

8.2 · Precision & Concision (C24)

Exercise 1 — Tighten the wordy phrase

1 to · 2 because (also since, as) · 3 now (also currently) · 4 eliminate · 5 decide · 6 can · 7 crucial (also vital, essential, key) · 8 conclude · 9 although (also though, even though) · 10 soon

Items 1–8 come from the online lesson. Wrong: 2 "due to" alone is possible before a noun (due to the rain) but not before a clause ("due to it rained" ✘) · 9 "despite" + clause ("despite the system is…" ✘).

Exercise 2 — Cut the redundant word

1 gift · 2 result · 3 history · 4 planning · 5 each or every · 6 proximity (in practice, rewrite: near, close to) · 7 outcome · 8 join

The "why" in each case: the cut word repeats something already inside the other word (a gift is already free; an outcome is already final). In 5, each and every can be kept for deliberate emphasis in speech.

Exercise 3 — Upgrade, cut, or keep?

1 exhausted (also worn out, drained) · 2 huge (also vast, enormous) · 3 astonished (also amazed, stunned) · 4 terrible (also severe, devastating) · 5 starving (also famished, ravenous) · 6 spotless (also immaculate)

7 CUT: "It's a simple change." (four empty qualifiers) · 8 KEEP: real emphasis, a deliberate choice · 9 KEEP: slightly gives a precise degree · 10 CUT: "We were pleased with the excellent feedback." (or delighted with the positive feedback)

In 4, devastating is stronger than really bad; accept it if the context supports it. In 10, "quite really" is also ungrammatical stacking: point it out.

Exercise 4 — Dig out the verb
  1. The committee will review the proposal.
  2. We discussed the budget on Monday.
  3. The manager explained the new policy.
  4. The volunteers helped (assisted) the new families.
  5. The council has improved the bus service.
  6. We need to consider all the options before we decide.

Discussion question: the original of sentence 1 might suit a formal committee report or official minutes, where a nominal style sounds impersonal and procedural ("A review of the proposal will be carried out"). Wrong: "We discussed about the budget" (discuss takes a direct object) · "The manager explained us the policy."

Exercise 5 — Untangle it
  1. Delays are common when staff are absent. (online lesson answer)
  2. Her proposal is similar to (like) the one we rejected last year.
  3. The results may be roughly accurate. (online lesson answer; also: The results may be broadly accurate.)
  4. A team was assembled to look into the matter raised at the last meeting. Its report was well received.

Item 3 has three negatives (not impossible · not entirely · inaccurate). Accept any version that keeps the tentative meaning; "The results were accurate" loses the hedge. In 4, accept other splits, e.g. "The team assembled after the last meeting wrote a report on the matter raised there. It was well received." Check that it really is two sentences, not a comma splice.

Dialogue — In the Editing Room

The four cuts: (1) a wordy phrase: due to the fact that → because; (2) padding removed: we are currently at a point where; (3) an intensifier upgraded: very high → soaring; (4) buried verbs dug out: made the decision to → decided to, carry out a review of → review. Word count: 29 → 11.

Writing — The 30% Cut Challenge (model versions)

Models deliberately go past 30% to show what's possible; any version at or below the target that keeps every idea is a success. The ideas the meaning-keeper checks are printed on the back of each text card.

Traps to watch for: A: cutting "8 to 10 a.m." or "one or more days" (facts); changing the register ("Hey guys"). B: cutting "renovated last year by volunteers" (an idea) or the opening hours. C: cutting "according to the developers' own research" (the source matters: that's critical reading, C1 6.1) or turning "report feeling" into "feel" (a claim becomes a fact). D: cutting "many adults think" (the view the paragraph argues against) or "or later." Good decisions not to cut: "seems to be" in A (a hedge), "report" in C, "Many adults think" in D.

Self-check
Q1
Which is the most concise rewrite of "We will make a decision at this point in time"?
A) We will decide now.
B) We will make a decision at the present time.
C) We will proceed in order to arrive at a decision now.
Why: A digs out the verb (make a decision → decide) and replaces at this point in time with now. B swaps one wordy phrase for another; C adds padding.
Q2
When is nominalization the right move rather than strong verbs?
A) When the genre calls for formal, impersonal abstraction: match the tool to the goal
B) Never: strong verbs are always better than nouns
C) Always: nominalization is more correct than plain verbs
Why: neither style is "correct" in the abstract. Nominalization (C1 3.1) suits reports and legal texts; strong verbs suit emails, summaries and arguments. The writer chooses for the reader and purpose.
Q3 · new
Best edit of "Due to the fact that a large number of staff were absent, we made the decision to postpone the review"?
A) Due to many staff being absent, a decision was made to postpone.
B) Staff absent, review postponed.
C) Because many staff were absent, we postponed the review.
Why: C makes all three cuts (because, many, postponed) and keeps every idea. A keeps a nominal, passive structure and loses "the review." B is headline style: articles and verbs are gone, so it is no longer a normal sentence (over-cutting).
Errors you will see most (8.2)

Over-cutting (articles, auxiliaries and that dropped: "Committee will review proposal") · cutting a number, name or source · cutting a hedge and turning a claim into a fact ("seems to be" → "is") · changing the register (an email becomes a text message) · "due to" + clause · "despite" + clause · "discuss about" · comma splices after "splitting" a sentence · replacing very + adjective with a word of the wrong strength (very good → "perfect"). For range, praise precise strong words (soaring, spotless, drained), sensible decisions not to cut, and varied sentence length.

8.3 · A Position Paper & Its Defense (C25)

Exercise 1 — Listen to a defense (Script 8.3-A)
  1. That city centers should be closed to private cars.
  2. That such a ban would hurt local businesses. She answers with evidence: foot traffic on car-free streets tends to rise, not fall.
  3. She concedes that a blanket ban would be unfair, then clarifies that her proposal exempts accessible transportation and permit holders, so the principle stands if the exemptions are well designed.
  4. Whether the rise in foot traffic only happens in wealthy cities that could already afford good public transportation.
  5. She hedges it: the effect is clearest where transportation is strong, the causation may run both ways, and the ban works best alongside investment in transportation, not instead of it.
  6. She checks the question ("If I've understood your question, you're asking…"), concedes she can't be certain, and proposes a trial period with a review after a year.
Exercise 2 — The model paper

(a) Parts: ¶1 = 1 thesis · ¶2 = 2 argument A (health) · ¶3 = 3 argument B (learning, + equity) · ¶4 = 4 counter-argument · ¶5 = 5 rebuttal · ¶6 = 6 conclusion.

(b) Hedges (any three): broadly agree · up to two hours · generally reported · some studies suggest modest gains · hardly surprising · typically · often · may be unable · would appear to offer.

(c) Tools: participle clause: Given this shift, … · inversion: Not only would a later start protect… · Rarely does a single decision promise… · Nor would the benefit be evenly spread · conditional: Were the school day aligned…, teachers would spend… (inverted conditional) · cleft / fronting: It is for this reason that… · it is typically students with the longest commutes… who lose the most sleep · academic collocations: chronic sleep loss · well documented · modest gains · a related consideration · practical rather than fundamental · the weight of evidence · a rare combination of low cost and high return. Also: nominalization and reference (These concerns, … the change) and concession (Admittedly, it might be objected that…). About 480 words + title.

Examiner questions (good examples): "You say some studies suggest gains in grades. How strong and how consistent is that evidence?" · "Wouldn't later finishing times simply move the problem to the evening, when students do homework and jobs?" · "Why 9 a.m. rather than the 8:30 the pediatric organizations recommend?" · "What would change your mind?"

Exercise 3 — The thesis test

1 ✔ arguable, clear and specific · 2 ✘ a fact · 3 ✘ a survey, not a position · 4 ✘ too vague (and over-hedged) · 5 ✘ an outburst (attacks people, no reason) · 6 ✔ arguable, clear and specific

Model rewrites: 3 → "This paper argues that homework for children under ten should be limited to reading." · 4 → "This paper argues that high schools should teach every student to evaluate online sources critically." · 5 → "This paper argues that stores should continue to accept cash, because going cashless excludes customers without bank cards." Accept any rewrite that is arguable, clear and specific.

Exercise 4 — Mixed C1 review

1 Given · 2 to · 3 suggests (also indicates, shows) · 4 however (also nevertheless, even so) · 5 appear (also seem) · 6 but · 7 Were · 8 What · 9 Because (also Since, As) · 10 decide

Items 1–6 and 9–10 come from the online lesson (Exercises 1 and 2). 7 is the inverted conditional (C1 2.1–2.2); 8 is a wh-cleft (C1 2.3). Wrong: 2 "threat for" · 4 "on the other side" · 6 "but also" is also correct (but it is also) · 7 "If" alone doesn't fit ("If the plan adopted" ✘).

Exercise 5 — Concede without collapsing

1 B · 2 A · 3 C

In each set, one response collapses (1A, 2C, 3B: it abandons the case), one stonewalls (1C, 2B, 3A: no concession, no reason) and one is poised (concede → hold → reason). 2A is a model of hedging a claim rather than defending it at all costs. Own answer: look for a named objection, a genuine concession and a reason for holding the position; accept any topic.

Planning frame, checklist, defense prep, rubric

Open tasks: no key. When you check planning frames (C24) and papers (after C25), use the paper rubric (materials set J). The two most common planning problems: a thesis that is a survey ("There are advantages and disadvantages…") and an objection that is a straw man ("Some people think children should never sleep"). Ask one question for each rather than rewriting it: "Would someone on the other side really say that? What would they actually say?"

Self-check
Q1
What single element most distinguishes a position paper from a simple opinion essay?
A) It is much longer and more detailed.
B) It stays neutral and presents every view equally.
C) It acknowledges a counter-argument and rebuts it.
Why: the concede-then-rebut move shows the writer has weighed the question. Length isn't the point (position papers are often short), and neutrality makes it a survey, not a position.
Q2
Someone raises a genuinely fair objection to your case. The most advanced response is to:
A) refuse to concede anything and restate your thesis more loudly
B) concede the fair point, then explain why your position still holds
C) immediately abandon your position and agree
Why: honest concession plus a reasoned "and yet" is the poised middle. A stonewalls; C collapses.
Q3 · new
You're not sure what exactly an examiner means. Which is the best start?
A) "Sorry, I don't understand. Next question, please."
B) "If I've understood your question, you're asking whether the evidence applies to smaller cities. To some extent, yes; however…"
C) Answer a different question that you prepared at home.
Why: B checks the question, buys a few seconds, and lets the examiner correct you before you answer. A refuses the question; C ignores it (examiners notice immediately).
Errors you will hear and see most (8.3)

In papers: a survey instead of a thesis · the thesis in the last paragraph · a straw-man objection · overclaiming ("This proves that…", "Everyone agrees…") · "According to me" / "In my opinion, I think" · "discuss about," "emphasize on," "explain me" · toolkit items forced in where they don't fit (three inversions in one paragraph) · mixed American and British spelling. In the defense: reading the paper aloud · answering the question they prepared instead of the one asked · collapsing ("You're right, my idea is bad") or stonewalling ("No, that's wrong") · "I am agree with you" · long silences before a hard answer. For range, praise genuine concessions, refined claims (So I'd put it more cautiously…), checking questions, and examiners' precise follow-ups.
