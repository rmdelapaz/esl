8.1

Keep Going — Fluency & Spontaneity

Class C23
New words — the fluency toolkit

Fluency at C1 is not speed. It is the ability to keep going coherently while your mind is still working. These phrases fill a pause with meaning instead of silence.

Buying time

That's a good question…Let me think for a second…Well, it depends…How can I put this?The way I see it…If you ask me…Bear with me a moment…

Talking around a word (circumlocution)

it's a kind of…the thing you use to…it's like…, but…the person who…it's when / it's where…what's the word…?the, oh, whatever it's called…

Repairing on the move

or rather…or should I say…what I mean is…let me rephrase thatto put it another way…
Strategy box
When this happens…Do thisFor example
A hard or unexpected questionBuy time with a phrase that carries meaning, then pause calmly."That's a good question. I'd say it comes down to trust."
A word will not comeDescribe it: category, function, comparison, role or situation."It's the thing you use to measure temperature." (thermometer)
A sentence goes wrongRepair it lightly and keep going. No apology, no restart."We arrived on Tuesday, or rather, Wednesday, and…"
You reach for a fillerKeep it only if it does a job; otherwise use a silent beat."Well, I actually think we should wait." (two markers, both useful)

A filler with a job: well opens a turn · actually flags a surprise or mild correction · I mean introduces a clarification · you know invites shared feeling · sort of marks an honest approximation. A filler that is a tic: the same word in every clause, doing nothing.

Grammar box — four cuts

1 · Wordy phrase → lean phrase

in order to → todue to the fact that → becauseat this point in time → now
in the event that → ifa large number of → manyhas the ability to → can
in spite of the fact that → althoughwith regard to → aboutin the near future → soon

Redundant pairs: keep the word that carries the meaning: end result · free gift · past history · advance planning · each and every · completely eliminate.

2 · Empty intensifier → one precise word

very tired → exhaustedreally important → crucial / vitalvery surprised → astonished
very big → huge / vastvery good → excellentvery bad → terrible / dire

Cut basically, just, really, quite, actually when the sentence means the same without them. Keep an intensifier that adds real emphasis (I'm absolutely certain) or a precise degree (slightly undercooked).

3 · Noun + light verb → strong verb

make a decision → decidereach a conclusion → concludecarry out an investigation of → investigate
give consideration to → considerhave a discussion about → discussmake an improvement to → improve

This is the reverse of nominalization (Lesson 3.1). Neither is wrong: strong verbs for clear, direct writing (emails, summaries, arguments); nominalization for formal, impersonal density (reports, legal texts). Match the tool to the goal.

4 · Untangle: It is not uncommon → It is common · not without merit → has some merit. If you can't read a sentence aloud in one breath and remember how it began, split it or put the main idea first.

Dialogue — in the editing room

Editor: Read me your opening line as it stands.

Writer: "Due to the fact that we are currently at a point where costs are very high, we have made the decision to carry out a review of our spending."

Editor: Right. First, "due to the fact that" is just "because." And "we are currently at a point where"? Cut it.

Writer: So: "Because costs are very high, we have made the decision to carry out a review of our spending."

Editor: Better. Now, "very high": is there one word for that? And there are two verbs hiding in nouns.

Writer: "Very high" could be "soaring." "Made the decision to" is "decided," and "carry out a review of" is "review."

Editor: Say the whole thing now.

Writer: "Because costs are soaring, we have decided to review our spending." Eleven words instead of twenty-nine, and it hits harder.

Editor: That's the craft. You didn't lose a single idea; you lost the padding around them.

What a position paper is — six parts

A position paper takes a clear stand on a debatable question and argues for it with reasons and evidence. It is not a neutral survey of views, and it is not a personal outburst. Its signature move is the counter-argument: you state the strongest objection fairly, then answer it.

PartJobTypical language
1 · Thesisstate your position clearly and earlyThis paper argues that… · It will be contended that…
2 · Argument Ayour first supported reasonThe primary reason is… · Research suggests that…
3 · Argument Ba second reason, linked to the firstFurthermore, … · A related consideration is…
4 · Counter-argumentthe strongest objection, stated fairlyAdmittedly, … · It might be objected that…
5 · Rebuttalwhy your position still holdsThis concern, while valid, overlooks… · However, …
6 · Conclusionrestate the position, sharpenedOn balance, … · The weight of evidence therefore suggests…

Language for the defense

If I've understood your question, you're asking whether…That's a fair point, and I don't want to brush it aside.You're right that…To some extent, yes; that said, …I take your point, but I'd still argue that…So I'd put it slightly more cautiously: …Let me come at that from another angle.The weight of evidence points to…

Later Bells, Better Learning: Why High Schools Should Start at 9 a.m.

Across much of the country, the high-school day begins at around 7:30 a.m., a timetable designed more for adult convenience than for adolescent biology. This paper argues that high schools should start no earlier than 9 a.m. Not only would a later start protect students' health, but it would also improve the quality of their learning.

The primary reason is biological. Sleep researchers broadly agree that during adolescence the body clock shifts: teenagers begin to feel sleepy up to two hours later than they did as children, yet they still need eight to ten hours of sleep. Given this shift, a student who must wake at 6 a.m. is, in effect, being asked to function in the middle of their biological night. The consequences of chronic sleep loss are well documented: lower mood, weaker concentration and, among newly licensed drivers, a higher risk of accidents on the way to school. It is for this reason that leading pediatric organizations have recommended that no middle or high school begin before 8:30 a.m.; this paper simply goes half an hour further.

A related consideration is academic performance. Schools that have moved their start time later have generally reported better attendance and punctuality, and some studies suggest modest gains in grades, particularly in first-period classes. This is hardly surprising, since a rested brain retains new information far more effectively than an exhausted one. Were the school day aligned with students' sleep patterns, teachers would spend less time repeating material and more time extending it. Nor would the benefit be evenly spread: it is typically students with the longest commutes, often those from lower-income families, who lose the most sleep and would gain the most.

Admittedly, it might be objected that a later start would disrupt family routines. Parents who begin work early may be unable to drop their children off, bus companies would have to redesign their routes, and after-school commitments, from sports practice to part-time jobs, would finish later in the evening.

These concerns, while valid, are practical rather than fundamental, and practical problems invite practical solutions. Schools could open their doors at 7:30 a.m. for a supervised breakfast and quiet study, so that no student is left waiting outside, and several districts that have made the change have reorganized their bus schedules within a single year. Moreover, the cost of adjusting a timetable is paid once, whereas the benefits of better-rested students recur every school day.

On balance, then, the weight of evidence points in one direction. A modest change to the timetable, one that asks adults to adapt rather than asking teenagers to fight their own biology, would appear to offer a rare combination of low cost and high return. Rarely does a single decision promise to improve health, safety and learning at the same time. High schools should seize that opportunity and move the first bell to 9 a.m.
