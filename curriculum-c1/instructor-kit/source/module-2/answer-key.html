Module 2 — Conditionals, Inversion & Emphasis: Answers

Answers to every Module 2 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the reasoning, acceptable alternatives, model versions of the open tasks (a case-study analysis, a rewritten speech, an edited article, a spoken correction) and the errors you are most likely to hear.

Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. A sentence is right when the structure is correct (the time logic of each conditional clause, auxiliary + subject after a fronted trigger, be agreeing with the focus in a cleft) and the choice suits the meaning and the occasion. An inversion in a text message, or three clefts in a row, can be grammatical and still be marked down for appropriacy: say so, and say why. Be generous with genuine options: was / were after if I / she (prefer were in formal writing), that / who / zero in clefts, provided / providing (that), so long as / as long as, must / should after On no account. American English is the model; accept British spellings and forms (realise, apologise, cancelled, got for gotten). When a learner produces a correct alternative to the target, accept it and nudge: "Correct. Now try it with but for."

2.1 · Mixed Conditionals & Alternatives to "if" (C4)

Exercise 1 — "The Hinge" podcast (Script 2.1-A)
1234567
had accepted · would beweren't · would have takenBut forprovided thatin casewere toUnless

Accept 'd be in 1, wasn't in 2 (but point out that the script uses weren't), providing (that) in 4. The hinge factor: Brightwell turned down the Swift Deliveries contract in 2022, largely because of the founder's cautious character; that caution later helped it survive the battery shortage. Items 1 and 2 are the two directions of mixed conditional: ask "When is the condition? When is the result?" for each.

Exercise 2 — Diagnose the time
  1. hadn't signed · wouldn't be paying (also wouldn't pay) · P→Pr
  2. were (also was) · wouldn't have resigned · Pr→P (a permanent trait explains a past action)
  3. had tested · would still trust · P→Pr
  4. spoke · would have gone · Pr→P
  5. had listened · might be · P→Pr
  6. weren't (also wasn't) · would never have offered · Pr→P

Wrong: 1 "wouldn't have paid … now" (habitual symmetry) · 2 "hadn't been … wouldn't have resigned" is a correct third conditional but loses the "he is impatient by nature" meaning: accept it grammatically, then ask "Is he still impatient?" · 3 "would have still trusted us today" · 4 "If I had spoken" (third conditional: possible only if the learner means a one-off past failure to speak it, not a general inability) · 5 "might have been in profit by now" (acceptable only as speculation about the past; by now signals the present).

Exercise 3 — The exact alternative
  1. on condition that (a formal, explicit stipulation from a bank)
  2. in case (a precaution, taken now)
  3. Supposing
  4. Even if (the result holds regardless)
  5. provided that or as long as
  6. unless (= except if)
  7. otherwise
  8. as long as or provided that (whichever was not used in 5)

5 and 8 are interchangeable; provided that sounds slightly more transactional, as long as slightly more conversational. In 1, provided that is also grammatical, but on condition that is the most formal fit for a bank's stipulation. Wrong: 2 "if sales drop" (then the money would be set aside only after the drop, which misses the precaution) · 6 "if the tests reveal" reverses the meaning.

Exercise 4 — Isolate the decisive factor
  1. But for the engineers' quick action, the data would have been lost. / If it hadn't been for the engineers' quick action, …
  2. If it weren't for the noise from the street, the office would be perfect. / But for the noise from the street, …
  3. If it hadn't been for a single investor (who believed in us), we would never have launched. / But for one investor's faith in us, …
  4. If it weren't for our volunteers, the festival would stop. / But for our volunteers, …
  5. But for a chance meeting at a trade fair, the partnership would never have happened. / If it hadn't been for a chance meeting…

Present reality (2, 4) → if it weren't for; past reality (1, 3, 5) → if it hadn't been for; but for works for both. Accept Were it not for / Had it not been for (previewed; taught in 2.2). Wrong: "But for the engineers acted quickly" (a clause after but for) · "If it wasn't for… would have been lost" (time mismatch in 1) · "If it hadn't been for the noise, the office would be perfect" in 2 (the noise is still there).

Exercise 5 — Right or wrong?
  1. ✘ …I would be an analyst now. (past condition → present result)
  2. ✘ I'd be amazed if she didn't get the promotion. (unless can't express an unreal negative emotion; test: "except if" doesn't fit)
  3. ✔ (a genuine precaution)
  4. ✘ If the price fell (or were to fall), we would buy more stock. (no would in the if-clause)
  5. ✘ But for your help, I would have missed the deadline. (but for + noun)
  6. ✘ Call me if you need anything, and I'll come straight over. (the call depends on the need: a condition, not a precaution)
  7. ✔ (tentative should, inverted; a preview of 2.2)
Dialogue — two co-founders look back, and ahead

Mixed conditionals: "If we hadn't opened five cafés in one year, we'd still have money in the bank" (P→Pr) · "if we weren't such an impatient pair, we'd never have gotten the first one off the ground" (Pr→P). But for: "but for Maria's weekend markets, Elm Street would have closed last spring." Alternatives: provided that · supposing · as long as · in case · even if · unless · on condition that (+ tentative if the board should ask). The precaution: "we hold three months' rent in reserve in case a renewal falls through."

Speaking — The Case Study Panel (model analysis, Case 1: Harbor Fresh)

"Harbor Fresh's turning point came in 2022, when it decided not to build its own warehouse. If it had built that warehouse, it would be the market leader today: GreenCart made exactly that investment and now holds that position. Why did Harbor Fresh hesitate? If its CEO weren't so wary of debt, he would have borrowed the $400,000 without a second thought. When its only wholesaler went bankrupt in 2023, the shelves were empty for three weeks, and but for the Riverside Farmers' Co-op, which supplied it free for two weeks, the company would have closed. Our recommendation is to accept the co-op's partnership offer, provided that the minimum order is reviewed every six months, and to keep a second wholesaler on standby in case the harvest fails. Even if costs rise slightly, the security is worth it. Should a competitor try to buy the co-op, Harbor Fresh would need the right to match the offer. And if the market were to recover strongly next year, a small warehouse of its own would finally make sense." (Tick: P→Pr, Pr→P, but for, even if + provided that + in case, should, were to.)

Accept any analysis that is faithful to the case facts and meets the checklist. Mark down for appropriacy if but for is used with a clause, or if in case is used for a condition ("we'll buy a warehouse in case the market recovers").

Self-check
Q1
"If I ___ that email yesterday, I wouldn't be in this mess now." Which completes this mixed conditional?
A) didn't send
B) wouldn't have sent
C) hadn't sent
Why: the condition is an unreal past action (yesterday), so it needs the past perfect; the result is present (now). A is a second-conditional form (present unreal); B is a result form, never used in the if-clause.
Q2
"I'll take my charger ___ the battery dies on the train." Which best fits a precaution taken in advance?
A) if
B) in case
C) unless
Why: in case = I take it now because the battery might die later. With if, you would only take the charger at the moment the battery died. Unless reverses the sense.
Q3 · new
"We'll sign the contract ___ the delivery date stays in March. That's our only condition."
A) provided that
B) in case
C) even if
Why: "our only condition" signals a strict, negotiated condition: provided that (or as long as / on condition that). In case is a precaution; even if means the signing happens regardless.
Errors you will hear most (2.1)

"If I had studied medicine, I would have been a doctor now." · "If it would rain, we'd cancel." · "I'll bring a jacket if it gets cold" when a precaution is meant, and the reverse, "Call me in case you need help" for a condition · "Unless she came, I'd be surprised." · "But for you helped me…" · "If it wasn't for the grant, we would have closed" (time mismatch: needed hadn't been) · "provide that" / "provided if" · "in the case that" for in case. Praise range: were to, should, but for, even if, on condition that, and modal variety in the result clause (might have caught, could be).

2.2 · Inversion for Emphasis & Style (C5)

Exercise 1 — The acceptance speech (Script 2.2-A)
123456
have I beendid I imaginedid you keepwas the response (from our neighbors)Had it not beenwill we be

Triggers: 1 Never · 2 Little · 3 Not only · 4 So + adjective · 5 conditional inversion · 6 Only by. The speech has six inversions in about 180 words: a good discussion point for the follow-up question. Most learners cut 2 or 6; any cut with a reason is acceptable.

Exercise 2 — Front and invert
  1. Seldom have I read such a persuasive report.
  2. At no time was the public in danger.
  3. Only when the audit was complete did we understand the scale of the problem.
  4. Not only did she meet the deadline, but she (also) came in under budget.
  5. Hardly had the meeting begun when the power went out.
  6. Under no circumstances should you leave the lab unlocked.
  7. Not until the following morning did the truth emerge.

In 3, the inversion happens in the main clause, not after when: "Only when the audit was complete did we understand." Wrong: "Only when was the audit complete…" · 4 "Not only she met" / "Not only did she met" · 5 "Hardly the meeting had begun" · 7 "Not until the following morning the truth emerged."

Exercise 3 — Drop "if"
  1. Had we known about the delay, we would have rebooked.
  2. Should you require further information, please contact our office.
  3. Were the factory to close, the town would lose its largest employer.
  4. Had it not been for the volunteers, the event would have been canceled.
  5. Were I in your position, I would accept the offer.
  6. Should you not receive a confirmation within 48 hours, please call us.

6 needs should because the original is a real (first-conditional) possibility; the inverted form adds politeness. Wrong: 4 "Hadn't it been for" (a question form) · 6 "Shouldn't you receive" · "Did we know about the delay" (only had, were, should invert) · 3 "Would the factory close".

Exercise 4 — Faulty or fine?
  1. Never have I seen such a turnout.
  2. Rarely does the director admit a mistake. (tense lives on does only)
  3. Had we not left early, we would have missed the train.
  4. ✔ Correct: Only Sarah is the subject, not a fronted adverbial.
  5. Not until the end of the meeting did she mention the budget.
  6. Look, here it comes! (no place inversion with a pronoun)
  7. So impressive were the results that the board approved the plan.
Exercise 5 — Weight or pomp?
  1. ✘ (unless the learner means it as a joke, which is a legitimate use; accept with that explanation) → "I've never eaten such an enormous burrito!"
  2. ✔ A tribute is exactly the occasion for weight.
  3. ✔ Safety notices use emphatic prohibition; this is standard.
  4. ✘ Too formal for a quick lunch email (shall adds to the stiffness) → "If you'd like to join us for lunch, we'll be at the café at noon."
  5. ✔ Reviews reward emphatic openings.
  6. ✘ Four inversions crowd four sentences: they stop emphasizing and start sounding pompous. → e.g. "Never have we grown so fast. We hired more people than ever, and both profits and morale rose. Now we can see the results."
Dialogue — the speechwriter's edit

"Never have I worked with anyone quite like Grace" (Never) · "Only when the water went down did we realize…" (Only when) · "So calm was she that nobody panicked" (So + adjective): the writer advised against it because it would be the third inversion in a row, and density turns weight into pomp · "Should you ever need a book found…" (conditional inversion, warm and slightly playful in a closing line).

Speaking — The Speechwriter (model, Brief 1: tribute to Walter Chen)

"For twenty-eight years, Walter Chen has kept Riverside High running. Never have I met anyone who knows a building so well. During the blizzard of 2011 he fixed the boiler, and told no one. Only when the heating bills arrived did we find out. So modest was he that most students never learned his name, though every one of them knew his whistle. Had it not been for Walter, this school would have closed more times than I can count. He never missed a graduation, and he never once complained. Should you ever need a door fixed, please don't call Walter: he's retired. Please raise your glasses: to Walter."

Five inversions, four different types, and the plain sentences between them ("He never missed a graduation…") are deliberate. A sixth ("Not until he took a vacation did we understand how much he did") is exactly what the editor should cut. Models for Briefs 2–4 are printed at the foot of each brief card (classroom materials, set D). When marking, check the tick-boxes, then judge: did every inversion earn its place?

Self-check
Q1
Which is the correct inversion of "She rarely admits a mistake"?
A) Rarely does she admit a mistake.
B) Rarely she admits a mistake.
C) Rarely does she admits a mistake.
Why: no auxiliary in the original, so borrow does, and the main verb goes bare. B has no inversion; C marks the tense twice.
Q2
Which correctly rewrites "If I had known, I would have called" with conditional inversion?
A) Had I known, I would have called.
B) Would I have known, I would have called.
C) Did I know, I would have called.
Why: drop if and front the auxiliary had. Only had, were and should can invert in a conditional.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) Not only she apologized, but also she offered a refund.
B) Not only did she apologize, but she also offered a refund.
C) Not only did she apologized, but she also offered a refund.
Why: fronted Not only needs did + subject + bare verb; the second clause keeps normal word order (but she also offered). A has no inversion; C double-marks the past.
Errors you will hear most (2.2)

"Never I have seen…" · "Never saw I…" (verb-second transfer) · "Rarely does she admits…" · "Little did I knew…" · "Hadn't I known…" · "Only when did the audit finish…" (inverting the wrong clause) · "Not only did he lie, but also did he…" (inverting both clauses) · "Here comes it." · "That book have I never read." (inverting after an ordinary topic) · Density: four inversions in a 100-word paragraph. Praise judgment as much as form: a learner who explains why they didn't invert a line has understood the lesson.

2.3 · Cleft Sentences & Fronting for Cohesion (C6)

Exercise 1 — The radio interview (Script 2.3-A)
123456
wasn't · thatWhat we noticedwasn't untilAll wewas thatwe didn't lose

Accept which in 1 (informal). The misconception: that Nadia banned laptops because she dislikes them. What actually happened: lunch customers were leaving because laptop users filled the tables at midday, so she asked for a two-hour pause, and the morning regulars stayed. Item 6 is fronting, not a cleft: ask learners to spot the difference.

Exercise 2 — Rewrite for focus
  1. It was the new manager who (that) changed the schedule.
  2. It was the weather, not the budget, that made us cancel the event. / It was because of the weather, not the budget, that we canceled the event.
  3. What I need is more time.
  4. What happened was (that) the server (simply) overheated.
  5. All I want is a straight answer.
  6. The reason (why) I'm calling is that the invoice is wrong.
  7. It wasn't until Monday that we noticed the error.

Wrong: 1 "It was the new manager which changed" · 3 "What I need it is more time" · 6 "The reason I'm calling is because…" (accept in speech, correct in writing) · 7 "It wasn't until Monday when we noticed" (common; prefer that) · "It was on Monday that we only noticed" (keeps only and the cleft).

Exercise 3 — Front the linking element
  1. The second (one), it accepted immediately. / The second it accepted immediately.
  2. The first one, we will never use again.
  3. Even more serious is the cost of further delay. (fronted comparative; be inverts)
  4. Of the three, training was the most popular request.

Only 3 inverts, because Even more serious is a fronted complement of be. Wrong: "The first one we will never use it again" (resumptive pronoun) · "The second one did it accept" (inversion without a trigger).

Exercise 4 — Move the weight to the end
  1. It surprised nobody that the project was late.
  2. It is (It's) still unclear whether the client will renew.
  3. It is impossible to finish the whole report by Friday.
  4. It is no surprise that she was offered the job.

Wrong: "Is still unclear whether…" (missing dummy it) · "It surprised nobody the project was late" (acceptable in speech; keep that in writing).

Exercise 5 — Which continuation flows better?

1 (a) · 2 (b) · 3 (b) · 4 (a)

1: a corrective it-cleft affirms the real cause and denies the assumed one in one move; (b) is clumsy. 2: the first is given (one of the two options), so fronting it links back and leaves the new verdict for the end; (a) front-loads a heavy clause. 3: end-weight: the heavy that-clause belongs at the end. 4: staff and money are given; time is the new, contrasting point, so the what-cleft saves it for the end; (b) is possible but clumsy, and "have not got" is British and informal.

Dialogue — setting the record straight

It-clefts: "it wasn't the money that closed it" (corrective) · "It was space" (a shortened cleft: It was space [that closed it]) · "It was in March that we first consulted members." What / all: "What the figures show is a return rate of ninety-six percent" · "All we're doing is moving them." Reason frame: "The reason it felt sudden is that the notice went up late." Fronting: "That part, I fully accept." The corrective cleft is the first one: it denies the money explanation and puts space in its place.

Speaking — The Editing Desk (model, Article A)

"Tallis Print Works, a small printing company in Riverside with 40 employees, moved to a four-day week last year. Many people expected productivity to fall. What actually happened was that it rose by eight percent. It wasn't the shorter week on its own that caused the rise, though; it was better scheduling. Every job is now planned on Monday morning. Even more surprising is the fall in sick days, which nobody predicted. The change was a risk, says the owner, Ruth Tallis, but it is one she is glad she took."

Two clefts, one fronted comparative, a passive that keeps "every job" (given) up front, and plain order everywhere else. Models for Articles B and C:

B (Eastgate nap pods): "Eastgate Distribution Center, a warehouse near the airport that runs around the clock, installed two nap pods for night-shift workers last year. Some managers laughed at the idea, but the results were no joke: accidents on the night shift fell by a third. It wasn't the pods alone that made the difference. What also mattered was a new rule, a twenty-minute break after every four hours, and it was the workers, not management, who suggested it. Other warehouses are now asking Eastgate for advice. As for the pods, they are staying."

C (Greenway garden): "The vegetable garden at Greenway Middle School was a parent's idea, and nobody expected much from it. What happened surprised everyone: attendance improved on Fridays, when the garden club meets. The garden has become a classroom, too; every week the science teacher's students measure how their plants have grown. The vegetables they grow, the cafeteria now serves, and students are eating more salad than ever. Summer is harder. It is neighborhood volunteers who water the garden while the school is closed."

Accept any version that keeps every fact, moves given information forward and new information to the end, and uses no more than three clefts. Mark down a version that clefts given information ("It was the company that moved to a four-day week").

Speaking — Setting the Record Straight (model corrections, cards 1–6)
  1. "Actually, it wasn't theft that closed the tool library; it was space. What the figures show is a return rate of 96 percent, and the service reopens at the community center in June."
  2. "It isn't technology that Nadia objects to. What she needed was lunch seating. All she did was ask for a two-hour pause at midday."
  3. "It wasn't a lack of riders that stopped the Number 9. Around 1,200 people use it every week. The reason it's paused is that the road works forced a merger with the Number 4 until September."
  4. "The pool isn't closing because it's unsafe. What's happening is a six-week closure for a new heating system, and when it reopens, the hours will actually be longer."
  5. "No data was lost. It was a power failure at the data center, not a hack, that caused the outage, and it was the backup system that protected every account."
  6. "Pay hasn't changed at all. What has changed is the length of the day: nine hours instead of eight, over four days. And productivity is up eight percent."
Self-check
Q1
Rewrite "I admire her honesty most of all" as a cleft that delivers the focus at the end. Which is the natural what-cleft?
A) It is her honesty that I admire most of all.
B) What I admire most of all is her honesty.
C) Her honesty, I admire most of all.
Why: the what-cleft withholds the focus and delivers it last. A is an it-cleft (focus up front); C is fronting, not a cleft.
Q2
Context: "We reviewed three designs." Which continuation best respects cohesion and end-weight?
A) That we rejected the last one outright is what happened.
B) We rejected the last one outright.
C) The last, we rejected outright.
Why: the last is given (one of the three just mentioned), so fronting it links straight back, and the new verdict (rejected outright) lands at the end. B is grammatical and acceptable, but the link to the three designs is weaker; A is top-heavy.
Q3 · new
Which is the best version for a formal report?
A) The reason sales fell is because the store moved.
B) The reason sales fell is that the store moved.
C) The reason sales fell, it is that the store moved.
Why: the reason already means "the cause," so formal writing uses is that. A is common in speech but redundant; C adds a resumptive it.
Errors you will hear most (2.3)

"What surprised us is the silence" (frozen is for a past event) · "What I need are more time" / "What we need is some answers" (agreement: accept is with plural focus in speech) · "It was her who…" (accept; formal It was she who is C2 and rare) · "The reason is because…" · "Is important that…" / "Is a pity that…" (missing it) · "That book, I have read it" (resumptive pronoun) · "That book have I never read" (inversion without a trigger) · clefting given information · cleft overload: "It was… What… The reason…" in three consecutive sentences.
