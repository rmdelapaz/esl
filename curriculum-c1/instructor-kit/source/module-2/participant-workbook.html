Conditionals, Inversion & Emphasis

Three lessons about choosing where the weight of a sentence falls. First, conditionals that cross time frames, and the precise alternatives to if that analysts and negotiators rely on. Then inversion, the grammar of speeches and formal warnings, and the judgment to know when it earns its place. Finally, cleft sentences and fronting: the quiet tools that make a paragraph flow and let you correct a misunderstanding gracefully.

2.1

Had We Known… — Mixed Conditionals & Alternatives to "if"

Class C4
New words — the language of decisions
in hindsighta turning pointa hinge factorto turn down (an offer)to pay offto backfireto fall througha contingency plana buffera stipulationa trade-offto scale up

a hinge factor = the single detail on which an outcome turned · to backfire = to produce the opposite of the intended result · a buffer = a reserve (of stock, time or money) that absorbs a shock

Setting terms and weighing what-ifs

provided (that) the terms hold…as long as we agree on…on condition that…unless something changes…supposing that had happened…in case things go wrong…otherwise, we'd have to…even if that's true…but for one detail…if it hadn't been for…

Precautions: things you take now, in case something happens later.

  • in case it rains
  • a charger, in case the battery dies
  • a copy, in case it's lost
  • a spare key, in case you're locked out
Grammar box

Mixed conditionals: the condition and the result sit in different times. Diagnose each clause separately.

Past → presentif + past perfect → would / could / might + base verbIf we had accepted the offer, we'd be twice the size now.
Present → pastif + past simple (were) → would / could / might + have + ppIf she weren't so cautious, she would have invested years ago.

❌ If I had studied finance, I would have been an analyst now. · ✅ …I would be an analyst now. (The result is now.)

Three shades of the same event: probability and formality.

If the merger fails…a real possibility we are planning for
If the merger should fail…unlikely, but worth considering (tentative)
If the merger were to fail…a formal, exploratory hypothesis

Alternatives to "if": each adds its own twist.

provided / providing (that)only if this condition is met (often negotiated)You can borrow it provided that you return it by Monday.
as long as / so long ason the single condition that; for the whole time thatI'll support the plan as long as costs stay flat.
on condition thata formal, explicit stipulationThe funds were released on condition that the audit continued.
unlessexcept if (the one exception)We'll leave at six unless you object.
supposing / what ifintroduces a hypothesis (mostly spoken)Supposing the client says no, what then?
in casea precaution taken in advance, not a conditionTake an umbrella in case it rains.
otherwiseif not; or elseHurry; otherwise we'll miss it.
even ifthe result holds regardlessEven if it rains, we'll go.

Isolating the decisive factor (+ a noun, never a clause):

but for + nounpast or present · formalBut for your help, I would have failed.
if it weren't for + noununreal nowIf it weren't for the traffic, the commute would be pleasant.
if it hadn't been for + noununreal pastIf it hadn't been for one witness, the case would have collapsed.

Dialogue — two co-founders look back, and ahead

Priya: If we hadn't opened five cafés in one year, we'd still have money in the bank.

Dan: True. But if we weren't such an impatient pair, we'd never have gotten the first one off the ground.

Priya: Fair point. So, the next twelve months?

Dan: We keep the seven profitable sites, provided that the landlords renew on the same terms.

Priya: And supposing they don't?

Dan: Then we relocate the two weakest, as long as we can find sites nearby. And we hold three months' rent in reserve in case a renewal falls through.

Priya: Honestly, but for Maria's weekend markets, Elm Street would have closed last spring.

Dan: Exactly. Even if the numbers improve, let's not expand again unless we've tested a site with a pop-up first.

Priya: Agreed, on condition that I design the pop-up menu.

Dan: Deal. And if the board should ask, the strategy is "slow and steady."

2.2

Never Before Have We… — Inversion for Emphasis & Style

Class C5
New words — speeches and rhetoric
a keynotea tributea toastan acceptance speecha call to actiona set pieceto land (a line)emphaticunderstatedpompousa flourishpoise

pompous = trying too hard to sound important · to land a line = to deliver it so that it has its full effect · a set piece = a carefully prepared passage, delivered for effect

Triggers worth knowing by heart

Never before have I…Not only… but also…No sooner had… than…Little did I know…Only then did I realize…Not until… did…Under no circumstances should you…At no time was…Nor do I…
Grammar box

The mechanism: front a negative or restrictive adverbial → switch to question word order (auxiliary + subject) → no auxiliary? Borrow do / does / did, and the main verb goes bare.

With an auxiliaryI had never seen… → Never had I seen…
Without oneI never imagined… → Never did I imagine… (not imagined)
Never (before) · Rarely · SeldomSeldom does one encounter a mind so original.
LittleLittle did he know what awaited him.
Not only… but (also)Not only did she resign, but she also exposed the fraud.
Not until…Not until midnight did the truth emerge.
No sooner… than · Hardly / Scarcely… whenNo sooner had we sat down than the alarm rang.
Only then / after / by / when / ifOnly by working together will we succeed.
On no account · Under no circumstances · At no time · In no wayAt no time was the public at risk.
Nor / NeitherShe didn't apologize, nor did she explain.

Conditional inversion: drop if. Only three auxiliaries: had, were, should. Negatives stay uncontracted.

If I had known…Had I known…
If she were to accept…Were she to accept…
If you should require…Should you require…
If it had not been for…Had it not been for… (never Hadn't it been for)

So / such: So great was the demand that the servers crashed. · Such was his anger that no one dared speak.

Place and direction (literary; full-noun subjects only): In the doorway stood a stranger. · Here comes the bus. but Here it comes (not Here comes it).

No inversion when the negative is not fronted (I have never seen it) or when only belongs to the subject (Only Sarah knew the answer).

Dialogue — the speechwriter's edit

Manager: Here's my opening for Grace's retirement party: "I have never worked with anyone like Grace."

Writer: It's sincere, but it's your first line, so give it some weight: "Never have I worked with anyone quite like Grace."

Manager: Better. And the part about the flood in the archive? "We didn't realize how much she had saved until the water went down."

Writer: "Only when the water went down did we realize how much she had saved." Pause after "down."

Manager: Could I add "So calm was she that nobody panicked"?

Writer: You could, but that would be three inversions in a row. Keep two, and let the rest of the speech breathe.

Manager: So: weight where it counts, plain prose everywhere else.

Writer: Exactly. Then close warmly: "Should you ever need a book found, you know exactly who to call."

Underline each inversion and name its trigger. Which inversion did the writer advise against, and why?

2.3

What Really Happened Was… — Cleft Sentences & Fronting for Cohesion

Class C6
New words — information and flow
given informationnew informationthe focusend-weightcohesionto flowchoppyto spotlighta misconceptionto set the record straighta spokespersonto clarify

given = what the listener already knows or has just heard · end-weight = the tendency to put long, heavy elements at the end of a sentence · to set the record straight = to correct a false account of events, calmly and publicly

Focus and cohesion frames

It was… that / who…It wasn't until… that…What really matters is…What happened was (that)…All I'm saying is…The reason… is that…The thing that… is…Even more… is…It's a pity (that)…
Grammar box
It-cleftIt + be + focus + that / who…It was her attitude that impressed me. (spotlight up front; often "…not something else")
Corrective it-cleftIt was X, not Y, that…It was the timing, not the price, that worried us.
Time frameIt wasn't until… that…It wasn't until later that I realized.
What-cleftWhat + clause + be + focusWhat impressed me was her attitude. (focus saved for the end)
Other framesAll / The thing / The reason / The place / The way / The one who… + beAll I want is a quiet evening. · The reason I left is that I was bored.
Frontingmove a (usually given) element to the front; no inversionWe tried three strategies. The first we dismissed at once.
Extrapositionlight it first, heavy clause lastThat you missed it is a pity. → It's a pity (that) you missed it.

Two principles behind all of these: ① given before new: start from what the listener knows and end on what is newsworthy. ② light before heavy (end-weight): long clauses belong at the end.

Agreement: What I need is a break. · What I need are some answers. · What surprised us was the silence (past). · Formal: The reason is that…, not The reason is because…

Fronting ≠ inversion. That book, I have never read. ✅ · That book have I never read. ❌ (Inversion needs a negative or restrictive trigger.)

Exercise 5 — Which continuation flows better?

Dialogue — setting the record straight

Reporter: So the library closed the Saturday tool-lending service because it was losing money?

Spokesperson: Actually, it wasn't the money that closed it. It was space: we needed the room for the new study center.

Reporter: But people say the tools were never returned.

Spokesperson: That's a common misconception. What the figures show is a return rate of ninety-six percent.

Reporter: Then why the sudden decision?

Spokesperson: It wasn't sudden. It was in March that we first consulted members. The reason it felt sudden is that the notice went up late. That part, I fully accept.

Reporter: So what happens to the tools?

Spokesperson: All we're doing is moving them. The service reopens at the community center in June.

Find three it-clefts, two what / all-clefts, one the reason-frame and one example of fronting. Which cleft is corrective?

Speaking — The Editing Desk & Setting the Record Straight

The Editing Desk: your group edits a choppy article so that it flows. Use at least one it-cleft, one what-cleft (or another frame), and two instances of fronting or extraposition, but no more than three clefts in total. Here is article A:

Article A · The four-day week at Tallis Print Works

Tallis Print Works is a small printing company in Riverside. It has 40 employees. It moved to a four-day week last year. Many people expected productivity to fall. Productivity did not fall. It rose by eight percent. Better scheduling caused the rise. The shorter week did not cause it on its own. The staff now plan every job on Monday morning. Sick days have also fallen. Nobody predicted that. The owner, Ruth Tallis, says the change was a risk. She is glad she took it.

Setting the Record Straight: Partner A reads a misconception from a card. Partner B, the spokesperson, corrects it politely with an it-cleft and a what-cleft, then adds one fact. Switch roles.

☐ It wasn't X that…, it was Y. · ☐ What actually happened was… · ☐ The reason… is that… · ☐ All we did was… · ☐ I stressed the focus word
