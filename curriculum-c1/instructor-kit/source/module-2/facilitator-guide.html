Module 2 — Conditionals, Inversion & Emphasis

Three classes about where the weight of a sentence falls. Learners already know the four conditionals, a few negative inversions and the basic cleft from B2; here those become deliberate choices. First, mixed conditionals and the precise alternatives to if (provided that, as long as, unless, in case, even if, but for, if it hadn't been for, should, were to) through a business case-study panel. Then inversion for emphasis and formality (Never before have we… · Had we known… · So great was…) through a speechwriting task, with as much attention to when not to invert as to the form. Last, cleft sentences, fronting and end-weight for cohesion, through an editing desk and a spoken "setting the record straight" activity.

3 lessons Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C4–C6 Weeks 2–3 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a demanding communicative task) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-conditionals-emphasis.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C4. It holds the flashcards (alternatives to if, precaution pictures, inversion triggers, cleft frames), the new identity cards, the Case Study Panel files and board-question cards, the Speechwriter briefs and WEIGHT / POMP cards, the Editing Desk articles and Record Straight A/B cards, The Emphasis Track board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and shadow the stress and pausing.
  • Refinement, not first meetings. At B2 learners met all four conditionals, unless / in case / provided that, Never have I… / Not only… and basic clefts (B2 Module 4.2). Check those in two minutes in each lead-in and spend the class on what is new at C1: time-crossing conditionals, the nuance of each alternative (in case ≠ if, unless ≠ if not in every case), the full inversion system and its register, and information structure (given → new, end-weight, extraposition).
  • Feedback on range, accuracy and appropriacy. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: accuracy (errors to fix), range (ambitious language to praise and spread) and appropriacy (correct but wrong for the occasion: an inversion in a chatty line, three clefts in a row, but for in small talk). In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column without names. At C1, appropriacy feedback is often the most valuable: "That sentence is perfect grammar. Would you say it at the dinner table?"
  • Peer review: Praise · Probe · Polish. Use one protocol all module. After a presentation, speech or edited text, each reviewer gives one praise (a line that worked and why), one probe (a genuine question: "Why in case there and not if?") and one polish (a single concrete improvement). No more than one of each, so feedback stays focused and nobody is flattened.
  • Dominant speakers. Every task has built-in roles. Give strong, talkative learners the jobs that require listening: the language monitor on the panel (ticks the checklist, speaks only at the end), the editor in the speechwriting pair (must cut, not add), the fact-checker at the editing desk. In board questioning, use a two-question cap per person. Say it warmly: "Great question, Omar. Let's hear from someone on the board who hasn't asked yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is economy and idiom: fixed conditionals (if need be, failing that, barring delays, come what may), rarer inversions (Not once did…, So quickly did it spread that…, as did his father), and text-level information structure (thematic progression across a paragraph). Ask strong learners to justify choices, and to find the line where a plainer structure would be better.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and forms (analyse, organised, apologise, realise, practising, theatre, flavour, judgement, memorise, topicalisation, travelled, got for gotten). Teach analyze, organized, apologize, realize, practicing, theater, flavor, judgment, memorize, topicalization, traveled, gotten. Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Weigh the options → add the weight → place the weight. In C4 learners weigh what was, what is and what might have been, and they set terms with exactly the right conjunction: If they had accepted, they'd be twice the size now · provided that the date holds · but for one loyal customer base. In C5 they add weight to single sentences for a formal occasion, and learn to stop before it becomes pomp: Never have I worked with anyone quite like Grace. In C6 they place weight across a paragraph, so that each sentence starts from what the listener knows and ends on what is new: It wasn't the shorter week that caused the rise; it was better scheduling. Every class ends with a real product that other learners hear and respond to: a panel analysis, a delivered speech, an edited article and a spoken correction.

Trauma-informed: invented cases, chosen distance

This module invites learners to talk about regret, turning points and "what might have been." For many adult learners, the biggest what-ifs in their lives involve leaving a country, a lost career, illness or family separation. Never ask learners to analyze why they left their home country, or what their life would be like if they hadn't. All class analysis uses the invented companies on the case cards (Harbor Fresh, Kestrel Software, Maple & Stone, Northgate Textiles) or the new identity cards. In the journal and the drill, offer three choices out loud every time: your real life, a new identity card, or a company or something invented. The online lesson's project mentions "a historical event (a treaty…)": in class, steer this toward discoveries and inventions (penicillin, the sticky note, the bicycle), never wars, political treaties or disasters. The speeches in C5 are tributes, awards, a science fair and a safety briefing; no political or religious speeches. The misconceptions in C6 concern invented libraries, cafés, bus routes and pools; learners correct a card, never a claim about a real person or group. Opinions in the board questioning are assigned by card.

  • Print the flashcards (one set for the board): the alternatives-to-if cards, the six precaution picture cards and the but for cards (C4); the inversion-trigger cards and the Had / Were / Should cards (C5); the cleft-frame and fronting cards (C6). Card stock helps. Cut them before C4.
  • For C4: the Case Study Panel files (4 cases × A "The story" + B "The numbers & hinge factors") and the board-question cards. One case per group of 3–4. With more than four groups, two groups can take the same case and present to each other.
  • For C5: the four Speechwriter briefs (one per pair; duplicates are fine) and one WEIGHT / POMP card per learner. A lectern, a music stand or even a chair turned around makes "the podium" feel real.
  • For C6: the Editing Desk articles B and C (article A is in the workbook) and the Record Straight A/B cards (6 pairs). The Emphasis Track board game (1 board per group, a die, counters) is for early finishers in C6 and the C7 warmer.
  • Print the new identity cards and leave them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking or explaining.
  • Optional: bring one real printed example of each structure from light, non-political sources: a customer-service letter ("Should you require…"), a product review with an inverted opening, a company newsletter with a cleft ("What makes this year different is…"). Learners enjoy finding them in the wild.

2.1 · C4

Mixed Conditionals & Alternatives to "if"

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • build a mixed conditional in either direction by diagnosing the time of each clause separately: If we had accepted, we'd be bigger now (past → present) · If she weren't so cautious, she would have invested (present → past)
  • vary the modal and the if-clause for nuance: might / could have in the result, tentative if + should, formal if + were to, continuous forms (If you'd been listening…)
  • choose the precise alternative to if, and keep in case for precautions taken in advance
  • isolate a decisive factor with but for / if it weren't for / if it hadn't been for + a noun, matching the time frame
  • present a short, structured analysis of a business decision and respond to hypothetical questions from a panel
Materials

Left: two time logics. ⏪ condition → 🟢 now: If we had accepted the offer, we'd be twice the size now. · 🟢 always → ⏪ then: If she weren't so cautious, she would have invested years ago. · Ask: When is the condition? When is the result?

Center: shades. If it fails… (real) · If it should fail… (unlikely) · If it were to fail… (formal hypothesis). Alternatives: provided that · as long as · on condition that · unless · supposing · in case (precaution!) · otherwise · even if.

Right: the decisive factor (+ NOUN). But for your help, I'd have failed. · If it weren't for the traffic (now), … · If it hadn't been for one witness (then), … · ✘ But for you helped. Phrase bank: In hindsight… · the turning point was… · the hinge factor was…

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: mixed conditionals. The condition and the result sit in different times because they genuinely do. Past → present: a decision not taken then explains a situation now. Present → past: a permanent trait or general situation explains a one-off past event.

"If we had accepted the offer, we'd be twice the size now." Did we accept? (No.) When was the decision? (In the past.) Are we twice the size? (No.) When is "twice the size"? (Now.) · "If she weren't so cautious, she would have invested years ago." Is she cautious? (Yes, she is; it's her character.) Did she invest? (No.) Is "cautious" about one moment or always? (Always.) So why weren't and not hadn't been? (Because she is still cautious now.)

Meaning: the three shades.

"If the merger fails, we'll cut costs." Is this a real possibility? (Yes, we're planning for it.) · "If the merger should fail…" More likely or less likely? (Less: unlikely, but we're considering it.) · "If the merger were to fail…" Where would you hear this? (A boardroom, a formal report: it's a hypothesis.) Does the event change? (No, only the probability and the formality.)

Meaning: the alternatives.

"I'll take an umbrella in case it rains." When do I take it? (Now, before.) Is it raining? (No, it might rain later.) · "I'll take an umbrella if it rains." When do I take it? (Only when it rains.) · "We'll leave at six unless you object." If you don't object? (We leave at six.) Can I say "except if"? (Yes: so unless is safe.) · "I'd be amazed unless she won"? Can I say "except if she won"? (No, so it's wrong: "if she didn't win.") · "You can borrow it provided that you return it by Monday." Is this a free choice or a condition? (A condition, and a strict one.) · "Even if it rains, we'll go." Does the rain change anything? (No.)

Meaning: but for.

"But for your help, I would have failed." Did you help? (Yes.) Did I fail? (No.) What made the difference? (Your help: one factor.) · "If it weren't for the traffic, the commute would be pleasant." Is there traffic now? (Yes.) Is the commute pleasant? (No.) · What comes after but for: a noun or a clause? (A noun: "your help," not "you helped.")

Form.

Past → presentif + past perfect, would / could / might + baseIf I had taken that job, I'd be living in Berlin now.
Present → pastif + past simple / were, would / could / might + have + ppIf I spoke Japanese, that meeting would have gone differently.
Tentativeif + should + base (→ Should you…)If you should see him, pass on my thanks.
Formal hypothesisif + were to + base (→ Were…to)If the deal were to collapse, jobs would be lost.
Alternativesprovided / providing (that) · as / so long as · on condition that · unless · supposing · in case · otherwise · even if+ present tense for real future conditions (no will)
Decisive factorbut for / if it weren't for / if it hadn't been for + nounIf it hadn't been for the grant, we would have closed.

Pronunciation. Contractions carry the naturalness: If I'd known /aɪd ˈnoʊn/, I'd be…, would have → /wʊdəv/ ("would've," never written "would of"), wouldn't have → /ˈwʊdntəv/. In mixed conditionals, stress the time words that justify the tense: "I'd be a doctor now" · "she would have invested years ago." Provided is /prəˈvaɪdɪd/ (three syllables; learners often drop the /ɪd/). In negotiation, provided that and on condition that take a small pause after them, which signals "here comes the term."

Drill script
  1. Diagnose and say (point at the arrows): T: "I didn't study medicine. I'm not a doctor now." → S: "If I had studied medicine, I'd be a doctor now." T: "He's afraid of flying. He didn't come to the wedding." → S: "If he weren't afraid of flying, he would have come to the wedding." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Now or then? T gives only the half and a time word: T: "If we hadn't missed that flight… now." → S: "…we'd be in Lisbon now." T: "If she were more organized… last week." → S: "…she wouldn't have missed the deadline last week."
  3. Swap the if (hold up an alternative card): T: "We'll sign if the date stays in March. Strict condition." → S: "We'll sign provided that the date stays in March." T: "Take a charger. The battery might die." → S: "Take a charger in case the battery dies." T: "We'll go. It doesn't matter if it rains." → S: "Even if it rains, we'll go."
  4. One factor: T: "You helped. I didn't fail." → S: "But for your help, I would have failed." T: "The traffic is terrible. The commute isn't pleasant." → S: "If it weren't for the traffic, the commute would be pleasant."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 states a fact about an invented company ("Cobalt Cycles took the Swift contract."); S2 turns it into a mixed conditional; S3 adds but for or an alternative. Then all pairs, 1 minute, using the case headlines on slide 8.
The Case Study Panel — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4 are consultants. Each group gets one invented case: Harbor Fresh (grocery delivery), Kestrel Software (clinic scheduling app), Maple & Stone (family furniture maker) or Northgate Textiles (recycled fabrics). Learner A holds "The story" (what happened, in order); learner B holds "The numbers & hinge factors" (costs, character traits, the lucky break, the offer now on the table). No showing cards.
  • Share (3 min): A and B tell the group their halves in ordinary spoken English. Others take notes on the workbook frame.
  • Prepare (6 min): the group plans a three-minute analysis for the board: what happened, the hinge factor, a past → present conditional, a present → past conditional (from a character trait), but for / if it hadn't been for, a recommendation with terms (provided that, as long as, unless, in case) and a projection with were to or should. Everybody speaks at least once.
  • Present and question (4 + 4 min per round): the group presents to another group (the board). The board draws two board-question cards ("Supposing the co-op raised its prices?") or asks its own, beginning Supposing… or What if…. The consultants answer on the spot. Then the groups swap roles.
  • Roles: lead analyst (opens and closes) · numbers expert (holds card B; handles fact questions) · strategist (the recommendation and terms) · language monitor (ticks the checklist during the presentation and gives one Praise · Probe · Polish at the end). Give the monitor role to a dominant speaker.
  • Say before you start: "These companies are invented. You're analyzing them, not judging anyone's real life."

Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C1 learners speak far more fluently than they write, or read slowly in the Latin script. Give a two-arrow sentence strip ("If ___ had ___, ___ would ___ now. · If ___ weren't ___, ___ would have ___.") and let them build their two mixed conditionals from it. Read Card A aloud to them before the share stage, or pair them as the numbers expert with a partner who reads the story. Workbook: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 3 items 1–6, Ex 4 items 1–3. Target: two correct mixed conditionals and one correct in case, said aloud in the panel.

Stretch (toward C2)

Add fixed conditionals: if need be · if so / if not · failing that · barring (further) delays · come what may · assuming (that). Ask for a continuous mixed conditional ("If they'd been listening to their customers, they'd be selling online by now"). In the panel, the stretch learner must use Were it not for or Had it not been for once (previewing 2.2) and answer one board question with a counter-hypothesis: "Even if that were the case, …". Early finishers rewrite the dialogue as formal written minutes and compare the registers.

L1 watch-outs
  • in case as a false friend: Spanish en caso de que, Italian nel caso in cui, Portuguese caso, German im Fall, dass / falls and French au cas où all work as plain conditions ("if"), so learners say "Call me in case you need help" meaning "if." Use the umbrella test every time: before or when?
  • would in the if-clause: "If I would have known…" is common from German and Dutch speakers (wenn ich … hätte / würde) and appears in informal American speech too. Say: "It's heard, but it isn't written. Keep would for the result."
  • One conditional form for all times: Russian, Polish and Ukrainian use one particle (by) for present and past unreal, and Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian mark time with adverbs, not tense. Mixed conditionals can feel arbitrary. Always anchor with a time word (now, years ago, last week) and draw the arrows.
  • unless overuse: learners who learned "unless = if not" produce "I'd be surprised unless…". Spanish a menos que and French à moins que have the same limit, so point out the parallel: "except if" must fit.
  • Arabic speakers often have a helpful distinction already (ʾidhā for real conditions, law for unreal ones): use it. "Law = the unreal ones today."
  • But for + clause: "But for you helped me" is common for everyone; Spanish/Portuguese si no fuera por + noun is a good bridge: "It's the same: por + noun."

2.2 · C5

Inversion for Emphasis & Style

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • invert auxiliary and subject after a fronted negative or restrictive adverbial (Never, Rarely, Little, Not only, Not until, No sooner, Hardly, Only then / by / when, Under no circumstances, At no time, Nor), adding do / does / did and a bare verb when there is no auxiliary
  • use conditional inversion with Had, Were, Should, keeping negatives uncontracted (Had it not been for…)
  • use So + adjective + be + subject and Such + be + subject with a that-clause of result, and recognize place inversion (In the doorway stood…)
  • avoid false inversion: a mid-position negative, Only + subject, a fronted ordinary topic, a pronoun after Here / There
  • judge register and density: where inversion adds weight, where it sounds pompous, and how many a paragraph can carry
Materials
  • Workbook 2.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Speechwriter frame)
  • Slides 9–12 · Inversion-trigger flashcards · Had / Were / Should cards · Speechwriter briefs (4) · WEIGHT / POMP cards (1 per learner) · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 2.2-A (Volunteer of the Year acceptance speech) and 2.2-B (dictation)

Left: the mechanism. I had never seen… → Never had I seen… · I never imagined… → Never did I imagine… (imagined) · 1 front the trigger → 2 auxiliary + subject → 3 no auxiliary? do / does / did + bare verb.

Center: the triggers. Never (before) · Rarely · Seldom · Little · Not only… but also · Not until… · No sooner… than · Hardly… when · Only then / by / when · Under no circumstances · At no time · Nor. If → inversion: Had I known · Were she to accept · Should you require · Had it not been for (Hadn't it). So great was the demand that… · Such was his anger that…

Right: weight or pomp? ✔ speeches · tributes · reviews · warnings · formal letters. ✘ texts · small talk · quick emails. Density rule: one well-placed inversion beats three. No inversion: I have never… · Only Sarah knew · Here it comes · That book, I've never read.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: inversion adds weight, not content. Fronting a negative or restrictive adverbial puts the emphasis first and signals a heightened, formal register. The propositional meaning does not change. The choice is whether to front at all; once fronted, inversion is obligatory.

"Never have I seen such a turnout." Does this mean something different from "I have never seen such a turnout"? (No, the same fact.) What's different? (It's more emphatic, more formal.) Where would you hear it? (A speech, a toast, a review.) · "Never I have seen…" Is this possible? (No: once you front Never, you must invert.) · "I have never seen it." Is there inversion here? (No: never isn't at the front.)
"Only when the water went down did we realize how much she had saved." Which clause inverts: "when the water went down" or "we realize"? (The main clause: did we realize.) · "Only Sarah knew the password." Inversion? (No: Only Sarah is the subject, not an adverbial.) · "Not only did she resign, but she also exposed the fraud." How many clauses invert? (Only the first.)

Meaning: conditional inversion.

"Had I known, I would have called." Did I know? (No.) Is this past or present? (Past.) Formal or neutral? (More formal than "If I'd known.") · "Should you require assistance, please ask." Do they expect you to need help? (Not really: it's polite and tentative.) Where do you see this? (Hotels, formal letters, customer service.) · "Hadn't I known…" Is this a condition? (No, it's a question! Say "Had I not known.")

Meaning: register and density.

"Never have I been so bored!" to a friend over coffee: natural or theatrical? (Theatrical: fine only as a joke.) · A paragraph with four inversions: more emphatic or less? (Less: they cancel each other out and sound pompous.) · How many in a one-minute speech? (Two or three, well placed.)

Form.

With an auxiliarytrigger + auxiliary + subject + restRarely have we faced such a choice.
Without an auxiliarytrigger + do / does / did + subject + bare verbLittle did he know. (knew)
Only / Not until + clausethe main clause invertsNot until the review ended did the truth emerge.
ConditionalHad / Were / Should + subject (no if) · negative: + subject + notHad it not been for the fog, we'd have arrived on time.
So / SuchSo + adj + be + subject + that · Such + be + subject + thatSo convincing were the results that critics fell silent.
Placeadverbial of place + verb + noun subject (not a pronoun)Down the road came the parade. · Here it comes.

Pronunciation. An inverted sentence is delivered, not rushed. Stress the trigger and pause lightly after the fronted phrase when it is long: "Only when the water went down | did we realize…". Keep the auxiliary weak (/dɪd wi/, /həv aɪ/) and let the stress fall on the main verb and the key noun: "Never have I seen such a turnout." In Had it not been for, not is stressed and never contracted. For the podium: slower than conversation, a falling tone at the end of each inverted sentence, and a real pause before the next one so it lands.

Drill script
  1. Front and flip (hold up a trigger card): T: "I have never seen such generosity. Never." → S: "Never have I seen such generosity." T: "She rarely admits a mistake. Rarely." → S: "Rarely does she admit a mistake." T: "He little suspected the truth. Little." → S: "Little did he suspect the truth." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Which clause? T: "We realized the cost only then." → S: "Only then did we realize the cost." T: "The truth emerged only after the audit." → S: "Only after the audit did the truth emerge."
  3. Drop the if (hold up Had / Were / Should): T: "If I had known…" → S: "Had I known…" T: "If you should need help…" → S: "Should you need help…" T: "If it hadn't been for the guide…" → S: "Had it not been for the guide…" (insist on not after the subject).
  4. Turn up the volume: T: "The demand was very great. The servers crashed." → S: "So great was the demand that the servers crashed." T: "His anger was enormous. Nobody spoke." → S: "Such was his anger that nobody spoke."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 gives a plain sentence about an invented event ("We had never had so many volunteers."); S2 delivers it "from the podium" with an inversion; S3 says WEIGHT or POMP and why. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Speechwriter — how to run it
  • Pairs (or threes) are a speechwriting team. Each gets a brief: the occasion, the speaker, the audience, and a plain, flat draft of about 120 words. Brief 1: a tribute to Walter Chen, retiring after 28 years as head custodian of Riverside High. Brief 2: the CEO of Kestrel Software apologizing to customers after a six-hour outage. Brief 3: the opening address at the tenth Riverside Community Science Fair. Brief 4: a safety briefing for new volunteers at the Harborview food pantry warehouse.
  • Rewrite (10 min): the team rewrites the draft for the occasion on the workbook frame. Requirements: 3–5 inversions in total, at least three different triggers, one conditional inversion, one so / such. The underlined lines on the brief are good candidates, but teams choose.
  • The editor's cut: before the podium, the editor must cut (or un-invert) one inversion that is doing no work and explain why to the partner. This is the heart of the lesson: judgment, not decoration.
  • Podium (10 min): one learner per team delivers the strongest paragraph (60–90 seconds) standing, from a lectern or a turned chair. After each, the audience holds up WEIGHT or POMP, and one named reviewer gives Praise · Probe · Polish. Keep a brisk pace: the whole round should take about 90 seconds per team.
  • Roles: writer (drafts), editor (checks the auxiliary and the bare verb, makes the cut), speaker (delivers). In a pair, the writer speaks and the editor edits. A dominant speaker is the editor.
  • Anxiety: nobody has to speak from the podium alone if it causes real distress. A pair may deliver alternately, one sentence each, or the learner may read seated. Offer this privately.
ICQs before the Speechwriter
How many inversions: as many as possible? (No, three to five.) · How many different triggers? (At least three.) · Must you have a Had / Were / Should sentence? (Yes, one.) · What does the editor do before the podium? (Cuts one inversion.) · How long is the paragraph you deliver? (60–90 seconds.) · What does the audience hold up? (WEIGHT or POMP.)

Literacy support

In the speechwriter task, the learner rewrites three underlined lines with the partner's help and delivers those three sentences, practiced once with you. Workbook: Ex 2 items 1, 2, 4, 6; Ex 3 items 1, 2, 4; Ex 4 items 1, 2, 6. Target: three correct inversions, delivered aloud with the stress on the trigger.

Stretch (toward C2)

Add rarer patterns: Not once did…, So quickly did the news spread that… (so + adverb), Nowhere is this more evident than…, Only later was it discovered that… (passive), comparative inversion (She worked hard, as did her colleagues · more than did any other team) and fixed forms (Suffice it to say… · Be that as it may…). In the speech, the stretch learner must achieve the effect with two inversions only and explain the placement. Early finishers write the same message twice, once as a formal statement and once as a friendly text, and annotate every difference.

L1 watch-outs
  • Verb-second transfer: German, Dutch, Swedish, Norwegian and Danish invert the main verb after any fronted element (Nie habe ich… / Gestern ging ich…). Expect "Never saw I such a thing" (no do) and over-inversion after ordinary adverbials: "Yesterday went I…", "That book have I never read." Contrast: English inverts only after negative / restrictive triggers, and only the auxiliary moves.
  • Free subject position: Spanish, Portuguese and Italian allow the subject after the verb (Nunca he visto yo…), so learners produce "Never have seen I…" or forget the subject. French often keeps normal order after jamais. Drill the auxiliary + subject pair as one chunk: have I · did we · was the.
  • No do-support in L1: Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers often skip do / does / did ("Rarely she admits") or double-mark tense ("Little did I knew"). Treat it as a question: "How do you ask? Did you know? Same shape."
  • Contracted negative conditionals ("Hadn't I known…") are universal. Write the question mark on the board: "Hadn't I known?"
  • Register transfer: learners from languages or schools where formal style is highly valued (for example, Arabic, Persian, Russian or French academic traditions) may enjoy inversion so much that they overuse it. Praise the ambition, then apply the density rule.

Cleft Sentences & Fronting for Cohesion

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use an it-cleft to spotlight one element, to contrast, and to correct politely (It was the timing, not the price, that worried us · It wasn't until Monday that…)
  • use a what-cleft and related frames (All / The thing / The reason / The way / The one who… + be) to save the focus for the end, with correct agreement and tense of be
  • front a given element to link a sentence back to the previous one, without inverting
  • apply given before new and end-weight, including extraposition with anticipatory it (It's a pity that…)
  • edit a choppy text into a cohesive one, and use clefts with restraint

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: it-clefts. The it-cleft lifts one element into the spotlight and backgrounds the rest. It often carries "…and not something else," which makes it the natural frame for polite correction.

"It was her attitude that impressed me." What impressed me? (Her attitude.) Is there a hint of contrast? (Yes: not her CV, not her experience.) · "Actually, it was the timing that worried us, not the price." What did the other person think? (That the price worried us.) Is the speaker rude? (No, it's a calm correction.) · "It wasn't until Monday that we noticed." Did we notice on Friday? (No.) When? (On Monday, later than expected.)

Meaning: what-clefts and frames. The what-cleft holds back the focus and delivers it at the end, which builds a little anticipation. Frames name the aspect: the reason, the thing, the way, the person.

"What we need right now is a clear explanation." Where is the important part: the start or the end? (The end.) Why use this and not "We need a clear explanation"? (It prepares the listener: something important is coming.) · "All I want is a quiet evening." A lot or a little? (A little: all minimizes.) · "The reason I called is because…" Is this OK in a report? (Not in formal writing: "The reason… is that…")

Meaning: fronting and end-weight.

"We tried three strategies. The first we dismissed at once." Is "the first" new or already known? (Known: we just heard "three strategies.") Why put it first? (It links back.) What's the new part? (Dismissed at once.) · "That you missed it is a pity." Natural? (Top-heavy.) Better? ("It's a pity (that) you missed it.") Where does the heavy clause go? (To the end.) · "That book have I never read." Correct? (No: fronting a topic doesn't invert. "That book, I have never read.")

Form.

It-cleftIt + be + focus + that / who + rest (who for people)It was my sister who suggested it.
Negative timeIt wasn't until + time / clause + that…It wasn't until later that I realized.
What-cleftWhat + clause + be + focus (be agrees with the focus and matches the time)What surprised us was the silence.
FramesAll / The thing / The reason / The way / The place / The one who + clause + beThe way she handled it was masterful.
Frontinggiven element first, then normal order (no inversion without a negative trigger)Of the three options, the first seems best.
ExtrapositionIt + be / verb + … + heavy clause at the endIt's unclear whether they'll agree.

Pronunciation. The grammar and the voice must agree: the focus carries the main stress. It-cleft: "It was her attitude that impressed me" (the rest is low and quick). Corrective: contrastive stress on both items: "It was the timing, not the price." What-cleft: a slight rise and a small pause after was / is, then a strong fall on the focus: "What we need ↗ | is time ↘." Fronted topics get their own tone unit: "The first ↗ | we dismissed at once ↘."

Drill script
  1. Spotlight it: T: "Maria fixed the printer. Spotlight Maria." → S: "It was Maria who fixed the printer." T: "Spotlight the printer." → S: "It was the printer that Maria fixed." T: "We met in Lisbon. Spotlight the place." → S: "It was in Lisbon that we met."
  2. Save it for the end: T: "I need a break." → S: "What I need is a break." T: "The engine overheated." → S: "What happened was (that) the engine overheated." T: "I want a quiet evening, nothing more." → S: "All I want is a quiet evening."
  3. Correct me (T states a wrong assumption; S corrects with contrastive stress): T: "So the price put customers off?" → S: "No, it was the parking that put them off, not the price." T: "You found the error on Friday?" → S: "No, it wasn't until Monday that we found it."
  4. Lighten the front: T: "That she said nothing surprised me." → S: "It surprised me that she said nothing." T: "To argue with him is pointless." → S: "It's pointless to argue with him."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 says a plain fact about an invented workplace; S2 asks a wrong-assumption question about it; S1 corrects it with a cleft. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Editing Desk & Setting the Record Straight — how to run them
  • Editing Desk (groups of 3, 10 min): each group gets one choppy article of 11–14 short sentences: A Tallis Print Works' four-day week (in the workbook), B nap pods at Eastgate Distribution Center, C the Greenway Middle School garden. The brief: every fact stays; use at least one it-cleft, one what-cleft (or another frame) and two instances of fronting or extraposition; no more than three clefts in total; check each change against given → new. Roles: writer, fact-checker (ticks each fact as it survives), reader (reads each new sentence aloud; if it sounds staged, the group reconsiders).
  • Read-out (5 min): two groups read their edited articles aloud. The class listens for flow and names one sentence that "clicked." One Praise · Probe · Polish each. Model versions are in the answer key.
  • Setting the Record Straight (pairs, 9 min): A reads the misconception from a "What people think" card as a journalist or a neighbor would say it ("So the pool is closing because it's unsafe?"). B, the spokesperson, holds the matching "What actually happened" card and corrects it politely: a corrective it-cleft, a what-cleft, one extra fact. Swap after 90 seconds and take a new card pair.
  • Tone matters: the goal is calm, courteous correction, not "winning." Model it: "I can see why people think that. Actually, it wasn't… What really happened was…". Every card is about an invented organization; no learner corrects a claim about a real person.
  • Early finishers play The Emphasis Track board game, which recycles the whole module.

L1 watch-outs
  • Missing dummy it: Spanish, Portuguese and Italian (Es importante que… · É uma pena que…) and Arabic often produce "Is a pity that you missed it" / "Is unclear whether…". Point at the empty subject slot: "English needs it."
  • Topic-comment transfer: Chinese, Japanese and Korean put the topic first very naturally, which helps with fronting, but learners often add a resumptive pronoun ("This problem, we must solve it"). Cross out the it.
  • Cleft overuse: French uses c'est… qui / que far more than English does, and learners may cleft every other sentence. Praise accuracy, then apply the restraint rule.
  • Frozen is and agreement: "What surprised us is the silence" (for a past event) and "What we need is answers" occur for all L1s. Accept plural focus with is in speech; ask for agreement in writing.
  • "The reason is because" is universal (and common in native speech). Correct it in writing only.
  • Free word order languages (Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, Czech) put new information last simply by moving words, so learners may produce "The engineering nobody mentioned" without a comma or pause, which can sound abrupt. Teach the pause and the fronted topic's own intonation.
