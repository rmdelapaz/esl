Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 1 — Precision in Time & Modality

Three classes that open the C1 level. Learners already control the future perfect and future continuous (B2 5.1), past deduction with must / can't / might have (B2 2.2), wish, would rather, it's time (B2 1.3) and suggest that + base verb (B2 3.3). The C1 work is precision and register: placing an event exactly on a timeline (will have been running · was to have launched · is on the verge of), setting certainty on a dial rather than a switch (may well · could conceivably · is bound to · can't possibly have), and using the subjunctive and other unreal forms to shape formal recommendations (It is essential that he be present · Were we to delay…). Class 1 also welcomes a new group and gives you a B2 diagnostic with a short speaking and writing sample.

3 lessons Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a demanding communicative or writing task) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-1-time-modality.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the grammar flashcards, the welcome question cards, the B2 diagnostic grid and writing-sample rubric, the new identity cards, the Project Briefing cards and stakeholder question cards, the Evidence Ladder case files, clue cards and ladder sheet, the Policy Memo issue briefs, memo frame and peer-review checklist, the Precision Board game and all the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. Nothing in this module is completely new to a learner who finished B2. Don't re-teach the future perfect or must have. Check them fast in each lead-in and spend the time on what is new at C1: the future perfect continuous used precisely, the future in the past and "on the brink" forms (was to have, is to, is about to, is on the verge of), the fine grain of certainty and the hidden jobs of will and would, and the mandative subjunctive as a register tool.
  • Feedback in three columns. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: range (ambitious, precise language to praise and spread), accuracy (errors to fix) and appropriacy (right form, wrong register or tone: a future perfect in casual chat, "It is imperative that you pass the salt," may as well where may well was meant). At C1, appropriacy is often the most useful column. In the feedback stage, board 2 errors, 1 appropriacy point and 1–2 range examples, without names.
  • Dominant speakers. C1 groups often contain one or two confident, fast speakers (frequently professionals used to leading meetings). All three freer tasks have built-in roles. Give the strongest speaker the job that needs listening: the timeline keeper in the briefing, the ladder keeper in the case files, the second reader in the peer review. Use a talking-turn token in group discussion. Say it kindly: "That's a sharp point, Daniel. Let's hear how the others read the evidence before we decide."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction in this module is economy and stance: one exact form instead of a paraphrase (was to prove decisive), modal and adverb collocations that signal a precise stance (may yet, could conceivably, in all likelihood), and conscious register choices (why the subjunctive here and should there). Ask strong learners to justify each choice, not only produce the form.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (realized, inquiries, behavior, analyze, organization, centerpiece, mail carrier, package). Learners who studied British English may write realised, enquiries, behaviour, postman, parcel: accept those forms. For the subjunctive this matters: the base form (that he be) is the normal American choice; that he should be is the typical British one, and Lesson 1.3 teaches both as valid.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Place it → weigh it → shape it. In C1 learners place events precisely in time, delivering a project briefing that says what will be in progress, what will have been completed and what nearly happened (By the launch we'll have been testing for six months · We were to have gone live in March · The supplier is on the verge of signing). In C2 they weigh evidence, moving hypotheses up and down an evidence ladder as clues arrive (It can't possibly have been the cleaners · The update may well be clashing with the backup · It's bound to happen again on Thursday). In C3 they shape an outcome, writing and peer-reviewing a formal recommendation (It is essential that every new hire be assigned a mentor · Were we to delay, …). Every class ends with a real product that the group hears or reads: a briefing, a verdict, a memo.

Trauma-informed: projects, puzzles and policies, not personal futures

The online lessons invite very personal content: "where you will be in five years," "a plan you once had that didn't unfold," "a decision facing you." For some adults (newcomers with uncertain status, refugees, people who have lost jobs or family), questions about the future and about plans that fell through can be painful. In class, anchor every task in a project, an organization or an invented persona. The Project Briefing uses invented projects; the Evidence Ladder uses invented workplace and community puzzles, never crimes, accidents, illness or real people; the Policy Memo uses invented workplaces. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card, or something invented. Positions in the memo task are assigned: learners argue the brief, not themselves. The online lesson 1.3 lists "God save the King / Queen" among the fixed subjunctive phrases: it is fine to recognize, but don't build tasks around it; use be that as it may, suffice it to say, if need be, come what may instead.

1.1 · C1

Welcome to C1 · Advanced Aspect — The Future in Detail

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • get to know the group and show the teacher their B2 range, accuracy and sense of register in a friendly mingle and a short piece of writing
  • place an event exactly relative to a future point: in progress (will be doing), completed by (will have done), ongoing up to (will have been doing), anchored with by, by then, by the time
  • use the future continuous as a tactful inquiry (Will you be needing the room?) and as a neutral "in the course of things" future (I'll be seeing her anyway)
  • describe a future seen from the past with was going to, would, was about to, was to / was to have + pp
  • choose the precise formal or imminent future: be to (official arrangement, instruction, "if we are to…"), be about to, be on the verge / point of, be due to, be set to

  • Workbook 1.1 (Welcome box, writing sample, New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Project Briefing planner)
  • Slides 1–8 · name tents · Welcome question cards (Set B) · B2 diagnostic grid + writing-sample rubric (your copies) · future-form flashcards and picture cards (Set A) · Project Briefing cards + stakeholder question cards (Set D) · New identity cards (Set C)
  • Listening scripts 1.1-A (Harbor Point stand-up) and 1.1-B (dictation)

Left: a timeline. NOW ———— ★ FIXED POINT (by the launch / by June / this time next week) ————. At ★: will be doing (in progress). Before ★ with an arrow ending at it: will have done (completed) · will have been doing (duration up to ★). Rule under it: By the time we launch (present!), we'll have…

Center: the past looking forward. A second timeline, THEN ———— NOW. From THEN, an arrow forward: was going to (plan) · would (narrative) · was about to (imminent) · was to have + pp (planned, didn't happen). Model: "We were to have gone live in March, but the supplier was about to go out of business."

Right: formal and imminent. The mayor is to open the site on May 12. (official) · You are to report to reception. (instruction) · If we are to meet the deadline, … (condition) · We're about to sign. (any minute; no time phrase) · on the verge of signing · due to arrive at 3 (timetable) · set to expand (news). Tact: Will you be using the car?

If you run long

The welcome mingle is the most important part of C1: the group needs it, and you need the diagnostic. Don't cut it. If time is short, the writing sample becomes homework (same prompt, same 120-word limit, "no dictionary, 10 minutes, please"), Ex 4 moves to the C2 warmer, and the class-wide briefing in the freer task is dropped. Keep at least 12 minutes for the Project Briefing.

Running the diagnostic — what to listen for

The mingle is a friendly first conversation, not an exam. Learners must never feel watched. Hold the grid on a clipboard, stand near a pair for 30–60 seconds, and join in sometimes ("And what would you have done if…?"). Mark each learner ✓ (confident and accurate) · ~ (communicates well, some errors or gaps) · ✗ (avoids or can't produce it yet) in four columns:

  • RANGE: Does the learner reach beyond safe B1 language? Listen for the narrative tenses including had been + -ing (LOOK BACK cards), third and mixed conditionals (WHAT IF), passives and reporting verbs, linkers such as whereas, despite, as a result, and precise vocabulary and collocation.
  • ACCURACY: Are errors systematic or slips the learner self-corrects? Typical B2 fossils: "If I would have known," "I am agree," "suggested me to," "When I arrived, the meeting already started," missing articles, "people is."
  • APPROPRIACY (the SAY IT TWO WAYS cards): Does the learner shift register between a friend and a manager? Softening (I was wondering if…, Would it be possible…), hedging, indirect questions, tone. Many strong B2 speakers are accurate but register-flat: that is exactly what C1 teaches.
  • FLUENCY & INTERACTION: long turns (a minute or more), follow-up questions, turn-taking, paraphrasing around a missing word, recovering from a false start.

The writing sample. Score it with the four-row rubric in the materials (Set B): task & register, range, accuracy, organization & cohesion, 0–3 each. As a rough guide: 9–12 = comfortable at C1 (give Stretch tasks); 6–8 = right level, typical C1 entry profile; 5 or less and mostly ✗ in the speaking grid = check the placement privately (the toolkit's oral interview takes 5 minutes); the learner may do better in the B2 course or with extra support from Day 1. A learner with strong speaking and a weak sample (or the reverse) is common; note it and target the weaker skill in feedback.

What to do with the results. Write one short, private, encouraging note to each learner by C3: one strength and one focus ("You shift register naturally. Focus: articles with abstract nouns."). Never share the grid with learners. Keep it; compare it with Progress Test 1 in C13.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: three vantage points. Stand at a future moment and look: what is happening then (future continuous), what is finished by then (future perfect), how long something has been going on by then (future perfect continuous).

"This time tomorrow, I'll be flying over the Atlantic." At that moment, is the flight finished? (No, in progress.) · "By the end of the year, we'll have completed the merger." Is the merger finished at the end of the year? (Yes.) Exactly when did it finish? (We don't know: some time before.) · "In August, they'll have been running the business for twenty years." Will they stop in August? (No, probably not.) What does the sentence measure? (The duration up to August.)

Meaning: the future continuous as tact.

"Will you use the car tonight?" / "Will you be using the car tonight?" Which one could sound like a request or a challenge? (The first.) Which just asks about the plan? (The second.) Why is it more polite? (No pressure to decide now: it treats the plan as already there.) · "I'll be seeing Tom tomorrow anyway." Did I plan the meeting to tell Tom something? (No, it's happening anyway.)

Meaning: the future in the past.

"We were going to fly, but the strike changed everything." Did we fly? (No.) Was it a plan? (Yes, an earlier plan.) · "She left in 2001; she would never return." Is this conditional? (No.) Is it a prediction or a fact, from today's point of view? (A fact: we know now.) · "The launch was to have taken place in March." Did it take place in March? (No: was to have + pp = planned, didn't happen.)

Meaning: formal and imminent futures.

"The mayor is to open the new library on May 12." Who arranged it? (Officials: it's a formal arrangement.) Where would you read this? (A press release, the news.) · "You are to report to reception at nine." Is this a suggestion? (No, an instruction.) · "If we are to meet the deadline, we need two more testers." Is the deadline certain? (No: if we want to / if we're going to meet it.) · "We're about to sign." Tomorrow? (No, very soon: minutes, hours.) Can I say "We're about to sign next week"? (No: no future time phrase with about to.) · "They're on the verge of signing." Close to it? (Yes, very close.) · "The train is due to arrive at 3:15." Whose plan? (The timetable's.)

Form.

Future continuouswill be + -ingThis time tomorrow I'll be flying. · Will you be joining us?
Future perfectwill have + past participleBy 2030 we'll have opened ten offices.
Future perfect continuouswill have been + -ingBy June I'll have been working here for a decade.
Time clauseby the time / before / when + presentBy the time you arrive, we'll have finished.
Future in the pastwas going to · would · was about to · was to (have + pp)The decision was to have far-reaching consequences. · The launch was to have taken place in March.
Formal / imminentbe to · be about to · be on the verge of + -ing · be due to · be set toThe CEO is to visit on Friday. · The firm is set to expand.

Pronunciation. In fast speech the auxiliaries shrink and the main verb carries the stress: "I'll be flying" /aɪl bi ˈflaɪɪŋ/ · "we'll have finished" /wiːl əv ˈfɪnɪʃt/ (have is /əv/: never write "will of") · "I'll've been working" /aɪləv bɪn ˈwɜrkɪŋ/, with been weak /bɪn/. In a briefing, don't over-reduce: keep a steady pace and stress the time anchor and the result: "By the launch | we'll have tested every screen." Tactful questions have a gentle rise: "Will you be needing the room ↗?"

Drill script
  1. The fixed-point chain (point at ★ on the board): T: "The launch is June 1. The testing: in progress then?" → S: "On June 1 we'll be testing." T: "The design: finished before?" → S: "By June 1 we'll have finished the design." T: "The team started in January. How long?" → S: "By June 1 the team will have been working on it for five months." Choral, then individuals.
  2. Make it tactful: T: "Will you use the projector?" → S: "Will you be using the projector?" T: "Will you need a parking space?" → S: "Will you be needing a parking space?" T: "Will you stay for lunch?" → S: "Will you be staying for lunch?"
  3. Nearly happened: T: "Plan: fly. Result: the strike." → S: "We were going to fly, but the strike changed everything." T: "Official plan: launch in March. Didn't happen." → S: "The launch was to have taken place in March." T: "Sign the contract. Seconds away. Then the phone rang." → S: "I was about to sign the contract when the phone rang."
  4. Headline to briefing: T reads a headline, S expands it: T: "MAYOR TO OPEN LIBRARY MAY 12" → S: "The mayor is to open the library on May 12." T: "TECH FIRM SET TO DOUBLE STAFF" → S: "The firm is set to double its staff." T: "TALKS ON VERGE OF DEAL" → S: "The two sides are on the verge of reaching a deal."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 names a fixed point and a project (real or invented): "The end of this course." S2 gives three sentences: in progress, completed, duration. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Project Briefing — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Each group gets one Project Briefing card: an invented project (a library app, a clinic move, a warehouse sorting system, a community solar garden, a college enrollment system, a bakery's second location), today's date, a fixed "steering meeting" date, and a timeline with finished, ongoing, delayed and imminent items.
  • Plan (5 min): on the workbook planner, the group writes one sentence per row: in progress at the meeting (will be doing), completed by then (will have done), duration (will have been doing … for), a plan that changed (was going to / was to have), imminent (about to / on the verge of / due to), a formal arrangement (is to) and a condition (If we are to…, we need…).
  • Brief (8 min): pair two groups. Group 1 briefs Group 2 in 2 minutes (everyone speaks); Group 2 are the stakeholders and ask 2–3 questions from their stakeholder question cards (each card forces a tactful future continuous: "Will you be needing extra staff in June?"). Swap roles.
  • Roles: the project lead opens and closes ("Thanks for your time. To sum up…"); the timeline keeper holds the card and checks every time phrase (by the time + present!) but speaks only for the one condition sentence; the risks officer covers delays and "on the brink" items. Give the timeline keeper job to a dominant speaker.
  • Class (3 min): one group briefs the whole class. The class listens for the most precise sentence and the most tactful question.
  • Everything is invented. If a learner wants to brief a real project from their job, that's fine, but nobody has to.
ICQs before the Project Briefing
Is this project real? (No, invented.) · How long is the briefing? (Two minutes.) · Who speaks? (Everyone in the group.) · The stakeholders' questions: "Will you use…" or "Will you be using…"? (Will you be using.) · After "by the time," present or future? (Present.) · Does the timeline keeper talk a lot? (No, checks the times and says one sentence.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

At C1, "literacy support" usually means a learner whose speaking is strong but whose reading and writing are slower (often a learner who learned English at work, or whose first script is not Latin). Give the planner with sentence starters already written ("At the steering meeting we'll be…" · "By then we'll have…" · "We were going to…, but…"). Let the learner say each sentence to a partner before writing it. In the writing sample, accept a shorter text (50–70 words) and score it on what is there. Target: three precise future forms, spoken clearly in the briefing.

Stretch (toward C2)

Add passive and modal futures: By then the site will have been handed over · The report should have been completed by Friday · The work is to be carried out overnight. Use narrative would and was to for a biographical sweep: "The prototype was to prove decisive." In the briefing, ask the learner to cut one sentence by replacing a paraphrase with a single precise form ("It is very likely that they will soon sign" → "They're on the verge of signing"). Ask: "Where is a future perfect over-precise? Change one back to will and justify it."

L1 watch-outs
  • Future in the time clause: "By the time I will finish…" · "When the client will arrive…". French (quand je finirai), Italian, Portuguese (future subjunctive quando eu terminar), Russian and Polish all mark the future in the time clause. Point at the by the time clause on the board: "present here, future there."
  • Present for the future: German, Dutch and the Scandinavian languages use the present with a time phrase ("By June I work here ten years"), and so avoid the future perfect continuous. Many learners also use since for a duration ("for ten years" → "since ten years": French depuis, Spanish desde hace, German seit).
  • Dropping the auxiliaries: "By June I will been working…" · "we will finished." Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers mark time with adverbs, not verb forms, and may drop have or been. Color will · have · been differently on the board.
  • be to ≠ have to: learners read "You are to report at nine" as possibility or confuse it with "You have to." Arabic, Turkish and Hindi/Urdu speakers sometimes translate a native "should / must" structure here. CCQ: "Is it an instruction?" (Yes: formal, from someone in charge.)
  • about to + a time phrase: "We're about to sign next month." Common for everyone, especially where the L1 equivalent (Spanish estar a punto de, French être sur le point de) is more flexible. Keep about to for "any moment now."
  • Stative verbs in the continuous: "will have been knowing / owning / believing." Common with Hindi/Urdu, Arabic and some African-language speakers, whose continuous forms work with more verbs.

Advanced Modality — Speculation & Nuance

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • set certainty on a dial: can't possibly have · can't · could conceivably · might just · might / may / could · may / might / could well · should (expectation) · is bound to · must
  • tell may well (it's quite likely) from may / might as well (there's no better option)
  • recognize and use the hidden jobs of will / won't (typical behavior, refusal, present deduction: That'll be the courier) and would (past habit, past refusal, criticism, politeness)
  • speculate about now with modal + be + -ing and about the past with modal + have + pp and modal + have been + -ing
  • analyze a case as evidence arrives and give a calibrated verdict with a softened recommendation

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: certainty as a dial. Each position is a statement about how likely the speaker thinks something is, and how much credibility they are staking on it. Well after may / might / could pushes a bare possibility toward probability. Conceivably pushes it down: "it's possible, but only just, and I'm being fair to the idea." Bound to is a confident prediction from what we know, almost must for the future.

"She might be exhausted." / "She may well be exhausted." Which speaker leans more toward "yes"? (The second.) Is either speaker certain? (No.) · "It could conceivably be the backup." Does the speaker think it's likely? (No, just not impossible.) Why say it? (To be fair; to keep the idea on the table.) · "Prices are bound to rise." Past or future? (Future.) How sure? (Very: a confident prediction.) · "They can't possibly have known." How sure is the speaker that they didn't know? (Completely: it's ruled out.)

Meaning: may well vs may as well.

"The train may well be late." Is this about probability or a decision? (Probability: it's quite likely.) · "The train's late, so we may as well get a coffee." Is the coffee likely? (Not the point: it's a decision.) Why get a coffee? (There's no better option; no reason not to.) Is there a feeling in may as well? (Yes, a little shrug, resignation.)

Meaning: should for expectation.

"They left at noon, so they should be home by now." Is this advice? (No, an expectation.) Is the speaker sure? (Fairly, "if all goes normally.") Compare "They must be home by now." Which one is stronger? (must: the evidence forces it.)

Meaning: will and would at work.

"There's the doorbell. That'll be the courier." Is this about the future? (No, now: a confident guess.) · "The car won't start." Does the car refuse? (It persistently fails: we talk about it as if it refuses.) · "Every summer we'd drive to the coast." Once or many times? (Many: a past habit.) Can I say "When I was young, I'd have a bike"? (No: have a bike is a state: I used to have.) · "You would say that!" Is it a compliment? (No: typical of you, a light criticism.) · "I'd suggest a short break." Is there a condition? (Not really: it's softening, politeness.)

Meaning: now or past.

"He must be joking." When? (Now.) · "He must have been joking." When? (Earlier.) · "She must have been waiting for hours." Is she still waiting? (Probably not: past, and long.) · "They can't have left already." Are we sure they're still here? (Yes, we think so: it's ruled out.)

Form.

Present speculationmodal + be / modal + be + -ingShe may well be tired. · He must be joking.
Past speculationmodal + have + ppThey can't possibly have known.
Past, in progressmodal + have been + -ingShe could have been planning this all week.
Adverb boostersmay / might / could + well · could + conceivably · might + just · can't + possiblyThis might just work.
bound tobe bound to + base verbIt's bound to happen again.
may / might as well+ base verb (a decision)We might as well walk.

Pronunciation. Stress carries the meaning. In may well the stress falls on well; in may as well it falls on the main verb that follows ("we may as well walk"), and as is a weak /əz/. Perfect modals reduce heavily: "must have been" /ˈmʌstəv bɪn/, "can't have" /ˈkænt əv/ (never "must of"). Characteristic will / would is stressed and not contracted when it's critical: "She will keep interrupting!" · "You would say that." Deduction 'll is contracted and light: "That'll be the courier" /ˈðætl bi/.

Drill script
  1. Dial up, dial down (point at a rung): T: "She's exhausted. Might." → S: "She might be exhausted." T: "Lower: only just possible." → S: "She could conceivably be exhausted." T: "Higher: quite likely." → S: "She may well be exhausted." T: "Top." → S: "She must be exhausted." T: "Bottom, past, 'she slept twelve hours'." → S: "She can't possibly be exhausted: she slept twelve hours."
  2. Now or past? T: "He's joking. Sure. Now." → S: "He must be joking." T: "He was joking. Sure. Earlier." → S: "He must have been joking." T: "She waited for hours. Sure." → S: "She must have been waiting for hours." T: "They knew. Impossible." → S: "They can't have known."
  3. That'll be…: T rings a bell / knocks on the table. → S: "That'll be the courier." T: (a phone buzzes) → S: "That'll be my manager." T: "She's just flown in from Tokyo." → S: "She'll be tired."
  4. Twins: T: "It's likely to rain." → S: "It may well rain." T: "The bus has gone. No better option: walk." → S: "We may as well walk."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 describes a small invented mystery ("The meeting room is booked every Friday, but nobody's ever in it."). S2 gives three explanations at three points on the dial. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Evidence Ladder — how to run it
  • Groups of 4. Each group gets one case file: a case summary card, 6 clue cards clipped in three rounds (1–2, 3–4, 5–6), and a ladder sheet (rungs from can't possibly at the bottom to must / is bound to at the top). Cases: 1 The Vanishing Deliveries (an office building) · 2 The 3 a.m. Alert (a server) · 3 The Empty Training Room (a community center) · 4 The Missing Saturday Volunteers (a community garden). Two groups can have the same case.
  • Roles: the case lead keeps the discussion moving and gives the verdict; the evidence reader holds the clue cards and reveals them only when the round starts; the ladder keeper writes each hypothesis on the sheet and moves it (arrows) as the evidence changes; the skeptic must challenge at least one claim per round with "But couldn't it be that…?" or "That can't be the whole story, because…". Give the ladder keeper job to a dominant speaker; give the skeptic job to a quieter learner who likes to think before speaking.
  • The rule of the ladder: every hypothesis is said with the expression of its rung. Not "Maybe it's the courier," but "The courier could conceivably be leaving them downstairs." When a hypothesis moves, the group says why: "Clue 4 makes this far more likely: it may well be the name change."
  • No blame on people. The cases have no villains. If a group jumps to "the new temp must be lazy," the skeptic's job is to ask for evidence, and your job is to steer: "What else could explain it?" Reputation is a real appropriacy issue at work: hedging protects colleagues.
  • Verdict (90 seconds per group): use the frame in the workbook: what can't possibly have happened; what must have / is bound to; what may well be true; one characteristic will / would sentence; a softened recommendation.
  • If time is short, run only Rounds 1 and 2 and skip the reveal; hand out the solutions at the start of C3 as a warmer.
ICQs before the Evidence Ladder
Can the evidence reader show all six clues at once? (No, two per round.) · Can you say "maybe"? (No: say the rung: "could conceivably," "may well"…) · What does the skeptic say? ("But couldn't it be that…?") · Can you blame a person without evidence? (No.) · How long is the verdict? (Ninety seconds.) · How does it end? (With a softened recommendation: "I'd suggest…")
Differentiation
Literacy support

The case files are reading-heavy. Give the learner the evidence-reader role (they read two short clues at a time, aloud, after a minute's preparation) or pre-teach the key words of one case the day before. Reduce the dial to five rungs: can't · might · may well · should · must. Give the verdict frame with the starters already written. Target: three hypotheses said on three different rungs, and one "must have" about the past.

Stretch (toward C2)

Add stance collocations: in all likelihood, there's every chance that, I'd be surprised if, it's by no means certain that, may yet ("The deal may yet go through"). Use perfect passive modals: "The label can't have been updated." Ask the learner to write the verdict as a two-sentence email to a manager, then as a one-line message to a colleague, and compare the modals chosen. Challenge: "Remove every maybe and definitely from your verdict."

L1 watch-outs
  • may well / may as well: the false twins confuse everyone, and a few languages have a similar-looking "also" structure (Spanish bien podría = may well; French autant + infinitive = may as well). Drill the test question.
  • One verb for must and should: French devoir, Spanish deber (de), Portuguese dever, Italian dovere and Chinese 应该 yīnggāi cover obligation, expectation and deduction. Learners say "He should be at home, the lights are on" when they mean must, or "must" when they mean mild expectation.
  • mustn't have for impossibility: "He mustn't have known." Spanish (no debe de haber sabido) and French (il n'a pas dû savoir) push learners toward mustn't. The logical negative of deduction is can't have.
  • will = want: German (ich will) and Dutch (ik wil) speakers may read "He won't listen" as "He doesn't want to listen." Close, but point out the stronger idea of refusal.
  • Perfect modal forms: "must have went," "should have came," "can't have be," "must of." Common for all L1s. Hold up the "have + past participle" card every time.
  • Hedging by particle or adverb: Japanese and Korean (sentence-final particles) and many other languages hedge with adverbs, so learners overuse "maybe" and "I think" and underuse modals. Praise every modal hedge.

The Subjunctive & Unreal Uses

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use the mandative subjunctive (base form, every person) after verbs (recommend, insist, propose, request, require), adjectives (It is essential / vital / imperative that…) and nouns (our recommendation that…), including the negative (that he not attend) and the passive (that it be reviewed)
  • choose between the subjunctive, should + base and the indicative as register choices, and tell a demand (insisted that he leave) from a statement of fact (insisted that he was innocent)
  • use the unreal were in hypotheses and wishes (If I were…, Were we to…, I wish it were…), and unreal forms after suppose / supposing, it's high time and would rather
  • use as if / as though with a present tense (possibly true) or a past tense (contrary to fact)
  • write and peer-review a short formal policy memo or meeting recommendation on a workplace issue

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the mandative subjunctive. The that-clause describes something that is required, recommended or willed, not something that is true. The base form marks it as "not yet real." It sounds impersonal and authoritative, which is why it dominates policies, minutes and reports.

"The board insists that he resign." Has he resigned? (No, not yet.) Is it a fact or a demand? (A demand.) Why not "resigns"? (It isn't a statement of fact: it's what the board wants.) · "It is essential that everyone be present." Is everyone present now? (We don't know: it's a requirement.) · "We ask that he not resign before the vote." Is this "don't resign"? (Yes, a request.) Where's do? (No do: not + base.)

Meaning: the lookalike.

"The evidence suggests that he is guilty." / "I suggest that he be released." Which suggest means "shows"? (The first.) Which means "propose"? (The second.) Which needs the subjunctive? (The second: it's a recommendation.) · "She insisted that she was innocent." / "She insisted that he leave." Which is a demand? (The second.)

Meaning: unreal were and friends.

"Were we to delay the launch, we'd lose the client." Are we delaying? (No, it's a hypothesis.) Same as? (If we delayed / If we were to delay.) More or less formal? (More.) · "Supposing the budget were cut by half, what would we drop first?" Has it been cut? (No: imagine it.) · "It's high time we reviewed this policy." Past or present? (Present: it's overdue now.) Have we reviewed it? (No, and we should have already.) · "I'd rather you didn't mention it." Whose action? (Yours.) Past? (No: the past form is unreal, polite.)

Meaning: as if / as though.

"He talks as if he owned the company." Does he own it? (No: past = contrary to fact.) When is he talking? (Now: the past form doesn't mean past time.) · "It looks as if it's going to snow." Might it snow? (Yes: present = possibly true.) · "She looked as though she had seen a ghost." Had she seen one? (No: past perfect, unreal, earlier.)

Form.

Mandativetrigger + (that) + subject + base formWe recommend that she chair the meeting.
Negativenot + base form…that staff not share passwords.
Passivebe + past participle…that the policy be reviewed annually.
Unreal werewere for all persons · inversion Were + subject (+ to)If it were possible… · Were she to refuse…
Suppose / high time / rathersuppose(-ing) + past / were to · it's (high) time + past simple · would rather + pastIt's high time we left. · I'd rather you didn't.
As if / as though+ present (possibly true) · + past / were (unreal) · + past perfect (unreal, earlier)as if he knows · as if he knew · as if he had seen

Pronunciation. The subjunctive is easy to miss in speech because be and the bare verb are unstressed. In formal reading, give the verb its full value and a small pause before that: "It is essential | that every member be present." In the negative, stress not: "that he not be told." Were is /wɜr/ when stressed at the start of an inverted clause ("Were we to delay…"), weak /wər/ elsewhere. As though is /əz ˈðoʊ/: the th is voiced.

Drill script
  1. Casual → memo: T: "Tom really needs to come to the meeting. (essential)" → S: "It is essential that Tom be present at the meeting." T: "She should chair it. (recommend)" → S: "We recommend that she chair the meeting." T: "Everyone has to sign in. (require)" → S: "We require that everyone sign in."
  2. Negative and passive: T: "Staff shouldn't share passwords. (request)" → S: "We request that staff not share passwords." T: "Someone must review the policy every year. (vital)" → S: "It is vital that the policy be reviewed every year."
  3. If → Were: T: "If I were in charge, I'd cancel it." → S: "Were I in charge, I'd cancel it." T: "If we delayed the launch, we'd lose the client." → S: "Were we to delay the launch, we'd lose the client."
  4. Overdue: T: "We should have updated the handbook already." → S: "It's high time we updated the handbook." T: "Someone should have fixed the printer weeks ago." → S: "It's high time someone fixed the printer."
  5. Real or unreal?: T: "He talks like an expert. He isn't one." → S: "He talks as if he were an expert." T: "He talks like an expert. Maybe he is." → S: "He talks as if he knows what he's doing."
Policy Memo + peer review — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Each group gets one issue brief (Set F): the situation, three facts with numbers, a constraint (budget, time, staffing) and an assigned recommendation. Brief 1: meeting overload (Lakeside Logistics) · Brief 2: new staff leaving within three months (Northgate Clinic) · Brief 3: the shared kitchen (Oakridge Tower) · Brief 4: hybrid desk booking (Crestview College admin office) · Brief 5: no-shows on the volunteer schedule (Riverside Community Center). Two groups may share a brief; give them opposite assigned positions (each brief has an A and a B position) and let them compare at the end.
  • Write (12 min) on the memo frame: To / From / Date / Re · Background (1–2 sentences) · Recommendations (three or more, numbered: three different trigger types, one negative, one passive) · Rationale (one unreal form: Were we to…, Supposing…, If X were…) · Next steps (It is high time… · If need be, …). 120–160 words. Roles: a writer, a drafter (says each sentence aloud before it's written) and a register checker (reads for tone: memo, not chat; not over-dramatic).
  • Peer review (8 min): the three-pass protocol. Groups swap memos. Each reviewer takes one pass with the checklist: Pass 1 Accuracy (base form after every trigger, no -s, not + base, be + pp, were after inversion) · Pass 2 Range (three trigger types? an unreal form? an as if or it's high time?) · Pass 3 Appropriacy (consistent formal tone, clear actions, fossil phrases used sparingly). Reviewers write in pencil in the margin and finish with one strength (quoted), one question, one suggested change. The authors decide what to accept: "It's your memo."
  • Read-out (3 min): two groups read their memo "to the committee." The class responds with one formal line: "Be that as it may, the cost…" · "If need be, we could…"
  • Handling dominant speakers: the drafter role keeps a fast speaker useful (they say sentences aloud) without taking the pen. In peer review, the strongest learner is the third reviewer (appropriacy), which means they speak last. Positions are assigned, so nobody argues their personal view; no one has to say what their real workplace is like.
ICQs before the Policy Memo
Do you choose your recommendation? (No, it's assigned on the brief.) · How many words? (120 to 160.) · How many trigger types? (Three: a verb, an adjective, a noun.) · After "It is essential that he…" do we add -s? (No, base form.) · The negative: "that staff don't share" or "that staff not share"? (Not share.) · In peer review, do you rewrite the memo? (No, pencil comments: a strength, a question, a change.) · Who decides what to change? (The authors.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the memo frame with the trigger phrases pre-printed at the start of each recommendation line ("It is essential that ___ · We recommend that ___ · Our proposal is that ___"). The learner completes each with a base-form verb. Focus on three structures: It is essential that + base, We recommend that + base, If I were…. In peer review, give them Pass 1 only (a single, concrete check: "Is there an -s after the trigger?"). Target: three correct mandative sentences and one If I were sentence.

Stretch (toward C2)

Use Should-inversion and Were it not for in the rationale ("Should the budget allow, … · Were it not for the new schedule, …"): a preview of Lesson 2.2. Recognize lest ("lest the problem recur") as formal and rare: meet it, don't force it. Ask the learner to write the same recommendation for three audiences (the board, the team, a friend over coffee) and justify each verb form. Early finishers edit their memo down by 20% without losing a recommendation (a preview of 8.2).

L1 watch-outs
  • "I want that you come": Spanish (quiero que vengas), Portuguese, Italian, French (je veux que tu viennes), Russian (хочу, чтобы) and Polish put a that-clause after want. In English want is not a mandative trigger: "I want you to come." Learners with a strong L1 subjunctive often overextend it.
  • Subjunctive after doubt or emotion: Romance-language speakers may try "It's possible that he be late" or "I'm glad that she be here." English uses the subjunctive only after demand, recommendation and importance.
  • Infinitive after the trigger: "They demanded that he to leave," "It is important that he to be informed." Common with Chinese, Arabic and Turkish speakers. Cross out to; point at the subject.
  • do-support in the negative: "We ask that she doesn't attend." Common for all L1s. Teach "that she not attend" as a chunk.
  • "It's time that we go": Spanish ya es hora de que nos vayamos and French il est temps que nous partions use a present subjunctive. English uses the past: "It's time we went."
  • as if + present for unreal: German, Dutch and the Scandinavian languages use a subjunctive or conditional here (als ob er … wäre), which may help. Chinese, Japanese and Korean speakers often keep the present ("as if he owns it") because the L1 doesn't mark it. Ask the truth-value question.

Recap on slide 18, "place it, weigh it, shape it": By the launch we'll have been testing for six months. The launch was to have taken place in March. (place it) → It can't possibly have been the cleaners; the update may well be clashing with the backup. (weigh it) → It is essential that every new hire be assigned a mentor.
