Module 1 — Precision in Time & Modality: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the project briefing, the evidence-ladder verdicts and case solutions, the policy memo), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 1.1–1.3Classes C1–C39 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C1, mark accuracy, range and appropriacy. A sentence is correct when the form is right (by the time + present, have + pp after a modal, the base form after a mandative trigger, were after inversion) and it expresses the exact meaning intended (in progress vs completed; may well vs may as well; real vs unreal as if) and it suits the situation. Be generous with legitimate variants: contractions; might / could for may in the "well" and "as well" structures; should + base as well as the bare subjunctive (typically British, fully correct); as though for as if; was after suppose / supposing and if I in informal speech (but only were after inversion); British spellings (analysed, apologise, realised). When a learner produces a correct sentence with a different structure from the target, accept it and point to the target: "Correct! Can you also say it with be to?"

1.1 · Advanced Aspect — The Future in Detail (C1)

First-day writing sample

No "answers." Score with the four-row rubric in the materials (Set B): task & register · range · accuracy · organization & cohesion, 0–3 each (max 12). Guide: 9–12 comfortable at C1 · 6–8 typical C1 entry · 5 or less: check placement privately. Give each learner a one-line private note (one strength, one focus); never a score.

Exercise 1 — The Monday stand-up (Script 1.1-A)
1were going to reopen
2will be working
3will have finished
4will have been running
5are about to sign ('re about to sign)
6is to visit
7Will … be needing

The project is behind schedule: item 1 (the April reopening was a plan that changed; it's now the end of May). Item 4 is the one to discuss: will have been running because the program is an ongoing activity measured by its duration up to the reopening. Item 7 is the tactful future continuous: the lead is asking about plans, not demanding a decision.

Exercise 2 — The most precise future form
  1. will be flying (in progress at that moment)
  2. will have completed (finished by the end of the year)
  3. will have been running (duration up to August)
  4. will be driving (in progress at six)
  5. arrive · will have checked (time clause in the present; completed before)
  6. will have known (know is stative: no continuous)
  7. Will you be presenting (a tactful inquiry about plans)
  8. will have taught (a number = a completed quantity = simple)

Wrong: 3 "will run / will have run" (loses the ongoing duration; will have run is marginal and implies it stops) · 5 "will arrive" in the time clause · 6 "will have been knowing" · 8 "will have been teaching more than three thousand students" (continuous + number). Accept: 7 "Are you presenting…?" (present continuous for an arrangement: correct, a little more direct).

Exercise 3 — The future seen from the past; formal futures
  1. We were going to sign the deal on Friday, but… (also: were to have signed, more formal)
  2. I was about to object when she cut me off. (was on the point of objecting means the same, but doesn't fit the frame)
  3. that meeting would change his life (also: was to change, more formal / literary)
  4. The bridge was to have opened in 2024. (also: was going to open / was supposed to open, less formal)
  5. The CEO is to visit the plant on Friday.
  6. The two companies are on the verge of reaching an agreement. (also: on the point of reaching / on the brink of)
  7. If we are to meet the deadline, we need two more testers.

Wrong: 4 "was to open" alone (this doesn't tell us it didn't happen; was to have opened does) · 6 "on the verge to reach" · 7 "If we will meet the deadline." Item 7 is useful in real briefings; praise it.

Exercise 4 — Precision and register
  1. Will you be using: more tactful to a senior colleague; asks about the plan, not a decision.
  2. arrive: present in a time clause.
  3. due to: a scheduled date; about to can't take a future time phrase.
  4. have known: stative verb.
  5. I'll be there: casual register; "I'll have been arriving" is also ungrammatical in meaning.
  6. is to: an official arrangement (formal); about to clashes with "next month."
  7. will have left: completed before the moment of reading. (will be leaving is possible only if the writer is leaving at that exact moment: unlikely.)
Exercise 5 — A future-point paragraph (model)

The new clinic opens on September 1. On opening day, the reception team will be welcoming the first patients while the IT team will still be testing the booking system. By then, we will have moved all the patient records and trained every nurse on the new software, and the project team will have been working on the move for almost a year. We were to have opened in June, but the building inspection was delayed. The final inspection is due to take place next week, and we are on the verge of signing the cleaning contract.

Check: one form per job, a by-anchor for each perfect, no future in time clauses. Praise a sentence that uses If we are to….

Dialogue — a project check-in

Future forms: meets (timetable present) · will it be finished · we'll have been running · we'll have collected · were to have launched · were about to go live · is due to arrive · we'll still be testing · is on the verge of signing · will you be joining · is to attend · is bound to have · If we're to get. A plan that didn't happen: "We were to have launched it in May." Tactful question: "Will you be joining the meeting?" It asks about Daniel's plans without asking him to decide on the spot or implying he must come.

Project Briefing — model (Project 2, Northgate Health Clinic)

Thanks for your time. At the steering meeting on June 30, the movers will be transferring the last of the equipment to the new building. By then, we'll have moved all the patient records and trained every receptionist on the new check-in screens, and the IT team will have been testing the phone system for six weeks. We were going to open on June 2, but the elevator inspection was postponed. The inspection is due to take place on July 3, and we're on the verge of signing the parking agreement with the city. The mayor is to open the building on July 15. If we are to keep that date, we'll need two extra IT staff in the first week.

Stakeholder questions (model): Will you be needing volunteers on moving day? · Will patients still be using the old entrance in July? · Will the pharmacy be opening at the same time?

Self-check
Q1
"By the time you arrive, we ___ for hours." Which form best expresses duration up to that future point?
A) will be waiting
B) have been waiting
C) will have been waiting
Why: the future perfect continuous measures an ongoing activity up to a future point (by the time you arrive). A shows only that we'll be waiting at that moment, not the duration; B is present perfect, anchored to now.
Q2
"We ___ take the earlier train, but it was fully booked." Choose the natural "future in the past" form.
A) will
B) were going to
C) would have taken
Why: was / were going to reports an earlier plan that wasn't carried out. A is present-future; C is a third-conditional result and would need a condition (would have taken it if…).
Q3 · new
"According to the press release, the director ___ visit the site on Friday."
A) is to
B) is about to
C) would
Why: be to reports an official arrangement, the language of press releases and announcements. B means "any moment now" and can't take a future time phrase like "on Friday"; C is a future seen from the past and doesn't fit a present report.
Errors you will hear most (1.1)

"By the time we will finish…" · "When the client will arrive, …" · "By June I will work here for ten years." (present or simple future for a duration) · "…since ten years" · "will have been knowing / owning" · "We're about to sign next week." · "we will finished" / "I will been working" (dropped auxiliary) · "You are to report" read as a suggestion. Appropriacy: future perfects in casual chat ("I'll have been eating by then, lol") and blunt "Will you use…?" to a senior colleague. For range, praise was to have + pp, If we are to…, and a well-placed on the verge of.

1.2 · Advanced Modality — Speculation & Nuance (C2)

Exercise 1 — The Tuesday slump (Script 1.2-A)
1can't beR ruled out
2might beP possible
3can't possibly have startedR ruled out
4may well have switchedL quite likely
5would always orderH past habit
6must beC near-certain
7are bound to beC near-certain (a prediction)
8might as well tryD a decision

Extra modals in the script to notice: she won't be the reason (deduction), It should bring some people in (expectation), It could conceivably work (a slim chance). Follow-up: "Is may well have switched a guess or a fact?" (A well-founded guess: the evidence is circumstantial.)

Exercise 2 — Rank by certainty

a. Least → most certain that it will rain: c, b, d, a (c rules it out · b bare possibility · d quite likely · a near-certain deduction).

b. Weakest → strongest claim: g, e, i, h, f, where "weakest" means the lowest position on the dial. g = ruled out (can't possibly have) · e = a slim chance (could conceivably) · i = possible (might) · h = quite likely (may well have) · f = near-certain prediction (is bound to).

Accept e and i in either order if the learner explains the difference well: could conceivably stresses "only just possible," bare might is neutral. Some learners argue that g is the "strongest" claim (it is very certain, but of a negative). Agree, and point out that the question is about the dial position.

Exercise 3 — Choose the nuance
  1. may as well (no reason not to; the best option left)
  2. must (near-certain deduction)
  3. may well (quite likely)
  4. can't (ruled out on the evidence)
  5. may as well (nothing stops us; a decision)
  6. may well (a strong but hedged prediction)

Accept might as well in 1 and 5 and could well / might well in 3 and 6 if learners change the word. Wrong: 1 "may well" (turns a decision into a probability) · 6 "may as well drop" (means "they might as well drop": nonsense here).

Exercise 4 — The job of will / would
  1. habitual past (a repeated routine)
  2. criticism / characteristic behavior ("that's so typical of him")
  3. politeness: softened advice (also a conditional frame, "if I were you": accept either with a reason)
  4. past refusal (an object's persistent failure)
  5. narrative future in the past
  6. present deduction (a confident guess about now)
  7. refusal now / persistent failure
  8. typical behavior (what she habitually does; her nature)
Exercise 5 — Now or past?
  1. He must have been joking.
  2. They can't have known about the changes. (also: can't possibly have known · couldn't have known)
  3. She might be waiting outside (right now). (also: may / could be waiting)
  4. They should have arrived by now. (also: ought to have arrived)
  5. The email may well have gone to spam. (also: might / could well have gone)
  6. She must have been working all night.

Wrong: 1 "must be joking" (present) · 2 "mustn't have known" (L1 transfer; the negative of deduction is can't have) · 4 "should arrive by now" · 6 "must have worked all night" (acceptable, but loses the ongoing activity) · any "must have went / gone to spam" confusion: "must have gone."

Dialogue — the dark office

must have left (near-certain, past) · may well have (quite likely, ellipsis of "left") · might just be (a slim chance, now) · won't answer (typical behavior / refusal) · might as well email (a decision) · should reply (expectation) · would be very her (characteristic behavior) · It would, wouldn't it (characteristic, echoed) · could have been planning (possible, past ongoing).

The Evidence Ladder — case solutions (read aloud at the reveal)
Case 1 · The Vanishing DeliveriesThe tenant on the 4th floor changed its company name in February (Brightline Design → Kestrel Studio), but the lobby directory and the couriers' address database still show the old name. Couriers who can't match the name leave packages at the 2nd-floor reception, where they sit until someone recognizes them. Red herrings: the new mail-room temp (started after the problem began) and the broken elevator (fixed in a day). Model verdict: It can't possibly have been the temp; the problem started two weeks before she arrived. The name change must have confused the couriers, and packages are bound to keep going astray until the directory is updated. I'd suggest we update the directory and email every supplier this week.
Case 2 · The 3 a.m. AlertThe new cloud backup (set up in October to run every Thursday at 3 a.m.) clashes with the automatic software update that the old IT contractor scheduled for the same night. Both jobs use the server at full capacity. Red herrings: the cleaning crew (they have no passwords and the server room is locked) and the vending-machine Wi-Fi. Model verdict: The cleaners can't possibly have caused it. The backup and the update may well be running at the same time; the timing points to that. It's bound to happen again next Thursday unless one of them is moved. We might as well move the backup to Sunday.
Case 3 · The Empty Training RoomSeveral causes together, which is the point: the course was advertised only online (the website and social media), the few printed flyers showed the wrong day (Tuesday instead of Thursday), and the sign-up form required an online account, which many of the people who asked for the course don't have. The date also falls in a school-holiday week. Model verdict: No single cause explains it. The wrong day on the flyer must have cost us some people, and the online-only form may well have put off many more. The holiday week could conceivably be a factor too. I'd suggest paper sign-up sheets at the front desk and a new date.
Case 4 · The Missing Saturday VolunteersIn March the city changed the Saturday bus schedule: the first Route 12 bus now arrives at 9:40, but the garden's volunteer shift still starts at 9:00. Most regular volunteers don't drive. Red herrings: the new coordinator (volunteers like her; weekday numbers are up) and the move to a group-chat app (Saturday messages are read). Model verdict: It can't be the new coordinator; weekday numbers have gone up since she started. The bus change must be the main reason: the first bus doesn't arrive until 9:40. People are bound to keep missing the start. We might as well move the shift to 10:00.

Groups rarely match the solution exactly. Mark the reasoning: did each hypothesis sit on the right rung for the evidence? Did it move when the evidence changed? Did the group avoid blaming a person without evidence? A group that keeps a hypothesis at could conceivably and says why has done excellent C1 work, even if it's not the answer.

Self-check
Q1
"The shops are shut, so we ___ order in." Which fits, meaning there's no better option?
A) may as well
B) may well
C) can't
Why: may / might as well = there's no better option, a decision with a shrug. B is about probability ("we'll quite likely order in"), which doesn't fit "so"; C contradicts the logic.
Q2
In "Every winter we'd skate on the frozen pond," what job is would doing?
A) Conditional (result of a condition)
B) Habitual past (a repeated routine)
C) Politeness / softening
Why: an established past setting ("every winter") with no condition, and a repeated action: habitual would, like used to for actions.
Q3 · new
"The lights are on and her car's in the driveway. She ___ gone away for the weekend."
A) can't possibly have
B) may well have
C) must have
Why: the evidence (lights on, car still there) rules the idea out, so the speaker needs the bottom of the dial, in the past: can't (possibly) have + pp. B and C point the wrong way on the evidence.
Errors you will hear most (1.2)

"It may as well rain" (meaning likely) · "He must have went / must of gone" · "He mustn't have known" (for impossibility) · "He must be lying yesterday" · "When I was a child I'd have a bike" (habitual would with a state) · "He should be at home: the lights are on" (where must is meant) · "It is bound to rain yesterday." · stacked hedges ("it may possibly perhaps be…"). Appropriacy: over-certainty about people ("It's definitely the temp's fault") and the criticism would ("You would say that") used to a manager. For range, praise could conceivably, may well have, is bound to and a well-timed "That'll be…".

1.3 · The Subjunctive & Unreal Uses (C3)

Exercise 1 — The committee's recommendations (Script 1.3-A)
1be updated5were
2attend6were
3not be asked7took
4have

Items 1–4 are mandative (base form: passive, active, negative passive, have not has). Items 5–6 are unreal were. Item 7: it's high time + past simple. Learners often hear "attends" or "has": replay the line and ask "Is it a fact or a recommendation?"

Exercise 2 — The mandative subjunctive
  1. be extended
  2. be
  3. not answer
  4. be retained
  5. have
  6. submit
  7. be reviewed · not be required

Also correct: should + base in every item (should be extended, should not answer). Wrong: 1 "is extended / extends" · 3 "doesn't answer / not to answer / didn't answer" · 5 "has" · 6 "submits" · 7 "is reviewed," "are not required," "don't be required."

Exercise 3 — Register and meaning
  1. should apply
  2. be waived
  3. should arrive
  4. not sign
  5. works (suggest = show: a fact, indicative)
  6. be tested (suggest = propose: subjunctive; also should be tested)
  7. had sent (insist = assert a fact; backshifted; also sent)
  8. send (insist = demand; also should send)

Items 1–4: both versions are correct; the subjunctive is more formal and the standard American choice, should the characteristically British one. Item 5 "work" (subjunctive) is the classic over-correction: praise the awareness, then show the lookalike.

Exercise 4 — Unreal uses
  1. is (real: the speaker thinks it genuinely might snow)
  2. owned (unreal: he doesn't own it)
  3. were (unreal were: we aren't old friends; accept was in informal use)
  4. knows (real: treated as plausibly true)
  5. updated (it's high time + past simple; also should update, less emphatic)
  6. Were (inversion: only were)
  7. rejected / were to reject (also was to reject informally)
  8. didn't forward (would rather + past for another person's action)

Wrong: 2 "owns" (treats it as possibly true) · 5 "update / will update" · 6 "Was" · 8 "don't forward / not forward / wouldn't forward."

Exercise 5 — Edit the memo
  1. We recommend that each manager send the report by Friday.
  2. It is vital that the kitchen not be left dirty overnight.
  3. They demanded that he apologize in writing. (also: They demanded an apology in writing.)
  4. Were I in charge, I would change the schedule. (also: If I were / was in charge, …)
  5. We ask that visitors not park in the loading area.
  6. He acts as if he owned the place. (he doesn't: unreal)
  7. The grammar is right but the register is wrong: a mandative subjunctive in a friendly text sounds absurdly official (or deliberately comic). → Don't forget the snacks on Saturday! / Can you bring snacks on Saturday?
Dialogue — preparing the recommendation

Mandative subjunctives (3): (I recommend) that every meeting have · that Wednesdays be kept · that managers not invite. Other unreal forms (4): It's high time we did · Were we to do nothing · Suppose the directors refused · I'd rather you did. As if: as if it were the end of the world. Fixed phrases (2): Be that as it may · If need be. Note the second conditional in "we'd lose" and "we'd pilot" as results of the unreal forms.

Policy Memo — model (Brief 1, meeting overload · Position A)

To: Senior Leadership Team · From: Operations Working Group · Date: March 14 · Re: Reducing meeting time

Background. Our staff survey shows that employees spend an average of 14 hours a week in meetings, and many report attending meetings as though attendance itself were the goal. Recommendations. 1. We recommend that every meeting have a written agenda circulated 24 hours in advance. 2. It is essential that Wednesdays be kept free of internal meetings, on a trial basis for one quarter. 3. Our proposal is that managers not invite more than six people unless a decision requires it. Rationale. Were we to recover even a quarter of this time, each employee would gain three and a half hours a week for focused work. Next steps. It is high time we measured the cost of meetings. If need be, the working group will pilot these changes in the Logistics department first. (132 words)

Check: three trigger types (verb, adjective, noun) ✓ · negative ✓ · passive ✓ · unreal form ✓ · as though ✓ · formal, consistent tone ✓.

Self-check
Q1
"The regulations require that each application ___ signed by a supervisor."
A) be
B) is
C) has to be
Why: require that is a mandative trigger: the base form (here a passive, be signed) for every person. B is the informal indicative, heard but not appropriate in regulations; C repeats the obligation already in require.
Q2
"He strode past us without a word, as if he ___ us at all." The speaker means he did recognize them but pretended not to.
A) doesn't know
B) wouldn't have known
C) didn't know
Why: the comparison is contrary to fact (he did know them), so as if takes a past form. A is present and treats it as possibly true; B is a conditional perfect that doesn't fit an unreal comparison.
Q3 · new
"For security reasons, we ask that staff ___ their passwords with anyone."
A) not share
B) don't share
C) not to share
Why: the negative mandative subjunctive is not + base form, with no do and no to. B is the indicative (informal, and odd after ask that); C mixes in the infinitive of "ask staff not to share."
Errors you will hear most (1.3)

"We recommend that he goes" · "that she doesn't attend" · "that he to leave" · "I want that you come" (want is not a trigger) · "It is possible that he be late" (overextension from a Romance L1) · "Was I in charge…" · "It's time that we go" · "as if he owns the place" (for unreal) · "The data suggests that the system be working" (lookalike over-correction). Appropriacy: subjunctives and fossil phrases in casual messages ("Suffice it to say the pizza was good"), and like for as if in a formal memo ("It feels like we are ignoring the problem"). For range, praise Were we to…, a well-placed passive subjunctive, and Be that as it may used once, not three times.
