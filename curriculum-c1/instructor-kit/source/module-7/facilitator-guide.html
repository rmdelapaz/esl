Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 7 — Listening, Speaking & Delivery

Three workshop classes about live spoken English: taking it in, giving it out, and managing it with other people. First, a lecture-listening and note-taking lab: learners hear a five-minute signposted mini-lecture twice, take Cornell notes, answer comprehension and inference questions and write a summary a classmate reviews. Then a pronunciation and intonation workshop: sentence stress and weak forms, thought groups and pausing, tonic placement and contrastive stress, and intonation for attitude, practiced by shadowing marked-up scripts. Last, a seminar: learners discuss an assigned reading in fixed roles (facilitator, summarizer, challenger) and practice turn-taking, building on others' points and respectful challenge.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 90 min per class CEFR C1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The usual shape still applies: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice → freer practice → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. These are skills classes, though, so plan each one as a workshop. There is less new language and more doing: listening twice, marking scripts, shadowing, discussing, then reflecting on how it went.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-7-delivery.html. Print materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html before C20. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Cornell notes sheet, the Pass the Notes mini-talk cards, the One Sentence, Many Meanings and attitude cards, the seminar pack (reading cards, role cards, move cards, observer sheet), the Signpost Bingo game and every script you read aloud, including the mini-lecture.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons and a 🎧 audio control (US or UK voice, slower speed). In this module that audio is especially useful: learners can shadow the model sentences at home.
  • You are the recording. The mini-lecture (C20) and the marked-up scripts (C21) are written to be read by you. Practice the lecture aloud once before class, with a timer: it should take about five minutes at a relaxed lecturing pace (about 650 words at 130–140 words a minute). Read it like a real lecturer, not like a dictation. Stress the signposts, pause at section breaks, and let the weak forms be weak. If a colleague with a different accent can record it for you, even better: C1 listeners need more than one voice.
  • Feedback on range, accuracy and appropriacy. In every freer task, take notes in three columns: range (ambitious language to praise and spread: "the upshot is," "I'd take that one step further"), accuracy (errors to fix: "discuss about," "I am agree," a rising statement) and appropriacy (correct English that lands wrong: a blunt "No, that's wrong," a paraphrase that sounds sarcastic, a fall-rise where the learner meant full agreement). At C1 the third column matters most. In the feedback stage, put 1–2 of each on the board without names.
  • Dominant speakers. The roles are built for this. In C20 give a dominant learner the job of summary reviewer; in C21 the coach in the delivery clinic, who listens and marks up and doesn't perform; in C22 the summarizer or the observer, not the challenger. The seminar move cards limit airtime without singling anyone out: when your cards are gone, you may only speak to hand over to someone else. Say it kindly and generally: "Great discussions have lots of voices. Let's hear from someone who hasn't come in yet."
  • Quiet learners. Many strong C1 learners go silent in groups because they don't know how to get in, not because they have nothing to say (lesson 7.3 says exactly this). Give quiet learners the facilitator role: the role is to hand over by name, so they speak often without having to argue. Warn them privately the class before if you can.
  • Stretching strong learners toward C2. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C2 direction for this module is implication and control: hearing what a speaker implies but doesn't say (C20), using prosody deliberately for irony, diplomacy and emphasis (C21), and managing a discussion so smoothly that nobody notices you are doing it (C22).
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (recognize, summarize, organized, center, neighborhood, sidewalk, parking lot, apartments, main streets, practice as a verb). The online audio lets learners choose a US or UK voice; either is fine, but model American stress and weak forms in class (for example for /fɚ/, or /ɚ/). Accept British forms (pavement, car park, flat, high street) when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Take it in → give it out → share the floor. In C20 learners listen to a talk as a structure: they hear the signposts, separate the claim from the example and put the argument on a page as a map, not a transcript. In C21 they turn the same knowledge around and make their own speech easy to follow: stress the words that matter, pause at meaning boundaries, fall to sound certain and fall-rise to sound diplomatic. In C22 they use both skills with other people: listening closely enough to build on and paraphrase what others said, and delivering challenge with the melody that keeps it respectful. The C20 mini-lecture comes back in C21 as a shadowing script, and its topic (how we learn) is one of the C22 seminar readings. Tell learners this: it shows them the module is one skill seen from three sides.

Trauma-informed: assigned sides, optional recordings, accents are welcome

Opinions are assigned. Every seminar reading gives two positions, and every contributor card assigns one. Learners argue the card, not themselves, so nobody has to reveal a personal view. The topics stay in the workplace, education, technology, health habits and city life. Don't swap in party politics, religion, immigration policy or national comparisons ("In your country, how…?"). Pronunciation work can feel exposing. At C1 it touches identity: "my voice," "my accent." Say explicitly (lesson 7.2 does too) that the goal is clear rhythm and melody, not a different accent. Never imitate a learner's accent, even affectionately. Recording is homework and is optional in class: learners who don't want to hear their own voice in front of others can shadow quietly with you or a partner instead. Listening and memory: the mini-lecture is about how we learn and forget. It's upbeat and practical, but some learners have had interrupted schooling. Frame the topic as "a tool for this course," never as "why you did badly before." For every personal task (the journal, the talk you choose for homework), offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card, or something invented.

Before the Module

  • Print the flashcards (one set for the board): the signpost-function cards and note symbols (C20), the prosody cards (C21) and the discussion-move cards, color-coded by function (C22). Cut them before C20.
  • For C20: one Cornell notes sheet per learner (two if you want them to redo it for homework), and the Signpost Bingo cards (one per learner, four versions) if you have time at the end or want a C21 warmer. Rehearse the mini-lecture (Script 7.1-A) aloud with a timer.
  • For C21: the Pass the Notes mini-talk cards (warmer, one card per pair), the One Sentence, Many Meanings cards and attitude cards (one set per pair), and the marked-up shadowing scripts, which are also in the workbook. Learners who have phones may record themselves for homework; nobody has to record in class.
  • For C22: the seminar pack. For each group of 5–6, one reading card (the same reading for everyone in the group), one set of role cards, one set of move cards (four per learner) and an observer sheet. Decide the groups in advance: mix first languages, and spread strong, quiet and dominant speakers across groups.
  • Print the new identity cards and keep them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking.
  • Optional: a phone timer with a gentle sound for the seminar rounds, and a small object for each group (a pen or a card) to act as a talking token if a group needs it.

Understanding Lectures & Talks — Following & Note-taking

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • recognize signposting language and say what each phrase predicts: a preview (This talk falls into three parts), a sequence (That brings me to my second point), an example (A case in point is…), emphasis (Crucially… · I can't stress this enough), a digression (As an aside… · I won't dwell on…) or a recap (To recap… · The upshot is…)
  • separate a main idea from its supporting detail in real time, and ask "an example of what?"
  • use discourse cues to predict (It's widely believed that… → a reversal is coming) and hear the speaker's stance (one explanation is = hedged; one of the most reliable findings = boosted)
  • take structured notes: keywords, stable abbreviations and symbols, indentation, and the Cornell layout (notes, cues, summary)
  • write a 3–4-sentence summary of a talk (claim, support, conclusion, no examples) and review a partner's summary against a checklist

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: top-down listening. A fluent listener uses knowledge of how talks are built (preview, points, recap) to predict and slot content in. Missing a word is normal; losing the structure is the real problem.

"You missed one word in the middle of point two." Is that a disaster? (No.) What do you do? (Let it go and listen for the next signpost.) · "The speaker said, 'This talk falls into three parts.'" How many things are you listening for? (Three.) What should you do on your page? (Leave space for three.)

Meaning: the signpost functions.

"As an aside, I won't dwell on his methods." Will this come back later? (No.) Should you write it down? (No: it's optional.) · "The upshot is that walkability is a public-health strategy." Is this new information or a summary? (A summary of the main idea.) Where does it go in your notes? (At the top of the takeaways, starred.) · "A case in point is the post-war suburb." Is this the claim or the example? (The example.) What's the question to ask? ("An example of what?")

Meaning: prediction and stance.

"It's widely believed that the more hours you put in, the more you remember…" What word are you waiting for? (But.) Does the speaker agree with the belief? (Only partly.) · "One explanation is that a little forgetting is useful." Is the speaker sure? (No: it's one possible explanation, a hedge.) · "It's one of the most reliable findings in psychology." Is the speaker sure now? (Yes: a booster.)

Meaning: main idea vs detail. The recap test: would this survive into the speaker's summary? Main ideas are flagged, stressed and repeated; details come in bunches, arrive with for instance, and are said once.

"Both students study for three hours. One crams, one spaces it out." Is this the main idea? (No, it's an example.) What's the main idea it supports? (When you study matters as much as how long.) · You remember the story but not the point. What did you keep? (The packaging.) What did you lose? (The content.)

Form: the summary. Reporting verbs and neutral register: The speaker argues / maintains / concedes / concludes that… One sentence for the claim, one or two for the support, one for the conclusion. Present simple is normal for summarizing a talk (She argues that…).

Pronunciation: what signposts sound like. Lecturers stress the content word in a signpost and pause after it: "That brings me to my second point ‖," "But here's the crucial part ‖," "So, to recap ‖." Learners from syllable-timed languages often expect every word to be clear. Show them that to, my, the and that are tiny (/tə/, /mə/, /ðə/, /ðət/) and the stressed syllable is the thing to catch. Emphasis is often carried by slowing down and a higher pitch as much as by the words: "I can't stress this enough."

Drill script
  1. Say it like a lecturer (choral, then individuals): "This talk falls into three parts ‖" · "That brings me to my second point ‖" · "A case in point is… ‖" · "But here's the crucial part ‖" · "So, to recap ‖." Tap the stressed syllable on the desk; exaggerate the pause.
  2. Function flash: T says a signpost; S says the function. T: "I won't dwell on this." → S: "Aside." T: "Turning to my third reason…" → S: "Sequence." T: "The upshot is…" → S: "Recap." Fast.
  3. Prediction ping-pong (Slide 5): T: "On the one hand…" → S: "On the other hand." T: "There are two problems with this." → S: "Problem one… problem two." T: "Now, you might think…" → S: "An objection, then the answer." T: "It's often assumed that…" → S: "But actually…"
  4. Sentence to note: T reads a sentence; S says the note aloud in keywords. T: "Remote work is causing city-center apartment prices to fall." → S: "remote work → city-center prices down." T: "This is not about laziness; it's about design." → S: "not laziness, design ⭐."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 reads a sentence from Workbook Ex 4; S2 compresses it into a note aloud. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
The Listening Lab — how to run it
  • Before you read. Everyone has a Cornell sheet and their personal shorthand. Say: "You will hear the lecture twice. The first time I'll stop four times so you can write. The second time is natural speed, with no stops. You won't catch every word, and that's normal."
  • Listening 1 (chunked). Read Script 7.1-A at a relaxed lecturing pace, a little slower than natural. At each ‖ mark, stop, say "Thirty seconds," and wait. Don't repeat, don't explain. Look at the notes as you walk around: sentences mean the learner is transcribing; a clear 1 · 2 · 3 means they have heard the preview.
  • Compare & cue (5 min). Pairs compare, mark gaps with "?" and write cue questions in the left column. This is where the learning happens: they have to decide what the main points were.
  • Listening 2 (natural speed). Read the whole script again, straight through, at your normal speaking speed. Keep the signpost stress and the pauses at section breaks, but don't slow down for learners. Learners fill gaps in a second color. Afterwards ask: "What did you catch the second time that you missed the first time? Which signposts helped?"
  • Questions. Comprehension first (from notes only), then inference in pairs. Inference questions have no single sentence to point to, so ask for the words that support each answer.
  • Summary & peer review. Writers have 5 minutes. Reviewers use the four-point checklist: ☐ the claim is in the first sentence · ☐ the three points are there, in order · ☐ no examples, names or dates · ☐ the conclusion is the speaker's, not the writer's opinion. The reviewer gives one "keep" (a sentence or phrase that works) and one "change" (a specific, doable edit), and says them in that order. Put the protocol on the board: "Keep: … · Change: … · Because: …"
  • Roles. A dominant speaker can be the reviewer for two pairs (they must listen and read before they talk). A learner who struggled with the notes can summarize from a partner's notes: it's still a summary.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a pre-structured Cornell sheet: write "Thesis:", "1.", "2.", "3.", "Recap:" down the notes column before class, so the learner fills slots rather than building the hierarchy. Allow notes in their first language or with drawings: the notes are for them. For the questions, do comprehension items 1–4 only, orally with you or a partner. For the summary, give a frame: "The speaker argues that ___. First, ___. Second, ___. Third, ___. She concludes that ___." Target: the three main points in the right order.

Stretch (toward C2)

Ask for stance and implication, not only content: "Where does the lecturer concede a point? Where is she ironic? What does she imply about students who say 'but I read my notes five times'?" Ask strong learners to take the second listening without the first (turn the paper over and start a clean page) and compare. Early finishers write a one-sentence summary (under 30 words), then a one-sentence critique: "The talk assumes that… but it doesn't address…"

L1 watch-outs
  • Waiting to hear every word clearly. Speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Tagalog, Turkish, Japanese, Cantonese) are used to each syllable being audible, so reduced function words sound like "missing" language, and they stop to look for it. Teach them to listen for the stressed syllables and accept that the small words are small.
  • Note-taking in full sentences. Learners from education systems that value copying the teacher's words (many, not only one region) transcribe. The fix is the rule "no verb be, no articles." Praise the ugliest, most efficient notes.
  • Summary with "the" and "about": "The lecture is about the cramming." Arabic, French, Spanish and Portuguese speakers overuse the with general nouns; Russian, Chinese, Japanese and Korean speakers may drop it where it's needed ("Speaker argues…"). Keep it light: one board example.
  • Reporting verbs + infinitive: "She suggests us to test ourselves." Review from B2: suggests testing ourselves / suggests (that) we test ourselves.
  • Summaries that judge. In some academic traditions a summary includes the writer's evaluation. At this stage, ask for the speaker's argument only; evaluation belongs in a separate sentence ("In my view…").

7.2 · C21

Pronunciation & Intonation for Fluency

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • speak with English stress-timing: stress content words and use weak forms for function words (to /tə/, of /əv/, and /ən/, can /kən/, for /fɚ/)
  • divide speech into thought groups and pause at meaning boundaries, not wherever they run out of breath, and hear how pausing changes meaning (my brother, who lives in Rome, … vs my brother who lives in Rome…)
  • place the tonic (the main stress in a thought group) on new information and move it for contrast (I didn't say SHE took it)
  • use intonation for attitude: a fall for certainty and completion, a rise for yes/no questions and "more is coming," a fall-rise for reservation and an implied "but," and avoid rising statements (uptalk) when they need to sound confident
  • mark up a text (CAPITALS, /, ↘ ↗ ↘↗, ‿) and shadow a model, then deliver a short extract clearly

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: stress-timing. In English the stressed syllables come at roughly regular intervals; everything between them is squeezed to fit. Weak forms are correct, standard pronunciation, and they make the stressed words stand out.

"I want TO go." Is a strong TO clearer? (No, it sounds less natural and pulls the focus to the wrong word.) · Which words carry the meaning in "I'd like to book a table for two"? (like, book, table, two.) Which words are weak? (I'd, to, a, for.) · Is a weak form lazy? (No, it's standard.)

Meaning: thought groups. Speech arrives in chunks of meaning with a small pause and a pitch movement at the end of each. Pause placement works like punctuation.

"My brother, / who lives in Rome, / is a chef." How many brothers? (One; the Rome part is extra information.) · "My brother who lives in Rome / is a chef." How many brothers? (More than one: this one, the Rome one, is a chef.) · A speaker pauses after "the" to breathe. Does it help the listener? (No: it breaks a meaning unit.)

Meaning: tonic placement and contrast. By default the tonic falls on the last content word of the group. It moves forward to new or contrasted information, and given information is de-stressed.

"Where's the report?" "It's on your DESK." Why not "It's on your desk, the RE-port"? (The report is already known; stressing it sounds like a contrast with something else.) · "I didn't say SHE took it." Did someone take it? (Yes.) Who? (Not her, someone else.) · "No, the RED one." What did the other person think? (Another color.)

Meaning: intonation for attitude.

"The food was nice ↘↗…" Did the speaker love the evening? (Not completely.) What word is hidden? (But.) · "Where do you work ↗?" with a rise. Wrong? (Not wrong, but a wh-question normally falls; a rise can sound surprised or like "Did you say…?") · "I finished the report ↗?" In an interview, how does the speaker sound? (Unsure, as if asking for approval.) · "First ↗, we book; then ↘, we pay." Why the rise? (More is coming.) Why the fall? (The list is finished.)

Pronunciation: American weak forms and the can / can't contrast. In American English can is normally weak, /kən/ ("I can COME"), while can't keeps a full vowel and is often stressed, /kænt/ ("I CAN'T come"), and the final /t/ may be barely audible. Drill script

Shadowing & the Delivery Clinic — how to run it
  • Shadowing means speaking along with a model, half a second behind it, copying the melody and rhythm rather than just the words. Do it first with you as the model (whole class, quietly, so nobody is exposed), then in pairs. Tell learners to exaggerate: over-doing the stress while it is conscious is exactly the practice stage.
  • Delivery Clinic, groups of 3. Roles rotate: speaker (delivers 60–90 seconds from a marked-up page), coach (holds the speaker's marked-up page and the coach's checklist, ticks what happened and chooses one thing to change), listener (doesn't look at any page and says the main point in one sentence at the end: that's the real test of clarity).
  • The coach's checklist: ☐ content words stressed, small words weak · ☐ pauses at the / marks, not in the middle of a phrase · ☐ statements end with a fall (no uptalk) · ☐ the tonic moves for contrast where marked · ☐ one fall-rise used where the text implies a "but" (if any) · ☐ pace: not rushed. Peer feedback protocol: the coach says one thing that worked ("Your pauses made the list really clear"), then one thing to change, with a demonstration ("Try 'crucially' slower, like this…"). Never more than one change per round.
  • Second try. If time allows, the speaker repeats only the part the coach chose. The group listens for the difference. This is the lesson's key moment: learners hear that one change makes them easier to follow.
  • Coaches, not judges. Accent is not on the checklist. If a coach comments on a sound ("your /θ/…"), redirect: "Today we're only coaching rhythm and melody."
  • Give the coach role to a dominant speaker first: they have to listen with the page and give exactly one comment.
ICQs before the Delivery Clinic

(The main point, in one sentence.) · How many changes does the coach suggest? (One.) · Does the coach talk about accent? (No, only stress, pauses and intonation.) · How long is each speaker's turn? Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with sound first, symbols second. Let the learner tap and hum the rhythm before marking anything. Give a pre-marked text (Script 7.2-B is fully marked up in the workbook) instead of asking them to mark their own. Reduce the mark-up code to two symbols: CAPITALS and /. In the clinic, give a 30-second extract (the last three sentences of the recap). Target: five clear beats and no pause in the middle of a phrase.

Stretch (toward C2)

Work on prosody for irony and diplomacy: the same words as sincere praise, faint praise (a low fall-rise) and irony (an exaggerated, slow high fall: "Oh, great."). Ask strong learners to mark up a paragraph from a real talk they heard for homework and explain why the speaker put the tonic where they did. Discuss pitch range: a wide range sounds engaged; a narrow, flat range can sound bored or unfriendly even with polite words. Early finishers write their own "one sentence, many meanings" card with five readings.

L1 watch-outs
  • Syllable-timed rhythm (equal weight on every syllable): Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Tagalog, Turkish, Japanese, Cantonese, Mandarin, Vietnamese and many others. Learners sound clear sound-by-sound but hard to follow. The desk-tapping drill is the fix.
  • Strong forms everywhere: learners who learned English from print pronounce to, of, for, and, can, was fully. Explain that full forms are for emphasis or the end of a sentence ("Who's it for?").
  • Stress on the last word by default: French speakers tend to put stress on the last syllable of every group and find contrastive stress unnatural (French uses c'est… qui instead). Link this to C1 Lesson 2.3 (clefts): English can do the same job with voice alone.
  • Tone languages (Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese, Thai, Yoruba): pitch is part of each word, so learners may not expect it to carry attitude across a sentence, and the fall-rise can come out as a word-level tone. Practice it on one word first ("Well ↘↗…").
  • Flat or rising statements: some varieties (and some learners' nerves) push statements up at the end; the listener hears uncertainty. Russian speakers often produce yes/no questions with a sharp rise-fall on the key word, which can sound like a demand in English; practice the gentle rise.
  • Vowel added to break consonant clusters: Spanish and Portuguese "es-tress", Arabic and Persian "fi-rist," Japanese "su-to-re-su." The added syllable breaks the beat. Tap the word as one beat: "STRESS."
  • Final consonants and linking: Vietnamese, Cantonese and Thai speakers may drop final consonants, so linking ("turn‿it‿off") disappears too. Practice linking as a way to keep the final sound: it moves onto the next vowel.
  • can / can't: everyone. Weak can /kən/ vs full can't /kænt/ in American English.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: interrupting vs coming in. Coming in is a request that leaves the speaker a moment to yield; interrupting takes the floor.

"Could I just come in here?" Is this a question or an instruction? (A question: the speaker can say "Let me just finish.") What does just do? (Makes the request smaller, more polite.) · Someone talks over a colleague with a brilliant idea. Is that OK in an English-language seminar? (Usually no: it costs goodwill.) · "What are your thoughts, Priya?" Why use the name? (It draws in a quieter person and shows you're tracking everyone.)

Meaning: building and paraphrasing.

"Building on Anna's point about skills…" Whose idea is this? (Anna's, extended.) How does Anna feel? (Heard.) · "So if I understand you correctly, your worry is coverage?" Is the speaker agreeing? (Not yet: checking.) What can the other person do? (Confirm or correct.) Does a paraphrase sound weak? (No: attentive and precise.)

Meaning: respectful challenge. The idea is challenged, not the person. The acknowledgment must be genuine, and it must name something specific.

"I take your point about speed, but I'm not sure we've thought through the cost." Does the speaker agree? (Partly: about speed.) Is the challenge weak? (No, it's clear: the cost.) · "No, that's wrong." Is the view stronger? (No, only the temperature is higher.) · "Great point, BUT…" said very fast every time. Does it sound genuine? (No: the acknowledgment must be specific.)

The Seminar — how to run it
  • Groups of 5–6, pre-planned: mixed first languages, one confident and one quiet learner in each, dominant speakers spread out. Each group discusses one reading card. The six readings: the four-day workweek, phones in meetings and classes, exams vs portfolios, car-free downtown streets, AI writing tools in college courses, and spaced practice vs cramming (the C20 lecture topic). All readings are short, neutral briefings with two positions and one seminar question.
  • Roles (role cards): Facilitator: opens with the question, hands over by name, watches the clock, refocuses ("Could we come back to the main question?"). Summarizer: tracks each person's key point on the observer grid, gives a half-time summary ("So far we broadly agree that…, but we're split on…") and the final summary. Challenger: tests claims on both sides, respectfully ("What's the evidence for that?" "Isn't there a risk that…?"), and must acknowledge before every challenge. Contributors A and B: argue the side printed on their card, not their own view. Observer (sixth learner, or rotate): doesn't speak; tallies moves on the observer sheet and gives feedback at the end.
  • Move cards. Each learner gets four cards (e.g., COME IN · BUILD ON · PARAPHRASE · RESPECTFUL CHALLENGE). Each time they use a move, they lay the card face up. Goal: all four down by the end. When your cards are all down, you may only speak to hand over to someone who still has cards. This limits dominant speakers and gives quiet ones a reason to speak, without anyone being singled out.
  • Timing. Call "Half time: summarizer" at minute 6 and "One minute: summarizer" at minute 11. The facilitator closes: "Thanks, everyone. So, to sum up…" then hands to the summarizer.
  • Observer feedback protocol (3 min): two strengths first, each with a name and a quote ("Lena built on Omar's point about training: 'That ties in with…'"), then one move nobody used, as a group target, not a personal criticism. No comments on accent or grammar here; that's your job in the feedback stage.
  • Your job during the seminar: circulate with the three-column sheet (range · accuracy · appropriacy). Don't join a group. If a group stalls, hand the facilitator a prompt card quietly ("Ask the challenger to respond to B's last point").
  • If you have fewer than five learners in a group, merge the summarizer and the facilitator. With a very large class, run two rounds of 10 minutes with new roles for the second round.

L1 watch-outs
  • Directness norms travel badly in both directions. Learners from more direct discussion cultures (for example Dutch, German, Russian, Hebrew or Polish) may sound blunt with "No, that's wrong," while learners from cultures that value waiting and harmony (for example Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Vietnamese or Thai) may never come in at all. Present both as habits to adjust, never as national traits, and practice the same moves with everyone.
  • Overlap as engagement: in many Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and Arabic conversations, talking at the same time shows interest. In an English-language seminar it reads as interrupting. The coming-in phrase is the bridge.
  • "I am agree" / "I am not agree" (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, French, Portuguese and Italian): agree is a verb: "I agree," "I don't agree."
  • "Discuss about" (Spanish discutir sobre, Arabic and others): discuss the idea, no preposition. Also "explain me" → explain to me.
  • "According to me" (French selon moi, Hindi, Urdu and Bengali speakers, Spanish según yo): use In my view, As I see it; according to introduces someone else's view.
  • Literal politeness: "Please give me the floor" or "I want to say something" (a direct translation of a polite form in the learner's language) can sound abrupt in English. "Could I just…" does the same job.

Recap on slide 18, "take it in, give it out, share the floor": signposts, the recap test and Cornell notes (take it in) → stress the new, pause at meaning, fall to finish, fall-rise for "but" (give it out) → come in, build on, paraphrase, acknowledge before you challenge, summarize (share the floor). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Revisit it in the C23 warmer; Signpost Bingo is ready for that if you didn't use it in C20 or C21.

Watch for in Module 8: Lesson 8.1 (fluency and spontaneity) builds directly on C22. Thinking-time phrases (That's a good question… · Let me think about that for a second) are the solo version of "holding the floor," and paraphrasing around a missing word uses the same skills as paraphrasing a colleague. Lesson 8.3 (the position paper and its defense) is the module's real test: learners will present with signposting (7.1), deliver with clear prosody (7.2) and handle challenges from the floor (7.3). Keep the seminar roles and the coach's checklist for that class.

Final assessment (C26) covers all eight modules. From this module, the listening section uses a signposted talk with comprehension and inference questions (signpost functions, main idea vs example, stance), and the speaking interview and discussion task reward turn-taking, building on the examiner's or partner's points, paraphrasing and respectful disagreement, delivered with clear stress, sensible pausing and statements that fall.
