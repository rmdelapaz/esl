Advanced English Workbook · Module 7

Listening, Speaking & Delivery

Three workshops on live spoken English. Follow a lecture by its structure and capture it as a map rather than a transcript. Make your own speech easy to follow through stress, pausing and intonation. Then take part in a seminar where you come in, build on other people's ideas, paraphrase, challenge respectfully and help the group reach a conclusion.

Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Level C1 Name: ____________________
Before you start

This module is about doing rather than memorizing: listening twice, marking up scripts, speaking, coaching and discussing. You will not catch every word of the lecture, and nobody expects you to. The aim of the pronunciation work is clear rhythm and melody, not a different accent. Recording yourself is recommended but always private. When a task is about your life, you decide what to share: your real life, a new identity card or something invented. In the seminar you argue the side printed on your card, which may or may not be your own view.

Language box — what each signpost tells you to do
PreviewingToday I'll argue that… · This talk falls into three parts.Draw the map: leave space for each part.
SequencingFirst… · Turning to my second point… · That brings me to… · Finally…Start a new heading, flush left.
ExampleFor instance… · A case in point is… · To illustrate… · The research shows…Indent it and keep it short. Ask: "an example of what?"
EmphasisThe key thing is… · Crucially… · Here's the crucial part…A main idea: write it in full and star it.
DigressionAs an aside… · In passing… · I won't dwell on…Optional detail: safe to skip.
RecapTo recap… · In short… · The upshot is… · So, to sum up…The main ideas, distilled: put them at the top of your takeaways.

Cues that let you predict: It's widely believed that… → a reversal is coming · Now, you might think… → an objection, then the speaker's answer · So why does this matter? → a main idea next · There are two problems with this. → listen for exactly two.

Stance: hedges (arguably, to some extent, one explanation is) make a claim tentative; boosters (clearly, undeniably, one of the most reliable findings) show commitment.

Exercise 3 — Main idea, detail or aside?

Read this extract from a talk on sleep. Write M (main idea), D (supporting detail) or A (aside) after each sentence.

(a) The central point I want to make is that sleep is not the opposite of learning; it is part of it.

(b) In one set of experiments, people who slept after practicing a finger-tapping sequence performed it faster the next day than people who stayed awake.

(c) As an aside, I'm told the coffee machine was the hardest-working piece of equipment in the lab.

(d) Crucially, the brain seems to replay and strengthen new memories while we sleep.

(e) For instance, patterns of brain activity recorded while rats ran through a maze have been observed again while the rats slept.

(f) So the upshot is simple: an all-nighter before an exam may cost you exactly the memories you were trying to build.

Your teacher will read a five-minute lecture twice. The first time, your teacher will stop four times so you can write. The second time is at natural speed, with no stops: use a different color to fill the gaps. Take notes on your Cornell sheet or on this page.

Part A — Cornell notes. Write your notes on the right while you listen. After listening, add cue questions on the left and a summary at the bottom.

Part B — Comprehension. Answer from your notes, not from memory of single words.

1. What is the speaker's thesis?

2. What are the three parts of the talk, in order?

3. How quickly did Ebbinghaus forget, and what happened each time he reviewed?

4. In the example of the two students, how is the study time organized, and who remembers more a week later?

5. What did the two groups do in the 2006 study, and how did the results change between five minutes and one week?

6. What is the one thing the speaker asks the audience to remember?

Inference. These answers are not stated directly. Discuss them with a partner and note the words that support your answer.

7. Which part of the talk does the speaker value most? Which two phrases tell you?

8. The speaker calls Ebbinghaus's methods "frankly, rather tedious." What is this comment doing, and does it belong in your notes?

9. "It's widely believed that the more hours you put in, the more you'll remember, and in one sense, of course, that's true." What is the speaker doing here, and what did you expect to hear next?

10. How certain is the speaker about (a) the spacing effect and (b) the explanation for it? Quote the words.

11. What does "the feeling of fluency is not the same thing as learning" mean? Why does rereading create that feeling?

12. What would the speaker probably say to a student who plans to reread her notes five times the night before an exam?

Dialogue — after the lecture

Lena: Did you get the three parts? I lost her somewhere around the German psychologist.

Omar: Forgetting, spacing, testing. She previewed them right at the start: "This talk falls into three parts."

Lena: Right. And the nonsense syllables? Did you write those down?

Omar: No. She said "as an aside" and "I won't dwell on his methods," so I let it go. What I did star was "the curve isn't fixed."

Lena: Good call. I've got "review → curve flattens" with a question mark. And the two students?

Omar: That's just the example. The point is that when you study matters as much as how long.

Lena: So the students are the packaging. Was the 2006 study the main idea, then?

Omar: The study's the evidence. The main idea is the one she flagged: "I can't stress this enough: the feeling of fluency isn't the same as learning."

Lena: And the upshot was: study at intervals and test yourself.

Omar: Exactly. Close your notes and see what you can remember. Which is what we should be doing right now, actually.

Say it with the right melody. A paraphrase ends with a rise (it's a real question). An acknowledgment takes a fall-rise ("I take your point ↘↗…"). The challenge itself falls, calmly.

Dialogue — a seminar in action

A team discusses whether to switch to a four-day week.

1 Dana: I think a four-day week would boost productivity. People work better when they're rested. What are your thoughts, Ben?

2 Ben: Building on what you said, there's real evidence for that: several trials found output holding steady on four days. To add to that, it's a huge draw for hiring.

3 Priya: Could I just come in here? I take your point about productivity, but I'd question whether it works for a client-facing team. We can't just close on Fridays.

4 Dana: So if I understand you correctly, your worry is coverage: clients expecting us five days a week?

5 Priya: Exactly. That's my main concern.

6 Marco: If I could just add something: we could stagger the days off, so someone's always in. That ties in with Priya's point: it keeps coverage and gives everyone the shorter week.

7 Priya: Up to a point, yes, but staggering means we're never all in on the same day. Isn't there a risk that collaboration suffers?

8 Dana: That's fair. Where we do agree, though, is that the shorter week is worth trying. It's the logistics we're split on.

9 Marco: So where does that leave us? Maybe we trial it for two months and measure both output and coverage?

10 Dana: So, to sum up, we broadly agree on trialing a four-day week, staggered, with a review after two months. Shall we move on to who'd own the trial?
