F · Seminar Pack — Reading Cards (7.3)

One reading per group; print one copy per group member. Each card is a short, neutral briefing with two positions (Side A and Side B) and one seminar question. Contributors argue the side printed on their role card, not their own view. The organizations and towns named in the questions are invented.

Reading 1 · work

The four-day workweek

In recent years, companies in several countries have run trials in which staff work four days a week for the same pay, with the goal of keeping output the same. Many participating companies reported that productivity held steady, that staff were less tired and less likely to quit, and most chose to continue after the trial. Critics point out that companies that volunteer for such trials may be the ones most likely to succeed; that many jobs (health care, retail, customer support) need people present five or more days a week; and that squeezing the same work into fewer days can raise stress.

Question: Should Brightline Services, a 40-person customer-support company, move to a four-day week?

Side A: Yes: pilot it, with staggered days off. · Side B: No: not for a client-facing team.

Reading 2 · technology

Phones in meetings and classes

Phones are now part of almost every meeting and classroom. Some studies suggest that switching between a task and a phone reduces attention and memory, and many teachers and managers say that phones on the table change the atmosphere even when nobody touches them. On the other hand, phones are also tools: learners use them for dictionaries, translation and photos of the board; workers use them for calendars and notes; and parents and caregivers may need to be reachable in an emergency. Many adults also dislike rules that feel as though they are aimed at children.

Question: Should the Riverside Adult Learning Center ban phones in classes and staff meetings?

Side A: Yes: a clear ban, with an emergency exception. · Side B: No: agree on norms instead of a ban.

Reading 3 · education

Final exams or portfolios?

Many college courses still end with a final exam: one timed test that counts for a large part of the grade. Supporters say exams are fair, because everyone does the same task under the same conditions; they are quick to grade and hard to outsource. A growing number of programs use portfolios instead: a collection of work produced during the course, often with a reflection. Supporters say portfolios reflect real tasks, show growth over time and reduce the pressure of a single day. Critics reply that portfolios take far longer to grade, are harder to compare fairly, and are easier to complete with outside help.

Question: Should the business program at Lakeside Community College replace its final exams with portfolios?

Side A: Yes: portfolios. · Side B: No: keep the final exam.

Reading 4 · city life

A car-free Main Street?

Many towns and cities have closed central streets to cars, permanently or on weekends, and turned them into spaces for walking, outdoor dining and markets. Supporters say pedestrian streets are safer and quieter, and that people on foot linger and spend in local shops. Some business owners worry about the opposite: that customers who drive, including older and disabled shoppers, will go elsewhere, and that deliveries will become harder. Results vary from place to place, and much seems to depend on parking nearby, public transportation and how the change is managed.

Question: Should the town of Maple Falls close three blocks of Main Street to cars every weekend from May to October?

Side A: Yes: close it and review after one season. · Side B: No: keep it open to cars.

Reading 5 · technology & education

AI writing tools in college courses

AI writing assistants can now draft, summarize and correct text in seconds. Some instructors welcome them: they are part of many workplaces, they can give instant feedback on grammar and organization, and they may help students with disabilities or those writing in a second language. Others worry that when a tool does the drafting, students skip the thinking that writing is meant to develop, and that grades no longer show what a student can do. Many colleges are still deciding their policies, and practice varies widely between courses.

Question: Should students at Lakeside Community College be allowed to use AI writing tools for their written assignments?

Side A: Yes, with clear disclosure of how the tool was used. · Side B: No, not for graded writing.

Reading 6 · learning (C20 topic)

Weekly quizzes or a review week?

Research on memory has found two strong effects: study spread out over time tends to be remembered longer than study crammed into one session (the spacing effect), and trying to recall information strengthens memory more than rereading it (the testing effect). Some teachers therefore replace a final "review week" with short, frequent quizzes. Supporters say quizzes turn every week into practice. Critics say frequent graded quizzes add stress, especially for adult learners with jobs and families who can't always study every week, and that a course grade should reflect what learners can do at the end, not along the way.

Question: Should this course replace its final review week with short weekly quizzes that count toward the grade?

Side A: Yes: weekly graded quizzes. · Side B: No: keep the review week (or make quizzes ungraded).

F · Seminar Pack — Role Cards (7.3)

One set per group. With five learners, drop the observer and ask the summarizer to keep the tally. With four, merge the facilitator and summarizer.

Role · facilitator

You run the discussion

  • Open: "Thanks, everyone. Today's question is… Who'd like to start? / What are your thoughts, ___?"
  • Hand over by name, especially to people who haven't spoken.
  • Refocus: "That's interesting, but could we come back to the main question?"
  • At the end: "Thanks, everyone. ___, would you sum up for us?"
Role · summarizer

You keep the thread

  • Note each person's key point (a few words each).
  • At half time: "So far, we broadly agree that…, but we're split on…"
  • At the end: agreement · disagreement · next step or open question. Link at least two people's points by name.
Role · challenger

You test every claim

  • Challenge both sides, fairly.
  • Always acknowledge first: "I take your point about…, but…"
  • Useful: "What's the evidence for that?" · "Isn't there a risk that…?" · "I'd question whether…" · "Up to a point, yes, but…"
  • Firm on the idea, warm to the person.
Role · contributor A

You argue Side A

  • Argue Side A as printed on the reading card, whatever your own view.
  • Make at least two points, and build on your partner's points.
  • When challenged: acknowledge, then answer. You may concede a small point ("That's fair, but…").
Role · contributor B

You argue Side B

  • Argue Side B as printed on the reading card, whatever your own view.
  • Make at least two points, and paraphrase Side A before you answer it: "So if I understand you correctly…"
  • Look for common ground before the end.
Role · observer

You listen and give feedback

  • Don't speak during the seminar.
  • Tally every move on the observer sheet, and write down one exact quote per person if you can.
  • Feedback (3 min): two strengths first, each with a name and a quote; then one move nobody used, as a target for the group. No comments on accent or grammar.

7.1-A · Mini-lecture: "Why Cramming Fails" (Workbook 7.1, Ex 5 · about 5 minutes · ‖ = stop in Listening 1 only)

Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to talk about something every one of you has done at least once: cramming. You know the routine. The exam is tomorrow, the coffee is strong, and you read your notes over and over until two in the morning. What I want to argue today is that cramming is one of the least efficient ways to learn anything, and that two simple changes to how you study can make a remarkable difference. This talk falls into three parts. First, I'll look at how quickly we forget. Second, I'll explain why spacing out your study works so much better than cramming. And third, the part I care about most, I'll show you why testing yourself beats rereading. ‖

So, let's start with forgetting. In the 1880s, a German psychologist called Hermann Ebbinghaus ran a series of experiments on the only subject he could rely on completely: himself. As an aside, he memorized long lists of nonsense syllables, things like "dax" and "bok," because he wanted material that had no meaning at all. I won't dwell on his methods; they were, frankly, rather tedious. What matters is what he found. Memory fades fast. Within a day, he had lost well over half of what he'd learned, and the decline continued, more slowly, for weeks. This pattern is now known as the forgetting curve. But here's the crucial part: the curve isn't fixed. Every time he reviewed the material, the curve flattened. He forgot more slowly the next time. ‖

That brings me to my second point: spacing. It's widely believed that the more hours you put in, the more you'll remember, and in one sense, of course, that's true. But the research shows that when you study matters at least as much as how long. Compare two students. Both study a list of vocabulary for three hours in total. The first does all three hours on Sunday night. The second does one hour on Monday, one on Wednesday and one on Friday. A week later, who remembers more? The second student, and often by a wide margin. This is the spacing effect, and it's one of the most reliable findings in the whole of psychology. Why does it work? One explanation is that a little forgetting is actually useful. When you come back to something you've half-forgotten, your brain has to work to rebuild it, and that effort makes the memory stronger. ‖

Which leads me to my third point, and the one I'd most like you to take away: testing yourself. Now, you might think a test is simply a way of measuring what you know. In fact, it's one of the best ways of learning. In a well-known study from 2006, the psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students a short passage to learn. Some students read it several times. Others read it once and then tried to recall it on a blank page. Five minutes later, the rereaders did slightly better. But a week later, the picture had reversed: the students who had tested themselves remembered considerably more. To put it simply, rereading feels productive. The text looks familiar, so we assume we know it. Retrieval is harder, and that's precisely why it works. I can't stress this enough: the feeling of fluency is not the same thing as learning. ‖

So, to recap. We forget quickly, but every review slows the forgetting down. Spacing your study beats cramming the same number of hours into one night. And pulling information out of your memory does far more than pushing it in again. The upshot is that the most effective learners aren't necessarily the ones who study longest; they're the ones who study at the right intervals and test themselves along the way. If there's one thing I'd like you to remember from this morning, it's this: don't just review your notes. Close them, and see what you can remember. Thank you.
