C · Critical-Reading Seminar — Role Cards & Pilot Fact Sheet (6.1)

One set of four per group. Give the Reporter card to the strongest or most dominant speaker (they must summarize the group, not argue), and the Evidence Checker card to a careful reader. In a group of three, the Chair also reports. The fact sheet is the information gap: only the Evidence Checker sees it.

Role · Chair

You run the discussion

  • Read out the two questions. Keep the group on them.
  • Everyone speaks once before anyone speaks twice.
  • At 7 minutes, ask for a provisional verdict on each question.

Let's start with the first question. · We haven't heard from… yet. · Can we come back to the difference between the case and the argument? · So, where are we?

Role · Evidence Checker

You hold the full figures

  • You have the company's official pilot figures (below / attached). Nobody else has them.
  • Share a figure only when someone makes a claim that the figure tests.
  • Report neutrally. Don't draw the conclusion for the group.

That's true, but compared with… · The full figure is actually… · The article doesn't mention that… · Over the whole six months, …

Role · Devil's Advocate

You argue against the group

  • Whatever the group seems to be concluding, argue the opposite at least twice.
  • You are not giving your own opinion. Be fair, not rude.
  • Use evidence from the articles, not personal stories.

Let me push back on that. · Couldn't Keane / Whitfield reply that…? · Isn't that just as true of the other article? · Are we being fair to…?

Role · Reporter

You speak for the group

  • Take notes on the group's reasons, not your own.
  • Prepare the four-move verdict (workbook frame): claims → evidence → weaknesses → verdict.
  • Speak for 60 seconds. Use precise verbs.

Our group felt that… · Whitfield contends…, whereas Keane… · On balance, we found… more persuasive, although…

D · Fallacy Card Sort (6.1)

Cut apart and shuffle; one envelope per pair. Round 1: match names to definitions. Round 2: add the examples. Round 3: find each fallacy in Article A or B and write the sentence number on the name card. The first five are the core set from the online lesson (give only these to learners who need literacy support); the last four are extensions.

NameUnsupported generalization
Definitiona sweeping claim about a whole group, based on little or no evidence
Example"Everyone who works from home spends half the day watching TV."
NameFalse cause
Definitionassuming that because B happened after A, A caused B
Example"We bought new chairs in March, and sales rose in April. The chairs clearly boosted sales."
NameCherry-picking
Definitionchoosing only the data that supports your view and ignoring the rest
Example"The new app is a hit: just read these five-star reviews." (There are 400 one-star reviews.)
NameFalse dilemma
Definitionpresenting only two options when others exist
Example"Either we buy the new software this month, or we fall behind forever."
NameAppeal to emotion
Definitionusing feelings (pity, fear, pride) in place of evidence
Example"How can you close the playground when you look at these children's faces?"
NameStraw man
Definitionattacking a distorted, weaker version of the opposing view
Example"My colleague wants shorter meetings. Apparently she thinks we should never talk to each other."
NameSlippery slope
Definitionclaiming that one small step will inevitably lead to an extreme result, without evidence for each step
Example"If we let staff wear jeans on Fridays, soon people will come to work in pajamas."
NameBandwagon
Definitionarguing that something is right or good because many people do it
Example"Every café in town now uses oat milk, so it must be the healthiest choice."
NameAttacking the person
Definitioncriticizing the person making an argument instead of the argument itself
Example"Why listen to his budget plan? He can't even keep his own desk tidy."

E · Essay Question Cards — Assigned Sides (6.2)

One card per learner. Say before you hand them out: "You argue the side on your card, not necessarily your own view." Evidence notes are invented but realistic; learners may add their own reasoning and examples. Cut off the teacher strip of model theses at the end.

Question 1 · Side A

Should employers let staff work from home at least two days a week? — Yes

  • Staff at one engineering firm saved an average of 52 minutes a day on commuting during a hybrid trial.
  • Job applications to the firm rose by 40% after hybrid work was advertised.
  • Shared desks cut office costs by 15%.
Question 1 · Side B

Should employers let staff work from home at least two days a week? — No, not as a general right

  • In a staff survey, 61% of new employees said they learned their job more slowly when working remotely.
  • Team leaders reported fewer quick, informal problem-solving conversations.
  • Staff in roles that cannot be done remotely (reception, warehouse) felt the policy was unfair.
Question 2 · Side A

Should high schools ban phones during the school day? — Yes

  • A school that introduced a ban reported that scores on focused reading tasks rose by 6% in a year.
  • Teachers reported more face-to-face discussion at lunchtime.
  • Reported online bullying incidents during school hours fell.
Question 2 · Side B

Should high schools ban phones during the school day? — No

  • Many parents want to contact their children in emergencies.
  • Phones are used as dictionaries, calculators and cameras in lessons.
  • Teachers at a school with a ban spent 10–15 minutes a day enforcing it; self-regulation may be a more useful skill to teach.
Question 3 · Side A

Should public libraries stop charging late fines? — Yes

  • After one city library dropped fines, membership rose by 12% and more overdue books came back.
  • Fines discourage low-income families from borrowing at all.
  • Staff spent around 6 hours a week collecting small fines.
Question 3 · Side B

Should public libraries stop charging late fines? — No

  • Fines at one library raised $18,000 a year, which paid for children's reading programs.
  • Other borrowers wait longer for popular books when there is no deadline pressure.
  • Small fines can be waived for hardship without abolishing them.
Question 4 · Side A

Should employers provide free healthy food at work? — Yes

  • At one company, staff who ate the free lunch took 20% fewer afternoon breaks for snacks.
  • In exit interviews, benefits like meals were among the top five reasons staff stayed.
  • Shift workers often have no healthy options near the workplace at night.
Question 4 · Side B

Should employers provide free healthy food at work? — No

  • A free-lunch program for 200 staff costs roughly $300,000 a year.
  • In a survey, 55% of staff said they would prefer the same value added to their pay.
  • Food preferences, allergies and dietary needs vary widely; one menu rarely suits everyone.
Question 5 · Side A

Should every new employee have a mentor for their first six months? — Yes

  • At one hospital, first-year staff turnover fell from 25% to 14% after mentoring began.
  • New staff with mentors reached full productivity about two months sooner.
  • Mentors reported that mentoring improved their own leadership skills.
Question 5 · Side B

Should every new employee have a mentor for their first six months? — No, not for every employee

  • Mentoring takes an experienced employee about 2 hours a week away from their own work.
  • Experienced hires often find compulsory mentoring unnecessary.
  • Poorly matched mentor pairs were rated as "unhelpful" by a third of participants.
Question 6 · Side A

Should restaurants be required to show calorie counts on menus? — Yes

  • A study of chain restaurants found customers ordered meals with slightly fewer calories (about 4–6%) after labels appeared.
  • Some restaurants reformulated their highest-calorie dishes.
  • Customers cannot make informed choices without basic information.
Question 6 · Side B

Should restaurants be required to show calorie counts on menus? — No

  • Calculating calories for a changing menu can cost a small restaurant thousands of dollars a year.
  • Calories say nothing about salt, fiber or overall nutrition.
  • Eating-disorder specialists warn that calorie labels can harm vulnerable customers.
Teacher strip · model theses (cut off before class)

1A While hybrid work demands deliberate effort to support new staff, employers should offer at least two remote days a week, because it widens recruitment and cuts costs without reducing output. 1B Although remote work suits some roles, a general right to two remote days would slow the learning of new staff and divide the workforce; flexibility should be decided role by role.

2A Although phones have legitimate uses, a school-day ban protects the concentration and face-to-face relationships that learning depends on. 2B While phones can distract, an outright ban removes useful tools and teaches avoidance rather than the self-regulation students will need as adults.

3A Although fines raise modest revenue, abolishing them would bring more people, and more books, back to public libraries. 3B While fines can deter some families, a system of small fines with hardship waivers is fairer to all borrowers than abolishing them.

4A Although free food is a significant cost, it is a sound investment in the health, retention and fairness of treatment of shift workers. 4B While healthy eating matters, free workplace food is an expensive and inflexible benefit; the same money is better spent on pay or better break facilities.

5A Although mentoring takes experienced staff away from their own work, it repays that time many times over in retention and faster learning. 5B While mentoring helps many newcomers, making it compulsory for everyone wastes senior time; it should be offered where it is wanted and well matched.

6A Although calorie labels are an imperfect measure of nutrition, requiring them gives customers basic information and encourages healthier menus. 6B While transparency is desirable, mandatory calorie counts impose real costs on small businesses and offer a narrow, sometimes harmful, picture of healthy eating.

H · Game — Fallacy Bingo (C18 warmer · C20 warmer)

A pair that completes a line calls "Bingo!" and must name and justify each marked square ("Number 7 is a slippery slope, because one coffee break doesn't inevitably lead to nobody working."). A wrong justification = the game continues. Some sentences fit two squares: accept either if justified.

I · Listening & Dictation Scripts (teacher)

Read at a natural speed, twice: once for the gist, once for detail. The commentary should sound opinionated and warm, like local talk radio; the essay introduction measured and academic; the voicemail natural and a little rushed. The 🔊 buttons in the online lessons give learners extra listening at home.

6.1-A · "Second Opinion" on Riverside Radio (Workbook 6.1, Ex 1 · about 1 minute)

This is Second Opinion on Riverside Radio. I'm Frank Delaney. Let me say what everybody is already thinking: self-checkout machines are ruining our supermarkets, and it's time to get rid of them. Last week my neighbor, who is eighty-one, spent twenty minutes fighting with one of these machines while a young employee watched from a distance. Is that the kind of town we want to live in? And it isn't only older shoppers. According to the manager of one store on Pine Street, theft there rose by twenty percent after the machines arrived. Of course, the supermarket chains will tell you the machines shorten the lines. But since when did a few saved minutes matter more than human contact? The choice is simple: either we keep real cashiers, or we accept a future where nobody talks to anybody. I know which town I'd rather live in. That's my second opinion. Tell me yours.

Answers: claim = remove self-checkout machines · anecdote (the 81-year-old neighbor) · statistics/testimony from one store (unrepresentative; cause unclear) · weaknesses: false dilemma (last lines), bandwagon ("what everybody is already thinking"), concede-and-dismiss (shorter lines), appeal to emotion / rhetorical question · tone: indignant, nostalgic.

6.1-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. The author concedes that costs rose but downplays their significance. · 2. The figures suggest a correlation; they do not establish cause. · 3. This is an anecdote presented as if it were typical. · 4. Whose voice is missing from this article?

Check: concedes /kənˈsiːdz/ · significance · correlation · anecdote /ˈænɪkdoʊt/ (not "antidote"!) · as if it were (subjunctive, Lesson 1.3).

6.2-A · An introduction, read aloud (Workbook 6.2, Ex 1)

Walk through the center of almost any mid-sized American city and you will notice that one of its most common uses of land is not housing, shops or parks, but parking. Downtown lots sit half-empty for much of the week, yet city councils are often reluctant to touch them, fearing that fewer spaces will mean fewer shoppers. Although drivers' needs cannot simply be ignored, converting underused lots into public squares would bring more people downtown, not fewer, and would give struggling town centers a reason to exist beyond the car.

Answers: hook = the observation about parking · context = half-empty lots, reluctant councils · thesis = the last sentence · concession word = Although · likely body paragraphs: foot traffic and business; community life; counter-argument about drivers and access.

6.2-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. Admittedly, the scheme is expensive; nevertheless, its long-term benefits justify the cost. · 2. This objection overlooks the fact that most delays occur before nine a.m. · 3. Taken together, these points indicate that the policy should be extended. · 4. The wider implication is that training is an investment, not a perk.

Check the punctuation in 1 (semicolon + nevertheless + comma). Ask: "Which sentence concedes? Which rebuts? Which synthesizes?" (1 · 2 · 3; 4 = implication).
