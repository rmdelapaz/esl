🎓 Lesson 5.3: Academic & Formal Vocabulary

"We got a lot of data, then we looked at it and found something big." Every word is correct — and none of it belongs in an essay, a report, or a research summary. Rewritten in academic register: "We obtained substantial data, examined it, and identified a significant finding." Same meaning, different world. At C1 you're expected to move fluently into formal vocabulary when the genre calls for it — choosing the precise, often single-word academic term over the vague verb-plus-particle of speech — while keeping the judgement to know when plain words serve you better.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Replace everyday words with academic equivalents (get → obtain, show → demonstrate, big → significant, start → commence)
  • Recognise and use key Academic Word List families (analyse, significant, establish, factor, approach, evident, derive, constitute)
  • Use formal stance and reporting verbs (suggest, indicate, argue, highlight, imply) to present ideas precisely
  • Judge register — match vocabulary to formality, prefer precise single verbs over vague phrasal ones in writing, and avoid over-formalising speech

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Take a casual, conversational paragraph and rewrite it in academic register, upgrading the vocabulary; then reflect on which changes sharpened the meaning and which merely raised the formality.

Register & Word Choice

Every word carries a register — a signal of how formal, how technical, how personal it is. Get, show, and big are neutral-to-informal; obtain, demonstrate, and substantial are their formal, academic counterparts. Academic and formal English favours vocabulary that is precise, often single-word, frequently Latinate, and impersonal. Choosing it deliberately is the vocabulary side of the register control you've been building all through this module.

Watch a sentence rise in register through word choice alone:

Casual: "The study got some interesting results that show the plan didn't really work."

Academic: "The study yielded notable results that demonstrate the plan was largely ineffective."

Nothing structural changed — no passive, no nominalisation, no clause compression. Only the words shifted: got → yielded, interesting → notable, show → demonstrate, didn't really work → was largely ineffective. That is the whole focus of this lesson: register-appropriate lexical choice.

🧠 How this complements Lesson 3.1

In Lesson 3.1 you built academic style through nominalisation — restructuring grammar to turn actions into nouns. Here the lever is different: word choice. You can write an academic sentence with everyday grammar simply by upgrading the vocabulary, and the two skills combine powerfully. But the guiding principle is the same one you've met before — formality is a tool, not a virtue. The goal is the right register for the reader, not the fanciest word available. 🌱

Everyday → Academic Equivalents

The fastest way to lift a text's register is to swap high-frequency everyday words for their formal equivalents. Build a working table of these pairs — but always check the equivalent genuinely fits the meaning, because near-synonyms rarely overlap perfectly.

⚠️ Equivalents aren't interchangeable — mind the fit

An academic swap only works if the meaning survives it. Big maps to substantial for size/amount ("a substantial increase") but to significant for importance ("a significant discovery") — and significant also has a technical statistical sense. About splits two ways: approximately for a number, concerning / regarding for a topic. Reaching for the formal word without checking its precise sense produces the tell-tale error of the over-eager upgrader: a grand word used slightly wrongly.

Academic Word List Families

Researchers have identified a core set of words that appear across academic writing in every discipline — the Academic Word List (AWL). Knowing them, and their whole word families, is one of the highest-value vocabulary investments at C1. Learn each headword together with its noun, verb, adjective, and adverb forms, so you can move it around a sentence at will.

💡 Learn the family, not just the word

The real power of an AWL headword is its flexibility across parts of speech. From significant you get significance (noun), signify (verb), and significantly (adverb) — which lets you say "the result is significant", "the significance of the result", "the result signifies", or "results improved significantly", choosing whichever fits your sentence's structure. This pairs beautifully with nominalisation from Lesson 3.1: master the family and you can package the same idea as a noun, a verb, or a modifier at will.

Stance & Reporting Verbs

Academic writing constantly reports and evaluates what others claim — and English offers a graded set of stance verbs that signal exactly how strongly a source commits to an idea. Choosing among them is a precision instrument: suggest is tentative, argue is assertive, demonstrate claims proof. Using the wrong strength misrepresents the source.

VerbStrength / nuanceExample
suggest / indicatetentative — points towardsThe data suggest a possible link.
implyhints without statingThe results imply a deeper cause.
argue / claim / contendasserts a position (may be contested)The author argues that policy failed.
highlight / emphasisedraws attention toThe report highlights three risks.
demonstrate / showstrong — treats as provenThe trial demonstrates efficacy.
note / observe / point outneutral — states a factSmith notes that costs rose.
maintain / assertstrong, sometimes stubborn claimCritics maintain the model is flawed.
acknowledge / concedeadmits a counter-pointShe concedes the sample was small.
the evidence suggests that… the findings indicate that… the results imply that… the author argues that… this study demonstrates that… it should be noted that… the report highlights the need for… critics maintain that… the writer acknowledges that… this raises the question of…

💡 Hedging: the academic reflex to be cautious

Notice how many of the tentative verbs (suggest, indicate, appear, tend to) let a writer make a claim carefully — "The data suggest" rather than "The data prove." This cautious phrasing, called hedging, is a defining feature of academic register: it signals intellectual honesty about the limits of the evidence. Beginners often over-claim ("This proves…"); advanced writers hedge deliberately, reserving strong verbs like demonstrate for when the evidence truly warrants them.

Precise Verbs over Vague Ones

Formal writing tends to prefer a single, precise, often Latinate verb over the vague verb + particle (phrasal verb) combinations that dominate speech. Phrasal verbs are not wrong — they're the lifeblood of natural conversation — but in academic prose their single-word equivalents read as more exact and more formal.

Phrasal (spoken)Single verb (formal)
find outdiscover / determine
look intoinvestigate / examine
go up / go downincrease / decrease
put offpostpone
set upestablish / found
cut down onreduce
bring aboutcause / produce
get rid ofeliminate / remove

make upconstitute / comprise

Spoken: "We looked into it and found out that costs had gone up, so we cut down on spending."

Formal: "We investigated the matter and determined that costs had increased; spending was therefore reduced."

⚠️ Precise ≠ automatically better — and phrasal ≠ always wrong

Don't purge every phrasal verb on sight. Some have no neat single-word equivalent (find out often reads better than the stiff ascertain), and a page of unbroken Latinate verbs can feel cold and clotted. The principle is precision and register-fit, not particle-hunting. Reach for the single verb when it is genuinely more exact and the genre is formal — and leave a natural phrasal verb where it simply reads better.

Don't Over-Formalise

Here is the C1 judgement that separates a stylist from a thesaurus-user: formal vocabulary in the wrong place is a mistake, not an achievement. Dropping commence, utilise, and endeavour into a friendly email or casual speech doesn't sound educated — it sounds stilted, pompous, or faintly comic. Register is a two-way dial, and turning it too far up is as much an error as leaving it too low.

⚠️ Over-formalised — a bad → better example

Over-formal (in a text to a friend): "I shall endeavour to commence the utilisation of the new application forthwith."

Natural: "I'll try to start using the new app right away."

The formal version isn't wrong in a grammatical sense — it's tonally mismatched. To a friend it reads as absurdly stiff. Note too that utilise rarely earns its length: nine times out of ten, plain use is the better word, in any register. Longer and more Latinate is not the same as clearer.

A practical rule: match the vocabulary to the formality of the situation. Save the academic register for essays, reports, formal letters, and considered analysis. In conversation, casual messages, and warm correspondence, prefer the plain word — and never choose a formal synonym just to sound impressive. The most advanced writers are precise, not ornate; they reach for the formal word only when it is genuinely the right word.

💡 The "use / utilise" test for empty formality

When tempted by a grand synonym, ask whether it adds meaning or merely length. Utilise almost never adds meaning over use; commence rarely improves on begin outside genuinely formal contexts; endeavour is heavier than try for no gain in most sentences. If the longer word is only there to sound serious, it's decoration — cut it. If it adds real precision (obtain for a specific kind of getting; demonstrate for proving), keep it. That's the difference between formal and merely fancy.

Casual vs Academic

The clearest way to feel register is to hear the same content in both keys. First, two people chatting; then, the written-up version. Notice how the vocabulary — not the ideas — carries the whole shift.

A: So we looked at the survey and found out that a lot of people got put off by the price.

B: Yeah, and the numbers went up once we cut the cost, which kind of shows price was the big thing.

A: Right. We should probably start doing that across the board.

And here is the same exchange rewritten for a report — academic vocabulary, stance verbs, and precise single verbs doing the work:

An examination of the survey indicated that a substantial proportion of respondents were deterred by the price. Enrolment increased once the cost was reduced, which suggests that price constituted the primary factor. It would therefore be advisable to implement this change more widely.

Trace the upgrades: looked at → an examination of, found out → indicated, a lot of → substantial, got put off → were deterred, went up → increased, cut → reduced, shows → suggests (note the hedge), the big thing → the primary factor, start doing across the board → implement more widely. Neither version is "better" in the abstract — each is exactly right for its setting.

Phrase bank — writing up casual observations formally

an examination of the data indicates… a substantial proportion of… this suggests that… the primary factor appears to be… it would be advisable to… these findings highlight the need for… the results demonstrate that… consequently, it is recommended that…

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Replace the informal word

Objective: Give a single academic equivalent for the informal word in brackets. (Several answers may be possible; type one good fit.)

  1. The team managed to (get) funding for the project.
  2. The graph clearly (shows) the upward trend.
  3. There was a (big) rise in applications.
  4. The report aims to (look at) the root causes.
  5. We did not have (enough) evidence to conclude.
  6. The programme will (start) next spring.
  7. The sample contained (about) two hundred people.
  8. Conditions began to (get worse) after the storm.
✅ Answer

1. obtain (or acquire) · 2. demonstrates (or illustrates) · 3. substantial (or significant) · 4. examine (or investigate) · 5. sufficient (or adequate) · 6. commence (or begin) · 7. approximately · 8. deteriorate

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the more formal option

Objective: For each pair in brackets, type the word that suits formal, academic writing.

  1. The study will (look into / investigate) the effects of sleep loss.
  2. Costs are likely to (go up / increase) next year.
  3. These three elements (make up / constitute) the framework.
  4. The author was forced to (put off / postpone) the launch.
  5. The data (suggest / prove) a possible connection. (pick the cautious verb)
  6. The charity was (set up / established) in 1990.
✅ Answer

1. investigate · 2. increase · 3. constitute · 4. postpone · 5. suggest (hedged — "prove" over-claims from mere data) · 6. established

🏋️ Exercise 3: Spot and fix over-formalisation

Objective: Each sentence is too formal for its casual context. Rewrite it in natural, plain English.

  1. (text to a friend) I shall endeavour to commence my journey imminently.
  2. (email to a colleague you know well) Kindly be advised that I shall utilise the conference room.
  3. (chatting) I obtained a coffee and subsequently proceeded to my desk.
✅ Answer

Model rewrites (yours may vary):

1. I'll try to leave soon.

2. Just to let you know, I'll use the conference room.

3. I grabbed a coffee and then went to my desk.

In each case the formal vocabulary was tonally mismatched — the plain words fit the casual setting far better.

🎯 Your Project: From Chat to Report

A two-part task in vocabulary register — an upgrade, then a reflection on which changes actually earned their keep.

  1. (3 min) Write or find a casual, conversational paragraph (5–7 sentences) describing something you noticed, read, or concluded — deliberately using everyday words (get, show, big, look at, a lot of) and a few phrasal verbs.
  2. (8 min) Rewrite it in academic register: swap in at least six everyday-to-academic equivalents, use at least two stance verbs (suggest, indicate, argue, demonstrate) with appropriate hedging, and replace at least two phrasal verbs with single formal verbs.
  3. (4 min) — Earn-its-keep check: go through each change and label it sharper (it added precision) or just formal (it only raised the register). Reverse any that were merely fancy — especially any utilise that should be use.
  4. (3 min) — Register test: imagine the paragraph landing in (a) a research report and (b) a message to a close friend. Confirm your academic version suits (a) and would sound absurd in (b) — that mismatch is proof you've genuinely shifted register.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My source paragraph was genuinely casual and everyday
  • ☐ I used at least six everyday-to-academic equivalents
  • ☐ I used at least two stance verbs with appropriate hedging
  • ☐ I replaced at least two phrasal verbs with single formal verbs
  • ☐ I labelled each change "sharper" or "just formal" and reversed the merely fancy ones
  • ☐ I confirmed the academic version fits a report but not a message to a friend

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Build a personal everyday → academic table and check each equivalent genuinely fits the meaning before using it.
  • Learn AWL words as whole families (analyse / analysis / analytical) so you can move an idea across parts of speech.
  • Choose stance verbs by strength: suggest / indicate to hedge, argue to assert, demonstrate only when proof warrants it.
  • In formal writing, prefer a precise single verb to a vague phrasal one — but only when it's genuinely more exact.
  • Let the situation set the register: academic vocabulary for reports and essays, plain words for messages and talk.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wrong-sense upgrade: using significant for physical size, or approximately for a topic — the formal word must fit the exact meaning.
  • Over-claiming: "This proves…" from limited data ❌ → hedge with "This suggests / indicates…" ✅.
  • Empty formality: utilise for use, endeavour for try, commence for start in casual contexts — length without meaning.
  • Register mismatch: academic vocabulary in a friendly email reads as stilted or pompous — grammatically fine, tonally wrong.
  • Phrasal-verb purge: replacing every phrasal verb with a Latinate one makes prose cold and clotted; keep the natural ones.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Academic vocabulary favours precise, often single-word, Latinate terms: get → obtain, show → demonstrate, big → significant / substantial, look at → examine, enough → sufficient, start → commence, about → approximately / concerning.
  • Learn Academic Word List families (analyse, significant, establish, factor, approach, evident, derive, constitute) across all their forms.
  • Stance verbs signal commitment: suggest / indicate (tentative), argue (assertive), demonstrate (proven) — and academic writing hedges deliberately.
  • Prefer a precise single verb over a vague phrasal one in formal writing (find out → determine, go up → increase) — but not mechanically.
  • Match vocabulary to formality: don't over-formalise. Utilise is rarely better than use; formal words in casual settings read as pompous.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now lift a text into academic register by word choice alone — swapping in precise equivalents, drawing on AWL families, and grading your stance verbs to say exactly how strongly a claim stands. Just as importantly, you've learned to hear over-formalisation as the error it is, and to reserve the formal word for when it's genuinely the right one. That balance — command of formal vocabulary and the restraint to use it well — is the mark of a truly advanced writer. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I always replace everyday words with academic ones in formal writing?

No — replace them when the academic word is more precise or the genre genuinely demands the register, not as a reflex. Some plain words (use, find out, help) are often the best choice even in formal prose. The aim is precision and appropriacy, not maximum formality.

What exactly is the Academic Word List, and why does it matter?

It's a research-based list of word families that recur across academic texts in every discipline (business, science, law, humanities). Because they appear everywhere, mastering them gives you disproportionate reach: learn a few hundred families and you unlock a large share of the vocabulary in any academic reading or writing task.

Why do academic writers hedge instead of stating things directly?

Because evidence usually supports a likely conclusion, not certainty. Hedging verbs (suggest, indicate, appear, tend to) accurately reflect that limit and signal intellectual honesty. Over-claiming ("this proves…") reads as naïve; careful hedging reads as rigorous — which is exactly the impression academic writing aims to give.
