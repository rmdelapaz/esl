🎭 Lesson 1.3: The Subjunctive & Unreal Uses

The subjunctive is English's quiet mood — the form we reach for when we demand, recommend, and imagine. It's easy to overlook because it so often looks like an ordinary verb; but a speaker who wields it deliberately sounds measured, formal, and in control. In this lesson you'll master the mandative subjunctive of proposals and requirements, the unreal were of hypothesis, and the as if / as though of imagined comparison — the grammar of situations that are willed or wished rather than merely reported.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the mandative (present) subjunctive after verbs, adjectives, and nouns of demand and recommendation — including negative and passive forms
  • Use the unreal were subjunctive in hypothetical and wished-for situations (if I were, were I, I wish it were)
  • Use as if / as though + past for unreal comparison, and distinguish it from the real (indicative) alternative
  • Recognise fixed subjunctive expressions and judge the register of each subjunctive choice

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Draft and deliver a short formal recommendation or proposal using mandative subjunctives, then an imaginative reflection using unreal were and as if.

A Mood You Already Half-Know

English has three moods: the indicative (statements and questions of fact — she resigns, does she resign?), the imperative (commands — resign!), and the subjunctive (the willed, the hypothetical, the imagined — we demand that she resign). The subjunctive is faint in modern English because its forms so often coincide with ordinary ones — but two places keep it alive and unmistakable:

"The board insists that he resign immediately." (present subjunctive — note: not "resigns")

"If I were in his position, I'd do the same." (past subjunctive — note: not "was")

Both feel slightly formal, and that's exactly the point. The subjunctive is the mood of proposals, requirements, wishes, and imagined worlds — the situations where you are not reporting reality but shaping it or standing outside it. Master its two live forms and you gain a register that sounds deliberate and authoritative.

🧠 Mindset

At C1, a grammatical point like the subjunctive rarely unlocks a whole new ability overnight; instead it sharpens something you can already half-do. You've said "If I were you" for years without naming the mood behind it. The gain here is awareness — knowing why the form is what it is, so you can extend it confidently to "were I in charge" and "we recommend that it be reviewed." That's the quiet, compounding polish of advanced study: naming the instinct turns it into a choice. 🌱

The Mandative (Present) Subjunctive

The mandative subjunctive ("mandative" = relating to a command or requirement) appears in a that-clause after expressions of demand, request, recommendation, and importance. Its form is simple and striking: the base form of the verb — no third-person -s, no do-support, no to — and it is the same for every person.

Trigger typeTrigger wordsExample (base form in the that-clause)
Verbssuggest, recommend, insist, demand, request, propose, urge, ask, advise, requireThe board demanded that he resign. · I suggest (that) she be promoted.
Adjectives (It is … that …)essential, vital, important, crucial, imperative, advisable, necessary, desirableIt is essential that everyone be present. · It's vital that the report reach them by Friday.
Nounsrecommendation, demand, requirement, proposal, suggestion, insistence, requesttheir demand that the fee be waived · a requirement that each applicant submit references

Because the verb is always the bare base form, three things follow that catch even advanced learners off guard:

FeatureRuleExample
Third personNo -s endingthat he go (not goes) · that she have (not has) full access
Negativenot + base form (no doesn't/don't)We ask that he not resign before the vote. (not doesn't resign)
Passivebe + past participleIt is imperative that this be done at once. · We propose that the rule be abolished.
Verb "to be"Always be (all persons)I insist that I be informed. · It's vital that they be ready.

⚠️ The two things to get right

  • No third-person -s: "The committee recommends that she chair the meeting" — never chairs.
  • No to: after these triggers you need a that-clause with a subject, not an infinitive. "We demand that he apologise" — never demand that he to apologise. (You can say "We demand an apology" or "We urge him to apologise" with urge/advise/ask — but those are different structures, not the subjunctive.)

💡 Beware the lookalike

Some of these verbs have an ordinary, non-subjunctive meaning. Compare "The evidence suggests that he is guilty" (= indicates; indicative, a statement of fact) with "I suggest that he be released" (= propose; subjunctive, a recommendation). Likewise "She insisted that she was innocent" (asserting a fact) vs "She insisted that he leave" (demanding an action). The subjunctive appears only when the meaning is a demand or recommendation.

Register: Three Valid Alternatives

Here is a nuance that trips up learners who think the subjunctive is simply "correct" and everything else "wrong." After a mandative trigger, English gives you three grammatical options, differing mainly in register and dialect:

OptionExampleRegister / note
1. Subjunctive (base form)I suggest that she be promoted.Most formal; standard in American English and in formal/academic writing everywhere.
2. should + base formI suggest that she should be promoted.Common in British English; slightly softer, still formal. (This is "mandative should.")
3. IndicativeI suggest that she is promoted.More informal/spoken British usage; can be ambiguous, so avoid it in careful writing.

All three are used by educated speakers. For C1 purposes: reach for the subjunctive when you want maximum formality and clarity (proposals, minutes, reports, legal or academic prose); use should when you want a slightly gentler, characteristically British tone; and recognise the indicative as a valid, more colloquial variant rather than an error.

✅ A note on connotation

The subjunctive carries a whiff of authority and impartiality — it states the requirement without a personal "I think you should." That's why it dominates institutional writing: "It is recommended that candidates be notified in writing." The passive subjunctive, in particular, sounds procedural and neutral, which is exactly what official prose wants.

The Unreal Were Subjunctive

The second living subjunctive is the past subjunctive, and in practice it shows up as a single distinctive form: were for all persons in unreal or hypothetical contexts. Where the indicative would use was (I/he/she/it), the subjunctive keeps were — signalling that the situation is imagined, not real.

StructureExampleMeaning
if I / he / she / it wereIf I were you, I'd accept. · If it were possible, we would.Hypothetical / contrary to fact
Inversion: were + subject (no if)Were I in charge, things would change. · Were she to refuse, we'd have a problem.Formal equivalent of an if-clause
I wish … wereI wish it were simpler. · I wish he were here.Wish about a present unreality
Suppose / imagine … were (to)Suppose she were to resign — what then?Tentative hypothesis
would rather / it's time … wereI'd rather he weren't told. · It's high time this were settled.Preference / overdue situation

💡 Was vs were: register, not right-and-wrong

In everyday speech, "If I was you" is extremely common and widely accepted. But the unreal were is the careful, formal choice, and it becomes obligatory in two places: (1) after inversion — "Were I you…" is fine, "Was I you…" is not; and (2) in the fixed expression "If I were you" when giving advice. When in doubt at C1, choose were for the unreal — it never sounds wrong, whereas was sometimes does.

This unreal were is the seed of the second and third conditionals and of inversion in conditionals — both of which you'll develop in the next lesson. For now, fix the instinct: imagined present → were.

As If / As Though + Past

As if and as though (interchangeable) introduce a comparison. The tense you choose signals whether the comparison is real or unreal — a genuinely useful piece of nuance:

FormExampleImplication
as if + past (unreal)He talks as if he knew everything.He doesn't know everything — the comparison is contrary to fact.
as if + present (real)He talks as if he knows what he's doing.Maybe he does — the speaker treats it as plausibly true.
as if + were (unreal)She treats me as though I were a stranger.I'm not a stranger — pure hypothesis (formal were).
as if + past perfect (unreal, earlier)It's as though time had stood still. · He looked as if he had seen a ghost.Unreal comparison to something before now.

⚠️ The unreal-past signal

The "backshift" to the past after as if does not mean the sentence is about the past. "He acts as if he owned the place" is about now; the past form simply marks the comparison as false / imagined. Compare "It looks as if it 's going to rain" (present — the speaker thinks it genuinely might) with "He behaves as if he owned the place" (past — but he doesn't). Choose the tense to match the truth-value you intend.

💡 Register footnote

In informal speech you'll hear like replacing as if ("He talks like he knows everything") — natural in conversation, but avoid it in formal writing, where as if / as though is the correct choice. And note that as though is marginally more literary than as if; both are safe in careful prose.

Fixed Subjunctive Expressions

A handful of set phrases preserve the old subjunctive as fossils. You don't build these from rules — you recognise and use them whole. Most are formal, literary, or emphatic in tone.

  • Be that as it may — even so; nevertheless
  • Come what may — whatever happens
  • Suffice it to say — I'll say only this much
  • If need be — if necessary
  • Far be it from me — I wouldn't presume (to…)
  • God save the Queen / King — set benediction
  • So be it — I accept it, reluctantly
  • Long live the … — may … endure

"The plan is costly. Be that as it may, we must proceed. Suffice it to say the alternative is worse — and come what may, we'll see it through. I'll cut my own budget if need be. Far be it from me to lecture the board, but the case is clear."

💡 Handle with care

These phrases are vivid but strongly register-marked: dropped into casual chat they can sound arch or theatrical. Use them where a formal, rhetorical, or wry-literary flavour is wanted — a speech, an essay, a considered email — and sparingly even there.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These five sentences train your ear for the mandative subjunctive and other unreal forms.

  • The committee insists that every application be submitted by Friday.
  • It's essential that he be told the truth before the meeting.
  • I would rather you didn't mention this to anyone just yet.
  • If I were in your position, I'd think very carefully before agreeing.
  • She spoke as though she had witnessed the whole thing herself.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the verb in the mandative subjunctive

Objective: Use the base form; watch for negative and passive.

  1. The committee recommends that the deadline (extend, passive) by two weeks.
  2. It is essential that every member (be) present at the vote.
  3. Her lawyer insisted that she (not, answer) any further questions.
  4. We propose that the old logo (retain) for continuity.
  5. It is imperative that he (have) full access to the records.
✅ Answer

1. be extended (passive: be + past participle) · 2. be present · 3. not answer (negative: not + base, no doesn't) · 4. be retained · 5. have (base form, not has).

🏋️ Exercise 2: Rewrite — subjunctive ↔ should alternative

Objective: Convert each sentence to the other formal option, keeping the meaning.

  1. (subjunctive → should) I suggest that she apply for the grant. → I suggest that she for the grant.
  2. (should → subjunctive) They demanded that the fee should be waived. → They demanded that the fee .
  3. (subjunctive → should) It is vital that everyone arrive on time. → It is vital that everyone on time.
✅ Answer

1. …that she should apply. · 2. …that the fee be waived. · 3. …that everyone should arrive. (Both versions are correct; the subjunctive is the more formal/American, should the characteristically British choice.)

🏋️ Exercise 3: As if — past (unreal) vs present (real)?

Objective: Choose the tense that matches the intended truth-value, and say why.

  1. It looks as if it (be) going to snow — the sky's grey. (the speaker thinks it genuinely might)
  2. He orders us around as if he (own) the company. (but he doesn't)
  3. She greeted me warmly, as if we (be) old friends. (we're not)
  4. He sounds as if he (know) the answer — let's ask him. (the speaker thinks he probably does)
✅ Answer

1. is (present — real; the speaker treats it as genuinely likely). · 2. owned (past — unreal; he doesn't own it). · 3. were (unreal were; we aren't old friends). · 4. knows (present — real; treated as plausibly true). The past/were forms flag the comparison as contrary to fact; the present forms treat it as possibly true.

🎯 Your Project: A Proposal & a Reflection

Two short pieces on one topic — ideally a real policy or workplace change you'd like to see (flexible hours, a recycling scheme, a curriculum reform). Deliver aloud to a partner/group, or record yourself; then keep the written draft.

  1. (4 min) The formal recommendation. Write 4–6 sentences proposing the change, using at least three different mandative triggers — e.g. "It is essential that…", "I recommend that…", "our proposal that…" — with correct base-form verbs. Include one negative ("that we not…") and one passive ("that it be…").
  2. (3 min) Deliver it in a formal register, as if to a committee. Keep the subjunctives crisp — no stray third-person -s.
  3. (3 min) The imaginative reflection. Now step back and imagine: "If I were in charge…", "Were this my decision…", "I wish it were simpler…". Add one as if / as though comparison ("People act as if change were impossible…").
  4. (2 min) Review. Check each subjunctive: base form after mandative triggers? were (not was) for the unreal? Does each as if tense match the truth you meant?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used at least three mandative triggers with correct base-form verbs
  • ☐ I included one negative ("that … not …") and one passive ("that … be …") subjunctive
  • ☐ No third-person -s slipped into a mandative clause
  • ☐ I used unreal were at least twice (including one inverted "Were I…")
  • ☐ I used as if / as though with a tense that matched my intended meaning

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • After a mandative trigger, drop straight to the base form — same for every person, no -s, no to.
  • Reach for unreal were in formal hypotheses, and always after inversion ("Were I…").
  • Let the tense after as if do real work: past for false, present for possibly-true.
  • Treat should-forms and the indicative as valid register variants, not mistakes.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Third-person -s: "I recommend that he goes" ❌ → "…that he go" ✅.
  • Do-support in the negative: "We ask that she doesn't attend" ❌ → "…that she not attend" ✅.
  • Infinitive after the trigger: "They demanded that he to leave" ❌ → "…that he leave" ✅.
  • Was after inversion: "Was I in charge…" ❌ → "Were I in charge…" ✅.
  • Wrong as if tense: "He acts as if he owns the place" when you mean he doesn't ❌ → "…as if he owned the place" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Mandative subjunctive = base form of the verb after triggers of demand/recommendation/importance (verbs, adjectives, nouns): no -s, no to; negative = not + base; passive = be + past participle.
  • Register: subjunctive (most formal/American) ≈ should + base (British, softer) ≈ indicative (informal) — all valid.
  • Unreal were for all persons in hypotheses and wishes: if I were, were I, I wish it were, suppose she were to….
  • As if / as though + past = unreal comparison (contrary to fact); + present = real (possibly true).
  • Fixed phrases (be that as it may, come what may, suffice it to say, if need be) are formal, set subjunctive fossils.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is the subjunctive dying out? Do I really need it?

The were subjunctive is thriving in "if I were you," and the mandative subjunctive is fully alive in formal and American English — it's the default in reports, minutes, and academic prose. You can survive without producing it, but you'll misread its nuance and sound less formal when the situation calls for it. At C1, comprehension and register control both matter, so yes, it's worth owning.

"If I were you" or "if I was you"? Which is right?

Both are heard; were is the careful, formal choice and never sounds wrong, while was is common in casual speech but marked as informal. After inversion, only were works ("Were I you…"). Default to were for the unreal and you'll always be safe.

How is the subjunctive were different from the conditionals coming next?

They're the same machinery seen from different angles. The unreal were is the past subjunctive that powers the second conditional ("If I were rich, I'd travel") and inverted conditionals ("Were I rich…"). This lesson isolates the form; the next assembles it into the full conditional and mixed-conditional system.
