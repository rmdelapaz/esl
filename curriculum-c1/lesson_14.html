🔗 Lesson 5.2: Advanced Prepositions & Prepositional Phrases

Ask an advanced learner where their remaining errors hide, and the honest answer is almost always the same: the small words. Not the grand structures — the prepositions. Do we comply with or comply to a rule? Are we aware of or aware about a problem? Is it an increase in, of, or on sales? These dependent prepositions are learned as fixed chunks, not deduced by logic — and getting them right is one of the clearest signals of genuine command. This lesson builds your stock of them systematically.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the correct preposition after common verbs (depend on, comply with, refrain from, account for, resort to)
  • Pair the right preposition with key adjectives (aware of, subject to, prone to, consistent with, devoid of)
  • Choose the preposition that follows abstract nouns (an increase in, a reason for, an alternative to, insight into)
  • Deploy fixed, formal prepositional phrases (with regard to, in the event of, on behalf of, in accordance with, in the wake of)

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Write a short formal message or report extract that deliberately uses a range of dependent prepositions and at least three fixed prepositional phrases correctly, then proofread specifically for preposition accuracy.

What "Dependent" Means

A dependent preposition is one that a particular word simply requires. It isn't chosen for its own spatial or logical meaning — it's fixed by the verb, adjective, or noun in front of it. We say depend on, consist of, aware of, a reason for, and no amount of reasoning explains why those particular prepositions. They are collocational facts, learned word by word.

That's what makes them a top C1 accuracy area. The grammar around them is easy; the pairing is the whole difficulty — and because a wrong preposition rarely blocks understanding, learners can carry small errors for years without noticing. The cure isn't a rule. It's deliberate chunking: store the word and its preposition together, as a single item.

Wrong preposition: "We must comply to the regulations." · "She's very aware about the risks."

Correct chunk: "We must comply with the regulations." · "She's very aware of the risks."

🧠 How this differs from Lesson 4.2

In Lesson 4.2 you studied collocation and idiom — which words partner each other (make a decision, heavy rain) and figurative fixed expressions. Here the focus narrows to one precise thing: the correct preposition inside a set pattern. It's still learning by chunk, but the target is that single small word. Don't try to derive it — notice it, collect it, and drill it until the wrong one sounds wrong. That instinct, more than any rule, is what fluency in these patterns feels like. 🌱

Verb + Preposition

Many verbs demand a specific preposition before their object. At C1, the useful ones extend well beyond the everyday listen to and look at into more formal territory. Learn each as verb + preposition, and note the meaning where it's less obvious.

Verb + prepositionMeaningExample
depend on / uponbe determined by; rely onThe outcome depends on the vote.
comply withobey, act in accordance withAll firms must comply with the law.
account forexplain; make up (a proportion)How do you account for the delay?
refrain fromhold back from doingPlease refrain from smoking.
resort toturn to (a last option)They resorted to legal action.
result in / result fromcause / be caused byCuts resulted in layoffs; the loss resulted from poor planning.
consist ofbe made up ofThe panel consists of five experts.
contribute tohelp cause / give toStress contributes to illness.
object todisagree with, opposeResidents objected to the plan.
benefit fromgain advantageStudents benefit from feedback.

⚠️ Same verb, different preposition, different meaning

Some verbs take more than one preposition, and the choice changes the sense — these are worth special attention:

  • result in (= cause) vs result from (= be caused by): the two point in opposite directions.
  • agree with a person / an opinion, but agree to a proposal, and agree on a plan.
  • consist of (be composed of) vs consist in (formal: have its essence in) — "Happiness consists in contentment."

When a verb offers a choice, the preposition isn't decoration — it carries the meaning.

Adjective + Preposition

Adjectives are just as demanding. After a linking verb (be, seem, become), many adjectives pull a fixed preposition. These pattern heavily in descriptive and analytical writing, so an advanced writer needs them at their fingertips.

Adjective + prepositionMeaningExample
aware ofknowing aboutShe's fully aware of the consequences.
subject toliable to; conditional onPrices are subject to change.
prone to / liable totending to (usually something bad)The soil is prone to erosion.
consistent within agreement withThe results are consistent with the theory.
devoid ofcompletely lackingThe report is devoid of detail.
indicative ofa sign ofThe trend is indicative of decline.
conducive tohelping to produceNoise isn't conducive to study.
reliant on / dependent onneeding, relying onThe region is reliant on tourism.
capable ofable to doThe engine is capable of more.
synonymous withclosely associated withThe brand is synonymous with luxury.

💡 Group by shared preposition to lighten the load

Rather than memorising fifty isolated pairs, cluster them by their preposition. Many "knowing / lacking" adjectives take of (aware of, devoid of, conscious of, indicative of, capable of). Many "matching / relating" adjectives take with (consistent with, compatible with, synonymous with, familiar with). Several "tending / liable" adjectives take to (prone to, subject to, susceptible to, conducive to). The clusters aren't perfect, but they turn a long list into a few memorable families.

Noun + Preposition

Abstract nouns — the very ones you build in academic and formal writing — carry their own fixed prepositions. Because Lesson 3.1 taught you to nominalise (turning increase the verb into an increase the noun), you especially need the preposition that the resulting noun requires, which is often not the one the verb took.

Noun + prepositionExample

an impact / effect ona serious impact on health
a demand / need forgrowing demand for housing
a lack ofa lack of evidence
an attitude to / towardsher attitude towards risk
a relationship / connection betweenthe relationship between diet and health

⚠️ The preposition can switch when a verb becomes a noun

Watch this carefully. The verb often takes one preposition and its related noun another: we increase something (no preposition) but speak of an increase in it; we solve a problem but offer a solution to it; we demand something but note a demand for it. So when you nominalise, don't carry the verb's preposition across by reflex — check the noun's own pairing.

Fixed Formal Phrases

Beyond single pairings, English has a stock of fixed prepositional phrases — multi-word connectors that function as prepositions or adverbials and belong distinctly to formal registers: reports, contracts, official correspondence, and considered speech. Learn them whole, complete with their internal prepositions.

PhraseMeaningExample
with regard to / with respect toconcerning, aboutWith regard to your query, I can confirm…
in the event ofif this happensIn the event of fire, use the stairs.
on behalf ofrepresenting (someone)I speak on behalf of the whole team.
in accordance withfollowing, in line withHandled in accordance with policy.
in the wake offollowing (an event), as a result ofIn the wake of the scandal, he resigned.
by virtue ofbecause of, throughShe chairs it by virtue of her seniority.
for the sake offor the benefit or purpose ofThey compromised for the sake of peace.
in light ofconsidering (new information)In light of the findings, we'll revise it.
in terms ofas regards, measured byStrong in terms of revenue.
at the expense ofwith loss/damage toGrowth at the expense of quality.
with regard to the matter… with respect to your point… in the event of a delay… on behalf of the committee… in accordance with the guidelines… in the wake of the decision… by virtue of the agreement… for the sake of clarity… in light of recent events… in terms of cost… at the expense of quality… with a view to improving…

✅ Register note

These phrases sound authoritative in a report or formal email but pompous in casual chat — "In the event of you being hungry, we could eat" is comically stiff for a friend. In conversation, prefer the plain equivalents: about for with regard to, if for in the event of, after for in the wake of. As always at C1, the skill is matching the phrase to the setting, not simply owning the fancier form.

Traps & Near-Misses

Some pairings trip up even confident speakers because a plausible-but-wrong preposition sits close by, often borrowed from a first language or from a similar English word. Here are the ones worth flagging.

Common errorCorrectWhy
comply to the rulescomply with the rulescomply fixes with, though conform to takes to
aware about the issueaware of the issueaware takes of; about feels natural but is wrong
an increase of crimean increase in crimeuse in for the area that rose; of for the amount ("an increase of 5%")
discuss about the plandiscuss the plan (no preposition)discuss is transitive — no preposition at all
depend of the weatherdepend on the weatherdepend fixes on/upon
married with a doctormarried to a doctormarried takes to for the spouse
capable to do itcapable of doing itcapable of + -ing; able to + verb

💡 The "amount" vs "area" split with numbers

One subtle pair deserves its own note. With change nouns like increase, rise, fall, reduction, use in for the thing that changed and of for the size of the change: "an increase in unemployment of three percent." Both prepositions can appear in one phrase, each doing its own job. Confusing them ("an increase of unemployment") is a classic near-miss.

In Context

Prepositions travel in ordinary sentences, so the best way to bank them is to meet them at work. Read this exchange between a manager and an analyst and notice the verb, adjective, and noun pairings, plus two fixed formal phrases.

Manager: With regard to the quarterly figures — how do you account for the drop in sales?

Analyst: It largely results from the supply delays. The dip is consistent with what we saw last winter, and it's certainly indicative of a wider slowdown.

Manager: Are we still reliant on the single supplier?

Analyst: For now, yes. I'd object to that in the long term — we need an alternative to them. In the event of another delay, we've no buffer at all.

Manager: Agreed. Draft a proposal, in accordance with the procurement policy, for a second source.

Analyst: On behalf of the whole team, I'd welcome that. It should have a real impact on our resilience.

Phrase bank — dependent prepositions in context

account for the change result from the delays consistent with the forecast indicative of a trend reliant on one supplier object to the proposal an alternative to the plan an impact on results with regard to the figures in accordance with policy

  2. In the of a cancellation, a full refund will be issued.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Correct the wrong preposition

Objective: Each sentence has one preposition error. Rewrite it correctly.

  1. The company must conform with the industry standards.
  2. I wasn't aware about the change in schedule.
  3. There was a sharp increase of unemployment last year.
  4. She is fully capable to manage the project alone.
✅ Answer

1. …must conform to the industry standards. (comply with, but conform to)

2. I wasn't aware of the change in schedule.

3. …a sharp increase in unemployment. (use "of" only for a figure: an increase of 5%)

4. She is fully capable of managing the project alone. (capable of + -ing)

Write a short piece of formal English and hunt your own prepositions to a professional standard.

  1. (3 min) Choose a realistic formal context: a reply to a customer complaint, a short section of a report, or a notice to staff. Note the situation in one line.
  2. (8 min) Write 120–160 words. Deliberately include at least four verb/adjective/noun + preposition pairings from this lesson (e.g. comply with, aware of, an increase in, an alternative to) and at least three fixed formal phrases (with regard to, in the event of, on behalf of, in accordance with).
  3. (3 min) — Preposition proofread: read through only for prepositions. Underline every one attached to a pattern word and ask, "Is this the chunk I've stored?" Fix any near-misses.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Store the chunk, not the word: record "depend on", "aware of", "an increase in" as single units in your notes.
  • Group pairings by their shared preposition (of-adjectives, with-adjectives, to-adjectives) to memorise them in families.
  • When you nominalise a verb, check the noun's preposition afresh — it often differs from the verb's.
  • Keep the formal phrases for formal genres; swap in plain equivalents (about, if, after) in conversation.
  • Do a dedicated preposition-only proofread of important writing — it catches errors your general reading skims past.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Logic over collocation: "depend of", "comply to" ❌ — you can't reason these out; they're fixed as depend on, comply with ✅.
  • Adding a preposition to a transitive verb: "discuss about", "enter into the room", "phone to her" ❌ → discuss it, enter the room, phone her ✅.
  • Increase of vs in: use in for the area ("increase in crime"), of only for the figure ("increase of 5%").
  • Carrying the verb's preposition to the noun: "solve the problem" but "a solution to the problem", not "a solution of".
  • Formal phrases in casual talk: "In the event of you calling…" ❌ to a friend → just "If you call…" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Dependent prepositions are fixed by the pattern word, not chosen by logic — they're learned as chunks, and they're a top C1 accuracy area.
  • Verb + preposition: depend on, comply with, account for, refrain from, resort to, result in / from, consist of, object to.
  • Adjective + preposition: aware of, subject to, prone to, consistent with, devoid of, conducive to, reliant on, capable of.
  • Noun + preposition: an increase in, a reason for, an alternative to, insight into, a solution to, an impact on — the noun's preposition often differs from the verb's.
  • Fixed formal phrases: with regard to, in the event of, on behalf of, in accordance with, in the wake of, by virtue of, for the sake of — formal only.
  • Watch the near-misses: comply with vs conform to; aware of not about; increase in (area) vs of (figure).

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is there any logic to which preposition a word takes, or is it pure memorisation?

Mostly it's collocation you learn by exposure, but there are helpful tendencies: "matching / relating" adjectives often take with, "knowing / lacking" ones often take of, "tending" ones often take to. Use those clusters as scaffolding — but always trust the attested chunk over the pattern, because the exceptions are many.

How is this different from the collocations I learned in Lesson 4.2?

Lesson 4.2 covered which words partner each other and figurative idioms broadly. This lesson zooms in on one element of collocation: the correct preposition in a set pattern. It's the same principle — learn by chunk — applied to the single word learners get wrong most often.

Can the same word ever take different prepositions correctly?

Yes, and the choice usually changes the meaning: agree with a person, agree to a proposal, agree on a plan; result in (cause) vs result from (be caused by); consist of (be composed of) vs consist in (formal: have its essence in). When a word offers options, the preposition is doing real semantic work.
