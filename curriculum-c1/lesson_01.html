⏳ Lesson 1.1: Advanced Aspect — The Future in Detail

Welcome to C1 — the level of precision and control. At B2 you learned to talk about the future; now you'll learn to place events within it with real accuracy: what will be in progress, what will already be finished, and how to describe a future seen from a point in the past. This is the fine-grained time reference of a genuinely advanced speaker.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the future continuous for actions in progress at a future moment (and for tactful enquiries)
  • Use the future perfect and future perfect continuous to look back from a future point
  • Express "future in the past" (was going to, would, was about to) for plans and predictions made earlier
  • Choose precisely between competing future forms to convey exact meaning and register

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

Beyond "Will" and "Going To"

By C1, will, going to, and the present continuous for arrangements are second nature. But English has a more delicate set of future forms that let you locate an event with surgical precision — not just that something will happen, but where it sits relative to another future moment, and whether it will be ongoing or already complete.

Consider the difference:

"This time next week, I 'll be lying on a beach." (in progress at that future moment)

"By this time next week, I 'll have finished all my exams." (completed before that moment)

"By June, I 'll have been working here for a decade." (duration up to that moment)

Three futures, three distinct vantage points. Mastering them is what separates "fluent" from "precise."

🧠 Mindset

At C1, the gains are subtle — you're not learning to say new things so much as to say familiar things with greater exactness, nuance, and style. That can feel less dramatic than early progress, and that's normal: this is the long, rewarding polish phase where "correct" becomes "elegant." Trust that small refinements compound into a noticeably more sophisticated voice. 🌱

The Future Continuous

Form: will be + -ing. It describes an action that will be in progress at a specific future time — the future equivalent of "was doing" at a past moment.

UseExample
action in progress at a future pointDon't call at 8 — we'll be having dinner then.
a future event seen as fixed/routine (no intention implied)I'll be seeing Tom tomorrow anyway, so I'll tell him.
polite, tactful enquiries about someone's plansWill you be using the car tonight? (softer than "Will you use…?")

💡 The tact of the future continuous

"Will you be joining us?" sounds more considerate than "Will you join us?" because it treats the action as a neutral, already-forming plan rather than a request for a decision on the spot. This is a hallmark of polished, diplomatic English — note it for Lesson 3.3.

The Future Perfect (Simple & Continuous)

Both forms let you stand at a future moment and look back at something already completed or ongoing by then. They almost always appear with a marker like by, by then, by the time, or before.

FormMeaningExample
Future perfect
will have + past participle		completed before a future point (result/achievement)By 2030, the company will have opened ten new offices.
Future perfect continuous
will have been + -ing		duration of an activity up to a future pointBy next month, I 'll have been living abroad for five years.

⚠️ Simple vs continuous (the familiar distinction)

Use the simple for completion, achievement, or quantity ("I'll have written the report" / "…finished three chapters"). Use the continuous for the ongoing activity and its duration ("I'll have been writing all day"). And, as ever, stative verbs resist the continuous: "By July I'll have known her for a decade," not "will have been knowing."

    P --> A["Looking back: will have done / will have been doing"]
    P --> B["At that moment: will be doing"]
    P --> C["After: will do"]

The Future in the Past

Sometimes we describe a future as it looked from an earlier vantage point — a plan, intention, or prediction that was "still to come" back then (whether or not it actually happened). English has a neat set of forms for this, mostly the past versions of ordinary future forms.

FormUseExample
was/were going toan earlier plan or intentionWe were going to fly, but the strike changed everything.
woulda future viewed from the past (esp. in narrative)She left in 2001; she would never return to that city.
was/were about toon the point of doing somethingI was about to leave when the phone rang.
was/were todestiny/arrangement (formal, literary)The decision was to have far-reaching consequences.

Watch how these thread through a narrative:

"By the time I reached the platform, the train was about to leave. I was going to wave it down, but of course it was hopeless. That missed connection, I later realised, would change the entire course of my trip."

💡 Would for the future-in-the-past

This narrative would ("she would never return") is common in storytelling and biography. It's distinct from conditional would and from habitual would (past habits) — context distinguishes them, and confident readers decode them instantly.

Choosing the Right Form

The art at C1 is selecting the form whose meaning matches your intention exactly. Ask yourself where the event sits relative to your reference point:

  • In progress at a future time → future continuous
  • Completed by a future time → future perfect
  • Ongoing up to a future time → future perfect continuous
  • Planned/expected earlier → future in the past
  • A tactful enquiry → future continuous

✅ A note on register

These forms lean subtly formal and precise, which makes them valuable in presentations, reports, and considered speech. Overusing them in casual chat can sound stiff — the skill is deploying them where precision earns its keep.

Objective: Choose future continuous, future perfect, or future perfect continuous.

  1. This time tomorrow, I (fly) over the Atlantic.
  2. By the end of the year, we (complete) the merger.
  3. In August, they (run) the business for twenty years.
  4. Don't phone at six — I (drive) home.

1. will be flying · 2. will have completed · 3. will have been running · 4. will be driving

🏋️ Exercise 2: Express the future in the past

Objective: Rewrite each, keeping the meaning.

  1. (earlier plan, cancelled) We intended to sign the deal on Friday. → We
  2. (on the point of) I opened my mouth to object when she cut me off. → I object when…

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Anchor the future perfect with by / by the time / before — it needs a reference point to look back from.
  • Reach for the future continuous to sound tactful when asking about plans.
  • Let context carry narrative would — it's a powerful, economical storytelling device.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Present after "by the time" (future): "By the time I will finish" ❌ → "By the time I finish, I'll have…" ✅ — the time clause stays present.
  • Stative verbs in continuous futures: "will have been knowing / owning" ❌ → use the simple form.
  • Overuse: peppering casual speech with future perfects sounds laboured — save them for where precision matters.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Future continuous (will be + -ing): in progress at a future time — and tactful for enquiries.
  • Future perfect (will have + pp): completed by a future point.
  • Future perfect continuous (will have been + -ing): duration up to a future point.
  • Future in the past (was going to / would / was about to / was to): a future seen from an earlier vantage point.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't "I'll be seeing him" just a fancy way of saying "I'll see him"?

Not quite. The future continuous frames the event as part of the natural course of things ("I'll be seeing him anyway"), removing any sense of special intention or request. That neutral, unforced tone is precisely its value.

How do I tell narrative "would" from conditional "would"?

Context and clause structure. Narrative would reports a real future-from-the-past in a story ("Years later, she would return"); conditional would pairs with a condition, real or implied ("she would return if she could"). Fluent readers rarely confuse them.

Can I use "going to" instead of the future continuous or perfect?

Sometimes for plans, but you lose precision. "Going to" states intention; the continuous and perfect specify in progress vs completed by. At C1, choose the form that carries the exact temporal relationship.
