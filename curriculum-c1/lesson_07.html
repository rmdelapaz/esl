🏛️ Lesson 3.1: Nominalisation & Academic Style

Two writers report the same fact. One writes, "They decided to expand, which reduced their costs." The other writes, "The decision to expand led to a reduction in costs." Same content — but the second sentence has quietly turned actions into things. That move, called nominalisation, is the engine of academic, scientific, legal, and bureaucratic English. In this lesson you'll learn to build the nominal style with control — and, just as importantly, to know when not to.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Turn verbs and adjectives into nouns using the main noun-forming suffixes (and zero-derivation)
  • Rewrite verb-heavy sentences in a compact, formal, noun-based style using light verbs and prepositions
  • Recognise the features of academic / formal register and contrast it with informal, agent-visible speech
  • Judge appropriacy — convert between verbal and nominal style, and spot when nominalisation has been overdone

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Project: Take a short, informal spoken-style text and rewrite it in formal academic style using nominalisation; then present a topic aloud in a formal register.

What Nominalisation Is

Nominalisation is the process of expressing actions and qualities as nouns rather than as verbs or adjectives. The information doesn't change; its grammatical packaging does. A verb like decide becomes the noun decision; an adjective like aware becomes awareness. Once an action is a noun, it can be the subject or object of a sentence, be counted, be modified, and be linked to other nouns — which lets you compress a great deal into a tidy phrase.

Watch the same idea move from verbal to nominal:

Verbal (spoken, agent-visible): "They decided to expand, which reduced their costs."

Nominal (written, abstract): "The decision to expand led to a reduction in costs."

Notice what happened. The verb decided became the noun the decision; the verb reduced became the noun a reduction. The who ("they") has vanished, and two clauses have collapsed into one clean sentence built around nouns. That is the whole move — and it is everywhere in the writing you're expected to produce and read at C1.

Verbal version (process as verb)Nominal version (process as noun)
The government invested heavily, so the economy grew.Heavy investment produced economic growth.
Because the climate is changing, sea levels are rising.Climate change is driving a rise in sea levels.
They failed to plan, and the project collapsed.A failure to plan caused the project's collapse.

🧠 Mindset

At C1 you already have the vocabulary — you know both decide and decision. What's new is a stylistic choice: which packaging serves this reader, this genre, this moment? That's a subtler kind of learning than acquiring words, and it can feel like you're "just moving things around." You are — and that rearranging is exactly what turns competent English into authoritative English. The plateau is real; the polish is worth it. 🌱

How: Forming the Nouns

Most nominalisation is done with a small set of noun-forming suffixes attached to a verb or adjective. Some words simply change class with no ending at all (zero-derivation): an increase, a change, a use. Learn the patterns, but always check the actual noun — English is full of irregulars (believe → belief, choose → choice).

💡 Two nouns from one verb — watch the meaning

Some verbs yield an abstract "process" noun and a concrete "result" noun, and they aren't interchangeable. Analyse gives both analysis (the process/result) and analyst (the person). Produce gives production (process) and product (thing). In academic style you usually want the process/abstraction noun — the analysis of the data, not the analyst of the data. Choose deliberately.

Why: Register & Effect

Why would a writer prefer a reduction in costs over they reduced costs? Because nominalisation does three things at once — and each of them is prized in formal writing.

EffectWhat it doesExample
1. Concisepacks a whole process into a noun phrase, so ideas can be linked in one sentence"the rapid expansion of the network" replaces "the network expanded rapidly, and…"
2. Objective / impersonalhides the agent (the who) — it pairs naturally with the passive"A decision was reached" — we're not told, or made to care, by whom
3. Abstract / formallifts the discussion from concrete events to general concepts and relationships"the relationship between investment and growth"

The abstraction is the point. Academic and scientific writing wants to discuss ideas, processes, and relationships rather than particular people doing particular things at particular times. Turning a process into a noun lets you name it, measure it, compare it, and make it the subject of a claim: "Deforestation accelerates biodiversity loss." Both key concepts are nouns, and the sentence reads as a general truth rather than a story about someone.

💡 Nominalisation's typical partners

Nominalisation rarely works alone. It travels with:

  • the passive voice ("An assessment was carried out") — both suppress the agent;
  • "there is / there are" ("There is widespread agreement that…") — presents a state impersonally;
  • long noun phrases stacked with modifiers ("a significant reduction in urban air pollution levels").

Recognise the cluster and you can recognise — and produce — academic register on sight.

Building the Nominal Style

Once an action is a noun, the sentence still needs a verb — but a much emptier one. The nominal style pairs a nominalisation with a "light" verb (a verb carrying little meaning of its own, like make, carry out, provide, reach, pose) or with a preposition. The content sits in the noun; the light verb just makes the grammar work.

Plain verb: "We investigated the cause."

Nominal + light verb: "We carried out an investigation into the cause." / "We conducted an investigation of the cause."

This construction is a favourite of formal English because the noun can now be modified (a thorough / preliminary / independent investigation) in a way a bare verb cannot. Here are high-frequency light-verb + nominalisation collocations worth banking:

Informal vs Academic — Side by Side

The clearest way to feel nominalisation's effect is to see the two registers together. Informal, spoken English keeps the verbs live and the agents visible: it's personal, dynamic, and easy to follow in real time. Formal, academic English converts the same events into nouns, hides the agent, and reads as impersonal and considered.

Informal / spoken (verbal, personal)Formal / academic (nominal, impersonal)
"We found that prices went up because more people were buying.""The findings indicated a price increase driven by rising demand."
"If we cut the budget, staff will get demoralised.""Budget cuts would lead to a decline in staff morale."
"They didn't explain why the trial stopped.""No explanation was provided for the termination of the trial."
"The new law really helped small businesses grow.""The legislation made a significant contribution to the growth of small enterprises."

Track the machinery across that table: verbs (went up, buying) become nouns (a price increase, rising demand); the visible we / they disappears; the passive and "there is / no… was provided" step in to keep the agent out of view. None of the informal versions is wrong — each is simply tuned to a different setting.

💡 Register note: match the packaging to the genre

The nominal style belongs to research articles, reports, essays, policy documents, and legal writing. The verbal style belongs to conversation, emails to friends, journalism aimed at a wide audience, and spoken presentations that need to feel human. Signalling the wrong register — chatty verbs in a research abstract, or dense nominalisations in a friendly email — reads as a mismatch, even when every sentence is grammatical. C1 is the level at which you're expected to control this dial consciously.

When Nominalisation Goes Wrong

Here is the C1 skill that separates a stylist from a rule-follower: nominalisation can be overdone. Because it removes agents and stacks abstractions, a sentence with too many nominalisations becomes airless, vague, and hard to parse. The reader can't tell who did what to whom, and the prose feels lifeless and bureaucratic. More formality is not automatically better writing.

⚠️ Over-nominalisation — a bad → better example

Over-nominalised (bad): "The implementation of the utilisation of renewable resources is a requirement for the achievement of a reduction in emissions."

Rebalanced (better): "To reduce emissions, we must use renewable resources."

The rewrite trades four hollow nouns (implementation, utilisation, requirement, achievement) for two working verbs (reduce, use). It's shorter, clearer, and — crucially — no less serious. Good writers balance nouns and verbs: they nominalise to link and abstract ideas, then let a plain verb carry the main action so the sentence still breathes.

A practical test: if you can't quickly say who is doing what in a nominalised sentence, and if the meaning would survive turning one or two nouns back into verbs, do it. Reserve nominalisation for where its concision and abstraction genuinely earn their keep — naming a concept you'll refer back to, linking cause and effect, keeping an agent out of view on purpose.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Rewrite in nominal / academic style

Objective: Convert each verbal, agent-visible sentence into a compact, impersonal, noun-based version. Use nominalisation, and the passive or a light verb where helpful.

  1. We investigated why the system failed.
  2. The company expanded quickly, and this reduced its unit costs.
  3. They didn't explain why the results differed.
  4. Because the population is ageing, healthcare costs are rising.
✅ Answer

Model answers (yours may vary in wording):

1. An investigation was carried out into the failure of the system.

2. The company's rapid expansion led to a reduction in its unit costs.

3. No explanation was provided for the difference in the results.

4. Population ageing is driving a rise in healthcare costs.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Spot and fix over-nominalisation

Objective: Each sentence is too noun-heavy. Rewrite it more clearly by turning some nominalisations back into verbs — while keeping a formal, serious tone.

  1. The facilitation of the improvement of communication is an objective of the reorganisation.
  2. There was a consideration by the committee of the possibility of the postponement of the vote.
  3. The utilisation of the software resulted in an enhancement of the accuracy of the measurements.
✅ Answer

Model rewrites (yours may vary):

1. The reorganisation aims to improve communication.

2. The committee considered postponing the vote.

3. Using the software improved the accuracy of the measurements. (One nominalisation, accuracy, sensibly stays — it names a quality.)

🎯 Your Project: From Spoken to Scholarly

A two-part task in register control — a rewrite, then a short formal talk.

  1. (3 min) Find or write a short, chatty, spoken-style paragraph (4–6 sentences) on a topic you know — a work update, a news reaction, an opinion. Keep it deliberately informal and verb-heavy, with visible agents ("we", "they", "I reckon…").
  2. (8 min) Rewrite it in formal academic style: nominalise the key actions, bring in the passive and light verbs, remove the personal agents, and stack a couple of longer noun phrases. Aim for concise, objective, abstract.
  3. (4 min) Now rebalance: read your academic version aloud and hunt for over-nominalisation. Turn one or two hollow nouns back into verbs so it stays clear. Note what you changed and why.
  4. (5 min) — Present: Solo — record a 90-second formal "mini-briefing" on the same topic in academic register. Partner/group — deliver it to a listener, then have them paraphrase it back in casual, verbal English, so you both feel the two registers switch.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My informal source text was genuinely verbal and agent-visible
  • ☐ My academic rewrite nominalised the main actions
  • ☐ I used the passive and/or a light-verb collocation at least once
  • ☐ I removed the personal agents ("we / I / they") appropriately
  • ☐ I checked for over-nominalisation and rebalanced at least one sentence
  • ☐ My spoken briefing sounded formal without becoming unclear

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Nominalise to name a concept you'll refer back to, to link cause and effect in one sentence, or to keep an agent out of view on purpose.
  • Bank light-verb collocations (carry out an investigation, pose a threat, reach a conclusion) — they're the mortar of academic prose.
  • Read your formal writing aloud. If you run out of breath or lose the thread, you've probably over-nominalised — turn one noun back into a verb.
  • Let the genre set the dial: dense-nominal for reports and essays, live-verbal for emails, talks, and conversation.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wrong suffix / irregular noun: "the deciding" / "the arrivement" ❌ → the decision, the arrival ✅. Always confirm the actual noun.
  • Process vs person/result noun: "the analyst of the data" (when you mean the process) ❌ → the analysis of the data ✅.
  • Over-nominalisation: stacking three or four abstract nouns until no one can tell who did what — dense ≠ intelligent. Rebalance with a working verb.
  • Register mismatch: heavy nominal style in a chatty email, or breezy verbs in a research abstract — grammatical, but tonally off.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Nominalisation = expressing actions/qualities as nouns rather than verbs/adjectives (decide → decision, aware → awareness).
  • Formed with suffixes -tion/-sion, -ment, -ance/-ence, -ity, -ness, -al, -ure, -th, or zero-derivation (increase, change, use).
  • It makes writing concise, objective/impersonal, and abstract — the hallmark of academic, scientific, legal register. It pairs with the passive, "there is", and long noun phrases.
  • Build the style with light verbs (carry out an investigation, pose a threat, reach a conclusion).
  • Don't overdo it: noun-heavy prose goes vague and lifeless. Balance nouns with verbs — the C1 skill is appropriacy.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've learned to change the grammatical packaging of an idea at will — to compress verbs into nouns for a compact, impersonal, scholarly register, and then to judge, sentence by sentence, whether that's the right choice. That dual skill — producing the nominal style and knowing when a plain verb serves the reader better — is exactly the register control expected of an advanced writer. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is nominalisation always more "correct" or better?

No — it's a stylistic tool, not a rule. It's excellent for academic and formal writing, where concision and abstraction matter, and poor for conversation, emails, and talks that need to feel human. And even in formal writing, too much of it destroys clarity. "Better" depends entirely on the genre and reader.

How do I know which suffix a verb takes?

The suffixes follow patterns (verbs favour -tion/-ment/-ance; adjectives favour -ity/-ness), but there are many irregulars (believe → belief, choose → choice, grow → growth). Treat the noun as a separate item of vocabulary and confirm it rather than assembling it by rule — and note the collocating light verb at the same time.

Why do nominalisation and the passive keep appearing together?

Because both suppress the agent. Nominalisation turns "they decided" into "the decision" (no they); the passive turns "we assessed it" into "it was assessed" (no we). Formal writing often wants the focus on processes and findings rather than on named actors, so the two tools naturally combine.

🔭 Looking Ahead

Nominalisation lets you name a concept as a noun — which is precisely what makes it easy to refer back to.
