🎓 Lesson 8.3: Review Project — A Position Paper & Its Defence

This is it — the final lesson of C1, and a chance to bring the whole level together in one satisfying, integrated task. You'll do what advanced thinkers do in universities, boardrooms, and public life everywhere: take a reasoned stand on a debatable question, set it out in a concise, well-structured position paper, and then stand up and defend it against challenging questions. Every skill you've built across these twenty-four lessons — the grammar, the vocabulary, the reading and writing craft, the seminar and fluency skills — comes into play here. Let's finish strong.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

What a Position Paper Is

A position paper is a short, formal piece that takes a clear stand on a debatable question and argues for it with evidence and reasoning. It is not a neutral survey ("here are all the views") and not a rant ("here's what I feel"). It is a reasoned case: you commit to a position, support it, honestly acknowledge the strongest objection, answer that objection, and conclude. Universities, think-tanks, professional bodies, and international organisations all run on documents shaped exactly like this.

What makes a question suitable? It must be genuinely arguable — reasonable people could disagree. "Should remote work be the default for office jobs?" works; "Is the sky blue?" does not. Some tried-and-tested C1 prompts:

  • Should university education be free for everyone?
  • Is social media doing more harm than good to public debate?
  • Should cities ban private cars from their centres?
  • Ought governments to regulate artificial intelligence more tightly?
  • Is remote work better for productivity than office work?

Structure: Thesis to Rebuttal

A position paper follows a reliable shape. Master it and you have a frame you can pour any argument into. The one move that lifts it above a school essay is the counter-argument: acknowledging the best objection and answering it. That single step signals a mind that has genuinely weighed the question.

PartJobTypical language
1. Thesisstate your position clearly and early"This paper argues that…" / "It will be contended that…"
2. Argument Afirst supported reason, with evidence"The primary reason is… / Research suggests…"
3. Argument Bsecond supported reason, linked to the first"Furthermore… / A related consideration is…"
4. Counter-argumentacknowledge the strongest opposing view fairly"Admittedly… / It might be objected that…"
5. Rebuttalanswer that objection; show why your view still holds"However… / This concern, while valid, overlooks…"
6. Conclusionrestate the position, widened or sharpened"On balance… / The weight of evidence therefore…"

The concede-then-rebut move (parts 4–5) is worth practising on its own, because it's where sophistication lives. You give the opposing side its due — genuinely, not as a straw man — and then show why, on balance, your position survives. The reader trusts an argument far more when it has clearly faced its opposition and not flinched.

💡 Keep it concise (Lesson 8.2 in action)

A position paper is short by design — often 250–400 words. That makes concision essential: every sentence must earn its place. Draft freely, then cut the padding, drop the empty intensifiers, and dig verbs out of nouns where directness helps. A tight, well-edited paper reads as far more authoritative than a padded one twice its length.

Defending Your Case

Writing the paper is half the task; defending it aloud is the other half — and the one that tests everything from Module 7 and 8. When you present, you'll face questions: some clarifying, some genuinely challenging. The goal is not to "win" by refusing to yield, nor to collapse at the first push. It's to respond with poise: concede what's fair, hold what's sound, and keep talking coherently under pressure.

Three moves carry a good defence:

  • Concession — grant the fair part of a challenge before answering: "You're right that costs are a concern, and I don't want to dismiss that…"
  • Hedging — calibrate your certainty rather than overclaiming: "I'd suggest that, in most cases…", "It seems likely that…"
  • Fluency strategies (Lesson 8.1) — buy time, paraphrase, and repair so you never freeze: "That's a good question — let me think for a second."

Phrase bank — presenting & defending a position

this paper argues that… the primary reason is… it might be objected that… while that concern is valid, it overlooks… admittedly, … however… that's a fair point — let me address it… I take your point, but I'd argue… to some extent, yes; that said… I'd suggest that, on balance… if I've understood your question… let me come at that from another angle… the weight of evidence points to…

✅ Concede without collapsing

The most impressive thing you can do in a defence is grant a real point and still hold your position: "You're absolutely right that the transition would be costly — I won't pretend otherwise. But the cost is one-off, whereas the benefit recurs every year, which is exactly why I'd still argue for it." That combination — honest concession, then a reasoned "and yet" — is the sound of a confident, advanced mind.

Listening: A Paper & Its Defence

Listen to Priya present the core of her position paper and then field two challenging questions in the Q&A. Notice the structure (thesis → argument → rebuttal), the hedging, and the way she concedes a fair point without abandoning her stand. Tap the 🔊 speaker on each line to hear it.

Priya: This paper argues that city centres should be closed to private cars. The primary reason is public health: given the levels of air pollution now recorded, the case for action is, I'd suggest, difficult to dismiss.

Priya: Furthermore, freeing that space transforms how a city feels — wider pavements, cleaner air, room for people rather than parking. Admittedly, it might be objected that such a ban would harm local businesses. That concern, while valid, overlooks the evidence: pedestrianised streets tend to see footfall rise, not fall.

Chair: Thank you. Let's open it up. First question.

Questioner 1: But what about disabled residents, or elderly people who genuinely can't walk far? Isn't a blanket ban unfair to them?

Priya: That's a fair point, and I don't want to brush it aside. You're right that a blanket ban would be unjust. That said, the proposal I'd defend isn't a blanket one — it exempts accessible transport and permit-holders. So the principle stands, provided the exemptions are properly designed.

Questioner 2: You claim footfall rises. Isn't that just true in wealthy cities that could afford good public transport anyway?

Priya: Hmm, that's a good challenge — let me think for a second. To some extent, yes: the effect is clearest where transport is strong. However, I'd argue the causation runs both ways — the ban itself creates the pressure to improve transport. So I'd hedge my claim slightly: it works best alongside investment, not instead of it.

Chair: A measured answer. Thank you both.

Practice & Capstone Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Mixed C1 review

Objective: Complete each gap, drawing on grammar and vocabulary from across the whole level. Read the hint in italics.

  1. the scale of the problem, action is overdue. (participle clause — one word)
  2. The policy poses a serious threat small businesses. (dependent preposition after 'threat')
  3. Research that footfall tends to rise. (academic reporting verb; 3rd-person -s)
  4. The costs are high; , the benefits recur every year. (cohesive contrast linker)
  5. It would that the evidence supports the change. (hedging: 'It would ___ that…')
  6. Not only is it costly, it is also unjust. (inversion pairs with 'not only… ___ also')
✅ Answer

1. Given (participle clause: "Given the scale…") · 2. to (pose a threat to) · 3. suggests (academic reporting verb) · 4. however (contrast cohesion) · 5. appear ("It would appear that…" — hedging) · 6. but ("Not only… but also")

🏋️ Exercise 2: Polish this sentence

Objective: Tighten each wordy sentence for a position paper. Type the concise replacement for the underlined-style phrase in each gap.

  1. "Due to the fact that" → the evidence is clear, we should act. (one word)
  2. The committee will "make a decision" → next week. (strong verb for 'make a decision')
  3. We must act "at this point in time" → . (one word)
  4. The plan is "very important" → to the city's future. (one strong adjective)
✅ Answer

1. because · 2. decide · 3. now · 4. crucial (or vital / essential). Each swap trades padding for a lean, direct equivalent — exactly the editing move a tight position paper needs.

🎓 Your Capstone Project: Position Paper & Defence

The final project of C1, in two acts — write, then defend. Give it the time it deserves; this is where the whole level comes together.

  1. (3 min) Choose your question. Pick a genuinely debatable question you care about (see the list above, or your own). Decide your position — commit to one side.
  2. (15 min) Write the paper (250–400 words). Follow the structure: thesis → argument A → argument B → counter-argument acknowledged → rebuttal → conclusion. Weave in the C1 toolkit — a participle clause, hedging, an academic collocation, a cohesive linker, perhaps an inversion in the conclusion.
  3. (5 min) Edit for concision (Lesson 8.2). Cut the padding, drop empty intensifiers, and dig out strong verbs. Aim to trim ~15–20% without losing meaning. Read it aloud.
  4. (8 min) Present it aloud. Deliver the paper (solo to a recorder, or to a partner/group). Use good prosody — stress your key claims, pause at your signposts.
  5. (8 min) Defend it. Have a partner (or your own prepared list) ask three challenging questions. Field each with concession, hedging, and fluency strategies — concede what's fair, hold what's sound, never freeze.
  6. (3 min) Reflect. Note which C1 tools you used, which question was hardest to answer, and one thing you'd sharpen next time.

Model excerpt — a strong thesis, one body point, and a rebuttal move:

Thesis: "This paper contends that university tuition should be free at the point of use. Not only does free access widen opportunity, but it also yields a more skilled and productive society."

Body point: "The primary argument is economic. Given that graduates pay higher taxes over a lifetime, public investment in their education tends to repay itself — a return that private fees, by deterring poorer applicants, actively undermine."

Rebuttal move: "Admittedly, it might be objected that free tuition is unaffordable. That concern, while understandable, overlooks the long-term fiscal return; the cost, though real, is an investment rather than an expense. On balance, then, the case for access outweighs the case for fees."

✅ Capstone Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My question is genuinely debatable and my thesis states a clear position
  • ☐ I gave two or three supported arguments, not just opinions
  • ☐ I acknowledged a real counter-argument and rebutted it fairly
  • ☐ I wove in several C1 structures on purpose (participle clause, hedging, cohesion, inversion, academic collocation)
  • ☐ I edited the paper for concision and read it aloud
  • ☐ I presented it with good prosody and defended it, conceding fairly and hedging where needed
  • ☐ I fielded challenging questions without freezing, using fluency strategies

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Commit to a clear thesis early — the reader should know your position by the end of the first sentence or two.
  • Give the counter-argument its due — steel-man it, then rebut it. A fair objection fairly answered is your most persuasive move.
  • Weave in the C1 toolkit deliberately — but only where the argument genuinely needs it, not as decoration.
  • Edit for concision before you present; a tight paper sounds authoritative.
  • In defence, concede what's fair and hold what's sound, using hedging and fluency strategies to stay composed.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • No real position: surveying every view without committing turns a position paper into a neutral summary. Take a stand.
  • Straw-manning the opposition: a weak, distorted counter-argument fools no one and weakens your rebuttal. Give the real objection.
  • Overclaiming: "This proves conclusively that…" invites easy attack. Hedge appropriately — "the evidence strongly suggests…".
  • Cramming in every structure: forcing inversions and nominalisations where they don't fit reads as showing off, not skill.
  • Collapsing under a question: abandoning your whole case at the first challenge — or, equally, refusing to concede anything. Aim for the poised middle.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A position paper takes a clear stand on a debatable question and argues it with evidence — it is neither a neutral survey nor a rant.

  • It's a chance to integrate the whole C1 toolkit — participle clauses, hedging, inversion, conditionals, nominalisation, cohesion, and academic collocation — used where the argument needs them.
  • Defending a case means conceding what's fair and holding what's sound, using hedging, concession, and fluency strategies to stay composed.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

My position paper still feels less polished than a native's. Is that normal at C1?

Completely normal — and it's worth reframing. C1 is not "indistinguishable from a native writer"; it's effective, flexible command across demanding tasks, with occasional slips that don't impede communication. Your paper argues clearly, concedes fairly, and reads formally. That's exactly the C1 target. Polish keeps growing for years; you've already crossed the threshold.

How can I keep improving now that I've finished the C1 lessons?

Use the language for real: read demanding material (quality journalism, academic articles), write position papers and reports on things you care about, and speak in situations that stretch you — debates, presentations, discussions. Keep a running list of collocations and structures you meet. And when you're ready for the next formal step, the C2 course is built to take you there.

Is C1 the end of the road?

Not at all — C1 is Advanced, and above it sits C2 (Proficiency), the highest CEFR level, where the focus shifts to near-effortless precision, nuance, idiom, and style. Completing C1 is a major achievement and a natural launch-point, not a finish line. If you want to keep climbing, C2 is the next step — and you're ready for it.
