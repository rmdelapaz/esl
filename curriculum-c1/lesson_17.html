📝 Lesson 6.2: Academic & Argument Writing — Structuring an Essay

A strong argument essay is not a pile of good sentences — it is an architecture. A reader should be able to feel the shape of your case before they've finished: here is the claim, here is the ground it stands on, here is the objection handled, here is the payoff. In the last lesson you learned to take an argument apart as a critical reader. Now you'll build one that could withstand that same scrutiny — a clear thesis, disciplined paragraphs, and the connective tissue that makes prose flow as a single, purposeful whole.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write a precise, arguable thesis statement and an introduction that moves from hook to context to thesis
  • Build body paragraphs on strong topic sentences, following the point → evidence → explanation → link pattern
  • Achieve coherence and cohesion across paragraphs with referencing back and signposting connectors (Furthermore, Nevertheless, Consequently, By contrast)
  • Acknowledge and rebut counter-arguments, and write a conclusion that restates, synthesises, and points to an implication — without introducing new points

⏱️ Estimated Time: 60–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Plan and begin drafting a four-to-five-paragraph argument essay on a given question — producing at minimum a full outline and a complete introduction.

The Shape of an Argument Essay

A formal argument essay does one job: it advances and defends a position in response to a question. Everything in it serves that position. The classic shape has three movements — an introduction that states the case, a body of paragraphs that build it, and a conclusion that consolidates it. Within that frame, two broad essay types recur:

Essay typeWhat it doesTypical thesis shape
For-and-against (discursive)weighs both sides of an issue before reaching a balanced judgement"While X has clear merits, the case for Y is ultimately stronger."
Opinion / persuasiveargues for one position and defends it throughout"This essay will argue that X should be adopted, for three reasons."

Both types are argued, not merely described — even the balanced for-and-against essay reaches a verdict. The difference is how much space the opposing view gets and where your commitment sits. Read the question carefully: "To what extent do you agree?" invites a nuanced, weighing answer; "Argue that…" asks for a committed one.

🧠 Mindset

At C1 you already have the vocabulary and grammar to say sophisticated things. What an essay tests is something else — your ability to organise thought so a reader can follow it effortlessly. This can feel oddly mechanical at first, as though structure were a cage around your ideas. It isn't: structure is what lets a reader receive your ideas at full strength. A brilliant point buried in a shapeless paragraph lands as a mumble. Give your thinking an architecture and it becomes an argument. 🌱

The Thesis Statement

The thesis statement is the single sentence (occasionally two) that states your position and, often, previews your reasoning. It is the spine of the essay: every paragraph should connect back to it. A weak thesis is vague, obvious, or merely announces a topic; a strong thesis is specific, arguable, and defensible.

Weak thesisWhy it failsStronger thesis
"This essay is about remote work."announces a topic, takes no position"Remote work benefits employers as much as employees, and should become the default rather than the exception."
"Pollution is bad."obvious; nobody would argue the reverse"Tackling urban pollution requires restricting private cars, not merely improving them."
"There are many views on tourism."describes debate but commits to nothing"Although mass tourism strains local communities, well-regulated tourism remains a net benefit for developing regions."

A model thesis (for-and-against type): "While social media has undeniably widened access to information, its unregulated design does more to erode public discourse than to enrich it — and the case for stronger oversight is now compelling."

Notice the moves: it concedes a genuine strength ("undeniably widened access"), states a clear position ("erode… more than… enrich"), and hints at the direction of the argument ("the case for stronger oversight"). A reader knows exactly where this essay is going.

💡 The test of a good thesis

Ask: could a reasonable person disagree with this? If not, it isn't a thesis — it's a fact or a truism. And ask: does it answer the actual question asked? A dazzling thesis on the wrong question earns nothing. Draft the thesis early, but be willing to sharpen it once your body paragraphs reveal what you really think.

The Introduction

An effective introduction funnels the reader from the wide world into your precise claim. The reliable pattern is three moves: hook → context → thesis.

graph LR
    A["Hook (engage / orient the reader)"] --> B["Context (background / narrow the topic)"]
    B --> C["Thesis (your specific arguable claim)"]
  • Hook — a striking fact, a pointed question, or a crisp statement of the problem that earns the reader's attention. Avoid the throat-clearing opener ("Since the dawn of time, humans have…").
  • Context — the minimum background the reader needs, and a narrowing toward your specific angle. Define any key term you'll rely on.
  • Thesis — your position, usually the last sentence of the paragraph, where it lands with the most force.

📄 Model introduction

"Every year, the average smartphone user unlocks their device more than a thousand times a week. (hook) As screens have colonised commutes, meals, and even bedtimes, educators and psychologists have begun to ask whether constant connectivity is quietly reshaping our capacity for sustained attention. (context) While digital tools offer genuine gains in access and flexibility, the evidence increasingly suggests that unmanaged smartphone use erodes deep concentration — and that schools, not just individuals, must take deliberate steps to protect it. (thesis)"

See how the paragraph tightens: a vivid statistic, a bridging sentence that frames the debate, and a thesis that both concedes ("genuine gains") and commits ("erodes deep concentration… schools must take steps"). By the end of one paragraph, the reader knows the subject, the stakes, and your position.

Body Paragraphs: The PEEL Move

Each body paragraph should defend one idea that supports the thesis, and it should do so in a predictable, reader-friendly rhythm. A useful pattern is PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link.

StageWhat it doesSignalling language
Point (topic sentence)states the paragraph's single claim up front"One clear advantage is…" / "A more serious concern is…"
Evidencesupports it with data, example, research, or authority"For instance…" / "A recent study found…" / "According to…"
Explanationinterprets the evidence — why it supports the point"This suggests that…" / "The implication is…"
Linkties back to the thesis and eases into the next paragraph"This strengthens the case that…" / "It follows that…"

The topic sentence is the load-bearing element. A reader should be able to skim only the first sentence of each body paragraph and still grasp your whole argument. If your topic sentences don't add up to your thesis, the structure is off — no amount of good detail will rescue it.

📄 Model body paragraph (PEEL)

"(Point) The most immediate cost of constant connectivity is fragmented attention. (Evidence) Studies of workplace behaviour have found that office workers switch tasks every few minutes when notifications are enabled, and that returning to a complex task after an interruption can take over twenty minutes. (Explanation) This matters because deep, uninterrupted focus is precisely what demanding intellectual work requires; a mind pulled away every few minutes never reaches the sustained engagement that produces genuine understanding. (Link) If even brief interruptions carry such a heavy cognitive toll, the case for deliberately protecting periods of undistracted work becomes difficult to dismiss."

💡 This is where Module 3 and 5 pay off

Argument writing is where the nominalisation and academic vocabulary you built earlier in the course come into their own. Notice the model's abstract, noun-based phrasing — "fragmented attention," "the sustained engagement that produces genuine understanding," "such a heavy cognitive toll." That impersonal, concept-focused register is exactly what formal writing rewards. Reach for it deliberately; just don't let it tip into the airless over-nominalisation you learned to catch in Lesson 3.1.

Cohesion & Signposting

An essay must be coherent (the ideas hang together logically) and cohesive (the sentences and paragraphs are stitched together with visible links). Two devices do most of the work: referencing back to earlier ideas, and signposting connectors that tell the reader what kind of move comes next.

RelationshipSignposting connectorsWhat they tell the reader
AddingFurthermore, Moreover, In addition, What is more"here is another point on the same side"
ContrastingHowever, Nevertheless, By contrast, On the other hand"expect a turn against what I just said"
ResultConsequently, Therefore, As a result, Thus"here is what follows from that"
ConcedingAdmittedly, Granted, It is true that"I accept this point, but wait"
ExemplifyingFor instance, To illustrate, A case in point"here is the evidence for it"

Referencing back keeps a text feeling like one continuous argument: pronouns (this, these, such), summarising nouns (this tension, that assumption, such measures), and short recaps ("Having established that…") all point the reader to what came before. Used well, they weave your paragraphs into a fabric rather than a list.

⚠️ Signposting is seasoning, not the meal

Connectors guide the reader, but a paragraph that opens every sentence with "Moreover… Furthermore… In addition…" reads like a checklist and buries the actual thinking. Use a connector when the logical relationship genuinely needs marking — a real contrast, a real consequence — and let clear sentence order carry the rest. And match the connector to the logic: "Consequently" must introduce a genuine result, "Nevertheless" a genuine concession. A mismatched connector confuses more than no connector at all.

Counter-Arguments & Rebuttal

Here is the mark of a mature argument: it takes the opposing view seriously. Acknowledging a counter-argument and then rebutting it makes your case stronger, not weaker — it shows you have considered the objections and still hold your ground. Ignoring them looks like you either missed them or feared them.

The reliable pattern is concede → rebut: state the opposing point fairly, then explain why your position survives it.

Concede: "Admittedly, critics argue that restricting phones in schools simply pushes the problem out of sight, teaching avoidance rather than self-regulation."

Rebut: "Yet this objection underestimates the value of protected space: self-regulation is a skill built through practice, and pupils cannot practise focus in an environment engineered to fragment it. Removing the distraction is not avoidance but the precondition for learning the skill at all."

The language of concession and rebuttal is a small, high-value phrase set: Admittedly / It might be argued that / Critics contend that to open the concession, and Yet / However / This overlooks / Such a view fails to account for to pivot into the rebuttal. Handled with genuine fairness — not a straw-man version of the other side — this is where an essay earns a reader's trust.

Phrase bank — building and defending an argument

This essay will argue that… There are three main reasons for this. One clear advantage is… A more serious concern, however, is… The evidence strongly suggests that… It might be argued that… Yet… Admittedly, … Nevertheless, … This overlooks the fact that… Taken together, these points indicate… On balance, the stronger case is that… In conclusion, the weight of evidence favours… The wider implication is that…

The Conclusion

A conclusion consolidates; it does not continue. Its job is to leave the reader with your argument settled in their mind. The dependable pattern is restate → synthesise → implication.

  • Restate the thesis in fresh words — not a copy-paste of the introduction, but the same position, now earned.
  • Synthesise the main threads — draw the body's points together into a single, weighted judgement rather than listing them again.
  • Implication — end on the "so what": a consequence, a recommendation, or a broader significance that gives the essay resonance.

📄 Model conclusion

"(Restate) The convenience of constant connectivity, then, comes at a measurable cost to the sustained attention that serious learning demands. (Synthesise) The fragmentation of focus, the difficulty of recovering it, and the failure of purely individual willpower to resist a technology engineered to interrupt all point the same way: the problem is structural, not merely personal. (Implication) If schools are serious about equipping young people to think deeply, protecting undistracted time cannot be left to chance — it must become a deliberate part of how education is designed."

⚠️ The cardinal rule: no new points

A conclusion is not the place for a fresh argument, a new piece of evidence, or a surprise twist. Introducing something new there leaves the reader with a loose thread just as you wanted them to feel resolution. If a point is worth making, it belongs in a body paragraph where it can be properly defended. The conclusion's only job is to gather what you have already argued and give it a sense of arrival.

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These five sentences are ready-made frames for the key moves of an argument essay — a thesis, a topic sentence, a concession, a rebuttal, and a conclusion.

  • This essay will argue that stronger regulation, rather than individual restraint, offers the more realistic solution.
  • One of the most compelling arguments in favour of this view is its impact on long-term wellbeing.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Order the parts of an essay

Objective: Number these components from 1 (comes first) to 6 (comes last) in a well-structured argument essay. Type the number in each box.

  1. Conclusion (restate, synthesise, implication) →
  2. Hook that opens the introduction →
  3. Thesis statement →
  4. Counter-argument and rebuttal paragraph →
  5. Context / background in the introduction →
  6. Body paragraphs supporting the thesis →
✅ Answer

Hook (1) → Context (2) → Thesis (3) → Body paragraphs (4) → Counter-argument + rebuttal (5) → Conclusion (6). The introduction funnels hook → context → thesis; the counter-argument sits late in the body, just before the conclusion consolidates.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the signposting connector

Objective: Type the connector that fits the logic. Choose from: Furthermore, Nevertheless, Consequently, By contrast, Admittedly.

  1. "The scheme raised costs sharply. , several branches were forced to close." (result)
  2. "Rail travel is efficient in dense cities. , in rural regions it is often impractical." (contrast between two things)
  3. "The policy is expensive. , its long-term benefits justify the cost." (concession then a turn)
  4. "Remote work cuts commuting time. , it widens the pool of available talent." (adding a point on the same side)
  5. ", some employees struggle to focus at home — yet most report higher productivity overall." (opening a concession)
✅ Answer

1. Consequently (result) · 2. By contrast (contrast) · 3. Nevertheless (concede then turn) · 4. Furthermore (add) · 5. Admittedly (open a concession)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Complete a thesis frame

Objective: Using the model frames below, write a specific, arguable thesis for the question: "Should governments invest more in public libraries in the digital age?" Draft one of each type.

  1. For-and-against frame: "While ______________, ______________ ; on balance, ______________."
  2. Opinion frame: "This essay will argue that ______________, for ______________ reasons."
✅ Answer

Model answers (yours will differ in wording):

1. "While much information has migrated online, public libraries remain vital community and learning spaces; on balance, continued public investment is not only justified but urgent."

2. "This essay will argue that governments should increase library funding, for three reasons: libraries reduce the digital divide, support lifelong learning, and anchor local communities."

Each takes a clear, arguable position and previews the reasoning — exactly what a thesis must do.

🎯 Your Project: Plan and Draft an Argument Essay

Choose one of these questions (or a comparable one that interests you):

  • "To what extent should university education be free for all?"
  • "Do the benefits of remote working outweigh its drawbacks?"
  • "Should governments ban single-use plastics outright?"

Then plan and begin drafting a four-to-five-paragraph essay. At minimum, produce a full outline and a complete introduction.

  1. (5 min) Decide your position and write a specific, arguable thesis statement.
  2. (10 min) Build an outline: a topic sentence for each body paragraph (two or three), a note of the evidence each will use, and a planned counter-argument with your rebuttal.
  3. (10 min) Write a full introduction using hook → context → thesis.
  4. (10 min, optional but encouraged) Draft one full body paragraph using the PEEL pattern, and sketch your conclusion's restate → synthesise → implication in note form.
  5. (3 min) Reread: do your topic sentences, read alone, add up to your thesis? Have you signposted only where the logic needs it?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My thesis is specific, arguable, and answers the actual question
  • ☐ My introduction moves clearly from hook to context to thesis
  • ☐ Each body paragraph has a strong topic sentence stating one point
  • ☐ My outline includes a counter-argument and my rebuttal to it
  • ☐ I used signposting connectors purposefully, matched to the logic
  • ☐ My planned conclusion synthesises without introducing new points

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Draft your thesis first, then check that every paragraph earns its place by connecting back to it.
  • Open each body paragraph with a topic sentence so strong that the essay's argument survives a skim of first lines alone.
  • Follow PEEL — don't stop at evidence; the explanation that says why it matters is where marks and persuasion live.
  • Concede and rebut a genuine counter-argument; engaging the strongest opposing point builds trust.
  • Signpost purposefully, matching each connector to a real logical relationship.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Thesis-free essays: describing a topic instead of arguing a position. If a reasonable person can't disagree, it isn't a thesis.
  • Evidence without explanation: dropping a fact or quotation and moving on. Always say why it supports your point.
  • Connector overload: "Moreover… Furthermore… In addition…" opening every sentence buries the thinking. Season, don't drown.
  • Straw-man rebuttals: knocking down a weak, distorted version of the other side fools no critical reader. Rebut the real objection.
  • New points in the conclusion: a fresh argument at the end leaves a loose thread. Consolidate only.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • An argument essay advances and defends a position; the two broad types are for-and-against (weigh, then judge) and opinion/persuasive (commit throughout).
  • The thesis is a specific, arguable claim; the introduction funnels hook → context → thesis.
  • Body paragraphs follow PEEL — Point (topic sentence) → Evidence → Explanation → Link — with one idea each.
  • Cohesion comes from referencing back and purposeful signposting (Furthermore, Nevertheless, Consequently, By contrast); concede → rebut handles counter-arguments.
  • The conclusion restates, synthesises, and points to an implication — and introduces no new points.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You now have a repeatable architecture for formal argument — a way to turn a position into a piece of writing a reader can follow effortlessly and finds hard to dismiss. Combined with the critical-reading eye from the last lesson, you can both build a case and test one. That two-way command of argument is the heart of academic and professional literacy. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I always have to include a counter-argument?

In a genuine argument essay, almost always — engaging the opposing view is what separates a persuasive case from a one-sided assertion. The exception is a very short piece or a purely expository task. Even then, a single sentence acknowledging the other side ("Although some maintain that…, …") usually strengthens your writing. When in doubt, concede and rebut.

How long should each part be?

As a rough guide for a five-paragraph essay: introduction and conclusion each about 10–15% of the total, and the body paragraphs sharing the remaining 70–80% roughly equally. The body is where the argument is made, so it deserves the bulk of your words. But let the argument, not a formula, set the exact proportions — a complex counter-argument may earn extra space.

Can I use "I" in a formal argument essay?

It depends on the register and the task. Traditional academic writing often prefers impersonal phrasing ("This essay argues…," "The evidence suggests…") over "I think." A persuasive opinion piece or a task that explicitly asks for your view can use "I" more freely. When unsure, lean impersonal — it reads as more objective — and follow any style guidance you've been given.
