🧩 Lesson 4.1: Phrasal Verbs & Multi-word Verbs

Few features of English separate the confident C1 speaker from the merely fluent as sharply as multi-word verbs. They are everywhere in natural speech — set off, take off, put up with, come across — yet they trip learners on two fronts at once: grammar (where does the object go?) and meaning (why does take off mean five different things?). In this lesson you'll master the four types, the separability rules that govern them, the leap from literal to idiomatic, and — crucially at C1 — how to choose between a phrasal verb and its more formal single-word synonym.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish the four types of multi-word verb and describe the grammar of each
  • Apply the separability rules, including the pronoun-placement rule that catches most learners
  • Interpret literal vs idiomatic meanings and cope with polysemous phrasal verbs that carry many senses
  • Choose between a phrasal verb and its single-word synonym to match register precisely

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Project: Narrate a process or story (a project, a trip, a career move) rich in phrasal verbs — then produce a formal version, swapping in single-word synonyms where the register calls for it.

What Counts as a Multi-word Verb

A multi-word verb is a verb combined with one or two small words — a particle — that together behave as a single lexical unit with a meaning often unpredictable from its parts. That particle may function as an adverb (off, up, out, away, back) or a preposition (after, with, for, into), and this distinction quietly governs everything about how the verb behaves in a sentence.

The term phrasal verb is often used loosely for the whole family. Precisely speaking, grammarians separate them, but for a learner the useful move is to recognise the four types below, because the type tells you the two things you most need to know: can it take an object? and where does that object go?

🧠 Mindset

Phrasal verbs have a reputation as the "impossible" corner of English — thousands of them, each with a fistful of meanings, learned (badly) as endless lists. Reframe it: you already understand hundreds from years of input; what remains is refining production — getting the grammar right and choosing the register-appropriate option. That's a C1 polish task, not a memorisation marathon. Learn them in context, notice them in what you read, and they'll settle into place far faster than a vocabulary list ever managed. 🌱

The Four Types (and Their Grammar)

Sort every multi-word verb into one of these four boxes and its grammar becomes predictable. The distinguishing questions are whether it takes an object and whether the object may sit between the verb and the particle.

TypeStructure & ruleExamples
1. Intransitive phrasal verb verb + particle, no object. Nothing can be inserted. The car broke down. · We set off at dawn. · She grew up in Lima.
2. Transitive — separable verb + particle + object; the object can go between the verb and particle. (A pronoun object must — see the rule below.) turn off the light / turn the light off · throw away the mail / throw it away
3. Transitive — inseparable (prepositional verb) verb + preposition + object; the object always follows the particle. Never split. look after the kids · deal with a complaint · cope with stress · rely on a friend
4. Three-part (verb + adverb + preposition) verb + two particles + object; always inseparable. The object follows both particles. look forward to the trip · put up with the noise · get on with a colleague · run out of time

Notice that types 2 and 3 both take a single object, yet behave in opposite ways: you can say turn the light off but never look the kids after. That is why the particle's identity (adverb vs preposition) matters — and why the safest habit is to learn each verb together with a sample object, so the pattern is stored, not guessed.

The Separability Rule

This is the single most error-prone point in the whole topic, so it earns its own card. For a separable (Type 2) verb, a noun object may sit in either position, but a pronoun object has no choice.

⚠️ THE PRONOUN RULE — memorise this

With a separable phrasal verb, if the object is a pronoun (it, them, him, her, us, you), it MUST go between the verb and the particle:

  • Turn it off. ✅    Turn off it. ❌
  • Throw them away. ✅    Throw away them. ❌
  • Pick her up at six. ✅    Pick up her at six. ❌

A noun object is flexible (turn off the light / turn the light off), but a pronoun is not. And beware the trap: inseparable verbs do the reverse — the pronoun stays after the particle: look after them ✅, not look them after ❌. Pronoun placement is the classic tell of a non-native speaker; getting it right is a quick, high-value win.

A practical corollary: when the object is long or heavy (a whole clause or a long noun phrase), speakers naturally push it to the end, keeping verb and particle together — She turned off every light in the house before she left sounds far better than splitting the particle from its verb across a long stretch of words. So the pattern is: short/pronoun object → split; long object → keep together.

ObjectSeparable verbInseparable verb
Nounfill in the form / fill the form in ✅ (both)deal with the form ✅ / deal the form with ❌
Pronounfill it in ✅ / fill in it ❌deal with it ✅ / deal it with ❌

Literal, Idiomatic & Polysemous

Many multi-word verbs began literally and drifted toward idiom. The particle sometimes keeps its plain directional sense (sit down, stand up, go out), but just as often the whole unit means something you could never assemble from the parts.

Literal-ish: "The plane took off on time." (rose from the ground)

Idiomatic: "The business really took off last year." (suddenly succeeded / grew fast)

The second challenge is polysemy: one phrasal verb, many meanings. Take off, put on, make out, and pick up each carry a surprising range, and only context (and collocation) tells you which is meant.

Phrasal verbSenseExample
take offleave the ground (literal)The flight took off late.
become successful (idiomatic)Sales took off after the launch.
remove (clothing) / deductShe took off her coat. · They took 10% off the price.
pick uplift / collect a person or thingI'll pick you up at eight.
learn informally / acquireHe picked up Portuguese while travelling.
improve / resumeBusiness is picking up. · Let's pick up where we left off.
make outmanage to see/hear/understandI could just make out a signature.
claim / imply (often dubiously)He made out that he'd done all the work.

💡 Learn them in context, not in lists

Because a bare phrasal verb can mean half a dozen things, an isolated list ("take off = succeed") teaches you one narrow slice and none of the crucial collocation. Store them in phrases you've actually met — the business took off, pick up a language, put up with the noise — and the right sense comes bundled with the words it lives among. This mirrors the collocation work coming in Lesson 4.2.

Register: Phrasal vs Single-word

Here is the distinction that most defines phrasal verbs at C1. As a broad rule, phrasal verbs are more informal and characteristic of speech, while their single-word synonyms — very often of Latin or French origin — are more formal and characteristic of writing. Neither is "better"; the skill is choosing by register.

💡 The register pairs — a C1 key point

In a chat you'd naturally put off a meeting; in a formal email you might postpone it. Same meaning, different register. Keep this table where you can revisit it — the left column belongs to conversation and informal writing, the right to reports, essays, and formal correspondence.

Phrasal (informal / spoken)Single-word (formal / written)
put offpostpone
find outdiscover
look intoinvestigate
go upincrease, rise
set upestablish
give upabandon, relinquish
put up withtolerate
carry outconduct, perform
get rid ofeliminate, remove
come acrossencounter

⚠️ Don't over-correct

The lesson is not "phrasal = bad, Latinate = sophisticated." A report stuffed with relinquish and eliminate where give up and get rid of would do reads as stiff and pompous. Equally, an academic essay peppered with find out and go up reads as under-dressed. Real command means reading the situation and picking the register the moment calls for — sometimes the phrasal verb is the more natural, precise choice, even in fairly formal speech.

Nominalised Forms

A neat bonus: many phrasal verbs have spun off nouns. The two parts fuse (often into one word, sometimes hyphenated) and, tellingly, the stress shifts to the first syllable — a hallmark of the noun form.

Verb (stress on particle)Noun (stress on first part)Example
break downa breakdowna nervous breakdown; a breakdown of the figures
break outan outbreak (note the flip)an outbreak of flu
take overa takeovera hostile takeover
come out / turn outan outcomea positive outcome
put ininputThanks for your input.

These nominalisations are highly useful in formal and academic writing, where turning an action into a thing lets you build denser, more abstract sentences — the very move you practised in Lesson 3.1 on nominalisation. Note the spelling: most are written as one word (breakdown, takeover, outcome, input), a few take a hyphen, and — a common trap — the noun is often not spaced like the verb (to break down the verb, but a breakdown the noun).

Phrase Bank & Dialogue

A working store of high-frequency phrasal verbs, grouped by theme so the collocations travel together:

set off (start a journey) get away (escape / holiday) check in / check out put up (accommodate) stop over take on (a task/staff) carry out (a plan) set up (a business) follow up lay off (staff) get on with (a person) fall out (with someone) make up (reconcile) put up with look up to (admire) work out (exercise / solve) cut down on give up (quit) come down with (an illness) get over (recover) look into find out point out bring up (a topic) sum up

Now hear them at work. Below, two colleagues speak naturally — dense with phrasal verbs — followed by the same content rewritten as a formal written update.

A: So how did the launch go? I heard it nearly fell through.

B: It almost did. Two suppliers pulled out at the last minute, so we had to put it off by a week and set up new contracts from scratch.

A: Ouch. How did the team hold up?

B: Honestly, they put up with a lot. A couple of them nearly burned out, but once things picked up, morale came back. Sales have taken off since.

A: Nice. Did you ever find out why the suppliers backed out?

B: We looked into it. Turns out a competitor had taken them over. We've since cut down on single-source deals to avoid getting caught out again.

Formal written version: The launch was nearly abandoned. Two suppliers withdrew at short notice, obliging us to postpone it by a week and establish new contracts from scratch. The team tolerated considerable pressure — several members approached exhaustion — but as performance recovered, morale returned, and sales have since increased sharply. On investigating why the suppliers withdrew, we discovered that a competitor had acquired them; we have consequently reduced our reliance on single-source agreements to avoid a recurrence.

Read them back to back and the register shift is unmistakable: the spoken version is warm, immediate, and idiomatic; the written version is measured and impersonal. Both are "correct" — each simply belongs to its own context.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Place the object correctly

Objective: Apply the separability and pronoun rules. Rewrite each sentence in every position that is grammatical; mark any impossible version with ❌.

  1. (separable) turn down + the offer / + it
  2. (inseparable) look after + the baby / + her
  3. (separable) throw away + these old receipts / + them
  4. (three-part) put up with + the delays / + them
✅ Answer

1. turn down the offer / turn the offer down (both ✅); pronoun must split: turn it down ✅ (turn down it ❌).
2. Inseparable — object always after: look after the baby ✅, look after her ✅; look the baby after / look her after ❌.
3. throw away these old receipts / throw these old receipts away (both ✅); throw them away ✅ (throw away them ❌).
4. Three-part, inseparable: put up with the delays ✅, put up with them ✅; you can never split it (put the delays up with ❌).

🏋️ Exercise 2: Match to the formal single-word synonym

Objective: Pair each phrasal verb with its more formal equivalent — the choice you'd make in a report.

PhrasalOptions
1. carry outa. tolerate   b. investigate   c. establish   d. conduct   e. eliminate   f. postpone
2. put off
3. look into
4. set up
5. put up with
6. get rid of
✅ Answer

1 → d (conduct) · 2 → f (postpone) · 3 → b (investigate) · 4 → c (establish) · 5 → a (tolerate) · 6 → e (eliminate). In each pair, the left belongs to speech and informal writing; the right to formal, written English.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Literal, idiomatic, or the right verb for the context

Objective: Decide the meaning, or fill the gap with a phrasal verb from the bank.

  1. Mark each take off as literal or idiomatic: (a) "The rocket took off." (b) "Her podcast took off overnight." (c) "Take off your muddy boots."
  2. Fill the gap: "I came a fascinating old letter while clearing the attic." (= encountered by chance)
  3. Fill the gap: "He grew up bilingual and later a third language just by living abroad." (= acquired informally)
  4. Choose the more formal option for a written report: "Prices by 4% last quarter." (went up / rose)
✅ Answer

1. (a) literal — leave the ground; (b) idiomatic — became successful; (c) idiomatic-ish — remove (the "off" sense of removal, not flight).
2. came across a fascinating old letter.
3. later picked up a third language.
4. rose — the single-word verb suits the formal register of a written report (went up would be fine in speech).

🎯 Your Project: Narrate, Then Formalise

Tell a story or describe a process twice — once naturally, once formally — and watch your register control in action.

  1. (4 min) Choose a narrative with movement and change: launching a project, a memorable trip, a career move, or how you took up a skill. Jot down 12–15 phrasal verbs you could use (raid the phrase bank).
  2. (6 min) Version A — spoken/informal. Tell the story aloud (to a partner or into a recorder), deliberately rich in phrasal verbs: set off, take on, run into, put up with, sort out, take off…. Keep it natural and warm.
  3. (6 min) Version B — formal/written. Rewrite the same events as a formal account (a report, a biography entry). Swap in single-word synonyms where register calls for it — but consciously keep any phrasal verb that is genuinely the more natural choice, and note why.
  4. (3 min) Compare. For three of your swaps, articulate the register reason. Check every pronoun object sits in the right place.

Solo option: record both versions and listen back, marking each phrasal verb and its formal counterpart. Partner/group option: tell Version A to a partner, then have them flag two phrasal verbs they'd formalise for a written report — and two they'd deliberately leave alone.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Store each verb with a sample object so its type and typical collocation come bundled in — put up with the noise, not a bare "put up with."
  • Let context disambiguate polysemous verbs; don't panic when make out or pick up appears — the surrounding words point to the sense.
  • Choose register on purpose: phrasal for warmth and speech, single-word for formal writing — and be able to say why you chose it.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Pronoun after the particle (separable): "switch on it" ❌ → "switch it on" ✅.
  • Splitting an inseparable verb: "look the children after" ❌ → "look after the children" ✅.
  • Over-formalising speech: "I shall relinquish caffeine" for casual chat sounds absurd — "I'm giving up caffeine" ✅.
  • Confusing verb and noun forms: "a break down" (noun) ❌ → "a breakdown" ✅; the noun fuses and shifts its stress forward.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Four types: intransitive (no object), transitive separable, transitive inseparable, and three-part (inseparable) — the type tells you where the object goes.
  • The pronoun rule: with a separable verb a pronoun object must split (turn it off); with an inseparable verb it stays after the particle (look after them).
  • Meaning: phrasal verbs run from literal to idiomatic and are often polysemous — learn them in context and collocation, not as isolated lists.
  • Register: phrasal verbs lean informal/spoken; their single-word (often Latinate) synonyms lean formal/written — the C1 skill is choosing by register, not defaulting to either.
  • Nominalisation: many phrasal verbs yield nouns (breakdown, outbreak, takeover, outcome, input) with fused spelling and forward stress.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've turned the "impossible" corner of English into a system: four predictable grammar patterns, one high-value pronoun rule, a strategy for polysemy, and — most valuable of all — the register judgement to choose the phrasal verb or its single-word synonym on purpose. That last skill is quietly what advanced writing and speaking are made of. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know whether a verb is separable or inseparable?

There's no perfect rule, but two habits help. First, learn each verb with a sample object so the pattern is stored, not guessed. Second, test with a pronoun: if turn it off sounds right, it's separable; if only look after it works, it's inseparable. Three-part verbs (put up with) are always inseparable. A good dictionary marks the pattern explicitly — worth checking when in doubt.

Are phrasal verbs really "informal"? I see them in serious writing too.

"Informal" is a tendency, not a law. Many phrasal verbs (carry out, point out, set up, bring about) appear happily in formal and academic prose, and sometimes the phrasal verb is simply the most natural, precise option. The reliable point is the pairs: where a phrasal verb has a Latinate single-word synonym (put off / postpone), the synonym is usually the more formal of the two. Judge each case rather than applying a blanket rule.

How can I possibly remember so many meanings for one verb?

You don't memorise a definition list — you meet each sense inside a phrase and let collocation do the work. Take off as "succeed" arrives bundled with sales/the business took off; as "remove" with take off your coat. Over enough encounters, context selects the sense automatically, exactly as it does in your first language.
