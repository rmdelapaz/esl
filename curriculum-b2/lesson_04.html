🔧 Lesson 2.1: Gerunds & Infinitives

Some verbs are followed by -ing, some by to do, and some by either — and choosing the wrong one is one of the most persistent B2 slips there is. The good news: there's real logic underneath the lists. In this lesson you'll learn the patterns that cover most cases, and then meet the small, high-value group of verbs where the choice actually changes the meaning.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the gerund and the infinitive after common verbs with confidence
  • Use the gerund after prepositions and as the subject of a sentence
  • Use the infinitive of purpose to explain why ("I went out to buy milk")
  • Handle verbs that take both forms with a change of meaning — remember, stop, try, forget

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Talk (or record) for two minutes about your habits, goals, and likes/dislikes, deliberately using a range of gerunds and infinitives.

Two Ways to Follow a Verb

When one verb follows another, the second verb has to take a shape. English gives it two main options:

  • the gerund — the -ing form used as a noun: cooking, learning, waiting
  • the infinitive — to + base verb: to cook, to learn, to wait

The tricky part is that the choice usually depends on the first verb, not on any rule you can hear. Enjoy demands -ing; want demands to. Compare:

✅ I enjoy cooking.    ❌ I enjoy to cook.

✅ I want to cook.    ❌ I want cooking.

So part of this is memory — but only part. Prepositions, subjects, and purpose all follow clear, learnable rules, and even the "both forms" verbs have a logic you can feel once you see it. Here's the map of the whole territory:

    A --> C["to do (infinitive)"]

🧠 Mindset

There is logic here and there are lists. Learn the patterns deliberately — prepositions take -ing, purpose takes to, the meaning-change verbs have their own quiet reasoning. Then absorb the rest the way natives did: by reading and hearing the combinations again and again until "enjoy to cook" simply sounds wrong to your ear. You don't have to memorise every verb today. You have to notice them for the rest of your life — and your brain will do the filing. 🌱

The Gerund (-ing)

The gerund is a verb doing a noun's job. There are three main places it shows up.

1. After certain verbs

A cluster of common verbs — many about starting, stopping, continuing, avoiding, or feeling about an activity — takes the gerund:

  • enjoy — I enjoy reading.
  • avoid — She avoids driving at night.
  • finish — Have you finished eating?
  • mind — I don't mind waiting.
  • suggest — He suggested taking a break.
  • imagine — Imagine living there.
  • consider — We're considering moving.
  • keep — Keep trying.
  • practise — I practise speaking daily.
  • miss — I miss seeing them.

💡 A memory hook

Many gerund verbs describe your relationship to an activity as a whole — enjoying it, avoiding it, finishing it, missing it. The activity is a "thing," so it takes the noun-like -ing form. It's not a perfect rule, but it's a useful feel.

2. After a preposition

This one is a hard rule: any verb straight after a preposition takes -ing — always. Prepositions include at, in, on, of, about, for, after, before, without, instead of, good at, interested in, tired of, keen on…

Preposition phrase+ gerund
good atShe's good at cooking.
interested inI'm interested in learning Japanese.
instead ofHe left instead of waiting.
before / afterWash your hands before eating.
withoutShe passed without studying.
tired ofI'm tired of arguing.
⚠️ Watch the little word to: sometimes to is a preposition, not part of an infinitive — and then it takes -ing! "I look forward to hearing from you," "I'm used to getting up early," "She's committed to helping." If you can put a noun after it ("look forward to the weekend"), the to is a preposition → use -ing.

3. As the subject of a sentence

When an activity is the subject — the thing the sentence is about — use the gerund, not the infinitive:

✅ Smoking is bad for you.    (not "To smoke is bad…")

✅ Learning a language takes time.

✅ Getting up early is hard in winter.

The Infinitive (to do)

The infinitive — to + base verb — also has three main homes.

1. After certain verbs

Many verbs about wanting, planning, deciding, hoping, and trying/agreeing to do something take the infinitive:

  • want — I want to go.
  • decide — We decided to stay.
  • hope — I hope to see you.
  • promise — He promised to call.
  • agree — They agreed to help.
  • offer — She offered to drive.
  • refuse — I refuse to lie.
  • manage — We managed to finish.
  • learn — I learned to swim.
  • seem — You seem to know.
  • afford — We can't afford to wait.

💡 A memory hook

Many infinitive verbs point forward to an intention, goal, or future action — you want, hope, plan, decide, promise to do something that hasn't happened yet. The to feels like an arrow toward the future.

2. After adjectives

When an adjective is followed by a verb, that verb usually takes the infinitive:

Adjective+ infinitive
happyI'm happy to help.
difficultIt's difficult to explain.
easyShe's easy to talk to.
readyWe're ready to leave.
surprisedI was surprised to hear it.

3. The infinitive of purpose

To say why you do something — the reason or goal — use the infinitive. It answers "What for?" and means the same as in order to / so as to:

I went out to buy milk.  (= in order to buy milk)

She's saving to travel next year.

I'm calling to confirm the appointment.

⚠️ A very common mistake

Learners often use for + -ing to express purpose: "I went out for buying milk." ❌ To explain the purpose of an action, use the infinitive: "I went out to buy milk." ✅  (For + -ing describes the general function of a thing: "This knife is for cutting bread." — that's fine.)

Verb + Object + Infinitive

A large group of verbs about influencing people — wanting, asking, telling, advising, allowing — follows the pattern verb + someone + to do:

Verb+ object + infinitive
wantI want you to come.
askShe asked me to wait.
tellHe told them to stop.
adviseI'd advise you to rest.
allowThey allow us to leave early.
encourageShe encouraged him to apply.

💡 The bare infinitive (no to)

Two situations drop the to and use the base verb alone:

  • after modal verbs (can, will, must, should, might…): "I can swim," "You should go" — never "can to swim."
  • after let and make (+ object): "Let me go," "They made him wait." Compare the to-pattern of allow: "They allowed him to wait."

Same Verb, Different Meaning

This is the heart of the lesson. A small group of verbs can take either form — but the two forms mean different things. Choose wrong and you say something you don't mean. The pattern to feel: the gerund often looks back at a real action/experience, while the infinitive often looks forward to a purpose, duty, or attempt.

⚠️ The key B2 table — memorise these

Verb+ gerund (-ing)+ infinitive (to do)
remember recall a past memory
I remember locking the door. (I did it, and I have the memory)		 not forget a duty
Remember to lock the door. (do it — don't forget!)
forget fail to recall a past action
I'll never forget meeting her.		 fail to do a duty
Don't forget to call me.
stop quit the activity
He stopped smoking. (he no longer smokes)		 pause in order to do something
He stopped to smoke. (he paused so he could smoke)
try experiment / do as a test
Try adding salt. (see if it helps)		 attempt / make an effort
I tried to open it. (it was hard / I failed)

Three more that work the same way, briefly:

Verb+ gerund+ infinitive
go on continue the same activity
She went on talking.		 move to a new activity next
She went on to talk about money.
regret be sorry about the past
I regret saying that.		 be sorry about bad news you're giving now
I regret to tell you…
mean involve / result in
This job means travelling a lot.		 intend
I meant to call you.

✅ The feel to hold onto

-ing = the real thing / the memory / the activity itself.  to = the purpose / the duty / the attempt. If you're recalling something that already happened, lean to -ing. If you're pointing at a goal or a job to do, lean to to.

💡 Verbs where the choice barely matters

With like, love, hate, prefer, begin, start, and continue, both forms are correct with little or no change in meaning: "I like swimming" ≈ "I like to swim." A light nuance: -ing often leans toward general enjoyment of the activity, while to can lean toward a choice or habit ("I like to check my email first" = it's my routine). Don't overthink these — either is safe.

A: So, are you still going to the gym?

B: Actually, I've stopped going — I got bored. But I've started running in the mornings instead.

A: Nice. I keep meaning to exercise, but I always put off starting.

B: Try going with a friend — it helped me. I remember hating it at first, but now I look forward to running.

A: Maybe. My goal is to feel fitter by summer. I just need to remember to set my alarm!

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. Watch which verbs take the -ing form, which take to, and how the meaning can shift.

  • I really enjoy cooking, but I always forget to clean up afterwards.
  • She promised to help me move, so I stopped worrying about it.
  • We can't afford to waste time arguing about small details.
  • He remembered locking the door, yet he still went back to check.
  • I'm considering taking a course, though I'd prefer to start next year.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: -ing or to?

Objective: Put the verb in brackets into the correct form (gerund or infinitive).

  1. I really enjoy (cook) at the weekend.
  2. She decided (study) medicine.
  3. He's very good at (solve) problems.
  4. We can't afford (buy) a new car.
  5. I'm looking forward to (see) you next week.
  6. They made him (apologise).
  7. Would you mind (close) the window?
✅ Answer

1. cooking · 2. to study · 3. solving (preposition at) · 4. to buy · 5. seeing (to here is a preposition) · 6. apologise (bare infinitive after make) · 7. closing

🏋️ Exercise 2: Which meaning?

Objective: Choose the correct form (-ing or to) to fit the meaning in brackets.

  1. On the drive, we stopped (have) lunch. (we paused in order to eat)
  2. Remember (send) the email before you leave. (don't forget this duty)
  3. I distinctly remember (post) that letter. (I have the memory of doing it)
  4. My knee hurts, so I've stopped (run). (I quit the activity)
  5. The soup's bland — try (add) some pepper. (experiment)
  6. I tried (fix) it myself, but I couldn't. (I attempted / made an effort)
✅ Answer

1. to have · 2. to send · 3. posting · 4. running · 5. adding · 6. to fix

🏋️ Exercise 3: Build the sentence

Objective: Complete each sentence with a preposition + gerund or an infinitive of purpose, as marked.

  1. Instead of , why don't you ask for help? (preposition + gerund: give up)
  2. I'm going to the library a book for my essay. (purpose: borrow)
  3. She's interested for a charity. (preposition + gerund: volunteer)
  4. He turned on the light better. (purpose: see)
✅ Answer

1. Instead of giving up · 2. to borrow a book · 3. interested in volunteering · 4. to see better.  (Sample sentences; wording may vary but the form must match.)

🎯 Your Project: Two Minutes About You

Speak out loud — to a partner, in a group, or into your phone's recorder. Your goal is a natural two-minute stretch that shows off a range of gerunds and infinitives.

  1. (3 min) Jot down notes under three headings: Habits ("I enjoy…", "I've stopped…", "I keep…"), Goals ("I want to…", "I'm hoping to…", "I'm saving to…"), Likes/Dislikes ("I love…", "I can't stand…", "I'm good at…", "I'm tired of…").
  2. (2 min) Add at least one infinitive of purpose ("I go running to clear my head") and one meaning-change verb used correctly ("I remember starting…" / "I must remember to…").
  3. (2 min) Speak for two minutes without long pauses. Don't script it word for word — talk from the notes.
  4. (2 min) If you recorded it, listen back and check the forms against the checklist below. Re-record once if you like.
  5. Partner/group option: After you speak, your listener asks two follow-up questions ("Why did you stop…?", "What are you hoping to…?") — answer using the correct patterns on the spot.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used at least three infinitive verbs (want, hope, decide, learn…)
  • ☐ I used a preposition + gerund ("good at…", "interested in…")
  • ☐ I used an infinitive of purpose ("…to clear my head")
  • ☐ I used one meaning-change verb correctly (remember / stop / try)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • After any preposition — including to when it's a preposition — reach for -ing, every time.
  • Use the infinitive of purpose ("to buy", not "for buying") to say why.
  • For remember / forget / stop / try, pause and ask: is this a memory/activity (-ing) or a duty/purpose/attempt (to)?
  • Read and listen widely — the verb + verb combinations sink in through exposure, not just lists.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "enjoy to do" ❌ → "enjoy doing" ✅. Enjoy, avoid, finish, mind, suggest all take -ing.
  • "for + -ing" for purpose ❌ ("I came for asking…") → infinitive ✅ ("I came to ask…").
  • "look forward to see" ❌ → "look forward to seeing" ✅ — here to is a preposition.
  • "To smoke is bad" sounds stiff → use the gerund as subject: "Smoking is bad."
  • Mixing up stop: "He stopped to smoke" (paused to have one) ≠ "He stopped smoking" (quit). Say what you mean.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The gerund (-ing) follows certain verbs (enjoy, avoid, finish…), all prepositions, and works as a subject ("Smoking is bad").
  • The infinitive (to do) follows certain verbs (want, decide, hope…), adjectives, and expresses purpose ("I went out to buy milk").
  • Verb + object + infinitive covers influencing people (ask/tell/advise someone to do); modals and let/make take the bare infinitive.
  • With remember, forget, stop, try (and go on, regret, mean), the form changes the meaning: -ing = memory/activity, to = duty/purpose/attempt.
  • With like, love, hate, begin, start, either form is fine — little or no change.

You've turned one of the messiest corners of English grammar into a set of patterns you can actually use. You know the reliable rules (prepositions, purpose, subjects), you know the useful hooks (forward-looking to, activity-as-a-thing -ing), and — most valuable of all — you can now say exactly what you mean with remember, stop, and try. ❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is there a rule that tells me every verb, or do I just memorise them?

A bit of both. The positions are rule-based (after prepositions → -ing; for purpose → to; as subject → -ing). But which individual verbs take -ing vs to is largely learned through exposure. The good news is that reading and listening do most of the filing for you — you don't have to sit and memorise a list to make progress.

How do I know if "to" is an infinitive or a preposition?

Try putting a noun after it. If a noun fits ("look forward to the trip", "used to the cold"), to is a preposition → use -ing ("look forward to going"). If a noun doesn't fit ("want to…", "decide to…"), it's part of an infinitive → use the base verb.

Does "I like swimming" really mean the same as "I like to swim"?

Almost. Both are correct. -ing leans toward general enjoyment of the activity; to can hint at a choice or habit ("I like to arrive early" = it's what I choose to do). The difference is tiny — with like, love, hate, begin, start you can safely use either.
