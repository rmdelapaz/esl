🧩 Lesson 6.1: Word Formation — Prefixes, Suffixes & Word Families

Here is one of the fastest ways to grow your English at B2: instead of learning words one at a time, learn how they are built. Once you know that decide gives you decision, decisive, and decisively, a single word quietly becomes four. In this lesson you'll see the machinery — the word families, the suffixes that signal a word's job, and the prefixes that flip or shade its meaning — and, crucially, how to pick the exact form a sentence needs.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognise and use word families — the noun, verb, adjective, and adverb built on one base
  • Identify what a suffix tells you about a word's part of speech (-tion, -ful, -ly, -ise…)
  • Use common prefixes to make negatives and shift meaning (un-, im-, re-, over-, mis-…)
  • Choose the correct form of a base word to fit a gap — a core B2 exam and writing skill

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Build and present a "word family map" for six useful base words, then use each family correctly in a short spoken or written text.

Why Word Formation Is a Multiplier

Most English words are made of parts: a base (or root) that carries the core meaning, plus a prefix at the front and/or a suffix at the end that adjust it. Learn to see those parts and you stop meeting words as strangers.

un + success + ful + ly = unsuccessfully

base = success · prefix un- = "not" · suffix -ful = adjective · suffix -ly = adverb

That single insight does two jobs at once. When you read, you can decode an unfamiliar word by stripping it back to a base you know. When you write or speak, you can reshape a word you already have into exactly the form the sentence needs — turning the plan was a success into a successful plan or we planned it successfully.

🧠 Mindset

You already know thousands of words. Word formation quietly multiplies them without asking you to memorise long lists. Start noticing endings when you read — -tion, -ment, -able, -ise — and asking "what's the base here, and what's this ending doing?" That habit, kept up over months, is worth more than any single study session. You're not learning new words so much as unlocking the ones you have. 🌱

Word Families

A word family is the set of words built on one base — usually a noun, a verb, an adjective, and an adverb. Knowing the whole family lets you keep the same idea while changing its grammatical job.

Base ideaNounVerbAdjectiveAdverb
decidedecisiondecidedecisivedecisively
successsuccesssucceedsuccessfulsuccessfully
createcreation / creativitycreatecreativecreatively
strengthstrengthstrengthenstrongstrongly
competecompetitioncompetecompetitivecompetitively
economyeconomy / economicseconomiseeconomic / economicaleconomically

Notice that the spelling often shifts as you move across the family — success → succeed, strong → strength, decide → decision. The idea stays constant; the surface changes. Some families even have two nouns with different meanings: economy (the system) versus economics (the subject), or a competition (an event) versus competitiveness (a quality).

💡 Watch the stress

As words change form, the stress often moves — which is why they can sound so different aloud even from the same base: ˈphotograph → phoˈtography → photoˈgraphic; ˈeconomy → ecoˈnomic. Say the whole family out loud when you learn it, so your ear stores the rhythm, not just the spelling.

Suffixes: What Job Is the Word Doing?

A suffix is an ending, and its main job is to tell you the word's part of speech — whether it's a noun, adjective, adverb, or verb. Once you recognise the common endings, you can often tell what a word does in a sentence before you even know what it means.

Suffixes that build nouns

SuffixOften meansExample
-tion / -sionan action or resultdecide → decision, educate → education
-mentan action or resultdevelop → development, agree → agreement
-nessa quality or statehappy → happiness, kind → kindness
-itya quality or stateable → ability, creative → creativity
-er / -ora person or thing that does itteach → teacher, act → actor

Suffixes that build adjectives

SuffixOften meansExample
-fulfull ofcare → careful, hope → hopeful
-lesswithoutcare → careless, hope → hopeless
-able / -ibleable to berely → reliable, sense → sensible
-ivehaving the quality ofcreate → creative, attract → attractive
-ousfull of / havingdanger → dangerous, fame → famous
-alrelating tonature → natural, tradition → traditional

⚠️ -ful and -less are a pair — but not always

-ful ("full of") and -less ("without") often mirror each other: hopeful / hopeless, careful / careless, useful / useless. But the pairing isn't automatic — there's no "friendless-ful" and no "beautiless." Learn each real word; don't invent the opposite. And note -ful as an adjective ending has only one l (careful), while the adverb adds -ly for two (carefully).

Suffixes that build adverbs and verbs

  • -ly → makes an adverb from an adjective: quick → quickly, careful → carefully, easy → easily.
  • -ise / -ize → makes a verb meaning "make/become": modern → modernise, real → realise. (Both spellings are correct; -ise is common in the UK, -ize in the US.)
  • -en → makes a verb meaning "make more": wide → widen, strong → strengthen, short → shorten.
  • -ify → makes a verb meaning "make into": simple → simplify, pure → purify, class → classify.

Prefixes: Flipping & Shading Meaning

A prefix goes at the front and changes the meaning — but, unlike a suffix, it usually leaves the part of speech alone. Happy and unhappy are both adjectives; read and reread are both verbs.

Negative prefixes: "not" / "opposite of"

PrefixUsed beforeExample
un-many wordshappy → unhappy, fair → unfair
in-many wordscorrect → incorrect, visible → invisible
im-before m / ppossible → impossible, patient → impatient
il-before llegal → illegal, logical → illogical
ir-before rregular → irregular, responsible → irresponsible
dis-verbs & adjectivesagree → disagree, honest → dishonest
non-neutral "not"fiction → non-fiction, smoker → non-smoker

💡 Why im-, il-, ir- exist

They're all really in- in disguise. The n changes to match the sound that follows so the word is easier to say: in- + possible would be awkward, so it becomes impossible; in- + legal becomes illegal; in- + regular becomes irregular. The rule of thumb: im- before m/p, il- before l, ir- before r, and in- elsewhere.

Other useful prefixes

PrefixMeaningExample
re-again / backwrite → rewrite, build → rebuild
over-too muchcook → overcook, work → overwork
under-too little / belowcook → undercook, paid → underpaid
mis-wrongly / badlyunderstand → misunderstand, spell → misspell
pre-beforeview → preview, historic → prehistoric

⚠️ A prefix isn't always negative

Be careful: in- does not always mean "not." In income, input, and invaluable it means something else entirely — invaluable actually means extremely valuable, not "worthless"! And flammable and inflammable mean the same thing (both "catches fire easily"). When a prefix produces a surprising meaning, treat the word as one to learn individually rather than trusting the pattern.

Choosing the Right Form

This is where word formation earns its keep — and it's exactly what B2 exams test. You're given a base word and a gap, and you have to supply the form the grammar of the sentence demands. The trick is to ask: what job does this slot need?

If the slot needs a…Look for a clue like…Example (base: SUCCESS)
nounafter a / the / this, or as subject/objectIt was a huge success.
verbafter a subject, needing a tenseThey succeeded in the end.
adjectivebefore a noun, or after be/seemIt was a successful launch.
adverbdescribing a verb / adjectiveThey finished it successfully.
negativemeaning tells you "not"The first attempt was unsuccessful.

So the process is two steps: (1) decide the part of speech the gap needs, then (2) add — or remove — the suffix and prefix to build it. Watch the meaning too: a gap can be positive or negative, singular or plural.

"Despite careful preparation, the first launch was a complete failure (FAIL). The team felt disappointed (DISAPPOINT), but they responded decisively (DECIDE): they made several key improvements (IMPROVE), and the second attempt was a genuine success (SUCCEED)."

Same passage, five different word forms — noun, adjective, adverb, plural noun, noun — all built from bases.

Tutor: Your essay is strong, but look at this line: "The government should modern the railways."

Student: Oh — that slot needs a verb, doesn't it? So… modernise the railways.

Tutor: Exactly. And here you wrote "a decide leader." What job does that slot need?

Student: It's before a noun, so an adjective — a decisive leader. And if I meant the opposite, indecisive.

Tutor: Perfect. You didn't learn new words — you just reshaped ones you already had.

Keep a few of these handy "reshaping" phrases in your ear when you're editing your own writing:

It needs a noun here. That slot wants an adjective. Add -ly for the adverb. The meaning is negative — add un-/in-. What's the base word? Does the stress move?

🏋️ Exercise 1: Form the correct word

Objective: Change the word in CAPITALS into the form that fits the gap.

  1. The new bridge was a huge (SUCCESS).
  2. She is one of the most (CREATE) people I know.
  3. He explained the plan very (CLEAR).
  4. They need to (STRONG) the team before the final.
  5. Unfortunately, the first attempt was a complete (FAIL).
  6. The instructions were full of (INFORM).
✅ Answer

1. success (noun) · 2. creative (adjective) · 3. clearly (adverb) · 4. strengthen (verb) · 5. failure (noun) · 6. information (noun)

  3. I completely agree with that decision.
  4. His handwriting is almost readable.
  5. That behaviour is totally responsible.
  6. The answer he gave was clearly correct.

🎯 Your Project: A Word Family Map

Pick six useful base words and map each one into a full family — then put the families to work. This turns passive recognition into active, usable vocabulary.

  1. (4 min) Choose six bases relevant to you (e.g. decide, create, succeed, compete, communicate, responsible). For each, write the noun, verb, adjective, and adverb — and at least one negative form (e.g. irresponsible).
  2. (3 min) Check a dictionary for any you're unsure of, and mark where the stress falls if it moves.
  3. (4 min) Write or say a short paragraph (6–8 sentences) that uses at least four different family members from your maps, in the correct form.
  4. (2 min) Read it aloud, or present it to a partner, paying attention to stress. Solo? Record it and check the forms.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I mapped six base words into full families
  • ☐ Each map has a noun, verb, adjective, and adverb
  • ☐ I included at least one negative-prefix form
  • ☐ My paragraph used four or more family members correctly
  • ☐ I noticed where the stress moved when I said them aloud

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • When you meet a new word, find the base and note the whole family — you learn four words for the price of one.
  • In a gap-fill, first decide the part of speech the slot needs, then build the form.
  • Let the suffix guide you: -tion/-ment/-ness = noun, -ful/-ive/-al = adjective, -ly = adverb, -ise/-ify/-en = verb.
  • Say new families out loud so you catch shifting stress (ˈphotograph → phoˈtography).

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wrong part of speech: "a success project" ❌ → "a successful project" ✅ (needs an adjective before the noun).
  • Guessing the negative prefix: "unpossible" ❌ → "impossible" ✅. Match the prefix to the following sound.
  • Assuming every prefix is negative: invaluable means very valuable, not worthless.
  • Inventing words: not every base takes every ending — check that a form actually exists before you use it.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Most words are built from a base plus a prefix and/or suffix — learn to see the parts.
  • A word family gives you noun, verb, adjective, and adverb from one idea (decide → decision → decisive → decisively).
  • Suffixes signal the part of speech: -tion/-ment/-ness/-ity (nouns), -ful/-less/-able/-ive/-ous/-al (adjectives), -ly (adverbs), -ise/-en/-ify (verbs).
  • Prefixes shift meaning: negatives (un-, in-/im-/il-/ir-, dis-, non-) and others (re-, over-, under-, mis-, pre-).
  • To fill a gap: decide the part of speech first, then build the form — and watch that the stress may move.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've picked up a genuine vocabulary multiplier. Instead of meeting each word as a stranger, you can now decode unfamiliar ones by their parts and reshape familiar ones to fit any sentence. This is one of the quiet skills that separates a B2 writer from a B1 one — and it will keep paying off every time you read, write, or sit an exam. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know which suffix a particular base takes — -tion, -ment, or -ness?

There's no perfect rule — it's largely learned per word. But there are strong tendencies: -ment often attaches to verbs (develop → development), -ness to adjectives (happy → happiness), and -tion/-sion to Latin-based verbs (educate → education). When in doubt, check a dictionary and learn the noun as part of the family, not in isolation.

Is it "-ise" or "-ize"? Am I making a mistake?

Neither is wrong. -ise (realise, organise) is the usual British spelling and -ize (realize, organize) the usual American one — both are widely accepted. Just be consistent within a single piece of writing, and follow whichever variety your course or exam expects.

Can I trust prefixes to always change the meaning the same way?

Mostly, but not always. Negative prefixes usually mean "not," yet invaluable means "extremely valuable" and inflammable means the same as flammable. When a formed word surprises you, treat it as an individual word to learn rather than trusting the pattern blindly.
