📊 Lesson 7.3: Making a Case — Presentations & Persuasion

Whether it's a work presentation, a class talk, or making the case for your idea in a meeting, being able to present persuasively is a high-value skill — and it's far more learnable than it looks. Good presenters aren't just confident; they use a clear set of moves: they signpost where they're going, present arguments with evidence, deploy persuasive techniques, concede fairly and counter cleanly, and handle questions with grace. In this lesson you'll learn each move and put them together into a short persuasive talk of your own.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Signpost a talk so your audience always knows where they are
  • Present an argument with evidence and reasons
  • Use persuasive techniques — rhetorical questions, tripling, contrast, and appeals
  • Concede and counter objections, and handle questions confidently

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Deliver a 1–2 minute persuasive mini-presentation on a topic you choose, using signposting, an argument, at least one persuasive technique, and a handled question.

What Makes a Talk Persuasive

You reviewed some of this language back in Lesson 4.3, where you applied a few presentation phrases. Now we're going to teach the skill properly — because persuasion isn't about big words or a loud voice. It's about structure and signals: a listener who always knows where they are, and who is walked clearly from a claim to the reasons that support it.

A persuasive mini-presentation has a simple shape:

graph LR
    A["Signpost
tell them the plan"] --> B["Argue
claim + reasons + evidence"]
    B --> C["Persuade
techniques + concede/counter"]
    C --> D["Close
sum up + call to action"]

Notice the difference the moves make:

Weak: "Um, I think remote work is good because it's, like, more flexible and stuff."

Strong: "Today I'll make the case for remote work. There are two main reasons it benefits companies. First, flexibility. Research suggests flexible workers are more productive — not less. Admittedly, it isn't right for every role; that said, for most office work, the evidence is clear."

🧠 Mindset

Many learners believe you have to be a naturally confident, charismatic person to present well. Not true — confidence in presenting comes from preparation and structure, not personality. When you know your signposts and you know your argument, your nerves have far less to grip onto. The audience isn't judging your accent; they're following your ideas. Give them a clear path to follow, and you'll sound persuasive even on a day you feel shaky. 🌱

Structuring & Signposting

Signposting means telling your audience where you are and where you're going. It's the single biggest thing that separates a talk that's easy to follow from one that loses people. There's a signpost for each stage.

StageSignpost phrase
Introducing the topicToday I'll cover… / In this talk, I want to show…
Starting the first pointFirst, let's look at… / To begin with…
Moving onMoving on to… / That brings me to my next point…
Adding a pointWhat's more… / On top of that…
Giving an exampleFor instance… / Take, for example…
ConcludingTo sum up… / In conclusion…

💡 Tell them three times

The classic presenter's rule: tell them what you'll say, say it, then tell them what you said. Open with a roadmap ("Today I'll cover two points"), signpost as you move through each one, and close by summing up. Repetition of structure isn't boring — it's what lets a listener follow a spoken argument they can't re-read.

A quick word on pace and pausing, because they belong to signposting too. When you reach a signpost — "Moving on to my second point…" — pause for a beat before you carry on. That silence tells the audience a new idea is starting, and it gives your own brain a moment to line up the next sentence. Learners often rush through transitions because a pause feels awkward; in fact, a confident pause is one of the clearest signals that you know exactly where you are.

Presenting an Argument

An argument is a claim plus the reasons and evidence that support it. Signalling that structure aloud makes even a simple point sound authoritative.

FunctionLanguage
State the main benefitThe main advantage is…
Give evidenceResearch suggests… / Studies show… / The figures indicate…
Give reasonsThere are two reasons for this. First,… Second,…
Draw a conclusionThis means that… / As a result,…

"The main advantage of cycling to work is your health. There are two reasons for this. First, it builds regular exercise into your day. Second, it cuts stress. Research suggests daily cyclists take fewer sick days. As a result, both the worker and the employer benefit."

⚠️ A claim without support is just an opinion

"Remote work is better" is an opinion; "Remote work is better because studies show a 13% productivity gain" is an argument. At B2, the persuasive step is always to attach a reason or a piece of evidence to your claim — "because…", "since…", "research suggests…". Don't leave a claim floating on its own.

Evidence doesn't have to be a formal statistic, either. A vivid example often persuades better than a number, because people remember stories more easily than figures: "Take my colleague Ana — since she started cycling in, she hasn't had a single sick day all year." Signal it clearly ("Take, for example…", "To give you a concrete case…") so the audience knows the example is doing the work of proof. The strongest arguments usually combine both: a figure that shows the scale, and an example that makes it real.

Persuasive Techniques

Beyond a clean argument, four classic techniques add force. Speakers have used these for centuries — and once you notice them, you'll hear them in every good speech.

1. Rhetorical questions

A question you don't expect answered — it makes the audience think and pulls them in: "Wouldn't we all like more time with our families? Isn't that worth considering?"

2. Tripling (the rule of three)

Three items in a row are memorable and rhythmic: "It's cheaper, faster, and greener." "This idea will save us time, save us money, and save us stress."

3. Contrast

Set two things against each other to sharpen your point: "We can carry on as we are — or we can do something better." "It's not a cost; it's an investment."

4. Appealing to the listener

Speak directly to your audience with you and we to make it personal: "Think about how much time you waste commuting. Together, we can change that."

Wouldn't it be better if…? It's cheaper, faster, and greener. It's not a cost; it's an investment. Think about how this affects you. Together, we can… Isn't that worth it?

Conceding & Countering

A persuasive speaker doesn't pretend there are no objections — that sounds naive. Instead they concede the other side has a point, then counter it. This actually makes you more convincing, because it shows you've thought it through.

Concede (acknowledge)Counter (push back)
Admittedly, it costs more at first……however, it pays for itself within a year.
That said, some people prefer the office……but most surveys show the opposite.
While it's true that there are risks……the benefits clearly outweigh them.
Of course, no plan is perfect……nevertheless, this is the best option available.
The concede–counter move in one breath: "Admittedly, the first month would be disruptive. That said, the long-term savings make it more than worth it." Acknowledging the objection first disarms it — your listener feels heard, and then hears your stronger point.

Presenter: To sum up, then, switching to a four-day week would boost both wellbeing and productivity. Wouldn't we all do better work if we were less exhausted? Thank you — I'm happy to take questions.

Audience member: But surely a four-day week just means less gets done overall?

Presenter: That's a great question. Admittedly, it sounds that way at first. That said, the trials we looked at found output stayed the same or rose, because people focused better in fewer hours. Let me come back to the exact figures at the end, but the short answer is: less time, not less work.

Audience member: Interesting — I hadn't thought of it like that.

Handling Questions

The question-and-answer moment worries learners most, because you can't script it. But a few reliable phrases buy you time and keep you composed — you rarely need more than these.

That's a good question. Thank you for raising that. Let me come back to that in a moment. If I understand you correctly, you're asking… I'm not sure, but my sense is… Does that answer your question?

💡 It's fine not to know

You don't have to have every answer. "That's a great question — I don't have the exact figure to hand, but I'll follow up" is a completely professional response, and far better than bluffing. Buying a second with "That's a good question" or "Let me come back to that" gives your brain time to organise a reply. Composure under questions impresses an audience as much as the talk itself.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Order the signposting stages

Objective: These signpost phrases are out of order. Type the number (1–5) showing where each belongs in a well-structured talk, from opening to close.

  1. "To sum up, the evidence is clear." → stage
  2. "Today I'll cover two main points." → stage
  3. "Moving on to my second point…" → stage
  4. "First, let's look at the cost." → stage
  5. "For instance, one company saved thousands." → stage
✅ Answer

1 = introduce ("Today I'll cover…"), 2 = first point ("First, let's look at…"), 3 = transition ("Moving on to…"), 4 = example ("For instance…"), 5 = conclude ("To sum up…"). So the gaps read: 5, 1, 3, 2, 4.

🎯 Your Project: A Persuasive Mini-Presentation

Deliver a 1–2 minute persuasive talk on a topic you feel something about. Speak it aloud — to a partner or group, or into your phone's recorder.

  1. (3 min) Choose a topic where you want to convince someone: a four-day week, learning a language, cycling to work, a policy at your school or job. Decide your one clear claim.
  2. (5 min) Plan the shape in notes: a signposted opening ("Today I'll cover…"), two reasons with a piece of evidence, at least one persuasive technique (rhetorical question, tripling, or contrast), one concede–counter move, and a closing summary or call to action.
  3. (3 min) Deliver it, standing if you can. Speak slowly and signpost clearly. Don't read a script — talk from the notes.
  4. (2 min) Take one question. Use a handling phrase ("That's a good question…") before you answer. Solo? Invent a likely objection and answer it aloud.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I opened with a signposted roadmap ("Today I'll cover…")
  • ☐ I made a clear claim supported by a reason and evidence
  • ☐ I used at least one persuasive technique
  • ☐ I conceded a point and then countered it
  • ☐ I closed with a summary or a call to action
  • ☐ I handled a question using a handling phrase

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Signpost constantly — "First…", "Moving on to…", "To sum up…" — so your audience never gets lost.
  • Attach a reason or evidence to every claim: "because…", "research suggests…".
  • Concede before you counter — acknowledging the other side makes you more, not less, convincing.
  • Buy time on questions with "That's a good question" — and it's fine to say you'll follow up.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • No structure: jumping between points with no signposts leaves listeners unable to follow. Give a roadmap and stick to it.
  • Claims with no support: "It's just better" convinces no one. Add a reason or evidence every time.
  • Ignoring objections: pretending there's no downside sounds naive. Concede and counter instead.
  • Panicking on questions: you don't need every answer. Acknowledge, buy a second, and answer honestly or offer to follow up.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A persuasive talk has a shape: signpost → argue → persuade → close.
  • Signposting ("Today I'll cover…", "Moving on to…", "To sum up…") keeps the audience oriented.
  • An argument is a claim plus reasons and evidence — "The main advantage is…", "Research suggests…".
  • Persuasive techniques: rhetorical questions, tripling, contrast, and appealing to the listener.
  • Concede and counter ("Admittedly… that said…") and handle questions calmly ("That's a good question. Let me come back to that.").

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've turned "presenting" from a vague fear into a set of concrete moves. You can lay out a roadmap, build an argument on evidence, add rhetorical force, deal fairly with objections, and stay composed under questions. That's the toolkit of everyone from a confident colleague to a keynote speaker — and it works in a meeting, a classroom, or an interview. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

I get so nervous presenting. How do I sound confident?

Confidence comes from structure and preparation, not personality. When you know your signposts and your argument by heart, there's far less for nerves to grip onto. Speak a little slower than feels natural, pause between points, and lean on your signpost phrases — they carry you through the moments your mind goes blank.

Isn't it weaker to admit the other side has a point?

Quite the opposite — conceding and then countering makes you more persuasive. It shows you've considered the objection and aren't hiding it, which builds trust. "Admittedly… that said…" lets your listener feel heard before you deliver your stronger point.

What if I don't understand a question I'm asked?

Ask for clarification — it's completely professional: "If I understand you correctly, you're asking…?" or "Could you say a little more about what you mean?" And if you don't know the answer, say so and offer to follow up. Bluffing is the only real mistake.
