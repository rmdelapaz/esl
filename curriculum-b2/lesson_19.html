🎩 Lesson 7.1: Register — Formal vs Informal English

You wouldn't text your best friend the way you'd write to a bank — and you wouldn't email a bank the way you'd text a friend. That instinct has a name: register. It's the skill of matching your language to the situation, the audience, and the purpose. Get it right and you sound natural and appropriate everywhere; get it wrong and even perfect grammar can feel oddly stiff or too casual. In this lesson you'll learn exactly what shifts between formal and informal English — in speech and in writing — and you'll build a real formal email.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognise the four features that shift with register — word choice, contractions, sentence length, and set phrases
  • Switch between casual and polite spoken English for the same situation
  • Structure a formal email or letter of complaint or enquiry from greeting to sign-off
  • Rewrite an informal message as a formal one (and hear the difference aloud)

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Write and read aloud a short formal email — a complaint or an enquiry — using the correct greeting, structure, and sign-off.

What Register Is

Register is not about "good English" versus "bad English." All of it is good English — the point is that language exists on a scale, from very informal to very formal, and a confident speaker slides up and down that scale to fit the moment. Think of it as a dial, not a switch:

graph LR
    A["Very informal
text a friend"] --> B["Neutral
talk to a colleague"]
    B --> C["Very formal
write to an official"]

The same idea can be dressed three ways:

Informal: "Can you get back to me soon?"

Neutral: "Could you let me know when you have a moment?"

Formal: "I would be grateful if you could reply at your earliest convenience."

Notice that none of them is wrong. The formal one to a friend would sound cold and strange; the informal one to a company you're complaining to would undercut you. Register is about fit.

🧠 Mindset

Learners often assume "more formal = better," and pile on long words to sound advanced. But real fluency is flexibility — knowing when to be relaxed and when to be careful. The most impressive thing you can do at B2 isn't to sound formal all the time; it's to read a situation and choose the right voice for it. That's a social skill as much as a language one, and you already have the instinct in your first language. This lesson just gives it English words. 🌱

The Four Features That Shift

When English moves from informal to formal, four things change in a predictable way. Learn these four and you can consciously "turn the dial" in either direction.

1. Word choice

Formal English tends to prefer single, often Latin-based verbs where informal English uses everyday words and phrasal verbs. The meaning is the same; the flavour is not.

Informal / everydayFormal
ask forrequest
getobtain / receive
buthowever
sotherefore
find outdiscover / establish
put offpostpone
needrequire
sorryI apologise

2. Contractions

Informal English is full of contractions — I'm, don't, we'll, it's, can't. Formal writing avoids them and spells the words out in full.

Informal: "I'm writing because I haven't received my order and it hasn't arrived."

Formal: "I am writing because I have not received my order, which has not yet arrived."

3. Sentence length and complexity

Informal English uses short, punchy sentences and lots of and / but / so. Formal English builds longer, more complex sentences with linking words, and states things more completely.

Informal: "The heater's broken. It's really cold. Can someone come?"

Formal: "As the heating system is no longer working and the apartment has become extremely cold, I would appreciate it if an engineer could visit as soon as possible."

4. Set phrases

Certain fixed openings, closings, and requests instantly signal the register. Swapping these is often the fastest way to shift a whole message.

FunctionInformalFormal
GreetingHi Sam,Dear Mr Clarke, / Dear Sir or Madam,
ReasonJust wondering…I am writing to enquire about…
RequestCan you…?I would be grateful if you could…
ClosingLet me know.I look forward to hearing from you.
Sign-offCheers, / Thanks,Yours sincerely, / Yours faithfully,

💡 Sincerely or faithfully?

A small but respected rule in formal British letters: if you opened with the person's name ("Dear Mr Clarke,"), close with "Yours sincerely," If you opened without a name ("Dear Sir or Madam,"), close with "Yours faithfully," A handy memory hook: never two S's together — Sir goes with faithfully, and a named person goes with sincerely.

Spoken Register: Same Situation, Two Voices

Register isn't only about writing. In speech we shift too — a casual, direct voice with friends, and a more polite, tentative voice with strangers or people in authority. Polite spoken English softens requests with modals (could, would), hedges (I was wondering, would it be possible), and fuller sentences. Here is the very same situation — asking to change a booking — handled two ways.

Informal — Ben: Hey Mia, can we push the table booking to 8 instead of 7?

Mia: Yeah, no worries. I'll just text the place.

Ben: Cheers. And can we grab one near the window?

Mia: Sure thing. Sorted.

Formal — Guest: Good evening. I was wondering whether it might be possible to move our reservation from seven o'clock to eight.

Host: Of course, sir. May I take the name the booking is under?

Guest: It's under Whitfield. And would it be possible to be seated near a window, if there's one available?

Host: Certainly. I'll do my best to arrange that for you. Everything is confirmed.

🔊 Listen & Repeat

These two exchanges say the same thing in two very different voices. Tap the 🔊 buttons (or the speaker names) to hear each line, and use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to slow the speed down. Say each line back, and really feel the difference: the clipped, contracted friendliness of the first versus the fuller, hedged politeness of the second. Notice how the formal version leans on would, might, possible, and complete sentences to sound respectful.

Before you move on, check your understanding of the two exchanges:

Which words make Ben's version sound casual?

The contractions (can we, I'll), the informal openers (Hey, Cheers), the phrasal verb push… to, and short, direct requests with plain can. There's no hedging at all — friends don't need it.

How does the guest soften the same two requests?

With tentative frames: "I was wondering whether it might be possible to…" and "would it be possible to… if there's one available." These distance the request and give the other person an easy way to say no — the essence of polite formality.

Would the formal version sound right between two close friends?

No — it would sound oddly stiff and even sarcastic. That's register in action: the formal voice is correct with a host or stranger, but a poor fit for a casual chat. Matching the voice to the relationship is the whole skill.

Written Register: The Formal Email

The place register matters most for a B2 learner is the formal email or letter — a complaint, an enquiry, a request. These follow a clear five-part structure. Once you know the skeleton, you only change the details.

PartWhat it doesTypical language
1. GreetingOpens respectfullyDear Mr/Ms Lee, / Dear Sir or Madam,
2. Reason for writingStates your purpose at onceI am writing to enquire about… / I am writing to complain about…
3. DetailExplains the situation clearlyOn 3 May, I purchased… / Unfortunately, the item…
4. Request / actionSays what you want doneI would be grateful if you could… / I would therefore request…
5. Sign-offCloses politelyI look forward to hearing from you. Yours faithfully,

Here is the whole thing assembled — a short letter of complaint:

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to complain about a jacket I ordered from your website on 3 May (order number 48213).

Although the item was advertised as waterproof, it leaked badly the first time I wore it in the rain. Unfortunately, the colour also differs considerably from the photograph on your site.

I would therefore be grateful if you could arrange a full refund and provide a prepaid label for the return. I have attached a copy of my receipt for your reference.

I look forward to hearing from you.

Yours faithfully,
Elena Marsh

A useful test: read your formal email and ask, "Could I say this to a stranger in a suit without cringing?" If any line sounds like something you'd only say to a mate — "Anyway, get back to me!" — reach for the formal set phrase instead: "I look forward to your reply."

⚠️ Formal doesn't mean rude, and it doesn't mean angry

A complaint is most effective when it stays calm and polite. Capital letters, exclamation marks, and sarcasm weaken your case. Formal register actually helps you here: phrases like "I was disappointed to find…" and "I would appreciate it if…" let you be firm and reasonable at the same time. Cool politeness is more persuasive than heat.

Your Phrase Bank

Keep these formal/informal pairs where you can reach them. When you need to shift register fast, swapping the set phrase does most of the work.

Openings

Hi… → Dear Mr/Ms… Just wondering… → I am writing to enquire… Sorry to bother you → I hope you don't mind my writing

Making requests

Can you…? → Could you possibly…? I want… → I would like to request… Send me… → I would be grateful if you could send…

Closings

Let me know → I look forward to hearing from you Thanks! → Thank you for your assistance Cheers, → Yours sincerely, Talk soon → Please do not hesitate to contact me

💡 One register per message

The most common register slip isn't being too formal or too casual — it's mixing them. A letter that opens "Dear Sir or Madam" and closes "Cheers!" jars the reader. Pick a register at the greeting and hold it all the way to the sign-off.

  • I am writing to enquire about the availability of your evening courses.
  • I would be grateful if you could send me further details as soon as possible.
  • Unfortunately, the product I received did not match the description on your website.
  • I was wondering whether it might be possible to reschedule our meeting.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Rewrite it formally

Objective: Here is a casual message. Rewrite it as a formal email, using a proper greeting, full forms (no contractions), formal word choice, and a correct sign-off.

Hi! So I got a phone from you guys last week but it won't charge. Pretty annoying tbh. Can you fix it or give me my money back? Get back to me asap. Cheers, Jay
✅ Answer (sample)

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to complain about a mobile phone I purchased from your store last week. Unfortunately, the device will not charge.

I would therefore be grateful if you could either repair the phone or provide a full refund. I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience.

Yours faithfully,
Jay Thompson

Wording may vary, but check: formal greeting, no contractions, "purchased" not "got", a clear request, and a matching sign-off.

🎯 Your Project: A Formal Email, Written & Read Aloud

Write a short formal email — either a complaint or an enquiry — of about 80–120 words, then read it aloud in a calm, professional voice.

  1. (3 min) Choose your situation: complain about a product or service that went wrong, or enquire about a course, job, or booking. Jot down the key facts (dates, names, order numbers).
  2. (5 min) Draft the email using the five-part structure: greeting → reason for writing → detail → request/action → sign-off. Use full forms (no contractions) and at least three formal set phrases.
  3. (2 min) Check the register is consistent all the way through, and that your sign-off matches your greeting (faithfully / sincerely).
  4. (3 min) Read it aloud — into your phone's recorder, or to a partner. Aim for a steady, polite, unhurried tone. With a partner, have them read a suitable reply.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used a formal greeting and a matching sign-off
  • ☐ I stated my reason for writing in the first line
  • ☐ I avoided all contractions
  • ☐ I used at least three formal set phrases
  • ☐ I made a clear, polite request or enquiry
  • ☐ I read the email aloud in a calm, professional voice

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Read the situation first. Ask who your reader is and how well you know them, then choose the register to fit.
  • Spell contractions out in full in formal writing — do not, I am, will not.
  • Lead with your purpose in a formal email: "I am writing to…" in the very first line.
  • Match your sign-off to your greeting — named person → sincerely; no name → faithfully.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Mixing registers: "Dear Sir or Madam … Cheers, Jay" ❌. Hold one register from greeting to sign-off.
  • Contractions in a formal letter: "I haven't received it" ❌ → "I have not received it" ✅.
  • Being too formal with friends: "I would be grateful if you could reply" to a mate sounds cold and odd. Casual is correct there.
  • Angry complaints: capitals and exclamation marks weaken your case. Stay calm — "I was disappointed to find…" is far more effective.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Register is matching your language to the situation, audience, and purpose — a dial from very informal to very formal.
  • Four features shift with register: word choice (request vs ask for), contractions (formal avoids them), sentence length, and set phrases.
  • Spoken formal English is polite and tentative — could, would, I was wondering — while casual speech is short and direct.
  • A formal email has five parts: greeting, reason for writing, detail, request/action, sign-off.
  • Sincerely follows a named greeting; faithfully follows "Dear Sir or Madam" — and never mix registers within one message.

This is the skill that makes your English fit any room you walk into. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is formal English always "better" than informal?

No — they're tools for different jobs. Formal English is right for officials, strangers, and complaints; informal English is right for friends and relaxed situations. Using a formal register with close friends can sound cold or even sarcastic. The skill is fit, not formality for its own sake.

Are phrasal verbs "bad" or "informal"?

Not bad at all — they're the natural heart of everyday English. They just lean informal, so in formal writing you often swap them for a single verb (find out → discover, put off → postpone). In speech and casual writing, use them freely.

What if I don't know the person's name for a formal email?

Open with "Dear Sir or Madam," and close with "Yours faithfully,". If you do know the name, use "Dear Mr/Ms [Surname]," and close with "Yours sincerely,". "To whom it may concern" also exists but sounds a little old-fashioned; "Dear Sir or Madam" is the safe modern choice.
