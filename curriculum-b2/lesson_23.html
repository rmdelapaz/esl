🌫️ Lesson 8.2: Vague Language & Being Approximate

It sounds like a paradox, but fluent speakers are often deliberately imprecise. "There were about twenty people, give or take." "I'll be there around six-ish." "We need milk, bread, and stuff like that." Being exact can sound cold, robotic, or even rude — while a well-placed vague expression sounds relaxed, natural, and considerate. In this lesson you'll learn the toolkit of vague language that makes English sound like a real human speaking, not a machine reading data.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Approximate quantity and number (about, around, roughly, or so, -ish, more or less)
  • Use general nouns and placeholders (stuff, things, that kind of thing, or something, thingy)
  • Soften statements with hedges (sort of, kind of, a bit, in a way)
  • Judge register — knowing that vague language is mostly informal and spoken, and when to be precise instead

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe a place, a routine, or a plan out loud using natural vague language, so it sounds relaxed and conversational rather than over-precise.

Why Vagueness Is Fluent

Many learners aim for maximum precision, assuming that's what "good English" means. But listen to how native speakers actually talk, and you'll hear the opposite: constant, comfortable approximation. There are good reasons for it.

  • Precision can sound cold. "The meeting lasted 47 minutes" is oddly clinical in casual chat; "about three quarters of an hour" is warmer and just as useful.
  • We often don't know the exact figure — and pretending to would be worse than admitting it. "Around fifty, maybe?" is honest.
  • Vagueness signals shared understanding. "…and stuff like that" invites the listener to fill in the rest, treating them as an equal who already gets the idea.
  • It softens and it's polite. "It's a bit expensive" lands more gently than "It's expensive."

Over-precise (robotic): "The journey takes 34 minutes. I bought 6 items. It cost 12.50."

Natural (vague): "The journey takes about half an hour. I picked up a few things. It was around twelve quid or so."

🧠 Mindset

If you've been treating vagueness as a weakness — something to eliminate — flip that idea. Used well, approximation is a mark of fluency and social ease, not sloppiness. You're not lowering your standards; you're gaining a register that precise textbook English can't reach. Learning to be comfortably vague is one of the clearest signs that your English is becoming genuinely natural. 🌱

Approximating Quantity & Number

The most common vagueness is around numbers, amounts, and times. English has a rich set of ways to say "roughly this much" without committing to an exact figure.

ExpressionPositionExample
about / aroundbefore the numberThere were about twenty people.
roughlybefore the numberIt costs roughly fifty euros.
or soafter the numberGive me ten minutes or so.
-ishattached to the number/timeLet's meet at seven-ish.
more or lessbefore/afterIt's more or less finished.
give or takeafter the numberFifty people, give or take.
a couple of / a fewbefore the nounI'll be a couple of minutes.
-oddafter a round numberThere were thirty-odd guests.

💡 The versatile "-ish"

-ish is one of the most useful little suffixes in spoken English. It attaches to times ("six-ish"), numbers ("twenty-ish"), ages ("she's fortyish"), colours ("bluish"), and even adjectives ("I'm tiredish", "it was goodish"). It means "approximately / roughly / sort of." It's very informal, so keep it for speech and casual messages.

You can also stack these for an even more relaxed feel: "There were, I don't know, fifty or so people, give or take." Native speakers do this constantly, and it sounds friendlier than a single hard number.

General Nouns & Placeholders

When you don't need to list everything — or can't remember, or trust the listener to fill in the rest — English uses general nouns and placeholder words. These are the verbal equivalent of "etc."

Vague noun / phraseMeans roughlyExample
stuffthings (uncountable feel)I've got loads of stuff to do.
thingsitems / mattersHow are things with you?
and that kind of thingand similarWe talked about work and that kind of thing.
and so onetc. (a touch more formal)Bring a pen, paper, and so on.
or somethingor a similar optionLet's get pizza or something.
whatsit / thingy / whatchamacallita name you can't recallPass me the thingy — the remote.
...and everythingand all the restShe brought snacks and everything.

⚠️ Placeholders are casual — don't overuse them

Words like thingy, whatsit, and stuff are friendly and very common in relaxed speech, but a sentence stuffed with them ("I put the stuff with the thingy near the whatsit") becomes impossible to follow. Use one where it genuinely helps — where the exact word doesn't matter, or you and your listener both know it — not as a substitute for vocabulary you actually need.

Notice how naturally the vague and the specific work together — one precise anchor, then a comfortable "and the rest":

"I need to grab a few things from the shop — milk, bread, that kind of thing."

"We could watch a film or something, I don't mind."

Softening: sort of, kind of, a bit

The third use of vague language is softening — taking the hard edge off a statement so it sounds gentler, more tentative, or more polite. These are called hedges.

HedgeEffectExample
sort of / kind ofmakes it approximate/tentativeIt's sort of difficult to explain.
a bit / a littlesoftens an adjectiveI'm a bit tired.
in a waypartly true / from one angleIn a way, you're right.
kind of / -ish on adjectivesapproximates a qualityThe film was kind of boring.
more or lessalmost / basicallyWe've more or less agreed.
I suppose / I guesstentative agreementI guess it's fine.

Softening is often about politeness and face. Compare a blunt statement with a hedged one:

Blunt (can sound harsh)Softened (more natural & polite)
Your idea won't work.Your idea sort of won't work, I'm afraid.
This is expensive.This is a bit expensive.
I disagree.I kind of see it differently.
You're wrong.In a way that's true, but…

💡 Hedging is not weakness

Learners sometimes worry that hedging makes them sound unsure or unconfident. In fact, over-precision and bluntness often sound aggressive or naïve, while a well-judged hedge sounds mature and socially aware. The skill is balance: hedge to be polite and natural, but don't bury a clear message under so many "sort of"s that no one can tell what you mean.

Register: When to Be Vague, When to Be Precise

Vague language belongs mostly to informal, spoken English and friendly messages. In some contexts, though, precision is exactly what's required — and vagueness would be a mistake.

Be vague / approximateBe precise
Chatting with friendsLegal, medical, or financial documents
Casual estimates ("about twenty people")Scientific reports and data
Small talk and friendly messagesInstructions and safety information
Softening opinions politelyContracts, deadlines, exact figures

Keep a set of natural vague expressions ready for relaxed conversation — they're the ones that make you sound like a local rather than a textbook:

about / around twenty seven-ish or so give or take and stuff like that that kind of thing or something sort of / kind of a bit more or less

⚠️ Read the room

The same vagueness that's warm and natural among friends can read as unprofessional or evasive in the wrong place. "The report's more or less done and it'll be ready seven-ish" is fine to a friend, but to a manager you'd want "The report is nearly complete; I'll send it by 7 pm." Match your precision to the situation — that judgement is itself a B2 skill.

Vague Language in Action

Read this relaxed conversation between two friends. Notice how every turn uses vague language — and how natural and friendly it sounds because of it.

Alex: So how was the party? Were there a lot of people?

Priya: Yeah, about thirty or so, give or take. It was kind of crowded, actually.

Alex: Nice. Did you eat before, or…?

Priya: We grabbed a few things — pizza, snacks, that kind of thing. Nothing fancy.

Alex: And what time did it finish?

Priya: Oh, midnight-ish? I left a bit early — I was sort of tired. We should do something or something next weekend, though.

Alex: Definitely. I'm more or less free after Thursday.

🌫️ What to notice: "about thirty or so, give or take" (approximate number), "a few things… that kind of thing" (general nouns), "midnight-ish" (approximate time), "a bit… sort of… more or less" (softening). None of it is imprecise by accident — it's how relaxed, friendly English actually sounds. A version with hard figures ("There were exactly thirty-one people; it ended at 00:14") would feel strange.

🎯 Your Project: Describe It Naturally

Choose something you know well and describe it out loud — to a partner, a group, or your phone's recorder — using natural vague language so it sounds like relaxed conversation, not a data report.

  1. (2 min) Pick a topic: a place you know (your neighbourhood, a café), a routine (a typical weekday, your morning), or a plan (an upcoming trip, this weekend).
  2. (4 min) Jot down rough notes. Deliberately plan to include: an approximate number/time (about / -ish / or so), a general noun (stuff / that kind of thing / or something), and a hedge (sort of / a bit / more or less).
  3. (3 min) Speak for 60–90 seconds. Aim for the relaxed feel of the dialogue above — approximate, friendly, not clinical.
  4. (2 min) If you recorded it, listen back. Did any part sound too precise or "textbook"? Re-say one sentence more naturally.
  5. Partner/group option: Your listener asks one follow-up ("Roughly how far is it?", "What kind of stuff?") — answer with more vague language on the spot.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use vague language to sound relaxed and friendly in casual speech — it's a mark of fluency, not sloppiness.
  • Reach for -ish, about, around, or so whenever an exact number would sound too clinical.
  • Use a hedge (sort of, a bit, in a way) to soften opinions and be polite.
  • Match your precision to the situation: vague with friends, exact in documents, data, and safety information.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Being too precise in casual chat: "It took 34 minutes" sounds robotic — "about half an hour" is more natural.
  • Over-stacking placeholders: "the stuff with the thingy near the whatsit" is impossible to follow. One vague word at a time.
  • Using vague language where precision is required: never "more or less" on a deadline, a dosage, or a contract figure.
  • Confusing "-ish" register: "seven-ish" is great in a text, but write "around 7 pm" in a professional email.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Fluent English is often deliberately approximate — vagueness sounds warmer, more honest, and more polite than over-precision.
  • Approximate quantity with about, around, roughly, or so, -ish, give or take, more or less.
  • General nouns and placeholders (stuff, things, that kind of thing, or something, thingy) stand in for a full list.
  • Hedges (sort of, kind of, a bit, in a way, I suppose) soften statements and opinions politely.
  • Vague language is mostly informal and spoken — be precise in documents, data, contracts, and safety information.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Doesn't vague language make me sound like I don't know the answer?

Not when it's used well. Among friends, "about twenty, give or take" sounds more honest and natural than a suspiciously exact figure. Vagueness only reads as evasive in the wrong context — a deadline, a report, a contract — where you should switch to precision. The skill is matching the two to the situation.

What's the difference between "sort of" and "kind of"?

Almost none — they're interchangeable hedges meaning "approximately / to some degree." Kind of is very slightly more common in American English and sort of in British English, but both are understood everywhere. In fast speech they reduce to "sorta" and "kinda" (see Lesson 8.1). Use whichever feels natural.

Can I use "stuff" and "thingy" in writing?

In casual writing — texts, chat, friendly emails — yes, they're fine and natural. In formal or professional writing, no: use specific nouns or a neutral word like "items" or "materials." As a rule, if you'd say it comfortably to a friend but not to your boss or an examiner, keep it out of formal writing.
