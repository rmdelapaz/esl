🧰 Lesson 5.2: The Causative — have/get something done

There's a huge difference between "I cut my hair" and "I had my hair cut." The first says you took the scissors to it yourself; the second says a professional did it for you. That small but vital distinction is the job of the causative — have/get + object + past participle — the structure English uses whenever someone else does a service for you. Master it, and you'll talk about repairs, appointments, and everyday arrangements exactly the way native speakers do.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the causative have/get + object + past participle across all tenses
  • Choose between have and get depending on register
  • Distinguish the "arrange a service" meaning from the "experience a misfortune" meaning ("She had her bag stolen")
  • Handle the related patterns get someone to do and have someone doing, and contrast the causative with the plain active

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe the services you arrange in your life — repairs, appointments, deliveries — in at least six causative sentences across different tenses.

Who Actually Does It?

English is careful about who performs an action. When you do something with your own hands, you use a plain active verb. But when you arrange for a professional — a mechanic, a hairdresser, a builder — to do it for you, you don't normally name them and say "The mechanic repaired my car." Instead you keep yourself at the centre of the sentence and use the causative:

Plain active: "I cut my hair." (I did it myself — scissors in hand)

Causative: "I had my hair cut." (a hairdresser did it; I arranged and paid for it)

That contrast is the whole engine of the lesson. Both sentences are grammatically correct — they simply mean different things. The causative lets you talk about the services that fill everyday life without turning every sentence into a story about the service provider.

graph LR
    A["A service to arrange"] --> B["I do it myself: I repaired my car"]
    A --> C["Someone does it for me: I had my car repaired"]

🧠 Mindset

The causative feels strange at first because your first language may not mark this difference — many languages simply say "I cut my hair" for both meanings. That's exactly why noticing it is so valuable: it's a place where careful English says something your instinct might miss. Don't aim to get it perfect in one sitting. Aim to catch yourself whenever you talk about a repair, an appointment, or a delivery, and ask "Did I do it, or did someone do it for me?" The habit builds fast. 🌱

The Pattern Across Tenses

The recipe is fixed: have/get + OBJECT + past participle. The object (the thing being worked on) always comes before the past participle. To move the pattern through time, you change only have or get — the object and the participle stay put.

Tense / formCausative (have + object + pp)
Present simpleI have my car serviced every year.
Present continuousShe is having her hair cut right now.
Past simpleWe had the house painted last month.
Present perfectI've just had my eyes tested.
Future (will)They'll have the roof repaired soon.
Going toI'm going to have the kitchen redone.
ModalYou should get your teeth checked.
Modal perfectWe should have had it fixed sooner.

Notice how regular this is. Whatever tense you need, you simply conjugate have (or get) as the main verb and leave the rest of the pattern alone:

I have it serviced → I had it serviced → I'll have it serviced → I've had it serviced → I'm having it serviced.

⚠️ Word order is everything

The object must sit between the verb and the participle: "I had my car repaired." ✅ If you put the participle first — "I had repaired my car" — you've written the past perfect, which means you repaired it yourself earlier. Same words, completely different meaning. Keep the order have/get → thing → done.

Have vs Get

In the causative, have and get mean essentially the same thing — someone does a service for you. The difference is one of register:

HaveGet
slightly more neutral / formalslightly more informal / conversational
We had the report translated.We got the report translated.
I need to have this document signed.I need to get this signed.

In everyday speech, get is extremely common and sounds completely natural — "I'm getting my phone fixed," "We got the carpets cleaned." In a formal report or a polite request, have is the safer choice. Both are correct; let the situation guide you.

💡 A useful nuance with "get"

Get can carry a faint sense of effort or achievement — of finally managing to arrange something: "It took weeks, but I finally got the boiler repaired." Have is more neutral about the effort involved. It's a subtle flavour, not a rule — but noticing it helps you pick the more natural verb.

A: Your car looks brand new! Did you clean it yourself?

B: No chance — I had it valeted at that new place. And I finally got the dent fixed too.

A: Nice. I really need to get mine serviced — the warning light's been on for weeks.

B: You should have it checked soon. My neighbour ignored hers and ended up having the whole engine rebuilt.

A: Ouch. Right — I'll book it in. While I'm at it, I might have the tyres replaced as well.

The "Misfortune" Meaning

The very same structure — have/get + object + past participle — has a second meaning that catches many learners off guard. It can describe something bad that happened to you, something you certainly did not arrange:

She had her bag stolen on the train. (a thief took it — she didn't arrange this!)

They had their flight cancelled at the last minute.

He got his phone confiscated in class.

How do you tell the two meanings apart? Context and common sense. Nobody arranges to have their bag stolen or their window broken, so the "misfortune" reading is obvious. When the action is a normal paid service — a haircut, a repair — it's the "arrange a service" reading. The grammar is identical; the meaning comes from the world.

Same structureMeaning
I had my car repaired.Service I arranged (a garage did it)
I had my car stolen.Misfortune (a thief took it)
She had her hair cut.Service (a hairdresser did it)
She had her window smashed.Misfortune (someone broke it)

⚠️ Don't confuse this with the past perfect

"He had his wallet stolen" (misfortune — someone stole it from him) is not the same as "He had stolen a wallet" (past perfect — he was the thief!). Once again, the order tells you everything: object before the participle = causative; participle before the object = past perfect. Two very different stories.

Get/Have Someone To Do / Doing

So far the object has been a thing ("my car", "her hair"). English has two close relatives where the object is a person you persuade or employ to act. You'll meet these often, so it's worth a brief look.

get + someone + to + infinitive

This means persuade or arrange for a person to do something:

I got him to help me move the sofa. (I persuaded him)

Can you get the electrician to call me back?

have + someone + do (bare infinitive)

This means arrange for someone to do something, often in a slightly more formal or managerial tone. Note there's no "to":

I'll have the porter bring your bags up. (hotel staff arranging a service)

The manager had the assistant reschedule the meeting.

have + someone/something + doing

With an -ing form, the pattern suggests an ongoing action you cause or allow:

She soon had everyone laughing. (caused them to laugh)

We had them waiting for an hour. (they were kept waiting)

💡 Keep the big picture

Don't let these relatives overwhelm the main pattern. The workhorse you'll use every day is have/get + thing + past participle ("I had my car repaired"). The "person" patterns — get someone to do, have someone do/doing — are worth recognising and using occasionally, but the object-plus-participle causative is the one to make automatic first.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Keep the order have/get → object → past participle every single time ("had my car repaired").
  • Change only have/get to move through tenses — the object and participle never change.
  • Use get for a more informal, conversational feel and have for a neutral or formal one.
  • Let context signal the "misfortune" meaning ("She had her window smashed") — the grammar is the same.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wrong word order: "I got repaired my car" ❌ → "I got my car repaired" ✅.
  • Confusing causative and past perfect: "He had his wallet stolen" (misfortune) ≠ "He had stolen a wallet" (he was the thief).
  • Using the causative when you do it yourself: if you cut your hair, say "I cut my hair," not "I had my hair cut."
  • Adding "to" after "have someone": "I'll have the porter to bring the bags" ❌ → "I'll have the porter bring the bags" ✅ (bare infinitive). With get, though, you do use to: "I'll get the porter to bring them."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The causative is have/get + object + past participle — someone else does a service for you ("I had my car repaired").
  • To change tense, change only have/get; the object always stays before the participle.
  • Get is more informal, have more neutral or formal — both mean the same thing.
  • The same structure expresses misfortune ("She had her bag stolen") — context tells you which meaning.
  • Related patterns: get someone to do, have someone do (bare infinitive), and have someone/something doing. Contrast the causative with the plain active ("I cut my hair" = myself).

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is there any real difference between "have" and "get" here?

In meaning, almost none — both say someone did a service for you. The difference is register: get is more informal and common in speech ("I'm getting my phone fixed"), while have is a touch more neutral or formal. Get can also hint faintly at effort ("I finally got it repaired"). Use either; just match the tone of the situation.

How do I know if "had something done" means a service or a misfortune?

Context and common sense. Nobody arranges to have their bag stolen or their window broken, so those are clearly misfortunes. A haircut, a repair, or a delivery is obviously a service you arranged. The grammar is identical — the meaning comes entirely from what the action is and the situation around it.

Why is "I had my hair cut" different from "I cut my hair"?

Because of who did it. "I cut my hair" (plain active) means you took the scissors and did it yourself. "I had my hair cut" (causative) means a hairdresser did it for you and you arranged it. English marks this difference deliberately, even though many languages use one form for both.
