🔗 Lesson 4.1: Linking Ideas — Contrast, Cause & Result

Ideas rarely stand alone. In real speech and writing, they push against each other, explain each other, and lead to one another. The words that manage those relationships — although, despite, because of, so…that, therefore — are the signposts that make you easy to follow. At B2, the challenge isn't choosing the right meaning; it's getting the grammar that follows each linker exactly right.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Express contrast and concession with although, though, even though, despite, in spite of, whereas, and however
  • Express cause and reason with because, since, as, because of, due to, and owing to
  • Express result and purpose with so, so…that, such…that, therefore, consequently, and so as to
  • Use the correct grammar — clause, noun, or -ing — after each linker, and punctuate connectors properly

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Project: Give a balanced two-minute mini-talk on a topic (remote work, city vs country, social media) that weighs both sides using a range of linkers for contrast, cause, and result.

Why Linkers Matter

Imagine two speakers making the same argument. The first says: "Remote work is convenient. It can feel isolating. Companies save money. Some people are less productive." Four flat facts, and the listener has to guess how they relate. The second says: "Although remote work is convenient, it can feel isolating. Companies save money because they need less office space; however, some people are less productive as a result." Same facts — but now every relationship is signposted, and the listener is carried smoothly from one idea to the next.

That's what linkers do. They tell your reader or listener, in advance, what kind of idea is coming: a contrast, a reason, or a result. At B2 you already know most of these words. The real work is the grammar that comes after them — because the biggest mistakes here aren't about meaning, they're about form.

🧠 Mindset

Think of linkers as signposts on a road. A good signpost doesn't just exist — it points in the right direction and is placed exactly where a driver needs it. When you master not only which linker to use but what grammar follows it, your writing stops feeling like a pile of sentences and starts feeling like a journey your reader can follow without effort. That's not a small skill — it's the difference between being understood and being a pleasure to read. 🌱

Contrast & Concession

Contrast shows two things are different. Concession admits one thing is true but says another is true anyway ("Yes, but…"). English gives you several tools — and each one demands a different structure after it. This is the heart of the lesson.

LinkerWhat follows itExample
although / though / even thougha clause (subject + verb)Although it was raining, we went out.
despite / in spite ofa noun or -ing formDespite the rain, we went out. · In spite of being tired, she finished.
despite / in spite of the fact thata clause (the bridge)Despite the fact that it was raining, we went out.
however / nevertheless / on the other handa new sentence (connector + comma)It was expensive. However, we bought it.
whereas / whilea clause, comparing two thingsHe likes tea, whereas she prefers coffee.

⚠️ The big one: despite and in spite of take a NOUN, not a clause

This is the single most common B2 error with these linkers. Despite and in spite of are followed by a noun or an -ing form — never by a subject + verb clause.

  • ❌ Despite it was raining, we went out.
  • ✅ Despite the rain, we went out. (despite + noun)
  • ✅ Despite being wet, we kept walking. (despite + -ing)
  • ✅ Despite the fact that it was raining, we went out. (the bridge to a full clause)

If you really need a full clause after despite / in spite of, add the bridge the fact that. Otherwise, switch to although: "Although it was raining…"

The mirror-image rule is just as useful: although and its family (though, even though) take a full clause and cannot be followed by a bare noun.

  • ❌ Although the rain, we went out. → ✅ Although it was raining, we went out.
  • Even though is the emphatic version of although: "Even though I warned him, he did it."
  • Though is a little more informal and can even come at the end of a sentence in speech: "It was expensive. We bought it, though."

💡 however vs although — they are NOT interchangeable

Although joins two clauses inside one sentence: "Although it was late, we stayed." However is a sentence connector — it links across a full stop or semicolon and takes a comma: "It was late. However, we stayed." or "It was late; however, we stayed." Don't write "However it was late, we stayed" — that role belongs to although.

Cause & Reason

These linkers answer the question "Why?" Again, the split is between those that take a clause and those that take a noun.

LinkerWhat follows itExample
becausea clauseWe stayed in because it was raining.
since / asa clause (reason already known)Since you're here, let's start. · As it was late, we left.
because ofa noun (or -ing)We stayed in because of the rain.
due to / owing toa noun (more formal)The match was cancelled due to bad weather.

⚠️ because vs because of

The meaning is the same; the grammar is not. because + clause (subject + verb); because of + noun.

  • ✅ We were late because the traffic was terrible. (clause)
  • ✅ We were late because of the terrible traffic. (noun)
  • ❌ We were late because of the traffic was terrible. (don't mix them)

💡 due to vs because of (a light note)

In everyday English these are used almost interchangeably before a noun, with due to and owing to sounding more formal and more common in writing. A traditional rule says due to should follow the verb be ("The delay was due to fog") while because of attaches to a whole clause ("We were delayed because of fog"). It's a useful instinct, but at B2 either is safe before a noun — the key point is simply that a noun must follow, never a clause.

Since and as are handy alternatives to because when the reason is already obvious or shared — they often open the sentence: "Since we've finished early, let's get a coffee." (Remember that since can also mean "from a point in time" — context makes it clear.)

Result, Consequence & Purpose

These linkers answer "And so what happened?" or "For what purpose?" Here the classic B2 trap is the pair so…that and such…that.

LinkerStructureExample
so+ clause (the result)It was late, so we left.
so + adjective/adverb + thatso + quality + that + clauseIt was so cold that the lake froze.
such + (a/an) + noun phrase + thatsuch + noun + that + clauseIt was such a good film that I watched it twice.
therefore / consequently / as a resultsentence connector (formal) + commaSales fell. Therefore, the shop closed.
so as to / in order to+ base verb (purpose)She left early so as to catch the train.

⚠️ so…that vs such…that

Both intensify a quality and lead to a result. The choice depends on what comes next:

  • so + adjective or adverb + that: It was so cold that we stayed in. · She spoke so quickly that I couldn't follow.
  • such + (a/an) + noun phrase + that: It was such a cold day that we stayed in. · They were such kind people that we cried.

Quick test: if a noun follows, use such; if only an adjective/adverb follows, use so. ❌ "It was so a cold day" / ❌ "It was such cold".

Therefore, consequently, and as a result are formal result-connectors that link across sentences, just like however: they usually follow a full stop or semicolon and take a comma. So is their everyday spoken cousin and simply joins two clauses: "I was tired, so I went to bed."

Register & Punctuation

Choosing the right linker isn't only about grammar — it's about register (how formal you sound) and the medium (spoken vs written).

FunctionMore formal / writtenMore neutral / spoken
Contrastnevertheless, however, whereasbut, though, on the other hand
Causeowing to, due to, asbecause, because of, since
Resulttherefore, consequentlyso, that's why

💡 Punctuating connectors

Sentence connectors — however, therefore, nevertheless, consequently, on the other hand — follow strong punctuation and take a comma after them:

  • Full stop: "It was risky. Nevertheless, they tried."
  • Semicolon: "It was risky; however, they tried."

Clause linkers — although, because, so, whereas — join clauses inside one sentence, usually with a comma when they come first: "Although it was risky, they tried."

Phrase Bank & Model

Keep these grouped by function so that, mid-sentence, you can reach for the right tool and remember what grammar it needs.

Although + clause Even though + clause Despite + noun/-ing In spite of the fact that… …, whereas … . However, … because + clause because of + noun due to + noun Since / As + clause …, so … so + adj + that such + noun + that . Therefore, … so as to + verb

Here is a short, balanced argument on remote work that puts the whole toolkit to work — notice how the linkers do all the connecting:

Although remote work has clear benefits, it also has real drawbacks. On one hand, employees save time because they no longer commute, and companies cut costs due to the smaller offices they need. However, not everyone thrives at home. Some people find it so isolating that their motivation drops, whereas others are so easily distracted that they get less done. In spite of these challenges, most teams keep some remote days, therefore combining flexibility with connection. It is such a significant change to working life that we are still learning how to do it well.

Contrast: although, however, whereas, in spite of · Cause: because, due to · Result: so…that, therefore, such…that.

And the same relationships in quick conversation:

A: You moved to the countryside, right? Do you miss the city?

B: A bit. It's much quieter here, whereas the city never sleeps.

A: But you're happy?

B: Very. Despite the longer travel, life feels calmer. The rent is so cheap that we could afford a garden.

A: Nice. I couldn't do it, though — I'd get bored.

B: Fair. Since you love the nightlife, you'd probably hate it. Therefore you should stay put!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose the right linker for the grammar

Objective: Pick the option that fits the structure that follows it.

  1. (Although / Despite) the traffic, we arrived on time.
  2. (Although / Despite) the traffic was terrible, we arrived on time.
  3. We cancelled the picnic (because / because of) the storm.
  4. We cancelled the picnic (because / because of) a storm was coming.
  5. (However / Although) the price was high, the quality justified it.
  6. The flight was delayed (due to / because) fog.
✅ Answer

1. Despite (+ noun) · 2. Although (+ clause) · 3. because of (+ noun) · 4. because (+ clause) · 5. Although (joins clauses in one sentence; However would need a full stop before it) · 6. due to (+ noun; because would need a clause: "because it was foggy").

🏋️ Exercise 2: Combine the pairs with the given linker

Objective: Join each pair, adjusting the grammar so the linker fits.

  1. It was very cold. We went swimming. (despite)
  2. She was tired. She kept working. (even though)
  3. The road was closed. There had been an accident. (because of)
  4. He likes action films. His sister prefers comedies. (whereas)
  5. The shop was losing money. It closed. (therefore)
✅ Answer

1. Despite the cold, we went swimming. (or Despite the cold weather / Despite it being cold) · 2. Even though she was tired, she kept working. · 3. The road was closed because of an accident. (noun — not "because of there had been…") · 4. He likes action films, whereas his sister prefers comedies. · 5. The shop was losing money. Therefore, it closed. (or …money; therefore, it closed.)

🏋️ Exercise 3: so…that or such…that?

Objective: Fill each gap with so or such (add a/an where needed).

  1. It was hot day that we stayed indoors.
  2. The music was loud that we couldn't talk.
  3. They were friendly people that we felt at home.
  4. She ran fast that no one could catch her.
  5. It was boring film that half the audience left.
✅ Answer

1. such a hot day (noun phrase) · 2. so loud (adjective) · 3. such friendly people (plural noun — no article) · 4. so fast (adverb) · 5. such a boring film (noun phrase).

🎯 Your Project: A Balanced Two-Minute Mini-Talk

Choose one topic where there's a genuine trade-off: remote work vs the office, city vs countryside, or social media — blessing or curse. Your goal is to weigh both sides using a range of linkers. Speak to a partner or group, or record yourself on your phone.

  1. (4 min) Brainstorm two or three advantages and two or three disadvantages. Jot them as short notes, not full sentences.
  2. (4 min) Plan your links: use a contrast linker to pivot between the two sides (however, although, whereas, despite), a cause linker to explain each point (because, since, due to), and a result linker to draw a consequence (so, so…that, therefore).
  3. (2 min) Deliver your talk. Open with a hook, present one side, pivot with a contrast linker, present the other side, and close with a balanced conclusion.
  4. (2 min) If you recorded it, listen back: did you follow despite/in spite of with a noun? Did because of / due to take a noun? Did so/such…that match its structure?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used at least two different contrast/concession linkers
  • ☐ I used at least one cause linker with a clause and one with a noun
  • ☐ I used at least one result linker (including so…that or such…that)
  • ☐ I followed despite / in spite of with a noun or -ing, never a clause
  • ☐ I punctuated my sentence connectors (however, therefore) correctly
  • ☐ My talk felt balanced — both sides got a fair hearing

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Before you write a linker, ask: does this one take a clause, a noun, or start a new sentence? Get the grammar right first, then the style.
  • Use despite / in spite of + the fact that when you want a full clause after them.
  • Vary your linkers within a paragraph — repeating but…but…but flattens an argument that however and whereas would sharpen.
  • Match your linkers to your register: therefore in an essay, so in a chat.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Clause after despite: ❌ "Despite it was raining" → ✅ "Despite the rain" / "Despite the fact that it was raining."
  • Clause after because of / due to: ❌ "because of the road was closed" → ✅ "because the road was closed" or "because of the closed road."
  • However as a clause linker: ❌ "However it was late, we stayed" → ✅ "Although it was late, we stayed" or "It was late. However, we stayed."
  • Mixing so and such: ❌ "so a cold day" / "such cold" → ✅ "such a cold day" / "so cold."
  • Missing comma after connectors: ❌ "However we stayed" → ✅ "However, we stayed."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Contrast/concession: although/though/even though + clause; despite/in spite of + noun or -ing (add the fact that for a clause); however/nevertheless start a new sentence; whereas/while compare.
  • Cause: because/since/as + clause; because of/due to/owing to + noun.
  • Result/purpose: so + clause; so + adj/adv + that vs such + noun + that; therefore/consequently connect sentences; so as to/in order to + verb for purpose.
  • The B2 skill is the grammar after the linker — clause, noun, or new sentence — plus matching register and punctuation.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've turned a pile of separate ideas into a connected argument. You can now signal contrast, reason, and result on purpose, and — just as importantly — get the grammar after each linker exactly right. That control is what makes writing flow and speech easy to follow. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When should I use despite instead of although?

They mean the same thing; only the grammar differs. Use although when a full clause (subject + verb) follows: "Although it was raining." Use despite / in spite of when a noun or -ing form follows: "Despite the rain / Despite being wet." If you want a clause after despite, add the bridge: "Despite the fact that it was raining."

Is there a real difference between due to and because of?

In practice, very little — both take a noun and both express cause. Due to and owing to sound more formal and are more common in writing. A traditional rule links due to to the verb be ("The delay was due to fog") and because of to a clause ("We were delayed because of fog"), but at B2 either is fine before a noun. The one rule you must keep: a noun follows, not a clause.

Can I use however in the middle of a sentence?

Yes, but as a parenthetical aside set off by commas, not as a clause-joiner: "The plan was risky. We decided, however, to go ahead." It still connects two full ideas across strong punctuation — it never replaces although inside a single clause pair.
