🔭 Lesson 5.1: Future Perfect & Future Continuous

You already know how to talk about the future with will, going to, and the present continuous. Now you'll add two more precise aspects that let you look at the future from the inside. The future continuous zooms in on a moment and catches an action in progress ("This time tomorrow I'll be flying to Tokyo"); the future perfect stands at a future point and looks back at something already finished ("By 2030, I'll have finished my degree"). Together they let you narrate the future almost as vividly as the past.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form and use the future continuous (will be + -ing) for actions in progress at a future time
  • Use the future continuous for polite, neutral enquiries ("Will you be using the car tonight?")
  • Form and use the future perfect (will have + past participle) for actions completed before a future point
  • Choose confidently between the two using time markers like by, by then, by the time, and in + period

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe your life and plans at a chosen future point, using at least six sentences that mix the future continuous and the future perfect.

Two New Angles on the Future

Most learners arrive at B2 able to predict and plan the future — "It will rain," "I'm going to study tonight." What's often missing is the ability to position an event in time relative to a future moment. That's exactly what these two aspects do.

Back at B1 you touched on future continuity and on time clauses ("When I arrive, I'll call you"). This lesson is the fuller B2 treatment: it keeps the future continuous and adds its natural partner, the future perfect. Think of them as two camera angles on a moment that hasn't happened yet:

Future continuous — the camera is inside the moment, watching an action unfold: "At 8 p.m. tomorrow, we'll be having dinner."

Future perfect — the camera stands at the moment and looks back at something already done: "By 8 p.m., we'll have eaten."

The word that unlocks both is a reference point in the future — a clock time, a date, an event. Once you fix that point, you can ask: is the action still happening then (continuous), or already finished by then (perfect)?

🧠 Mindset

Don't think of these as difficult "extra" tenses to be feared. Think of them as tools for precision — a way to say exactly where an event sits on the timeline. Native speakers use them constantly and casually ("I'll have left by then," "We'll be landing soon"). The forms are completely regular, so the only real work is training your ear to reach for them at the right moment. That comes with noticing and repeating — and you'll do plenty of both today. 🌱

The Future Continuous

The future continuous describes an action that will be in progress at a specific time in the future. The form is delightfully simple and never changes:

will be + verb-ing

This time tomorrow, I will be flying to Tokyo.

Don't call at nine — we 'll be having dinner.

By midnight, they 'll be dancing in the streets.

FormExample
PositiveI will be working late tonight.
ContractedShe 'll be waiting at the station.
NegativeWe won't be using the meeting room.
QuestionWill you be staying long?

1. An action in progress at a future time

This is the core meaning. Fix a future moment, and picture the action already underway around it — it started before that moment and will still be going after it:

At 3 o'clock tomorrow, I 'll be sitting an exam. (the exam is underway at 3:00)

Next summer, they 'll be travelling around South America.

2. Polite, neutral enquiries

Here's a use that surprises many learners. The future continuous can make a question sound softer and more neutral than a direct will question, because it asks about circumstances rather than requesting a decision. Compare:

Direct (can feel like a request)Neutral (just asking about plans)
Will you use the car tonight?Will you be using the car tonight?
Are you going to come to the meeting?Will you be coming to the meeting?
How long will you stay?How long will you be staying?

The neutral version doesn't pressure the listener; it simply enquires about their expected plans. Waiters, receptionists, and colleagues use it all the time: "Will you be paying by card?"

💡 Why "be flying," not "fly"?

The -ing form is what gives us the sense of an action stretched across the reference point — mid-flight, mid-dinner, mid-journey. "I'll fly to Tokyo tomorrow" simply reports a plan; "I'll be flying to Tokyo this time tomorrow" puts you right in the cabin with the seatbelt sign on. That vividness is the whole point of the aspect.

The Future Perfect

The future perfect describes an action that will be completed before a certain point in the future. You stand at that future point and look back on the finished action. The form is, again, completely regular:

will have + past participle

By 2030, I will have finished my degree.

By the time you arrive, we 'll have left.

In three years, she 'll have saved enough to buy a flat.

FormExample
PositiveThey will have finished by Friday.
ContractedI 'll have written the report by then.
NegativeHe won't have arrived yet at six.
QuestionWill you have eaten before we meet?

The key mental move is the deadline. The future perfect almost always answers the question "finished by when?" You set a future boundary, and the action lands somewhere before it:

By the end of the month, we 'll have paid off the loan.

Hurry — by the time the film starts, all the good seats will have gone.

⚠️ "By" is not the same as "until"

The future perfect loves by (= no later than that point): "I'll have finished by six." Avoid until here — until describes a duration up to a point, not a deadline: "I'll be working until six" (future continuous idea) is different from "I'll have finished by six" (future perfect). If you can replace the word with "no later than," you want by and probably the future perfect.

💡 A quick note on the future perfect continuous

There is a rarer cousin — will have been + -ing — for the duration of an action up to a future point: "By December, I will have been working here for ten years." You don't need to master it now; just recognise it. It answers "for how long?" rather than "finished by when?"

In Progress vs Completed

This is where the two aspects earn their place. Put them side by side against the same future moment and the difference is crisp: the future continuous is mid-action; the future perfect is post-action.

Reference pointFuture continuous (in progress)Future perfect (already done)
At 8 p.m.We'll be eating dinner. (still at the table)We'll have eaten dinner. (plates cleared)
This time next yearI'll be studying in Berlin.I'll have graduated.
When you get hereThey'll be waiting for you.They'll have gone home.

Here is the same idea placed on a single future timeline. Notice how the continuous action straddles the reference point while the perfect action sits entirely before it:

graph LR
    A["Now"] --> B["Future point / by then"]
    B --> C["Future continuous: action in progress across the point"]
    B --> D["Future perfect: action finished before the point"]

And in a natural stretch of speech, they combine effortlessly:

A: So where do you see yourself this time next year?

B: Well, I 'll have finished my training by then, so I 'll probably be working at the new clinic downtown.

A: Nice. And by the time you start, will you have moved into the city?

B: I hope so. By September we 'll have signed the lease, and by winter we 'll be settling into the new place.

A: It sounds like this time next year you 'll be looking back on a huge year — and you 'll have earned the rest.

✅ The feel to hold onto

Future continuous = the film is still rolling at your chosen moment.  Future perfect = the credits have finished before your chosen moment. Ask yourself: at that point, is it happening or has it happened?

Time Markers & Register

Both aspects lean on time markers that fix the future reference point. Learning the markers is half the battle, because each one nudges you toward the right aspect.

MarkerTypical partnerExample
by / by thenfuture perfectBy then, I'll have paid it all back.
by the time (+ present)future perfectBy the time you read this, I'll have left.
in + periodeither aspectIn two years, she'll have qualified. / In two hours, we'll be landing.
this time tomorrow / next weekfuture continuousThis time next week, we'll be lying on a beach.
at + clock timefuture continuousAt 10, I'll be presenting to the board.

⚠️ After "by the time," use the present — not "will"

By the time introduces a time clause, and like other time clauses (when, before, after, as soon as) it takes the present tense, not will: "By the time the guests arrive (not will arrive), I'll have finished cooking." The future meaning lives in the main clause; the time clause stays present. This is the B1 time-clause rule you already met — now working hand in hand with the future perfect.

Keep a few ready-made frames on hand for talking about future milestones and plans:

By this time next year… By the time you get here… In a few years' time… I'll have finished by then. We'll be waiting for you. Will you be joining us? They'll have left by now. This time tomorrow…

💡 Register: neutral, planned, and professional

The future continuous carries a polite, businesslike tone in enquiries — hotels, airlines, and offices use it to sound helpful without pressing you ("Will you be needing anything else?"). The future perfect sounds confident and organised, which is why it fills project plans and forecasts ("The team will have completed testing by Q3"). Reaching for these on purpose instantly lifts the register of your speech and writing.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. Feel the difference between an action still in progress ("will be…") and one already finished ("will have…").

  • This time tomorrow, I will be flying somewhere over the Pacific.
  • By the end of the year, we will have paid off the last of the loan.
  • Will you be using the meeting room this afternoon, or can I book it?
  • By the time you read this message, I will have already left the office.
  • In a few years, she will have qualified, and she will be working abroad.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Continuous or perfect?

Objective: Put the verb in brackets into the future continuous (will be + -ing) or the future perfect (will have + pp) to match the meaning.

  1. Don't phone at eight — we (have) dinner then.
  2. By 2030, they (build) the new railway line.
  3. This time next week, I (lie) on a beach in Greece.
  4. Hurry up — by the time we get there, the show (start).
  5. At three o'clock tomorrow, she (take) her driving test.
  6. In two hours, the plane (land) in Rome.
✅ Answer

1. will be having · 2. will have built · 3. will be lying · 4. will have started · 5. will be taking · 6. will have landed

The "in progress at a moment" sentences (1, 3, 5) take the continuous; the "finished before a point" sentences (2, 4, 6) take the perfect.

🏋️ Exercise 2: "By then" & "by the time"

Objective: Complete each sentence. Remember: after by the time the verb is in the present, and the future perfect goes in the main clause.

  1. By the time you (arrive), we will have finished the meeting.
  2. I'll retire in 2040. By then, I (work) here for thirty years.
  3. By the time the film (end), it will be past midnight.
  4. Book early — by the time you (decide), the tickets will have sold out.
  5. She's saving hard. By next summer she (save) enough for the trip.
✅ Answer

1. arrive · 2. will have worked · 3. ends · 4. decide · 5. will have saved

Notice 1, 3 and 4 stay in the present because they follow by the time; the future perfect sits in the other clause.

🎯 Your Project: A Postcard From the Future

Choose a future point — this time next year, in five years, or the day of a big event you're planning for. Picture your life at that moment and describe it out loud (to a partner or group, or into your phone's recorder) in at least six sentences.

  1. (3 min) Pick your future point and jot notes: what will be happening around you (in progress), and what you will already have done (completed) by then.
  2. (4 min) Draft six-plus sentences. Deliberately include at least three future continuous ("I'll be living…", "I'll be working…") and at least three future perfect ("I'll have finished…", "I'll have moved…"), tied together with markers like by then and by the time.
  3. (3 min) Deliver it as a confident "postcard from the future." Solo? Record it. With others? Present, then answer one question ("What will you be doing on the actual day?").
  4. (2 min) Review: did each verb match its moment — in progress vs already finished?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used at least six future sentences
  • ☐ I used at least three future continuous verbs (will be + -ing)
  • ☐ I used at least three future perfect verbs (will have + pp)
  • ☐ I used by then or by the time at least once
  • ☐ Each verb matched its moment (in progress vs completed)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Fix a future reference point first, then ask: is the action in progress (continuous) or already finished (perfect) at that point?
  • Pair the future perfect with by / by then / by the time, and the future continuous with at + time / this time tomorrow.
  • Use the future continuous for polite, neutral enquiries ("Will you be needing a receipt?").
  • Keep the verb after by the time in the present, not will.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Using "will" after by the time: "By the time you will arrive" ❌ → "By the time you arrive" ✅.
  • Dropping "have" in the future perfect: "By 2030 I will finished" ❌ → "I will have finished" ✅.
  • Confusing "by" and "until": "I'll have finished until six" ❌ → "I'll have finished by six" ✅.
  • Choosing the wrong aspect: "At 8 I'll have eaten" (already done) ≠ "At 8 I'll be eating" (still at the table). Say what you mean.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The future continuous is will be + -ing — an action in progress at a future moment ("This time tomorrow I'll be flying").
  • The future continuous also makes polite, neutral enquiries ("Will you be using the car tonight?").
  • The future perfect is will have + past participle — an action completed before a future point ("By 2030 I'll have finished").
  • Match markers to aspect: by / by then / by the time / in + period for the perfect; at + time / this time tomorrow for the continuous.
  • After by the time, the verb stays in the present; the future perfect goes in the main clause.

You've added two precise camera angles to your future — one that catches an action mid-flow, one that looks back on it already done. You can now place events on a future timeline with real accuracy, ask questions with a polite, professional tone, and talk about milestones and deadlines the way native speakers do. That control over time is unmistakably B2. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When should I use the future continuous instead of just "will"?

Use the future continuous when you want to picture an action in progress at a specific future moment ("At 8 I'll be cooking") or when you want a question to sound softer and more neutral ("Will you be joining us?"). Plain will is fine for simple predictions and instant decisions; the continuous adds the "already underway" feeling.

What's the difference between "by" and "until" with the future perfect?

By means "no later than that point" and pairs naturally with the future perfect: "I'll have finished by six." Until describes a duration lasting up to a point and does not work with the future perfect. If you can swap the word for "no later than," use by.

Didn't I already learn future time clauses at B1?

Yes — B1 introduced future continuity and the rule that time clauses (when, before, by the time) take the present, not will. This lesson keeps all of that and adds the future perfect so you can say not just when something happens but that it will already be finished by a future point. It's the fuller, more precise B2 version of the same territory.
