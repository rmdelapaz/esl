🎤 Lesson 8.3: Review Project — A Structured Debate

This is it — the final lesson of Confident English (B2). Instead of new grammar, you'll bring everything together in one satisfying task: a structured debate. Building a balanced case — arguing for, arguing against, and reaching a reasoned conclusion — naturally recycles the whole level: future forms, the passive, register, persuasion, comparison, indirect questions, storytelling, and the natural spoken language you've just mastered. Listen to a real debate, then build and deliver your own. Let's finish strong. 💪

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure a balanced argument — introduction, points for, points against, and a reasoned conclusion
  • Concede and counter a point ("That may be true, but…", "While it's fair to say…, the fact remains…")
  • Combine the B2 toolkit — signposting, persuasion, comparison, and hedging — in extended speech
  • Listen to a structured debate and identify how each speaker presents and challenges a view

⏱️ Estimated Time: 60–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Prepare and deliver a balanced case on a motion — for, against, and a conclusion — solo as a balanced talk or with a partner as a debate.

Everything Comes Together

Over twenty-three lessons you've built an enormous amount of English. A structured debate is the perfect way to use it all at once, because a good argument naturally demands nearly every skill you've learned:

  • To predict consequences, you reach for future forms and conditionals ("If we banned it, prices would rise").
  • To sound objective and formal, you use the passive and reporting language ("It is widely believed that…").
  • To persuade, you use the rhetoric and signposting from Module 7.
  • To weigh options, you use advanced comparison ("far more effective than…", "the single biggest factor").
  • To sound natural and human, you soften with the vague language and hedging from Module 8.

This lesson doesn't teach a new rule — it gives you the frame and the phrases to pour all of that into one confident performance.

🧠 Mindset

A capstone can feel intimidating: "Can I really do all of this at once?" You can — and you've been preparing for exactly this. Don't aim for a flawless, native-level debate; aim for a clear, structured, confident one that shows what you can do. Mistakes are fine; the goal is to use the language boldly. This is a celebration of your progress, not an exam to fear. 🌱

The Shape of a Balanced Argument

A structured debate or balanced talk follows a clear, reliable shape. Whether you're speaking solo (presenting both sides) or debating a partner (taking one side), the architecture is the same:

graph TD
    A["Introduction: state the motion"] --> B["Arguments FOR"]
    B --> C["Arguments AGAINST / rebuttal"]
    C --> D["Concede and counter"]
    D --> E["Reasoned conclusion"]
StageWhat you doTypical opener
1. IntroductionState the motion; say what you'll cover"Today I'd like to consider whether…"
2. Arguments forGive 2–3 supporting points with reasons"The strongest argument in favour is…"
3. Arguments againstGive the opposing points fairly"On the other hand, it could be argued that…"
4. Concede & counterAdmit a fair point, then answer it"While that may be true, the fact remains…"
5. ConclusionWeigh both sides; state a reasoned view"On balance, I would argue that…"

💡 "Balanced" doesn't mean "no opinion"

A strong balanced argument still reaches a conclusion. The point of presenting both sides fairly is to show you've weighed the evidence — which makes your final position more convincing, not less. Present the other side honestly, then explain why, on balance, you land where you do.

Language for Debating

Here is the phrase toolkit for each move. Keep these ready — they're the connective tissue that makes a debate sound organised and persuasive.

Introducing and giving your view

The question we need to consider is… In my view / As I see it… There's a strong case for saying… It's often argued that…

Adding supporting arguments

The main argument in favour is… What's more / Furthermore… Not only that, but… A further point worth making is…

Conceding and countering

Admittedly / It's true that… While I accept that…, I still believe… That may be so, but… Even if that's the case… The other side would say…, however…

Concluding

Weighing it all up… On balance, I'd argue that… All things considered… The evidence points to…

⚠️ Concede before you counter — don't just contradict

A common B2-to-C1 leap is learning to concede a point before answering it. Instead of flatly saying "No, you're wrong," acknowledge the opposing view's merit first — "That's a fair point, and it's true that…" — then pivot with "however / but / that said…". This makes you sound reasonable and confident rather than combative, and it's far more persuasive.

💡 Balance formal and natural

The mark of a confident B2 speaker is switching smoothly between formal structures (the passive, comparison, cleft sentences) and natural spoken touches (hedging, vague language). A debate that is all formal sounds stiff; one that is all casual sounds unprepared. Aim for a formal backbone with human, natural connective tissue — that blend is exactly what you've been building all level.

Listen: A Structured Debate

Here's a short structured discussion between three speakers on the motion "Cities should ban private cars from their centres." Read it, then use the audio tools to hear it — notice how each speaker presents, concedes, and challenges.

Chair: Today's motion is that cities should ban private cars from their centres. Let's hear the case in favour first.

Maya: Thank you. The strongest argument in favour is public health. It is widely accepted that car emissions damage our lungs, and if centres were car-free, air quality would improve dramatically. What's more, the space now given to traffic could be turned into parks and cycle lanes — by far the more pleasant use of a city.

Chair: And the case against?

Tom: Admittedly, cleaner air is a real benefit — I won't pretend otherwise. That said, a total ban would hit the people who can least afford it. Not everyone can cycle: the elderly, the disabled, and shift workers would be left stranded. If deliveries were forced out, small shops would suffer too.

Maya: That's a fair point, and it's true that access matters. However, most schemes don't ban everyone — exemptions can be arranged for those who genuinely need them, while ordinary commuters are encouraged onto public transport.

Tom: Even so, we might ask whether the public transport is good enough yet. In many cities, it simply isn't.

Chair: So, weighing it all up?

Maya: On balance, I'd argue the health benefits outweigh the difficulties — provided the transition is gradual and fair. It's sort of a trade-off, but a worthwhile one.

  2. Which groups does Tom say a ban would hurt? — People who can least afford it: the elderly, the disabled, shift workers, and small shops that rely on deliveries.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Mixed B2 review

Objective: Complete each sentence with the correct B2 structure. This spans the whole level.

  1. If the ban introduced, prices would rise. (second conditional: use the subjunctive of "be")
  2. It is widely that the policy has failed. (impersonal passive: report verb, "It is widely ___ that")
  3. This solution is by the most effective one available. (emphasise a superlative: "by ___ the most")
  4. We should have the report before we publish it. (causative: have + object + past participle of "check")
  5. What really is whether people can afford it. (cleft sentence: "What really ___ is…")
  6. Not only it cost more, but it also takes longer. (inversion after "Not only"; present simple auxiliary)
✅ Answer

1. were · 2. believed (also "accepted/thought") · 3. far · 4. checked · 5. matters · 6. does  (These recycle conditionals, the passive, comparison, the causative, cleft, and inversion.)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Correct the mistake

Objective: Each sentence has one common B2 error. Write the corrected word or short phrase.

  1. ❌ "It should have done earlier." → It should have earlier. (modal-perfect passive needs "been")
  2. ❌ "He is thought to steal the money last year." → He is thought to the money last year. (earlier action → "to have + pp")
  3. ❌ "I look forward to hear from you." → I look forward to from you. ("to" is a preposition here → -ing)
  4. ❌ "If I would have known, I'd have told you." → If I known, I'd have told you. (third conditional: no "would" in the if-clause)
  5. ❌ "The meeting lasted a bit exactly two hours." → The meeting lasted two hours. (natural approximator before the number)
  6. ❌ "This is more cheaper than the other." → This is than the other. (no double comparative)
✅ Answer

1. been done · 2. have stolen · 3. hearing · 4. had · 5. about (also "around/roughly") · 6. cheaper

🎯 Your Capstone Project: A Structured Debate

This is your B2 finale. Take a motion, build a balanced case — introduction, arguments for, arguments against (or a rebuttal), and a reasoned conclusion — and deliver it aloud. Do it solo as a balanced talk, or with a partner as a live debate.

  1. (2 min) Choose a motion you have views on. Examples: "Social media does more harm than good," "Remote work should be the default," "University should be free," "Cities should ban private cars."
  2. (6 min) Plan the shape: an introduction stating the motion; two arguments for; two arguments against; at least one concede-and-counter; a reasoned conclusion.
  3. (4 min) Deliberately plant the B2 toolkit: a conditional to predict a consequence, an impersonal passive ("It is widely believed that…"), an advanced comparison ("by far the most…"), a signposting/persuasion phrase, and a natural hedge ("sort of / on balance").
  4. (4 min) Deliver it — 2–3 minutes of speech. Solo? Record a balanced talk covering both sides. With a partner? Take opposite sides, then each give a closing conclusion.
  5. (3 min) Review against the checklist. If you recorded it, listen back and note one thing you did well and one to sharpen.

Model excerpt — motion: "Remote work should be the default."

For: "The strongest argument in favour is flexibility. It is widely accepted that people work better when they control their own time, and if commuting were removed, workers would gain hours back every week — by far the biggest quality-of-life gain on offer."

Against: "Admittedly, remote work suits many people. That said, it can be isolating, and younger staff, in particular, may learn far less without a team around them. Not only does collaboration suffer, but company culture can quietly fade."

Balanced conclusion: "Weighing it all up, I'd argue that remote work should be an option rather than the default — a hybrid model, in a way. The evidence points to flexibility being valuable, provided we don't lose the human connection that offices, for all their faults, still provide."

✅ Capstone Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I introduced the motion clearly and signposted my structure
  • ☐ I gave at least two arguments FOR with reasons
  • ☐ I gave at least two arguments AGAINST fairly
  • ☐ I conceded a point and then countered it
  • ☐ I used a conditional, a passive, and an advanced comparison
  • ☐ I added a natural hedge so it didn't sound stiff
  • ☐ I reached a reasoned conclusion, not just a list
  • ☐ I spoke for 2–3 minutes with confidence

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Signpost every stage — "The main argument for…", "On the other hand…", "Weighing it all up…". Structure is half the battle.
  • Concede before you counter — acknowledge the other side, then answer it. It sounds reasonable and confident.
  • Plant the B2 toolkit on purpose — a conditional, a passive, a comparison, a hedge. Aim to show your range.
  • Reach a conclusion. A balanced talk still lands somewhere — weigh the sides and take a reasoned position.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Listing without weighing: giving points for and against but never concluding leaves the argument unfinished.
  • Flatly contradicting: "You're wrong" is weak. Concede, then counter ("That may be true, but…").
  • All formal or all casual: a wall of passives sounds stiff; nonstop hedging sounds unprepared. Blend the two.
  • Ignoring the other side: a one-sided talk isn't a balanced argument. Present the opposing view fairly.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

I still make mistakes — am I really at B2 level now?

Yes. B2 is not about perfection; even highly fluent speakers make slips. B2 means you can handle a wide range of topics, express opinions with nuance, argue a case, and be understood clearly in most situations — which you can now do. Mistakes are part of every level, including native speech. Keep using the language boldly and the accuracy keeps climbing.

How do I keep improving after finishing B2?

Use English as much as possible in real situations — reading longer texts, listening to podcasts and films without subtitles, speaking and writing regularly, and keeping your learning journal going. The C1 course is designed to take you further (see "Looking Ahead"), but even between courses, consistent exposure and use are what move you forward. Fluency grows with mileage.

Should I review earlier B2 lessons, or move straight on?

A little of both. It's worth revisiting any structure that still feels shaky — the passive, conditionals, or the meaning-change verbs, for instance — using the exercises and journal prompts. But don't wait for perfection before advancing; you learn a great deal by meeting familiar structures again in the harder, richer contexts of C1. Review your weak spots, then keep climbing.
