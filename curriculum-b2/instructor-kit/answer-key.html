Module 1 — Narrating & Looking Back: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (Story Reconstruction orders and model retells, Chain of Consequences model chain, dictations), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 1.1–1.3Classes C1–C39 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, then range. An answer is correct if the time relationship is right (earlier / background / duration; real vs unreal; now vs past) and the form is right. Be generous about optional choices: the past perfect is optional after before / after; had worked and had been working are both fine with for + a period for many verbs; contractions (I'd, would've, 'd rather) are always fine; was after I wish I / If only it is fine in speech (were is safer in writing). American English is the model; British forms (got for gotten, travelled, organised, film, flat, flatmate, queue) are acceptable when a learner uses them. In open tasks, reward a correct richer form with a tick and a "B2!" in the margin.

1.1 · Narrative Tenses — Past Perfect & Continuous (C1)

Gist: The speaker got on the express train by mistake (they were reading and didn't hear the announcements).

Also accept: 4 had got (British) · 5 had travelled · contractions (I'd been looking). Wrong: 4 "got" (the story needs the earlier event) · 6 "was reading" (possible in another story, but the script has had been reading, which explains the result).

Exercise 2 — Choose the narrative tense
  1. had already started
  2. had been working
  3. was driving
  4. had been working (also: had worked: with for 30 years both are natural)
  5. hadn't met
  6. rang
  7. had been walking
  8. had eaten

Wrong: 1 "already started" (order unclear) · 2 "has been working" (present perfect inside a past story) · 5 "didn't meet" (acceptable in casual speech, but before that evening calls for the past perfect) · 6 "was ringing" (the ringing interrupts) · 8 "had been eating" (the cookies are all gone: a finished result = simple).

Exercise 3 — Simple or continuous?

1 had written (a number: six emails) · 2 had been reading (activity → result: tired eyes) · 3 had known (state verb) · 4 had been cooking (activity since lunchtime; the smell is the result) · 5 had painted (a number: three rooms) · 6 had been waiting (activity → result: wet).

Also accept 6 had waited with a note: correct grammar, but the continuous explains the result better. Never accept 3 "had been knowing."

Exercise 4 — Sequencing words

1 While (also: As) · 2 By the time · 3 just · 4 until · 5 when · 6 already.

Note on 1: When I was cooking dinner, my brother called is also possible and common in speech; while is the model for the long background action. Note on 2: When is grammatical but loses the "too late" meaning.

Exercise 5 — From flat to layered (open)

Model: By the time I got to work, the meeting had already started. I'd been sitting in traffic for over an hour, and my phone had died, so I couldn't call anyone. When I walked in, my boss was standing by the door, and she didn't look happy.

Check for: one past continuous (background), one past perfect (earlier), one past perfect continuous (duration), one sequencing word, and a story that still makes sense. Many good versions are possible.

Dialogue — the four tenses

Past simple: happened, went out, came over, knew, came back, was · past continuous: was just finishing, was sitting · past perfect: Had you saved, hadn't saved, you'd lost, she'd already found · past perfect continuous: I'd been working, She'd been having. Any one of each is correct.

Speaking — Story Reconstruction (Set D)

Story A · The Wedding Cake: C – F – A – H – B – G – E – D. Story B · Locked Out: E – B – G – A – H – C – F – D. Literacy-support short versions (★ strips): A = C · F · B · E · D; B = E · B · A · H · D.

Clues learners should notice: A-C "had been baking since five" is the scene-setter; A-D "By the time they arrived" must come last; B-A "As the door was closing" must follow B-G "ran out of the apartment"; B-H introduces Mrs. Ortiz before B-C uses "She."

Model retell (A, taxi driver): "I'd been driving since six that morning, and I was waiting at a red light when the van in front of me just died. A woman got out. She'd been driving for twenty minutes with a strange noise in the engine, and she was holding a huge wedding cake! By the time we'd pushed the van to the side of the road, she was nearly crying, so I drove her to the hotel. The guests had already sat down, but the cake was perfect, and I got the first slice." Model retell (B, Mrs. Ortiz): "I was watering my plants when I heard a door slam. The young man next door had been looking forward to his new job for weeks, and he'd just locked himself out! I'd been keeping a spare key for the building manager for years, so I called him. While we were waiting, I made Daniel coffee and a sandwich. He told me later his supervisor had been locked out herself the week before."

Self-check
Q1
"When I got to the airport, the plane ___." The plane left before I arrived.
A) had already taken off
B) took off
C) was taking off
Why: the past perfect shows the plane left before the story moment. B means I arrived and then it left; C means it was in the middle of leaving.
Q2
"She was exhausted because she ___ all night."
A) had been studying
B) studied
C) has been studying
Why: the past perfect continuous stresses the duration of an activity that explains a past result. C is present perfect: wrong time frame inside a past story.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) I had been knowing him for ten years…
B) I had known him for ten years when we finally met in person.
C) I was knowing him for ten years…
Why: know is a state verb, so it stays in the simple form even with a duration.
Errors you will hear most (1.1)

"When I arrived, the train already left." (no past perfect) · "Yesterday I had gone to the market." (past perfect for no reason) · "I had been knowing / was knowing her." · "By the time I arrived, the party has finished." (present perfect in a past story) · "She had been wrote three emails." · "While the phone rang, I was cooking." (while / when reversed) · "I was go to work." Correct the missing past perfect when the order is unclear; treat an unnecessary past perfect gently (it isn't wrong, just heavy).

1.2 · The Third Conditional — Past Hypotheticals (C2)

Exercise 1 — The picnic that wasn't (Script 1.2-A)

What really happened (any two): they didn't check the forecast · it rained all afternoon · they didn't bring an umbrella · they got very wet · they went into a café · they met their old friend Leo · they have lunch with him every month now.

  1. had checked … wouldn't have planned
  2. had brought … wouldn't have gotten
  3. hadn't gone … wouldn't have met
  4. hadn't met … wouldn't be

Why is 4 different? The result is about now (they are friends again now), so it is a mixed conditional: would + base verb. Also accept: 2 wouldn't have got (British); contractions (If we'd checked). Wrong: 4 "wouldn't have been friends now" (two pasts with now).

Exercise 2 — Put it in the third conditional
  1. had known … would have bought
  2. wouldn't have missed … had set
  3. had listened … wouldn't have gotten (British: got)
  4. would have won … hadn't made
  5. had asked … would have helped
  6. had been … could have bought

Also correct: contractions (I'd known, I'd have bought, would've); might have in 5 changes the meaning (less sure) but is grammatical. Wrong: "If I would have known" · "would of" · "If I knew" (second conditional) · 6 "could buy."

Exercise 3 — Second or third conditional?

4had warned3rd: unreal past

Exercise 4 — would, could or might?

1 would (certain) · 2 might (not sure) · 3 could (it was possible) · 4 could (criticism: it was possible and you didn't) · 5 might ("Who knows?" = uncertain).

Note on 3: would is also grammatical (it means "I definitely would have"), but the sentence says "It was possible," so could is the target. In 4, might have called exists as a complaint in British English ("You might have told me!"), but could is the everyday American choice.

Exercise 5 — Build a mixed conditional
  1. If I had learned to drive, I would be (more) independent now.
  2. If he were (was) more organized, he wouldn't have forgotten the meeting (yesterday).
  3. If Mia hadn't taken a free cooking class (two years ago), she wouldn't run / wouldn't be running a food truck today.
  4. If I hadn't stayed up until 2 a.m. (last night), I wouldn't be so tired now.
  5. If Sam weren't (wasn't) so shy, he would have spoken at yesterday's meeting.

Types: 1, 3, 4 = past cause → present result (a) · 2, 5 = present trait → past result (b). Other natural phrasings are fine if both time frames are right (e.g. 1 If I'd learned to drive, I wouldn't need rides from my friends now). Also accept learnt, organised. Wrong: "…I would have been independent now" · "If he had been more organized…" in 2 (grammatical as a third conditional, but loses the "always" meaning: mark with a note).

Dialogue — find the structures

could have criticism: you could have checked the traffic · might have: we might have made it anyway · mixed conditional about now: if we hadn't gotten lost, we wouldn't be sitting in this great little café right now. (Also in the dialogue: pure third conditionals If we hadn't taken the highway, we would've gotten there, If you'd told me…, I would've listened; and a type (b) mixed conditional if I were a bit more assertive, I probably would've insisted.)

Speaking — Chain of Consequences (Set E) · model chain

Start card 1: Monday, 7 a.m.: Ana's alarm didn't go off. → If Ana's alarm had gone off, she wouldn't have missed the bus. → If she hadn't missed the bus, she wouldn't have walked through the park. → If she hadn't walked through the park, she wouldn't have smelled the tacos from a little food cart. → If she hadn't smelled the tacos, she wouldn't have stopped to buy some. → (ending card: …runs a food truck today) → If she hadn't stopped, she wouldn't have started talking to the cook, who was looking for a partner. → If he hadn't been looking for a partner, she might never have learned to make tacos. → If Ana hadn't learned to make tacos, she wouldn't be running her own food truck today!

Check each slip: if-half = had / hadn't + pp; result = would / could / might (not) have + pp; the last link = would (not) + base / be + -ing with now / today. The story can be as silly as the group likes.

Small turning points (open)

Check for: one correct third conditional, one mixed conditional (with now / today / still), one could have / might have. Model: Three years ago a coworker invited me to a salsa class. If she hadn't invited me, I would never have tried dancing. I might have joined a gym instead! If I hadn't gone, I wouldn't have so many new friends now. Don't mark the content, and don't ask for more personal detail.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is written correctly?
A) If I had seen it, I would have told you.
B) If I had seen it, I would of told you.
C) If I would have seen it, I told you.
Why: if + past perfect, then would have + past participle. B is a spelling error ("of" is how 've sounds); C puts would have in the if-clause.
Q2
"I didn't take the job in Berlin. If I ___ it, I ___ living in Germany now."
A) had taken … would be
B) had taken … would have been
C) took … would be
Why: a past cause (had taken) with a present result (would be… now) = mixed conditional. B uses a past result with now; C makes the cause present.
Q3 · new
Your friend didn't tell you about a big sale. You're annoyed.
A) You could have told me!
B) You could told me!
C) You could of told me!
Why: could have + past participle criticizes something that was possible but didn't happen. B is missing have; C is the "of" spelling error.
Errors you will hear most (1.2)

"If I would have known, I would help." · "If I knew (yesterday), I would tell you." · "If she hadn't missed the bus, she wouldn't meet him." (second-conditional result for a past) · "If I had taken the job, I would have been rich now." · "I would of / could of…" (writing) · "If I had know…" (wrong participle) · "What you would have done?" Correct would in the if-clause and the missing have every time; treat mixed-conditional slips gently. They are a stretch at B2.

1.3 · Past Regrets — Wish, If Only, Would Rather & It's Time (C3)

Exercise 1 — The office kitchen (Script 1.3-A)

Also accept: 1 'd brought · 5 bought (meaning OK) · 7 we went to / we talked to (meaning OK if the verb is past simple). Items 2, 5, 6, 7 are "now / future" even though 5 and 7 use a past form: that is the point of the lesson. Wrong: 5 "gets / get" · 7 "we ask."

Exercise 2 — Present wish or past regret?

1 earned (present) · 2 had taken (past regret) · 3 were (present; was is common in speech) · 4 hadn't told (past regret) · 5 could (present) · 6 hadn't missed (past regret) · 7 had brought (past regret: the moment of packing is over) · 8 were / was (present).

Wrong: 1 "would earn" · 5 "can" · 7 "brought" (sounds like a present wish about a general habit) · 8 "is."

Exercise 3 — Rewrite with I wish… / If only…
  1. I wish I had saved more money.
  2. I wish I hadn't eaten so much. (also: I wish I'd eaten less.)
  3. If only we had booked a table.
  4. I wish I hadn't forgotten her birthday. (also: I wish I'd remembered her birthday.)
  5. I wish my roommate would close the door.
  6. I wish it would stop raining.

1–4 must use wish / if only + past perfect; 5–6 use wish + would (annoyance, someone or something else). Also accept 5 I wish my roommate closed the door (a present wish, less annoyed). Contractions are fine. Wrong: "I wish I would have saved…" · 6 "I wish it stopped raining" (grammatical, but it sounds like a general wish, not an annoyed one: mark with a note).

Exercise 4 — would rather or it's time?

1 walk (same subject → base verb) · 2 didn't park (different subject → past simple) · 3 did · 4 went (also: left) · 5 called · 6 talk · 7 to start (no subject → to + base).

Wrong: 1 "to walk" · 2 "don't park / not park" · 3 "does / do" · 4 "go" · 7 "we started" is correct grammar if a learner adds a subject (It's time we started the meeting): accept it.

Exercise 5 — Find the mistake
  1. I wish I slept earlier. / I wish I could sleep earlier. / I wish I went to bed earlier. (no wish + would about yourself)
  2. It's time we left. (or: It's time to leave.)
  3. I'd rather you didn't go.
  4. I wish I had studied.
  5. I'd rather stay home tonight. (or: I'd prefer to stay…)
  6. I hope you pass your driving test! (possible → hope; also I hope you'll pass)
Dialogue — Tom's three wishes

About the past: I wish I hadn't stayed up so late (also If only I'd started it on Monday). A complaint about someone else: I wish my manager wouldn't give me things at the last minute. (The third, I wish I had more free weekends, is a present wish.)

Speaking — Would You Rather? & Wish List Meeting (open)

Listen for: I'd rather + base + than + base (not "than to"); I'd rather you + past simple in the follow-up; the meeting targets (2 present wishes, 1 regret with hadn't / had + pp, It's high time + past, I'd rather they + past, a polite disagreement). Model report (scenario A): "We wish the elevator worked properly. It's high time the owner fixed it. We wish they hadn't moved the trash cans to the back, and we'd rather people didn't do laundry after 10 p.m. So our top three are: fix the elevator, move the trash cans back, and quiet hours after ten."

My wishes & preferences (open)

Check each ticked structure: tense after wish matches the time; would rather + base (same subject) or + past (different subject); it's (high) time + subject + past or to + base. Mark the grammar only. Don't comment on how serious or personal the content is, and never ask for more.

Self-check
Q1
You didn't apply for the scholarship, and now you regret it.
A) I wish I had applied for the scholarship.
B) I wish I applied for the scholarship.
C) I wish I would apply for the scholarship.
Why: a regret about a finished past event = wish + past perfect. B is a present wish; C is wish + would about yourself.
Q2
"It's high time you ___ the dishes."
A) washed
B) wash
C) had washed
Why: it's (high) time + subject + past simple, with a present / future meaning. The past perfect isn't used after it's time.
Q3 · new
Your neighbor plays loud music every night.
A) I'd rather you didn't play your music so loud at night.
B) I'd rather you don't play your music so loud at night.
C) I'd rather you not to play your music so loud at night.
Why: would rather + a different subject + past simple (a present / future meaning). B is very common in casual speech but is not standard; C mixes in to.
Errors you will hear most (1.3)

"I wish I would exercise more." · "I wish you will pass." (for hope) · "I wish I had spoken French." (meaning now) · "I'd rather you don't…" · "It's time we go." · "I'd rather to stay." · "I rather walk." (missing 'd) · "I wish I would have known." Correct the wish / hope mix-up and the now / past mix-up every time (they change the meaning); treat "I'd rather you don't" gently in speech, but correct it in writing.

Module 2 — Verb Patterns & Modality: Answers

Answers to every Module 2 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (Pattern Path, the Mystery of Room 12, Find someone who), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 2.1–2.3Classes C4–C69 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, but hold learners to the form that changes the meaning: stopped to buy vs stopped buying, must have vs can't have, used to vs be used to. Be generous about genuine alternatives: might / may / could have are interchangeable for possibility; can't have and couldn't have are both correct; like / love / hate / start take either form; contractions (must've, I'm, 've) are fine; American I've gotten used to and British I've got used to are both correct. American English is the model; British spellings and words (apologise, practise, flat, cinema, at the weekend) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

2.1 · Gerunds & Infinitives (C4)

Exercise 1 — Kai's new routine (Script 2.1-A)
123456789

Why: 1 decide + to · 2 stop + -ing = quit the habit · 3 try + to = make an effort · 4 purpose · 5 hate takes either form: also accept to get · 6 preposition about · 7 look forward to (preposition) · 8 avoid + -ing · 9 remember + to = a duty. Wrong: 3 "trying being" · 4 "for clearing" · 7 "to meet."

Exercise 2 — -ing or to?

1 cooking · 2 to study · 3 solving (preposition at) · 4 to buy · 5 seeing (to is a preposition here) · 6 apologize (bare infinitive after make) · 7 closing · 8 to pay · 9 helping (committed to + noun works, so to is a preposition) · 10 to explain (adjective + to).

Also accept apologise (British spelling). Wrong: 6 "to apologize" (the most common error: no to after make + person) · 9 "to help" (very common, even among advanced learners: mark it, but explain gently).

Exercise 3 — Which meaning?

1 b (Remember to meet him = a duty) · 2 b (tried to = an attempt that failed) · 3 a (forget seeing = a memory) · 4 b (regret to inform = bad news now, formal) · 5 b (went on to = moved to a new activity) · 6 b (meant to = intended) · 7 a (stopped to get gas = paused in order to).

Exercise 4 — Error Hunt
  1. ✗ I'm looking forward to seeing you on Friday.
  2. ✗ She suggested taking a taxi. (or: She suggested that we take a taxi.)
  3. ✗ We went to the store to buy some bread.
  4. ✓ correct (let + person + base verb)
  5. ✗ Learning a language takes time. (The infinitive is possible but stiff and formal; the gerund is the natural subject.)
  6. ✓ correct (preposition about + -ing)
  7. ✗ They made us wait for an hour.
  8. ✗ I want you to call me tomorrow.
  9. ✓ correct
  10. ✗ She's good at cooking. (The gerund was right; the preposition was wrong.)
  11. ✗ I don't mind working on Saturdays.
  12. ✓ correct (would like + to)

8 wrong, 4 correct. If learners use this as the test in C4, count how many of the 12 each pair got right; most classes score 6–8 before the lesson.

Exercise 5 — Build the sentence

1 Instead of giving up · 2 to borrow a book · 3 interested in volunteering · 4 to see better · 5 to travel next year · 6 without saying a word.

Also accept: 2 and 4 in order to borrow / so as to see (more formal). Wrong: 2 "for borrowing" · 3 "interested to volunteer" (possible in a different meaning, "curious to," but not the target here) · 5 "for traveling."

Dialogue — patterns

Verb patterns: 've stopped going · 've started running · keep meaning · meaning to exercise · put off starting · Try going · remember hating · look forward to running · goal is to feel · need to remember · remember to set · want to come · can't stand getting up · promise to try. Memory: I remember hating it at first. Duty: I need to remember to set my alarm. Note: Try going with a friend = an experiment (try it and see if it helps).

Speaking — Two minutes about me, and Pattern Path

Two minutes about me is open: listen for at least three -ing verbs, three to verbs, one preposition + -ing, one purpose and one meaning-change verb used correctly. Model: I really enjoy cooking, but I can't stand washing the dishes. I've stopped buying lunch at work, because I'm saving up to take a trip. I'm hoping to run a 10K this year, so I go running to clear my head. I'm not very good at getting up early. I remember hating running at school, so this is a big change! Pattern Path: the full referee sheet is in the classroom materials (set C).

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) I'm tired of to wait.
B) I'm tired of waiting.
C) I'm tired for wait.
Why: a verb straight after a preposition (of) always takes -ing. C also has the wrong preposition.
Q2
"On the way home, he stopped ___ a coffee." (He paused in order to get one.)
A) to buy
B) buying
C) for buying
Why: stop to do = pause in order to do something. Stopped buying would mean he quit the habit. For + -ing doesn't express the purpose of an action.
Q3 · new
Which sentence means "I have a memory of doing it"?
A) I remembered to call my mom.
B) I remember calling my mom on her birthday.
C) I remember to call my mom every Sunday.
Why: remember + -ing looks back at something you did. A and C are about a duty (not forgetting to do it).
Errors you will hear most (2.1)

"I'm looking forward to see you." · "I went there for buying…" · "She suggested me to go." · "They made me to wait." · "Let me to help." · "I want that you…" · "I enjoy to cook." · "without say" / "before eat" · "To learn English is difficult." (stiff, not wrong) · "I stopped to smoke" when the learner means they quit. Correct the meaning-change errors every time (they say the opposite of what the learner means); treat look forward to see as a high-priority fix because it is so frequent in emails.

2.2 · Modals of Deduction in the Past (C5)

Exercise 1 — Read the clue
  1. must have lived
  2. can't have graded (or couldn't have graded)
  3. might have gone … might have fallen (may / could have also correct)
  4. must have enjoyed
  5. must have been
  6. can't have left (couldn't have left)
  7. must have studied

Accept might have lived in 1 if the learner explains that excellent English could also come from years of study (good reasoning; the target is must have = strong evidence). In 5, might have been is acceptable if the learner reads it as "maybe she was lucky, maybe she was clever." Wrong: "mustn't have graded" · "must have grade" · "must had enjoyed."

Exercise 2 — How sure are you?
  1. She must have forgotten about the meeting.
  2. He might / may / could have taken the earlier bus.
  3. They can't / couldn't have seen the sign.
  4. She might not / may not have gotten my message. (British: got)
  5. It must have been a mistake.
  6. He can't / couldn't have finished the report in one hour.

In 4, not couldn't have gotten (that is "sure it didn't," too strong for "it's possible"). Explain the difference: might not have = maybe not · can't have = surely not.

Exercise 3 — Correct the error
  1. He can't have heard the alarm. (or couldn't have heard)
  2. You could have told me the meeting was canceled!
  3. She must have been exhausted after that long flight.
  4. They can't have received the invitation, because nobody came.
  5. I should have booked the tickets earlier.
  6. He must have forgotten my number.
  7. It must have rained last night.

Contractions (could've, must've, should've) are correct in informal writing. mustn't = prohibition, never deduction.

Exercise 4 — React (sample answers)
  1. I should have checked the forecast. / I should have taken my umbrella.
  2. You shouldn't have lent him the money. / You should have said no.
  3. I shouldn't have stayed up so late. / I should have gone to bed earlier.

Marking principle

At B2, mark accuracy and range. A sentence is correct when the structure is right (be + past participle in every passive, the right infinitive for the timing, -ing vs past participle in a reduced clause, the same subject in a participle clause, the right pattern after a reporting verb) and the meaning matches the original. Be generous about optional things: that can be used or dropped; have and get are both fine in the causative; will be and is going to be are both fine; in suggest / insist that, accept the base verb (that we go, American), should + base, and the backshifted form (that we went, common in British English). Contractions are fine. American English is the model; British spellings (analysed, apologised, cancelled) are acceptable when a learner uses them. When a learner writes a correct sentence that uses a different structure from the target, accept it and point to the target: "Correct! Can you also say it with Pattern B?"

Exercise 3 — Both ways
  1. A: It is said that the castle is haunted. · B: The castle is said to be haunted.
  2. A: It is believed that the economy is recovering. · B: The economy is believed to be recovering.
  3. A: It was reported that the CEO had left the country. · B: The CEO was reported to have left the country.
  4. A: It is known that she was a brilliant scientist. · B: She is known to have been a brilliant scientist.

Exercise 4 — Now or earlier?

1 to have been built (earlier, and the castle received the action) · 2 to be announced (future/same-time expectation) · 3 to be living (in progress, "at the moment") · 4 to have escaped (earlier) · 5 to be (he is a great cook now) · 6 to have been sold (earlier; "last week").

Q1
"People think the manager left the company last year." Which impersonal passive is correct?

B) The manager is thought to have left the company last year.
C) It is thought the manager to have left the company last year.
Why: the leaving happened before the thinking, so Pattern B needs to have + past participle. A puts the action at the same time. C mixes Pattern A (It…) with Pattern B (…to have left).

B) I had fix my brakes yesterday.

B) should have been sent

Marking principle

At B2, mark grammar, meaning and choice together. With linkers, the key is the grammar after the linker (clause, noun / -ing, or a new sentence); a different linker from the same family with correct grammar (even though for although, owing to for due to, as a result for consequently) is correct. Punctuation of connectors counts in writing, not in speech. With clefts, check that the spotlight is on the right element and that be matches the time. With inversion, the auxiliary must come before the subject; Scarcely for Hardly and Seldom for Rarely are fine. Opinion and discussion tasks are open: mark the moves and the language, never the opinion. American English is the model; British forms (emphasise, apologise, realise, cancelled, practise, full stop, programme, holiday) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, then range. An answer is correct if the meaning is right (in progress vs finished · who did the work · how much of the group) and the structure is right: will be + -ing / will have + p.p., present after by the time, by not until for a deadline; have / get + object + p.p. in that order, get … to / have … (no to); singular agreement after every, each, neither, either, and of only before the / my / them. Be generous about optional things: contractions (I'll have, we'll be, won't have) or full forms; have or get in the causative unless the item asks for one; going to where will is not required; none of + plural with a singular or a plural verb; neither of them are in speech (not in the formal items). American English is the model; British forms (flat, trousers, tyres, neighbour, film, timetable, whilst) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, then range. Exercise 1 — Presentation phrases

Exercise 2 — Name the technique

1 RQ · 2 C · 3 3 · 4 CC · 5 A · 6 C

Exercise 3 — Concede and counter

1 d · 2 e · 3 c · 4 a · 5 b

Punctuation: after a semicolon, the counter starts with lowercase however / that said / nevertheless; after a period, with a capital (4 → a). While it's true that…, takes a comma and no second connector (2 → e).

Exercise 4 — Handling questions

1 B · 2 A · 3 B · 4 C

1: never invent a figure; offering to follow up is professional. 2: checking your understanding buys time and avoids answering the wrong question. 3: concede, then counter. A is combative, C gives up the case. 4: check back politely before moving on.

Speaking — The Pitch Panel (model, topic card 1: bike parking at work)

S1: Good morning. Today we'll cover a small problem with a simple solution. First, the problem: twelve of us cycle to work, and there's nowhere safe to lock a bike. Last month, two bikes were stolen.

S2: Our proposal is a covered bike rack for twenty bikes next to the side entrance. The main advantage is health: research suggests regular cyclists take fewer sick days. Exercise 5 — Dictogloss (Script 8.1-D)

Original text: Hi, it's Dana from work. Listen, I was going to call you yesterday, but I ran out of time. The meeting has been moved to next Tuesday, and they want us to bring a copy of the report. I don't know about you, but I've got to finish my part tonight. Do you want to get together on Monday and check it over? We could meet at the café next to the station at around ten. Let me know what you think. Oh, and don't forget: it's Sam's last day on Friday, so we're going to take him out for lunch. Talk to you soon!

How to mark a reconstruction (it doesn't need to be word for word):

  • Content (6 points): meeting moved to next Tuesday · bring a copy of the report · Dana must finish her part tonight · meet on Monday to check it · café next to the station, around ten · Sam's last day Friday, lunch.
  • Grammar words restored (the lab's target): going to call · has been moved · want us to bring · a copy of the report · at the café · at around ten · take him out. Every missing one is a WF (weak form) label.
  • Acceptable variations: The meeting is moved / was moved / has moved to next Tuesday (meaning OK; praise has been moved) · I have to finish · Can we meet on Monday… · about ten.
  • Not accepted: reductions in the written text (gonna, wanna, dunno) · "next Thursday" (a mishearing of Tuesday: good to discuss) · "take him out to lunch" is fine, but "take out for lunch" (no him) is a WF miss.

Exercise 3 — Natural or sloppy?
  1. ✅ Natural: a casual estimate to a friend.
  2. ❌ Dosage must be exact: Take two tablets every four hours. Do not take more than eight tablets in 24 hours.
  3. ❌ Too casual for a manager: The report is nearly complete. I'll send it by 7 p.m.
  4. ✅ Natural between roommates. (If you need something specific, name it.)
  5. ❌ The landlord needs facts: The kitchen faucet is leaking. There's water under the sink.
  6. ✅ Natural and honest. Pain is hard to measure; the approximate time helps. Then add the precise facts you know (where exactly, how bad, since when).
  7. ❌ Sounds unprofessional in an interview: I've worked in retail for five years. (for about five years is acceptable; the problem is like… or something.)
  8. ✅ Natural: a polite hedge, then a clear, specific request.

Self-check
Q1
Which phrase best concedes a point and then counters it?
A) "While I accept that it's expensive, I still believe it's worth it."
B) "No, you're completely wrong about that."
C) "What's more, it would also save time."
Why: A grants a point (it's expensive) and then turns (I still believe…). B only contradicts; C adds a point, it doesn't concede.
Q2
A balanced argument should end with…
A) a reasoned conclusion that weighs both sides and takes a position.
B) no opinion at all, to stay perfectly neutral.
C) a repeat of only the arguments in favor.
Why: "balanced" means you weighed both sides fairly, not that you have no conclusion. In a debate this is the summary speaker's job ("Weighing it all up…").
Q3 · new
In a formal debate, what does the rebuttal speaker mainly do?
A) introduces the motion and explains the rules
B) answers the other team's arguments, conceding what is fair and countering it
C) counts the votes at the end
Why: A is the chair's job; C is the timekeeper's. The rebuttal must respond to what the other side actually said, which is why listening (C23) matters so much.
Errors you will hear most (8.3)

"I am agree / I'm not agree" · "If we will ban phones, families will talk more" (a real-conditional form where the speaker means a hypothetical: If we banned…, …would…) · "Not only it costs more, but…" (no inversion) · "It is widely believe that…" (missing -d) · "more cheaper," "the most best" · "On the other side,…" for On the other hand · "Actually" meaning "currently" · "discuss about" · "In my opinion, I think…". In the debate itself, the biggest non-grammar error is a rebuttal that ignores the other side and reads a prepared speech. Praise every real response, even an imperfect one.
