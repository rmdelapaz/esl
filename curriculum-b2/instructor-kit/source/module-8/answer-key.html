Module 8 — Fluency & the Final Project: Answers

Answers to every Module 8 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model answers for the open tasks (the dictogloss, "Just right," blunt → balanced, the debate prep sheet), the classroom-materials keys (Decode It, the sort cards, Register Switch), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, then range. In the listening lab (8.1), a dictation answer is correct if every word is there in its full written form. Accept contractions (I've got to, We've) but not reductions (gonna, wanna) in written answers. In 8.2, many gaps have more than one natural answer: accept any expression that does the same job (≈ / 🧺 / 🪶) in the right position. In the open tasks, judge register: is the language right for the listener? In 8.3, a concede-and-counter is correct if it (1) grants something real, (2) turns with but / however / that said / still, and (3) gives a reason. American English is the model; British forms (favour, centre, neighbourhood, film, shop, cycle lanes) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

8.1 · Understanding Natural Speech (C23)

Exercise 1 — Can or can't? (Script 8.1-B)
12345678
can'tcancancan'tcancan't · cancancan't · Can

The clue is the vowel and the stress, not the t: quick /kən/ = can; long, stressed /kænt/ = can't. In 8, the question "Can you speak up?" begins with a weak /kən/ too. If most of the class gets 6 or 8 wrong, re-read them slowly and exaggerate the contrast: the double-item sentences are the hardest.

Exercise 2 — Expand the relaxed form
  1. I'm going to call her tonight.
  2. Do you want to come with us?
  3. I've got to finish this report. (also: I have to finish…)
  4. It's kind of cold in here.
  5. What are you doing this weekend?
  6. Let me see. Give me a second.
  7. I don't know. Ask them.
  8. Did you see the game?

Wrong: 1 "I'm going call her" (the to is the whole point) · 3 "I've got finish" · 5 "What do you doing" (whatcha + -ing = what are you; whatcha + base verb = what do you: "Whatcha want?") · 7 "Ask him" ('em = them).

Exercise 3 — Find the hidden sounds

A. 1 of · 2 to · 3 was · 4 can · 5 and · 6 t · 7 for · 8 them

B. Linking (‿) and silent letters (x):

  1. turn‿it‿off → "tur-ni-toff"
  2. pick‿it‿up → "pi-ki-tup"
  3. an‿apple → "a-napple"
  4. this‿evening → "thi-sevening"
  5. far‿away → "fa-raway" (American English pronounces the r, and it links)
  6. next day
  7. sandwich
  8. most people
  9. last night
  10. friendship

Accept learners who also cross out the d in sandwich and mark the n as "m" (assimilation: "sam-wich"): that is a good, advanced observation.

Exercise 4 — Micro-dictation (Script 8.1-C)
  1. I was going to call you, but I ran out of time. (12)
  2. Do you want a cup of tea? (7)
  3. What are you going to do about it? (8)
  4. I don't know. Let me think about it. (8)
  5. Tell them to turn it off at the end of the day. (11)
  6. We've got to leave at six, so we can't stay long. (11)

Contractions count as one word (don't, We've, can't). Also accept 6 We have got to / We have to (count changes; fine). Most common misses: 1 was, to, of · 2 "Do you want to cup of tea" (wanna here = want a) · 3 are, to · 5 them, to, the, of · 6 "can stay long" (the can't trap).

Exercise 5 — Dictogloss (Script 8.1-D)

Original text: Hi, it's Dana from work. Listen, I was going to call you yesterday, but I ran out of time. The meeting has been moved to next Tuesday, and they want us to bring a copy of the report. I don't know about you, but I've got to finish my part tonight. Do you want to get together on Monday and check it over? We could meet at the café next to the station at around ten. Let me know what you think. Oh, and don't forget: it's Sam's last day on Friday, so we're going to take him out for lunch. Talk to you soon!

How to mark a reconstruction (it doesn't need to be word for word):

  • Content (6 points): meeting moved to next Tuesday · bring a copy of the report · Dana must finish her part tonight · meet on Monday to check it · café next to the station, around ten · Sam's last day Friday, lunch.
  • Grammar words restored (the lab's target): going to call · has been moved · want us to bring · a copy of the report · at the café · at around ten · take him out. Every missing one is a WF (weak form) label.
  • Acceptable variations: The meeting is moved / was moved / has moved to next Tuesday (meaning OK; praise has been moved) · I have to finish · Can we meet on Monday… · about ten.
  • Not accepted: reductions in the written text (gonna, wanna, dunno) · "next Thursday" (a mishearing of Tuesday: good to discuss) · "take him out to lunch" is fine, but "take out for lunch" (no him) is a WF miss.
Dialogue, Decode It, Speaking

Reductions in the Sam & Jo dialogue: Whatcha · D'you · wanna · gotta · kinda · Lemme · gonna · 'n' · ya. Sam's first two lines in full: What are you doing this weekend? Do you want to grab a coffee?

Decode It (classroom materials set C), full forms. Card A: 1 Do you want to grab lunch? · 2 What are you going to do tonight? · 3 I've got to go to the bank. · 4 Let me know if you need a hand. · 5 Did you see the game last night? · 6 It's kind of cold. Can you close the window? Card B: 1 Give me a minute. I'm looking for my keys. · 2 Don't you want to come with us? · 3 I don't know what time it starts. · 4 Tell them I'll be there at six. · 5 We were going to call you, but we ran out of time. · 6 Would you like a cup of coffee? Any natural reply is fine ("Sure!" · "Nothing much, you?" · "No worries!").

Self-check
Q1
You hear "I wz gonna call ya." What is the full, standard sentence?
A) I was going to call you.
B) I want to call you.
C) I will call you.
Why: wz = the weak form of was, gonna = going to, ya = you. B would be wanna; C has no will anywhere.
Q2
A speaker says a quick, unstressed /kən/ before "help." Which is most likely?
A) "I can help" (a weak, unstressed can)
B) "I can't help" (the negative)
C) "I could help" (a reduced could)
Why: quick and unstressed = can. Can't is long and stressed (/kænt/). There's no reduction of could to /kən/.
Q3 · new
You hear "I dunno. Lemme check." What did the speaker say?
A) I don't know. Let me check.
B) I do now. Let me check.
C) I didn't know. Let me check.
Why: dunno = don't know (present), and lemme = let me. B confuses the sound; C changes the tense (that would be "I didn' know").
Errors you will hear most (8.1)

Writing reductions in answers ("I'm gonna…") · "I'm going call you" (the lost to also appears in learners' own speech and writing) · can / can't reversed · missing articles and of in the dictogloss ("bring copy of report," "a cup tea") · hearing next Tuesday as next Thursday · "I've got finish." In speech: learners who now over-reduce in formal contexts ("Gimme the report, please" to a manager). Praise the listening; remind them that full forms are always safe when speaking.

8.2 · Vague Language & Being Approximate (C24)

Exercise 1 — Insert the vague expression

1 about (also around, roughly, approximately) · 2 -ish · 3 thing · 4 kind of (also sort of; a bit and a little are also natural) · 5 of · 6 something · 7 -odd (thirty-odd) · 8 take

Wrong: 1 "fifty or so" in the gap before the number (position!) · 5 "a couple minutes" is common in American speech, but the written target is a couple of · 7 "Thirty-ish" is possible for numbers, but the gap is after a round number with a hyphen: -odd is the target; accept -ish with a note.

Exercise 2 — Soften the over-precise sentence

1 a bit (also a little) · 2 half · 3 sort of · 4 take · 5 In a way · 6 more or less

Wording may vary slightly; the softening effect is what matters. In 5, In some ways is also fine.

Exercise 3 — Natural or sloppy?
  1. ✅ Natural: a casual estimate to a friend.
  2. ❌ Dosage must be exact: Take two tablets every four hours. Do not take more than eight tablets in 24 hours.
  3. ❌ Too casual for a manager: The report is nearly complete. I'll send it by 7 p.m.
  4. ✅ Natural between roommates. (If you need something specific, name it.)
  5. ❌ The landlord needs facts: The kitchen faucet is leaking. There's water under the sink.
  6. ✅ Natural and honest. Pain is hard to measure; the approximate time helps. Then add the precise facts you know (where exactly, how bad, since when).
  7. ❌ Sounds unprofessional in an interview: I've worked in retail for five years. (for about five years is acceptable; the problem is like… or something.)
  8. ✅ Natural: a polite hedge, then a clear, specific request.
Exercise 4 — Just right (model answers)

A. Robotic → natural: The trip takes about half an hour. I picked up a few things at the store. It was twelve bucks or so. There were, I don't know, twenty-ish people in line. Any version with 3+ vague expressions in the right positions is correct. Accept twelve dollars or so, around twenty people.

B. Too vague → clear (details will vary): Put the clean towels in the blue cabinet next to the washing machine. Then fold the tablecloths and put them on the top shelf. Correct if a new coworker could follow it without asking a question: real nouns, a place, an order.

Dialogue, sort cards, Register Switch, Describe It Naturally

Alex & Priya: ≈ about thirty or so, give or take · midnight-ish · more or less · 🧺 a few things · pizza, snacks, that kind of thing · bowling or something · 🪶 kind of crowded · a bit early · sort of tired. Any one of each is correct. (more or less free can also be read as a hedge.)

Sort cards (set D): ✅ natural: 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12 · ❌ sloppy: 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, 11. Model fixes are printed on the cards. Accept any fix that gives the facts the listener needs (a dose and time, a price, a route, a description, a date, what happened / when / where).

Register Switch (set E), model for card A1: Friend: "Sorry I missed your party! I was kind of sick all week, fever and stuff. I'm more or less OK now." Manager: "I'm calling to let you know that I've had a fever since Tuesday night. I have a doctor's appointment on Thursday at 9:30, and I expect to be back at work on Monday, October 13. I'll bring a doctor's note." Correct if the friend version has 2+ vague expressions and the manager version has every fact with exact times and dates.

Describe It Naturally (open): 60–90 seconds with at least one ≈, one 🧺 and one 🪶, and still clear. Model: My neighborhood's pretty quiet. There are maybe forty apartments in my building, give or take. There's a little park about five minutes away, with a playground, benches, that kind of thing. The buses are a bit slow, to be honest, but there's a café that opens at seven-ish, so mornings are nice.

Self-check
Q1
A friend asks how many people came. Which reply sounds the most natural in casual speech?
A) Precisely thirty-one people attended.
B) About thirty or so, give or take.
C) Some, I don't know, whatever.
Why: B is relaxed but still useful. A is robotic and too formal for a friend. C is so vague it sounds rude or uninterested.
Q2
In which situation should you avoid vague language and be precise?
A) Writing dosage instructions on medicine
B) Telling a friend about your weekend
C) Gently disagreeing with someone
Why: dosages are safety information: vagueness could hurt someone. B and C are exactly where vague language and hedges belong.
Q3 · new
Which sentence uses vague language in a sloppy way?
A) Pass me the thingy, the remote.
B) I put the stuff with the thingy near the whatsit.
C) We talked about work and that kind of thing.
Why: B stacks three placeholders, so the listener learns nothing. In A, the placeholder is immediately explained; C uses one general phrase at the end of a list, which is natural.
Errors you will hear most (8.2)

"many stuffs" / "a lot of stuffs" · "How are you?" "More or less." (meaning "so-so") · "or so" before the number ("or so twenty people") · "thirty-odd" misunderstood as "strange" · "a couple minutes" in writing · stacked hedges that hide the message ("It's kind of sort of a bit maybe difficult") · vague language in the precise role-plays ("I'll be back Monday-ish" to a manager) · "like" as a filler in every sentence. Correct the first two every time; treat the others as register choices and discuss them.

8.3 · Review Project — A Structured Debate (C25)

Exercise 1 — The model debate (Script 8.3-A)
  1. Public health: car emissions damage our lungs, so car-free centers would have much cleaner air. (Also: the space could become parks and bike lanes.)
  2. Older people, people with disabilities, shift workers, and small shops that rely on deliveries.
  3. She concedes that access matters, then counters that most plans don't ban everyone: exemptions can be arranged for people who need them, and commuters are encouraged onto public transportation.
  4. Whether public transportation is good enough yet (in many cities, he says, it isn't).
  5. The transition must be gradual and fair (a conditional, hedged conclusion: "It's sort of a trade-off, but a worthwhile one.").

Moves: all seven are heard. The strongest argument in favor is… (Maya) · It is widely accepted that… (Maya) · Admittedly… (Tom) · That said… (Tom) · That's a fair point… However… (Maya) · Even so, we might ask whether… (Tom) · On balance, I'd argue… (Maya).

Exercise 2 — Mixed B2 review

1 were · 2 believed (also accepted, thought, known, agreed) · 3 far · 4 checked · 5 matters · 6 does

1 was is common in informal speech (accept in speaking, mark for writing). 3 by far is the fixed phrase; by much is wrong. 6 Not only does it cost: the auxiliary comes before the subject (inversion).

Exercise 3 — Correct the mistake

1 been done · 2 have stolen · 3 hearing · 4 had · 5 about (also around, roughly, almost) · 6 cheaper

5 is the Module 8 item: "a bit exactly" mixes a hedge with a precise word. Accept exactly two hours too (the learner chose precision) with a note that the target was an approximator.

Exercise 4 — Concede and counter: match

debac

Clues: 3 ends with a comma after While I accept that…, so it needs the main clause I still believe… (b). 4 That may be so for experienced staff pairs with new employees (a). 5 Even if meetings are easier in person needs most of them can be replaced… (c). 1 and 2 are full sentences, so they take the sentence-starting counters That said… (d) and However… (e).

Exercise 5 — From blunt to balanced (model answers)
  1. Admittedly, phones can be distracting at dinner, but they can also bring people together, for example, a video call with a grandparent.
  2. While I accept that a four-day week sounds attractive, I still believe the cost to small businesses would be too high.
  3. That may be true for some children. That said, I think a little homework helps kids build good habits.
  4. It's true that cars cause pollution. However, many people, like shift workers, genuinely need them to get to work.

Correct if it concedes something real, turns with but / however / that said / still, and gives a reason. Remove "stupid" and "you're wrong": attack ideas, not people.

Debate prep sheet, rubric self-check and final journal (open)

Prep sheet: check that each argument has a reason and an example or consequence, that the team has predicted at least 2 opposing arguments with a concede-and-counter for each, and that notes are key words, not sentences. Model row (Motion 2, proposition): argument: productivity · reason: rested staff work better · example: a trial at a local company, sick days down · who: opening speaker. Model prediction: they'll say: customers need us 5 days → concede: true for some roles → counter: staggered days off; the office stays open.

Rubric self-check: no right answers; compare the learner's self-assessment with your checklist notes and discuss big differences kindly at C26. Final journal: model three sentences: For: If workplaces introduced a four-day week, staff would be far less tired. Against: Admittedly, it would suit many offices, but a hospital couldn't run that way. Conclusion: On balance, I'd argue it should be an option rather than a rule.

Self-check
Q1
Which phrase best concedes a point and then counters it?
A) "While I accept that it's expensive, I still believe it's worth it."
B) "No, you're completely wrong about that."
C) "What's more, it would also save time."
Why: A grants a point (it's expensive) and then turns (I still believe…). B only contradicts; C adds a point, it doesn't concede.
Q2
A balanced argument should end with…
A) a reasoned conclusion that weighs both sides and takes a position.
B) no opinion at all, to stay perfectly neutral.
C) a repeat of only the arguments in favor.
Why: "balanced" means you weighed both sides fairly, not that you have no conclusion. In a debate this is the summary speaker's job ("Weighing it all up…").
Q3 · new
In a formal debate, what does the rebuttal speaker mainly do?
A) introduces the motion and explains the rules
B) answers the other team's arguments, conceding what is fair and countering it
C) counts the votes at the end
Why: A is the chair's job; C is the timekeeper's. The rebuttal must respond to what the other side actually said, which is why listening (C23) matters so much.
Errors you will hear most (8.3)

"I am agree / I'm not agree" · "If we will ban phones, families will talk more" (a real-conditional form where the speaker means a hypothetical: If we banned…, …would…) · "Not only it costs more, but…" (no inversion) · "It is widely believe that…" (missing -d) · "more cheaper," "the most best" · "On the other side,…" for On the other hand · "Actually" meaning "currently" · "discuss about" · "In my opinion, I think…". In the debate itself, the biggest non-grammar error is a rebuttal that ignores the other side and reads a prepared speech. Praise every real response, even an imperfect one.
