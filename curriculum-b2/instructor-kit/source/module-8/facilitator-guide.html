Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 8 — Fluency & the Final Project

The last three classes of the course. First, a listening lab on natural, connected speech: weak forms and the schwa, can vs can't, linking, elision and assimilation, and casual reductions (gonna, wanna, gotta, kinda, d'you, whatcha), practiced through micro-dictation and a dictogloss. Next comes vague language: approximating (about, around, -ish, or so, give or take), general nouns and placeholders (stuff, and that kind of thing, or something) and hedges (sort of, a bit, in a way), and knowing when vagueness is natural and when it is sloppy. Last, the B2 review project: a structured debate with a motion, teams on assigned sides, a chair, a timekeeper, rebuttals and questions from the floor.

3 lessons Lessons 8.1–8.3 Classes C23–C25 Weeks 12–13 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C23 and C24 follow the usual shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C23 is a listening lab, so its "controlled practice" is mostly listening and its freer stage is a group reconstruction task. C25 is a task-based class: pre-task → planning → rehearsal → task (two debates) → feedback.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-8-debate.html. Print materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html before C23. It holds the flashcards (reductions, weak forms, vague expressions, debate moves), the listening-lab scripts (each with a natural-speed and a slow version), the Decode It A/B cards, the "Appropriate or sloppy?" sort cards, the Register Switch role cards, the Roughly Speaking board game, the new identity cards, the debate kit (motion cards, side cards, role cards, the chair's script, timekeeper cards, floor-question and voting slips), the debate rubric and the teacher observation checklist.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each has 🔊 Listen buttons and an audio control that can slow speech down, which is exactly what learners need after C23.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. Learners met weak forms informally in every earlier pronunciation note, hedging in 7.1 (register) and 7.3 (persuasion), and conceding and countering in 7.3. Many already say about and stuff. The new B2 work is (1) naming the connected-speech processes so learners can decode fast speech on purpose, (2) using vagueness deliberately and knowing when it becomes sloppy, and (3) sustaining a formal, rule-bound exchange where you must answer what the other side actually said.
  • American English throughout. The online lessons and this kit both use American English and American sounds: recognize, favor, center, neighborhood, color, practice, memorize, movie, store, bucks / dollars, veggies, bike lanes, and the phonemic table gives the American strong forms (of /ʌv/, was /wʌz/, are /ɑr/). Accept British forms and pronunciations when learners use them (favour, centre, colour, film, shop, quid, veg, cycle lanes; /ɒv/, /wɒz/, /ɑː/).
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Hear it → loosen it → argue it. In C23 learners stop waiting for clear, separate words and learn how fast English really arrives: "I wz gonna callya" = I was going to call you. In C24 they learn that fluent speakers are often deliberately imprecise (about thirty or so, give or take) and that the skill is judging when to be vague and when to be exact. In C25 they put the whole level into one formal event: a debate in which they must listen to the other team at natural speed, concede what is fair, counter it, and switch between a formal backbone (the passive, conditionals, comparison, inversion) and natural spoken touches (hedges, vague language). Every class ends the same way: understanding and responding to real, fast, spoken English.

Fluency before accuracy — feedback on accuracy and range

During freer stages, do not interrupt. Walk around with a notepad divided into three columns: Fix (errors that matter), Upgrade (correct but plain sentences a B2 speaker could lift), and Wow (great language to praise). Correct after the task, on the board, without names. At B2, feedback is not only about mistakes: every feedback stage in this module boards two errors to fix and one or two sentences to upgrade, e.g. "Cars are bad for health." → "It's widely accepted that car fumes damage our health." Correct on the spot only when the error blocks meaning or is the target of a drill.

Dominant speakers are the biggest risk in C24's board game and in C25's debate. Use structure, not criticism: (1) the timekeeper is real: when the card says STOP, the speaker stops mid-sentence (make it a joke, but keep it); (2) give a dominant learner the chair or the rebuttal role (they must respond to others, not deliver a speech); (3) floor questions use one slip per person: when your slip is used, you're done; (4) a quiet word before class: "You're a strong speaker. Today, can you help your team by making sure everyone gets their turn?" Quieter learners get the opening speech (fully prepared from notes) or the summary (short, planned), and 30 seconds of thinking time before any spontaneous turn.

Trauma-informed: assigned sides and safe motions

A debate asks people to argue in public, and for some learners disagreement with a group, a raised voice, or "taking a side" carries real risk from past experience. Three rules protect everyone. (1) Sides are assigned by drawing a card, never chosen. Say it every time: "You argue the side you're given. It says nothing about what you believe. Lawyers and debaters do this every day." Nobody explains their own view, ever. (2) Motions stay in everyday, community and workplace territory: phones at dinner, a four-day week, homework for children, a car-free main street on weekends. The online lesson's examples include "University should be free": in class, don't use it or any motion about politics, religion, immigration, policing, or national identity. (3) The vote is secret and it is about the debate: "Which team made the stronger case?", never "What do you think?" In C24, personal description tasks (your week, your neighborhood) always allow a new identity card or an invented answer, and anyone can say "Pass."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: stress-timing. In English the stressed syllables come at roughly regular beats, and everything between them is squeezed to fit. Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives) carry the beat; grammar words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) are squeezed.

"I was GOing to CALL you." How many beats? (Two, on go- and call.) Which words are squeezed? (I, was, -ing to, you.) Are they gone? (No, still there, just short and quiet.) Is this lazy English? (No, everyone speaks like this, newsreaders too.)

Meaning: weak forms and the schwa. Most grammar words have a strong form (alone or stressed) and a weak form (most of the time). In the weak form the vowel becomes the schwa /ə/, the most common sound in English.

"It's for you." Do you hear "four"? (No, /fər/.) · When do we say the strong "FOR"? (When it's stressed: "Who's it FOR?", or at the end of a sentence.) · "a cup of tea" → "a cuppa tea": which word disappeared? (of.) Is it still in the meaning? (Yes.)
can / can't: "I can go." Is can long or short? (Short, /kən/.) · "I CAN'T go." Long or short? (Long, stressed, /kænt/, and the t is often almost silent.) · So how do you know which one you heard? (The vowel and the stress: short "kn" = can; long "kænt" = can't. Don't wait for the t.)

Meaning: linking, elision, assimilation.

"turn it off" → "tur-ni-toff." Where did the n go? (To the start of the next word.) · "go on" → "go-won." Is there a new word "won"? (No, a small /w/ glide.) · "next day": do you hear the t? (No.) Is it spelled? (Yes.) Do we write "nex day"? (Never.) · "Don't you know?" → "doncha": is that a new word? (No, /t/ + /j/ blend into "ch.")

Meaning: reductions. Some chunks shrink so often that they have informal spellings. They show the sound, not a new word.

"I'm gonna call her." Full form? (I'm going to call her.) · Can I write "gonna" in a work email? (No.) In a text to a friend? (Some people do; in class, practice the full form.) · "I'm going to the store." Can this be "gonna"? (No! Only going to + verb becomes "gonna.") · "I gotta go." Full form? (I've got to go / I have to go.)

Pronunciation (American English). The flap t: between vowels, American t sounds like a quick soft d: water "wadder," got a "godda," what about "whadabout," let it "ledit." Many learners have never been told this, and it explains a lot of "missing" words. Final t before a consonant is often a glottal stop: can't go [kæʔ goʊ], not now [nɑʔ naʊ]. Production goal: learners don't need to produce reductions. Clear full forms are always fine. What helps their own speech is the rhythm: stress the content words, and let the grammar words be light.

Drill script
  1. Clap the beat: T claps two beats and says "CALL you LAter." Class repeats. Add small words without adding beats: "I'll CALL you LAter." → "I was GOing to CALL you LAter." The claps don't slow down, so the small words must squeeze. Choral, then rows, then individuals.
  2. Strong or weak? T: "Who's it for?" (strong, end of sentence) → "It's for you." (weak) · T: "Can you swim? Yes, I can." (strong) → "I can swim." (weak). Learners repeat both and say which is strong.
  3. can / can't hands: T says a sentence naturally; learners show a thumbs-up for can, thumbs-down for can't. Then pairs: A says one, B shows the thumb.
  4. Backchaining for linking: T: "off" → S: "off" · T: "toff" → S: "toff" · T: "ni-toff" → S: "ni-toff" · T: "turn it off" (linked) → S: "turn it off." Repeat with pick it up, this evening, far away.
  5. Reduce / expand ping-pong: T: "going to" → S: "gonna" · T: "wanna" → S: "want to" · T: "let me" → S: "lemme" · T: "dunno" → S: "don't know." Fast, choral, then individuals.
The dictogloss — how to run it
  • Natural means natural. Read Script 8.1-D at your normal speaking speed, both times. The temptation is to slow down "to help." Don't: the slow version comes at the end, as the check.
  • Content words only on the second listening. Learners who try to write everything fall behind and panic. Model it: write "Dana … call … yesterday … ran out … time" on the board.
  • Rebuild, don't transcribe. Groups write a correct, complete text with the same meaning. It doesn't have to be word for word, but every grammar word must be there. Circulate and ask: "What's missing between going and call?"
  • One writer per group, and the writer is not the strongest learner. The strong learner is the "checker": they may only ask questions ("Are you sure it's want us bring?"). This keeps the group talking and stops one person doing it all.
  • The check: read the slow version while groups compare, then show the text (project the Script 8.1-D page from the classroom materials, or write it on the board). Groups underline their misses and label each: WF (weak form), L (linking), E (elision), R (reduction). "Which label did you write most?"

Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on three things only: can / can't (thumbs), gonna / wanna / gotta (flashcards with the full form on the back), and "listen for the big words." In the micro-dictation, give a gapped version with the content words already printed ("I ___ ___ ___ call ___, but I ran ___ ___ time.") so learners listen only for the small words. In the dictogloss, be the note-taker's partner: learners say the words they heard and you (or a partner) write them. Target: Ex 1 with 6/8 correct and 4 reductions expanded aloud.

Stretch

Explore the American flap t and the glottal stop (above) and find three examples in the dictogloss. Notice when a reduction is impossible: "I want her to come" can never be "I wanna her come"; "I'm going to the store" never becomes "gonna the store." Toward C1: listen to 60 seconds of an unscripted podcast and transcribe it, then compare with the auto-captions and find where the captions are wrong. Early finishers write the Sam & Jo dialogue as a formal message (no reductions, full sentences).

L1 watch-outs
  • Syllable-timed languages (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Turkish, Tagalog, and many West African languages) give every syllable similar length, so learners expect to hear to, of, was as full words and "lose" the sentence when they don't. Clapping the beats is the fix. Japanese (mora-timed) speakers have the same issue.
  • Adding vowels to clusters: Spanish and Portuguese speakers ("es-tudent"), Arabic speakers ("nex-i-t day"), Japanese and Korean speakers ("nexu-to"). When the English speaker drops a sound (nex(t) day), these learners hear something very different from what they expect. Show both on the board: what they'd say vs what they hear.
  • Dropped final consonants: Vietnamese, Cantonese, Mandarin and Thai speakers often release final consonants weakly, and so miss the consonant that links to the next vowel (pick‿it‿up → they hear "pi… i… up"). Train them to listen for the consonant at the start of the next word.
  • French speakers have an advantage: English linking is like French liaison. Tell them so; it builds confidence.
  • Missing articles in the dictogloss (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean): "meeting has been moved to next Tuesday," "bring copy of report." Their L1 has no articles, and the English weak form /ə/ is almost silent, so they neither hear nor expect them. Board the missing the / a in feedback.
  • can / can't: learners from every background wait for the final t, which in American English is often silent. Teach the vowel: /ə/ vs /æ/.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: approximating.

"There were thirty-odd people." Were the people strange? (No! -odd = a bit more than thirty.) · Exactly thirty? (No, 31, 33, 35…) · "Let's meet at seven-ish." Can I arrive at 7:10? (Yes.) At 8:30? (No, too late.) · "Fifty people, give or take." Could it be 47? (Yes.) Could it be 90? (No.) · "It's more or less finished." Is it 100% finished? (No, almost.)

Meaning: general nouns and placeholders.

"We need milk, bread, and that kind of thing." Only milk and bread? (No, similar things too: eggs, butter…) · Does the speaker know every item? (Maybe not, and it doesn't matter.) · "Pass me the thingy, the remote." Why "thingy"? (The word didn't come to mind right away.) · "I've got lots of stuffs." Correct? (No: stuff is uncountable, like water.)

Meaning: hedges.

"It's a bit expensive." Is it cheap? (No.) Is the speaker angry? (No, polite, soft.) · "I kind of see it differently." Does she agree? (No.) Why not just say "I disagree"? (Softer; keeps the relationship.) · Can you hedge too much? (Yes: "It's sort of kind of a bit maybe difficult" = nobody knows what you mean.)

Meaning: register, the key B2 judgment.

Medicine label: "Take two-ish tablets every four hours or so." OK? (No! Dangerous.) · Email to your manager: "The report's more or less done, I'll send it sevenish." OK? (No: "The report is nearly complete. I'll send it by 7 p.m.") · To a friend: "I'll be there sevenish." OK? (Perfect.) · At the doctor's: "It hurts sort of here, for about a week." OK? (Yes, honest; then give the facts you do know.)

Pronunciation. Vague expressions are light and quick, like the weak forms from C23: kinda, sorta, a bit, "abou' twenny." The number or noun keeps the stress: "about TWENty," "SEVen-ish," "PIZza or something." A rising, trailing voice on or something… ↗ invites the listener in; a falling voice on the precise version ↘ sounds firm and professional. -ish can even stand alone as an answer: "Are you ready?" "Ish." (very informal, a nice stretch item).

Drill script
  1. Make it vague: T: "There were 31 people." → S: "There were about thirty people." / "thirty-odd" / "thirty or so." T: "We'll meet at 7:00." → S: "We'll meet at seven-ish." T: "It took 58 minutes." → S: "It took about an hour."
  2. Finish the list: T: "We need milk, bread…" → S: "…and that kind of thing." T: "Let's get pizza…" → S: "…or something." T: "Bring a pen, paper…" → S: "…and so on."
  3. Soften it: T (blunt): "This is expensive." → S: "This is a bit expensive." T: "I disagree." → S: "I kind of see it differently." T: "You're wrong." → S: "In a way that's true, but…"
  4. Now be precise (flip): T: "The report's done-ish. Sevenish." (to your manager) → S: "The report is nearly complete. I'll send it by 7 p.m." T: "Take a couple of pills or so." → S: "Take two tablets every six hours."
Roughly Speaking and Register Switch — how to run them
  • Roughly Speaking (board game, classroom materials set F): 22 squares between START and FINISH. Each square names a job (≈ APPROXIMATE, 🧺 GENERAL NOUN, 🪶 HEDGE, 🎯 PRECISE, ⏸ STOP) and a prompt ("How long does it take you to get here?"). Answer for about 30 seconds. The group gives a thumbs-up if you used the right kind of language. First to the finish wins, but the real point is the talk. Everyone may "pass" a personal prompt and take the next square's prompt instead.
  • The PRECISE squares are the twist: "Tell a new coworker how to get out of the building in a fire." A learner who says "go out that way or something" goes back 2 squares. It makes the register rule memorable.
  • Register Switch (A/B cards, set E): the speaker tells the same facts twice. Listener 1 (friend) reacts warmly; listener 2 (manager, doctor, landlord, teacher) asks for exact details ("What time exactly?" "How many?"). Afterward pairs say which facts they made vaguer or more precise, and why.
  • Dominant speakers: in Roughly Speaking, the 30-second rule is timed by the player on the right. In Register Switch the roles are fixed and short, so turns stay even.
ICQs before the games
Roughly Speaking: how long do you talk? (About 30 seconds.) · On a PRECISE square, do you use "about" and "stuff"? (No, exact facts.) · Don't want to answer a personal question? (Say "Pass" and take the next square's prompt.) · Register Switch: how many times do you tell the story? (Twice.) · Who is the first listener? (A friend.) The second? (The person on the card: a manager, a doctor…) · Are the facts different? (No, the same facts, different language.)

Stretch

Vague language in writing registers: in a formal report, approximately, roughly, in the region of, a number of, various, tends to, appears to are "vague" but professional. Rewrite one casual sentence formally ("thirty-odd people turned up" → "approximately thirty people attended"). Toward C1: hedging in argument (It could be argued that…, This may suggest…) is vague language for grown-ups, and it's exactly what C25 needs. Early finishers write the lesson's journal paragraph and a "robotic" version of it.

L1 watch-outs
  • "More or less" as "so-so": Spanish más o menos, Portuguese mais ou menos, Italian più o meno answer "How are you?" In English, "How are you?" "More or less." sounds strange. English more or less = almost / basically ("It's more or less finished"). For "so-so," teach not bad, OK, could be better.
  • "stuffs," "informations," "equipments": many languages count these nouns. Stuff is uncountable: a lot of stuff, never many stuffs.
  • "-odd" and "odd": learners who know odd = strange hear "thirty-odd people" as "thirty strange people." Teach it as a chunk after round numbers.
  • Hedging feels dishonest to some learners from more direct communication styles (German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew, Polish): "Why say 'a bit' if it's very expensive?" Explain that in English the hedge is politeness, and everyone decodes it. Learners from indirect styles (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Filipino) may hedge everything and bury the message. Aim for the middle: one hedge per opinion.
  • "like" as a filler: learners who picked up English from TV and social media may use like in every sentence ("It was, like, like thirty people"). Fine among friends, sloppy in an interview. It goes in Ex 3.

Review Project — A Structured Debate

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • follow a structured debate at natural speed and identify how each speaker presents, concedes and counters
  • build a case for an assigned side of a motion: 2–3 arguments, each with a reason and an example or consequence
  • deliver a timed opening, rebuttal or summary speech from notes, with signposting

Why a team debate in class, but a solo balanced talk online?

The online lesson (8.3) lets self-study learners give a 2–3 minute balanced talk alone, presenting both sides and concluding. In class you have an audience and opponents, so the real B2 challenge becomes possible: listening to an argument at natural speed and answering it on the spot. That's why the rebuttal and the floor questions matter more than the prepared opening. If a learner's opening is short but their rebuttal answers a real point, that is a strong B2 performance. Note it on the checklist.

Running a debate — the chair's order and timing (about 16–17 minutes)
  1. Chair opens (1 min): welcome, the motion, the teams, the rules (time limits, one question each, respect). Secret vote 1: floor members write P or O on a slip: "Which side do you expect to argue better?" (No personal views.)
  2. Proposer (first proposition speaker, 2 min) → Opposer (first opposition speaker, 2 min).
  3. Huddle (1 min): teams whisper and pass notes; rebuttal speakers choose which points to answer.
  4. Proposition rebuttal (1½ min) → Opposition rebuttal (1½ min): concede → counter → one new point if time.
  5. Floor questions (4 min): the chair takes questions one by one, alternating teams. Any team member may answer, briefly. Each floor member asks one question at most.
  6. Summaries (1 min each): the opposition first, then the proposition (the proposition always opens and closes).
  7. Secret vote 2 + close (1 min): "Which team made the stronger case?" The timekeeper counts. The chair announces the result and which team gained more votes since vote 1, then: "Please thank both teams."
The rubric and the observation checklist → C26

The debate rubric (classroom materials set H, and the self-check in Workbook 8.3) has six criteria: 1 structure & signposting · 2 arguments & support · 3 rebuttal: concede & counter · 4 range of B2 language · 5 accuracy & register · 6 fluency, delivery & interaction. Three levels: ★★★ strong B2 (reaching toward C1) · ★★ B2 · ★ not yet. Floor members fill a peer star and wish for one speaker (assign who listens for whom). Learners self-assess at home. You can use the full rubric for your notes, but don't grade C25: it's practice. Tell learners so.

The observation checklist (set I) has one row per learner and 10 can-do items. Three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help, or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You won't see every learner do everything: listen to each learner for their whole speech and at least one floor question or answer. After class, use it to:

  • Plan each learner's C26 assessment. The B2 final assessment is an individual speaking interview (about 8–10 minutes) plus short listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's B2 final assessment. Before C26, read it and match your checklist notes to its speaking criteria: which topics suit each learner, and where to start so that each learner begins with something they did well.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become a short review before the assessment starts.
  • Give each learner one sentence of feedback at the end of C25 or the start of C26: one real strength from the debate, named specifically ("Your concession to Omar's point was excellent").
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who froze or stopped early gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is one-to-one and friendly. No team, no timer on the wall. You'll be fine."

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am agree" / "I am not agree" (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, French je suis d'accord, Portuguese estou de acordo): still common at B2 under pressure. I agree / I don't agree. Board it after the debate, not during.
  • False friends in argument: actually ≠ "currently" (Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, German aktuell); eventually ≠ "possibly" (Spanish eventualmente, French éventuellement); sensible ≠ "sensitive." A speaker who says "Actually, 40% of people work from home" means "currently."
  • "On the other side" for on the other hand (Spanish por otro lado, Portuguese por outro lado), and "In my opinion, I think…" (double opinion markers, many L1s). Accept in fluency, fix in feedback.
  • Very direct disagreement ("This is not true," German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew, Polish) or none at all (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Vietnamese and Filipino learners may find public disagreement very uncomfortable). The assigned side helps both groups: it's a role, not a relationship. Teach both the English middle: concede, then counter.
  • Written-style speeches: learners from exam systems that reward memorized speeches may write and read the whole opening. Allow a full script at home for confidence; in class, the prep sheet has key words only. Collect scripts at the start of the debate if needed, kindly.
  • "Discuss about" (Spanish discutir sobre, Arabic, Hindi and others) and "explain me" (Romance languages): discuss something · explain it to me.

Recap on slide 18: "I wz gonna callya" → I was going to call you · about thirty, give or take (to a friend) → exactly 31 (to the fire marshal) · Admittedly…, but… → on balance. The C26 final assessment covers the whole B2 course. From this module the most relevant skills are: understanding natural, connected speech; using vague language appropriately and switching to precision; and presenting, conceding and countering in an extended spoken turn. Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, the B2 final assessment: an individual speaking interview (about 8–10 minutes) plus short listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's B2 final assessment. Read it before C26 and tell learners how the class will be organized (the order of the tasks and when each learner will be interviewed), so there are no surprises. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next step, the C1 course, Advanced English.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You'll talk with me one-to-one for about ten minutes, and do some short listening, reading and writing. It's everything we've practiced. Then we celebrate!"

Reduce anxiety: show B2 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you have argued a side you didn't choose, for two minutes, in front of everyone, and answered questions?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that the recording of their balanced talk is the best practice for an extended turn.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite 2–3 volunteers to give a 1-minute "closing speech" using the summary language from the debate ("Weighing it all up, this course has been…").

Teacher reflection (after C25)

Look at your checklist and answer three questions: Which two can-dos need a C26 warm-up? Which learners need a quiet word of encouragement, and which strength will you name for each? Which learners are clearly ready for C1, and which would benefit from more B2 practice first? Write your answers on the back of the checklist.
