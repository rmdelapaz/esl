Module 4 — Fluency & Style: Answers

Answers to every Module 4 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (linker sort, Plain → Dramatic cards, Set the Record Straight), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 4.1–4.3Classes C10–C129 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B2, mark grammar, meaning and choice together. With linkers, the key is the grammar after the linker (clause, noun / -ing, or a new sentence); a different linker from the same family with correct grammar (even though for although, owing to for due to, as a result for consequently) is correct. Punctuation of connectors counts in writing, not in speech. With clefts, check that the spotlight is on the right element and that be matches the time. With inversion, the auxiliary must come before the subject; Scarcely for Hardly and Seldom for Rarely are fine. Opinion and discussion tasks are open: mark the moves and the language, never the opinion. American English is the model; British forms (emphasise, apologise, realise, cancelled, practise, full stop, programme, holiday) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

4.1 · Linking Ideas — The Argument Builder (C10)

Exercise 1 — City or countryside? (Script 4.1-B)

a) Neither: the speaker thinks the right choice depends on your stage of life. Accept "It depends."

Accept sensible same-grammar alternatives if a learner mishears (as for 2, while for 5, because of for 7, Although for 9). Don't accept because of for 1 or although for 6: the grammar that follows doesn't fit. Learners sometimes label 5 as Ca; it compares two places, so it's contrast.

Exercise 2 — Circle the linker that fits the grammar

1 Despite (+ noun) · 2 Although (+ clause) · 3 because of (+ noun) · 4 because (+ clause) · 5 However (new sentence after a period) · 6 whereas (+ clause, comparing two people) · 7 due to (+ noun) · 8 so as to (+ base verb)

In 5, the first idea ends with a period, so the second sentence needs a connector: However,. Although would have to join both ideas inside one sentence (Although the rent is lower here, the commute is much longer.). In 8, "so that to" does not exist; so that + clause is possible ("so that she could improve…").

Exercise 3 — Join the ideas
  1. Although the office is modern, nobody likes working there. (or: Nobody likes working there, although the office is modern.)
  2. In spite of being nervous, he gave an excellent presentation. (or: In spite of his nerves / In spite of the fact that he was nervous…)
  3. The flight was canceled due to a technical fault. (noun: not "due to there was…")
  4. Online shopping is quick, whereas shopping in person is more social. (or: Whereas online shopping is quick, shopping in person…)
  5. The bakery raised its prices. Consequently, it lost customers. (or: …its prices; consequently, it lost customers.)
  6. Despite the fact that it was raining, the concert went ahead.

Wrong: "In spite of he was nervous" · "due to there was a fault" · "The bakery raised its prices, consequently it lost customers." (comma splice: accept in speech, correct in writing). British cancelled is fine.

Exercise 4 — so or such (a / an)?

1 so (adjective) · 2 such a (a + adjective + noun) · 3 such (plural noun, no article) · 4 so (adverb) · 5 such an (an before the vowel sound of awful)

Exercise 5 — Find and fix
  1. Although it was expensive, we booked the hotel. (or: Despite the price / Despite the fact that it was expensive…)
  2. The road was closed because there was an accident. (or: because of an accident)
  3. Although the job was hard, she loved it. (or: The job was hard. However, she loved it.)
  4. Although the prices are low, the quality is poor. (delete but; or: The prices are low, but the quality is poor.)
  5. It was such a good book that I read it in one night. (or: The book was so good that…)
Speaking — The Argument Builder planner (open)

Look for: a hook, one point per side with a cause linker, a contrast linker at the pivot, a result linker (so / so…that / such…that / therefore) and a conclusion that stays balanced. Model (working from home): Hook: Most of us have tried it by now. · Side 1: no commute → time and money, because you don't travel. · Pivot: However, … · Side 2: it can be so isolating that motivation drops; meetings online → misunderstandings. · Conclusion: Therefore, a mix of home and office days is probably the best of both.

Common mis-sorts: despite under "+ clause" (the key error of the lesson), however under "+ clause," because of under "+ clause." so, so…that, such…that and so as to / in order to are not in the sort; they have their own flashcards (Set A).

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) Despite the train was delayed, we caught it.
B) Despite the delay, we caught the train.
C) Although the delay, we caught the train.
Why: despite + noun. A puts a clause after despite (fix: Although the train was delayed or Despite the fact that…); C puts a bare noun after although.
Q2
"It was ___ interesting book ___ I finished it in one night."
A) so … that
B) such an … so
C) such an … that
Why: a noun phrase follows (an interesting book), so such an… that. A would need "so interesting that"; B uses the wrong second word.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
A) The shop was losing money; therefore, it closed.
B) The shop was losing money, therefore it closed.
C) The shop was losing money therefore, it closed.
Why: therefore is a sentence connector: a semicolon (or period) before it and a comma after it. B is a comma splice; C has no break before therefore.
Errors you will hear most (4.1)

"Despite it was…" · "Despite of…" · "because of the rent is high" · "However it was late, we stayed." · "Although…, but…" / "Because…, so…" · "so a good day" / "such good" / "such a good people" · "…, however, …" comma splices in writing · "In spite being tired" (missing of) · "Therefore" in a relaxed chat (a register slip, not an error: offer so). In the Argument Builder, note errors for the feedback stage only; never stop a speaker.

4.2 · Adding Emphasis — Make It Emphatic (C11)

Exercise 1 — The team lunch (Script 4.2-A)

1 Tomás (not the speaker: "It was Tomás who organized it.") · 2 on Monday (not Friday: "It was on Monday that I booked it.") · 3 the service ("What impressed me most was the service.") · 4 the price ("The thing that surprised me was the price.")

5 Never have I seen waiters so calm with twenty people. · Not only was it cheaper than we expected, but they also gave us free dessert. · No sooner had I sat down than I wanted to book it again.

Follow-up: "What did the confirmation email change?" (It came on Friday, which is why A thought B booked late: "It was the confirmation that came on Friday.")

Exercise 2 — Make an it-cleft
  1. It was my neighbor who (that) fixed my bike.
  2. It was in Chicago that we first met.
  3. It was the roof that the storm damaged.
  4. It was in 2019 that they moved.
  5. It was the manager's email that caused all the confusion.

Accept which for things in 3 and 5 (less common). Don't accept "It was in Chicago where we first met" in writing (accept in speech). It is for past events is wrong: "It is my neighbor who fixed…" → It was.

Exercise 3 — Build up to the key idea
  1. What I admire is her honesty. (or: What I admire about her is her honesty.)
  2. What frustrates me is the traffic.
  3. What he did was (to) simply apologize. (also: What he simply did was apologize.)
  4. The thing that worries me is the cost.
  5. The reason (why) I called is to say thank you. (or: The reason I called was to say thank you.)
Exercise 4 — Make it dramatic
  1. Never have I tasted such good coffee.
  2. Not only did she call, but she also came in person.
  3. No sooner had we sat down than the alarm went off. (than, not when; past perfect for the first action)
  4. Rarely does he ask for help. (no auxiliary → does; base verb ask)
  5. Only later did they realize the danger.

In 2, accept "Not only did she call; she also came in person." In 3, accept "Hardly had we sat down when…" only if the learner changed the trigger on purpose.

Exercise 5 — Find and fix
  1. Never have I worked on such a busy project. (auxiliary before subject)
  2. No sooner had we arrived than it started to rain.
  3. What I need is a long rest. (no extra it)
  4. Rarely does she complain about her job.
  5. It was Tomás who organized the party last Friday. (past event → was)
Speaking — Make It Emphatic planner (open)

Look for: a frame to open, an it-cleft that corrects a common view, a what-cleft build-up, and at most one inversion. Model (public transportation): The thing that really bothers me about buses in this town is the timetable. People think it's the price that keeps them away, but it's the waiting that does it. What we really need is a bus every ten minutes. Never have I waited so long for a bus as last winter.

Set the Record Straight (Set E) — model corrections

Pair 1 (the office party): It was Nadia who organized it. · It was on Friday that it happened. · It was the music that was the problem (not the pizza). Pair 2 (the broken washing machine): It was the upstairs neighbor who called the repair person. · It was the dryer that broke, not the washing machine. · It was on Tuesday that it was fixed. Pair 3 (the family trip): It was my brother who planned the trip. · It was by train that we went. · It was the museum that everyone loved. Pair 4 (the new manager): It was Mr. Park who got the job. · It was in March that he started. · It was the schedule that he changed first. Any accurate what-cleft follow-up is a bonus.

Self-check
Q1
Someone thinks you broke the vase, but it was the cat. Which sentence corrects them best?
A) What broke the vase is the cat.
B) The cat broke the vase.
C) It was the cat that broke the vase.
Why: the it-cleft spotlights the real culprit and pushes back on the wrong idea. A is clumsy (and uses is for the past); B is neutral, with no correction.
Q2
Which sentence has the correct word order?
A) Never I have felt so proud.
B) Never have I felt so proud.
C) No sooner I felt proud when it faded.
Why: after Never at the start, the auxiliary comes before the subject. A keeps statement order; C has no inversion and the wrong partner word (than, not when).
Q3 · new
Which what-cleft is correct?
A) What I need is a quiet weekend.
B) What I need it is a quiet weekend.
C) What I need a quiet weekend is.
Why: What + clause + be + key idea. B adds an extra it; C puts the key idea in the wrong place.
Errors you will hear most (4.2)

"Never I have seen…" · "Not only it was cheap, but…" · "Rarely she complains." · "No sooner… when" / "Hardly… than" · "No sooner we had left…" (no inversion) · "What I need it is…" · "It is John who broke it yesterday." (tense of be) · "It was in Chicago where we met." (accept in speech) · a flat cleft with no stress on the spotlight · three inversions in one minute (a style issue: praise the control, then help them choose one).

4.3 · Review Project — Present, Argue & Discuss (C12)

Exercise 1 — What is the phrase doing?

1 S · 2 C · 3 P · 4 H · 5 S · 6 C · 7 H · 8 P

5 is a signpost (it flags the conclusion), even though it also summarizes. 8 is a what-cleft used to persuade: a nice link to 4.2.

Exercise 2 — Should we try a four-day week? (Script 4.3-A)

a) I'd like to talk about 1 · I'll cover three points 2 · Let me start with 3 · Moving on to 4 · Finally 5 · To sum up 6 (workbook order: 4 · 6 · 1 · 5 · 2 · 3)

b) ✓ impersonal passive (It is widely believed that…; also It could be argued that…) · ✓ what-cleft (What most people need is…) · ✓ participle clause (Having spoken to several managers…) · ✓ mixed conditional (If we had tried this a year ago, we might already be seeing the results.) · ✓ negative inversion (Not only would it make people happier, but…) · ✗ used to · ✗ must have

c) Dana: some jobs can't be done in four days (nurses, for example). The speaker concedes ("You're right to an extent. Hospitals would need a different model.") and counters ("Even so, I'd argue that most office jobs could make it work.").

Exercise 3 — Rewrite for range
  1. It is widely believed that online shopping has changed our towns forever. (or: Online shopping is thought to have changed…)
  2. If we had planned ahead, we wouldn't have this problem now.
  3. What concerns me most is the cost.
  4. Having looked at the data, I reached my conclusion.
  5. Not only does the plan save money, but it also saves time.

Other valid versions exist; the target structure must be present and accurate. In 2, "wouldn't have had this problem" is a third conditional (past result), not the mixed conditional asked for: accept with a note.

Exercise 4 — Correct the error
  1. Not only does it save money, but it also saves time. (inversion after Not only)
  2. It could be argued that we acted too late. (passive: past participle)
  3. Although it is popular, the app has many problems. (or: Despite its popularity / Despite being popular…)
  4. We discussed the plan for an hour. (no about)
  5. If we had started earlier, we would have finished. (no would in the if-clause; Module 1)
Exercise 5 — Concede, then counter (models)
  1. "That's a fair point for people with a few items. However, with a full cart, a cashier is often quicker, and there's no one to help when the machine stops."
  2. "I take your point; young children need time to play. Even so, a little homework helps them build good habits."
  3. "You're right to an extent, since most people pay by card. That said, some older people will always prefer cash, so it probably won't disappear completely."

Mark: a real concession (not "Yes, but no") + a counter with a reason. Any side is fine.

Project Parts A and B — the talk and the discussion

Open. Score with the C12 speaking checklist (classroom materials, Set H). Talk planner: look for an opening, a preview, 2–3 points each with a reason and an example, a close with To sum up, and two objections with concede-and-counter answers; notes, not full sentences. Discussion card: 2 points for the assigned side and one ready concession. The model presentation (Script 4.3-A) shows the full shape.

Self-check
Q1
Your partner makes a point that is partly true, but you still disagree. What's the best reply?
A) "No, that's completely wrong."
B) "That's a fair point; however, the bigger picture tells a different story."
C) "Yes, you're totally right. I have nothing to add."
Why: it acknowledges the valid part, then counters. A contradicts flatly and sounds combative; C gives up your position.
Q2
Which sentence uses the B2 grammar correctly?
A) "Not only it reduces costs, but it also improves safety."
B) "It is believe that the plan will work."
C) "Not only does it reduce costs, but it also improves safety."
Why: Not only at the start needs inversion (does it reduce). A has no inversion; B needs the past participle (It is believed).
Q3 · new
You've finished your first point and want to start the second. You say:
A) "Moving on to my second point, …"
B) "To sum up, …"
C) "I'd like to talk about…"
Why: Moving on to… signals a transition. B signals the end; C opens the talk.
Errors you will hear most (4.3)

"I am agree." · "We discussed about…" · "According to me…" · "Resuming, …" (→ To sum up) · "Actually" meaning "currently" · "At the end, …" (→ Finally / In the end) · "It could be argue…" · "Not only it…" · "If we would have…" · "Despite it is…" · persuade phrases in every sentence ("The fact is… The bottom line is… The fact is…") · conceding without countering, or countering without conceding. In C12, note errors for delayed feedback only; never stop a talk or a discussion to correct.
