Module 4 — Fluency & Style

Three classes that finish the first half of B2 and move learners from correct English to effective English. First comes linking ideas: contrast, cause and result, and above all the grammar that follows each linker (although + clause, despite + noun / -ing, however + new sentence). Then emphasis: it-clefts, what-clefts and other cleft frames, and basic negative inversion (Never have I…, Not only did…, No sooner had…than). Last, a task-based review, Present, Argue & Discuss: learners give a short signposted presentation and take part in a small-group discussion with assigned sides, while you score a speaking checklist that feeds Progress Test 1 (C13).

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, discussion) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C12 is task-based: the tasks come first and the language work comes out of them.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-fluency-style.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C10. It holds the flashcards, the linker sort cards, the Argument Builder kit (topic cards, point cards, linker play cards, listener tally), the Plain → Dramatic rewrite cards, the Set the Record Straight A/B cards, the emphatic-opinion prompts, the C12 presentation topics, side cards and persona cards, the C12 speaking checklist, the Module 1–4 Review Race board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. At B1 (Module 3 and 4.3) learners met because, so, although, however, relative clauses and the basic opinion phrases. They know what most linkers mean. The B2 work in 4.1 is the grammar after the linker (clause, noun / -ing, or a new sentence) and the register and punctuation of connectors. Clefts and negative inversion (4.2) are genuinely new for most learners; keep inversion to the triggers the lesson teaches (Never, Not only, No sooner…than, Hardly / Scarcely…when, Rarely / Seldom, Little, Only then / Only after).
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a linker or an inversion. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. At B2, feed back on accuracy and range: one or two "fix it" sentences, and one or two "upgrade it" sentences (a correct but plain sentence that a cleft or a better linker would make stronger). Praise the argument first ("That was really convincing"), then the form.
  • Dominant speakers. Argument tasks reward the loudest voice, so build in turn-taking: talking tokens in the discussions, fixed-order rounds before open rounds, and jobs for strong speakers (the chair who must invite everyone, the listener who keeps the linker tally, the devil's advocate who may speak only after two others). Say it kindly: "Great point, Ivan. Let's hear the other side first."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Don't hand them new C1 grammar. Stretch them with choice and control: vary linkers (no linker twice in one talk), match register to the situation (therefore in the talk, so in the chat), use the rarer triggers the lesson teaches (Seldom, Little did…, Only after…) sparingly and exactly, hedge their claims (arguably, to some extent), summarize the other side fairly before countering, and self-correct in real time.
  • American English throughout. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (emphasize, apologize, realize, practiced, organized, program, canceled, movie, vacation, moved, period). Accept British forms when learners use them (emphasise, programme, cancelled, film, holiday, moved house, full stop).
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Connect it → stress it → use it all. In C10 learners stop producing lists of facts and start building arguments: Although remote work saves time, it can feel isolating, so many teams keep an office day. In C11 they learn to put the spotlight exactly where they want it: It was Maria who booked it, not me. What we really need is more time. Not only did the deadline move, but we also lost two people. In C12 everything from Modules 1–4 goes to work in two real tasks: a short signposted presentation and a small-group discussion in which learners concede, counter and persuade. Every class ends with a speaking task where the argument matters as much as the grammar.

Trauma-informed: argue the card, not your life

Opinion tasks can feel risky for adults who have lived where disagreeing in public was dangerous, and some topics touch raw nerves. The online lesson 4.3 suggests broad topics ("technology & society, the environment, education… the media"). In class, keep every topic everyday: work, the neighborhood, shopping, technology at home, school and learning, health habits, free time. No politics, religion, immigration policy, war or national identity; if a learner raises one, thank them and steer back: "Interesting, but let's keep today's topics local." Every opinion task has assigned sides (the side cards and Argument Builder cards), a devil's advocate role and persona cards, so nobody has to reveal what they really think. Say it every time: "Good speakers can argue any side. Today you argue your card." Anyone can say "Pass" on a personal question, and anyone may present to you alone instead of to a group.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: contrast vs concession. Contrast = two things are different (He likes tea, whereas she prefers coffee.). Concession = "Yes, this is true, but the other thing is true anyway" (Although it was raining, we went out.).

"Although it was raining, we went out." Was it raining? (Yes.) Did we go out? (Yes.) Is that surprising? (A little: we expected them to stay in.) · "Even though I warned him, he did it." Stronger or weaker than although? (Stronger: more surprise.)
"He likes tea, whereas she prefers coffee." Is one fact surprising because of the other? (No, we're just comparing two people.) Can I say "Although he likes tea, she prefers coffee"? (It sounds strange: there's no surprise. whereas / while is better.)

Form: what comes after the linker. This is the heart of the lesson.

"Despite ___, we went out." Can I put "it was raining" there? (No: despite takes a noun or -ing.) What can I put? (the rain · the bad weather · being tired.) And if I really want the full sentence? (Despite the fact that it was raining… or change to Although.) · "Although ___, we went out." "the rain"? (No: although needs subject + verb.)
"It was late. However, we stayed." How many sentences? (Two.) What's after However? (A comma.) Can I say "However it was late, we stayed"? (No, that's although's job.)
"We were late because of the traffic." "…because the traffic was terrible." Same meaning? (Yes.) Same grammar? (No: because of + noun; because + clause.) "The match was canceled due to rain." Formal or informal? (More formal; common in announcements and writing.)
"It was so cold that the lake froze." "It was such a cold day that we stayed in." What comes after so? (An adjective: cold.) After such? (a / an + noun: a cold day.) "They were such kind people." Why no a? (Plural noun.) · "She left early so as to catch the train." Why did she leave early? (To catch the train: purpose.)

Pronunciation. In speech, a sentence connector is its own small chunk with a pause after it: "It was expensive. | However, | we bought it." Stress: alTHOUGH, deSPITE, howEVer, THEREfore, CONsequently, WHEREas. Though at the end of a sentence is light and falling: "We bought it, though ↘." Due to /ˈdu tə/ (American) — accept /ˈdjuː/. In so…that and such…that, the stress goes on so / such and the adjective, and that is weak /ðət/: "It was SO COLD that we stayed in." Teach the "pivot" intonation for the mini-talk: a fall at the end of side 1, a pause, then a fresh, higher start on the contrast linker.

Drill script
  1. Although → despite (T gives a clause, S gives the noun version): T: "Although it was raining…" → S: "Despite the rain…" T: "Although she was tired…" → S: "Despite being tired… / Despite her tiredness…" T: "Although the tickets were expensive…" → S: "Despite the expensive tickets… / Despite the price of the tickets…"
  2. Because → because of / due to: T: "because the traffic was bad" → S: "because of the bad traffic." T: "because it was foggy" → S: "due to the fog." T: "because the rent is high" → S: "because of the high rent."
  3. But → However (two sentences, say the pause): T: "It's cheap, but it's far." → S: "It's cheap. | However, | it's far."
  4. So or such? T: "cold" → S: "so cold that…" T: "a cold day" → S: "such a cold day that…" T: "friendly people" → S: "such friendly people that…" T: "quickly" → S: "so quickly that…"
  5. Chain the argument (open pairs → closed pairs): S1 says a plain fact ("Online shopping is convenient."); S2 adds a contrast ("…whereas shopping in person is more fun."); S3 adds a cause ("…because you can touch things."); S4 adds a result ("…so I still go to the market."). Then all trios, 1 minute.
The Argument Builder — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one kit per group (Set C). Each kit has a topic card (e.g., Working from home is better than working in an office.), 6 point cards (3 FOR, 3 AGAINST, written as short notes, not sentences: "no commute → more free time"), 12 linker play cards and a listener tally.
  • Round 1 — assigned sides. A takes the three FOR cards, B the three AGAINST cards. Each speaker holds 4 linker cards (dealt at random). When a speaker uses a linker with the right grammar after it, they put the card down. C (the listener) says "✔" or "Check the grammar!" and ticks the tally. Most cards played wins a point, but C's verdict ("…convinced me more, because…") is the real prize.
  • Round 2 — the balanced mini-talk. Draw a new topic. Each learner plans for 1 minute with the point cards, then speaks for up to 2 minutes following the shape on slide 8: hook → side 1 → pivot (contrast linker) → side 2 → balanced conclusion (with a result linker). Listeners tally linkers and write one "best sentence" they heard.
  • Assigned sides make it safe. Nobody argues their real opinion in Round 1, and the Round 2 talk is balanced by design. Topics are everyday trade-offs only (work, city and country, phones, shopping, school, transportation).
  • Dominant speakers get the listener role first: they have to stay quiet for two minutes and judge fairly. Quieter learners speak second in Round 1 (they hear a model first).
  • Walk around with a notepad. Write down 2 sentences to fix and 2 to upgrade for the feedback stage. Don't correct during the talks.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with one linker from each column only: although (+ clause), because of (+ noun), However, (new sentence), plus so. Give a sentence frame strip: "Although ___, ___. · ___ because of ___. · ___. However, ___. · ___, so ___." The point cards have an arrow picture ("no commute → free time"), so learners can speak from the picture. In the Argument Builder, the learner is the listener in Round 1 (they hear two models) and gives a one-minute talk in Round 2 with the frame strip. Target: 4 linked sentences with the right grammar.

Stretch

No linker twice in one talk. Use the more formal set (nevertheless, owing to, consequently, whereas) in Round 2 and the informal set (though at the end, that's why, so) in Round 1, and notice the difference in tone. Use despite / in spite of + -ing and + the fact that in the same talk. In Workbook Ex 3, write each answer two ways. For homework, write the Round 2 talk as a 120-word paragraph with correct connector punctuation.

L1 watch-outs
  • "Although…, but…" and "Because…, so…": Chinese (suīrán… dànshì, yīnwèi… suǒyǐ), Vietnamese (tuy… nhưng), Korean, Thai and Indonesian speakers often use both halves of a pair. In English, one linker is enough: Although it was cheap, but I didn't buy it.
  • "Despite of": Spanish a pesar de, Portuguese apesar de and Italian nonostante + the English in spite of mix into "despite of." Say: "in spite of, but despite alone."
  • "Despite + clause": learners whose L1 linker takes a full clause (French bien que, Spanish aunque, Arabic raghma anna) put one after despite. Point at the middle column every time.
  • "However" as a clause linker / comma splice: Spanish sin embargo, Portuguese no entanto and many other L1s allow "…, however, …" joining two clauses with commas. In writing, however needs a period or semicolon before it.
  • "so a good movie" / "such good": Spanish tan, French si / tellement, Portuguese tão and Russian takoy / tak map onto so and such differently. The quick test: a noun follows → such.
  • Long "and" chains: Arabic (wa) and Tagalog (at) storytelling style links many clauses with "and." Praise the fluency, then offer one upgrade: "and" → whereas / so / as a result.
  • "since" = time or reason? German seit, Spanish desde and French depuis are time only. Show both: "Since 2020…" (time) · "Since you're here, let's start." (reason).

Adding Emphasis — Make It Emphatic

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • spotlight a person, thing, time or place with an it-cleft (It was Maria who booked it. · It was on Monday that I called.), especially to correct a wrong assumption
  • build up to a key idea with a what-cleft (What I need is a vacation. · What he did was (to) apologize.)
  • use other cleft frames: The thing (that)… is · The reason (why)… is · The place where… is · All (that) I want is…
  • use negative inversion with question word order after the triggers the lesson teaches (Never, Not only, No sooner…than, Hardly / Scarcely…when, Rarely / Seldom, Little, Only then / Only after), and know that it is formal and dramatic
  • give a 60–90 second emphatic opinion with at least two clefts and, optionally, one inversion

Meaning: the it-cleft spotlights one element and suggests a contrast.

"It was Maria who booked the hotel." Who booked it? (Maria.) Did the listener think someone else booked it? (Probably, yes: maybe me.) · "It was on Friday that she sent the report." What's the important information? (Friday.) What might the listener have thought? (Another day, like Thursday.)
"It is her decision that matters." Is this about the past? (No, now: is.) · "It was the noise that woke me." Why that, not who? (The noise is a thing.) · "It was my sister who / that paid." Both OK? (Yes: for people, who or that.)

Meaning: the what-cleft delays the key idea to the end, where English puts its weight.

"What I need is a vacation." What's the most important word? (vacation.) Where is it? (At the end.) · "What he did was ignore me." Is the focus a thing or an action? (An action: ignoring.) Can I say "What he did was to ignore me"? (Yes, both are fine.) · "All I want is a quiet weekend." Do I want a lot? (No: only that; nothing more.)

Meaning: negative inversion = drama and formality.

"Never have I seen such a mess." Is it a question? (No.) Why does it look like one? (After Never at the start, we use question word order.) Would you say this to a friend about their kitchen? (Only as a joke: it's dramatic.) Where is it natural? (Speeches, stories, formal writing, a strong ending.)
"No sooner had we sat down than the alarm went off." Which happened first? (We sat down.) How long between them? (Almost no time.) Why had sat? (Past perfect for the earlier action.) Can I say "…when the alarm went off"? (No: No sooner… than; Hardly… when.) · "Little did they know what was coming." Did they know? (No, not at all.)
it-cleftIt + be + spotlight + who / that + restIt was your support that got me through the hardest weeks.
what-cleftWhat + clause + be + key ideaWhat I really need right now is a good night's sleep.
actionWhat + subject + did + was (to) + base verbWhat she did was (to) call the manager.
framesThe thing (that)… is · The reason (why)… is · The place where… is · All (that)… isThe thing that annoys me most is being interrupted.
inversiontrigger + auxiliary + subject + main verb (add do / does / did if needed)Not only did she apologize, but she also offered to fix it.
partnersNo sooner + had + subject + p.p. … than · Hardly / Scarcely + had … whenHardly had I arrived when it began to rain.

Pronunciation. Clefts only work if the stress lands on the spotlight: "It was MAria who booked it" (low, quick "who booked it" after the peak). In what-clefts, the first part rises and hangs, then there's a small pause and the key idea falls: "What I need ↗ | is a vaCAtion ↘." In inversions, stress the trigger and the main verb, and keep the auxiliary weak: "NEVer have I SEEN such a mess." Not only takes a strong stress on only, and but also is said as one chunk. Model a slightly slower, deliberate pace: emphasis is partly about timing.

Drill script
  1. Moving spotlight (hold up the sentence strip My colleague sent the report on Friday; point at one part): T points at my colleague → S: "It was my colleague who sent the report on Friday." T points at the report → S: "It was the report that my colleague sent on Friday." T points at on Friday → S: "It was on Friday that my colleague sent the report." Stress the spotlight.
  2. Correct me: T makes a wrong statement; S corrects with a cleft. T: "You broke the vase!" → S: "No, it was the cat that broke it." T: "You called on Tuesday." → S: "No, it was on Monday that I called."
  3. Build it up (what-clefts): T: "I need a vacation." → S: "What I need is a vacation." T: "The traffic frustrates me." → S: "What frustrates me is the traffic." T: "He just apologized." → S: "What he did was apologize." Say the rise-pause-fall together.
  4. Question first, then flip: T: "I have never eaten such good food." → S (question): "Have I ever eaten…?" → S: "Never have I eaten such good food." T: "She rarely complains." → S: "Does she complain?" → "Rarely does she complain."
  5. Two-part triggers: T: "She won. She also broke the record." → S: "Not only did she win, but she also broke the record." T: "We had just sat down. Then the alarm went off." → S: "No sooner had we sat down than the alarm went off." Then open pairs with the Plain → Dramatic cards, 1 minute.
Make It Emphatic — how to run it
  • Part 1 · Set the Record Straight. Pairs get matching A/B cards (Set E). A reads three wrong "facts" as questions with a confident face ("So Sam organized the party, right?"). B looks at the true facts and corrects each one with an it-cleft, then adds a what-cleft about what really happened ("Actually, it was Nadia who organized it. What Sam did was bring the music."). Swap to the second pair of cards. The scenarios are invented office, neighborhood and family-trip situations; nobody talks about their real life.
  • Part 2 · Plain → Dramatic. Each learner draws a card with a plain sentence and a hint (e.g., "We had just arrived when the power went out." → No sooner…). They say two versions aloud: the one on the hint, and a cleft of their choice. The partner says "✔" or "Try the question first."
  • Part 2 · The emphatic opinion. Trios. Each learner takes a Set F prompt (e.g., Something about public transportation that should change) or their own everyday topic, plans for 1 minute, and speaks for 60–90 seconds: open with a frame ("The thing that really bothers me is…"), give a reason, correct a common view with an it-cleft ("People think it's the price, but it's the waiting that…"), and close with a what-cleft or one inversion ("Never have I…").
  • Keep inversion honest. One inversion per talk is plenty. If a learner stacks three, praise the control and ask: "Which one is the strongest? Keep that one." That is a real B2/C1 skill: choosing.

L1 watch-outs
  • "Never I have seen…": in Spanish (Nunca he visto), Portuguese, Italian and Arabic the subject can disappear or stay after the adverb without inversion, so learners keep statement order. Always go through the question first: "Have I ever seen…? → Never have I seen…"
  • Over-inversion from German / Dutch (verb-second rule): learners may invert after any fronted adverb ("Yesterday went I…", "Often do I go…"). English inverts only after the negative / limiting triggers.
  • Topic-fronting instead of a cleft: Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese speakers often front the topic ("The window, John broke it."). Accept it in speech and show the English cleft.
  • "What I need it is…" (an extra pronoun): Arabic, Persian and some Romance-language speakers repeat the subject. Rule: no it between the what-clause and is.
  • Tense of be in the cleft: French c'est… qui and Spanish es… quien are often present even about the past, giving "It is John who broke it yesterday." Match be to the time: It was.
  • "No sooner… when" / "Hardly… than": many learners from all L1s swap the partner words. Teach them as fixed pairs on one card each.
  • Stress: speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Tagalog, Turkish, Cantonese) may give every word the same weight, which flattens the cleft. Exaggerate the peak in the drill and let learners overdo it at first.

Review Project — Present, Argue & Discuss

90 min
A task-based review, not a new-grammar class

There is no new grammar today. The new language is functional: signposting a talk, building a case, and the discussion moves (concede, then counter; persuade; hedge). The class is built around two real tasks: a two-minute signposted presentation given to a small group, followed by questions, and a small-group discussion in which learners argue assigned sides. Learners plan, rehearse, then perform while you listen with the speaking checklist (Set H). Language work comes from the tasks: a short focus-on-form "clinic" between them, not a presentation. The checklist results feed C13 (Progress Test 1): they are your spoken evidence for Modules 1–4, and they tell you who goes to which catch-up station.

Objectives — learners can…
  • structure a short presentation: open and state the topic, preview the structure, move between points, conclude (I'd like to talk about… · I'll cover three points… · Moving on to… · To sum up…)
  • build a case with reasons, ranking, balance, evidence and hedging (One key advantage is… · On the one hand… on the other hand… · The evidence suggests… · It could be argued that… · arguably)
  • answer a challenge by conceding, then countering (That's a fair point, but… · I take your point; however,… · Granted, but we shouldn't forget that…)
  • take part in a 12-minute group discussion from an assigned side, responding to the previous speaker and inviting others
  • use a range of B2 structures from Modules 1–4 in extended, fluent speech

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review focus)

Meaning: signposts are a map for the listener (and for you).

"I'll cover three points: the cost, the benefits and one objection." What does the listener know now? (How many parts, and what they are.) Who else does it help? (The speaker: it's a plan.) · "Moving on to…" Is the speaker starting, changing or finishing? (Changing to the next point.) · "To sum up…" How much longer will the talk be? (Very little: it's the end.)

Meaning: concede, then counter.

"That's a fair point, but the evidence suggests they still emit less overall." Does the speaker agree completely? (No.) Do they agree at all? (Yes, partly.) Why start with "That's a fair point"? (It shows they listened; it keeps it polite; it makes their own view sound reasonable.) · "No, you're wrong." Same effect? (No: it sounds combative, and the discussion stops.)
"Arguably, the office is still important." How sure is the speaker? (Quite sure, but not claiming it's a fact.) Is hedging weak? (No, in formal English it sounds careful and credible.) · "The bottom line is, a rested team costs less." Hedge or persuade? (Persuade: their strongest point.) How often should you use it? (Once or twice, or it loses force.)

Presentations and the discussion — how to run them
  • Two minutes, not four. The online project asks for 3–4 minutes; in class, two minutes per speaker keeps the circles moving and still shows structure. The full version is homework (a phone recording).
  • Topics. Presentation topics are everyday proposals and trade-offs (the four-day week, phones at the dinner table, cashless shops, homework for children, self-checkout machines, learning online vs in person). A learner's own topic is welcome if it stays everyday. The presenter may argue either side of their topic; the side they choose is not their personal opinion unless they want it to be.
  • Questions after each talk. Every listener has a question starter (Set G: "What about people who…?" · "Isn't there a risk that…?" · "How would that work for…?"). The presenter answers at least one by conceding, then countering. The timekeeper stops the Q&A after one minute, kindly.
  • Side cards make it safe. In the discussion, learners argue the card's side (they may swap inside the group if a topic feels personal). The devil's advocate argues whichever side is losing and speaks third in Round 1. The chair keeps time, invites quiet speakers ("Mei, where do you stand on this?"), stops long turns ("Thanks, Pavel. Let's hear the other side.") and sums up fairly. Give the chair card to a dominant speaker: the job needs listening.
  • Talking tokens in Round 2: 3 each; put one in the middle every time you speak. No tokens left? Listen until everyone has used theirs, then everyone gets 3 more.
  • Persona cards (a night-shift nurse, a café owner, a college student, a parent of three, a retired bus driver, a delivery driver) give learners a point of view that isn't theirs: "As a café owner, I'd have to say…". Great for quieter learners and for anyone who doesn't want to speak for themselves.
  • If a group gets heated, the chair (or you) says: "Great energy. Let's hear one reason from each side and sum up." Push the idea, never the person.
The speaking checklist — how to score it (feeds C13)

Five criteria, 0–3 points each, /15: 0 = no response · 1 = not yet B2 · 2 = B2 · 3 = strong B2 (moving toward C1). Score the whole performance across the presentation, the Q&A and the discussion. Tick the module boxes (M1–M4) when you hear a structure from that module used correctly. The full grid and descriptors are in the classroom materials (Set H).

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am agree" / "Are you agree?" (Spanish estar de acuerdo, French être d'accord, Portuguese, Italian): still common at B2 under pressure. Drill "I agree. · Do you agree?" once, then leave it for delayed feedback.
  • "We discussed about…" (Spanish discutir sobre, Arabic, Russian obsuzhdat' o): discuss has no preposition. Note that Spanish discutir can mean argue (have a fight).
  • False friends in signposts: "Resuming…" (Spanish resumiendo, French en résumé) → To sum up; "Actually…" meaning "currently" (Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, German aktuell); "Eventually…" meaning "possibly" (Spanish eventualmente, French éventuellement, German eventuell). "At the end" → Finally / In the end.
  • "make a presentation": fine in American English; also give a presentation. Not "do a speech" → give a speech.
  • "According to me…" (French selon moi, Spanish según yo, Indian English): according to is for other sources ("according to a recent study"). Offer "As far as I can tell…" or "It seems to me that…".
  • Directness differs. Dutch, German, Russian and Hebrew speakers may counter without conceding ("No, this is wrong."); Japanese, Korean, Thai and Filipino speakers may concede without countering, so their view disappears. Teach the English middle path, concede and then counter, as a technique, not a personality change. Both styles are normal; English discussion just has its own.

Recap on slide 18: Although… / Despite… / However, · because of… / due to… · so… that / such… that · Therefore, · It was… who… · What I need is… · Never have I… · I'd like to talk about… · That's a fair point, but… Learners tick the three "I can…" lists. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Use those notes, the Review Race observations and your speaking checklist to plan the C13 catch-up stations.

Celebrate the halfway point. Learners have finished half of B2. Ask each person to say one thing they can do now that they couldn't do in Week 1, with an emphasis structure if they like ("What I'm proudest of is…", "Never have I talked so much in English!"). Keep it short and warm.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it can test: the grammar after each linker (although / despite / because of / due to / however), despite the fact that, so…that vs such…that, connector punctuation, it-clefts and what-clefts, the other cleft frames, negative inversion with the lesson's triggers (question word order, do / does / did, No sooner…than / Hardly…when), and signposting and concede-and-counter phrases. The C12 speaking checklist gives you the spoken evidence.

Watch for in Module 5: the future perfect and future continuous (5.1) bring new auxiliary chains (will have finished, will be working); learners who have just practiced inversion may over-apply it ("Rarely will have I…"). The causative (5.2) looks like the passive from 3.1 (have it done vs it was done). Keep a 2-minute "linker tennis" or "spotlight it" warmer in C14–C16: it is quick, and it keeps Module 4 range alive in speech.
