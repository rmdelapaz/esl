Module 6 — Words & Questions

Three classes about precision: the right form of a word, the right size of a difference, and the right shape of a question. First comes word formation: word families, the suffixes that signal a word's job, the prefixes that flip or shade its meaning, and the stress that moves as a word changes form. Then advanced comparison: far / much / slightly / nowhere near as…, twice as… as, the more…, the more… and better and better. Last, asking skillfully: indirect questions for politeness (Could you tell me where the station is?), negative questions for surprise, agreement and persuasion (Isn't it…? Don't you think…?), and subject questions with no auxiliary.

3 lessons Lessons 6.1–6.3 Classes C17–C19 Weeks 9–10 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, role-play) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-6-words-questions.html. Print materials/module-6/classroom-materials.html before C17. It holds the affix, scale and opener flashcards, the job and product picture cards, the new identity cards, the Word Family Grid A/B cards, the Prefix/Suffix Race cards, the Comeback Story bingo, the Compare & Recommend fact sheets and client cards, the Polite Enquiry role-play cards and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons (with a US or UK voice and a slower speed), so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. Learners already know simple comparatives and superlatives (A2), as… as (A2), reported questions with statement word order and if / whether (B1 3.2), Could you…? requests (B1 7.2) and question tags (B1 8.1). Check in each lead-in and go faster where they are strong. The new B2 work is choosing the exact form (a gap-fill skill), saying how big a difference is, the parallel comparative, and the register and tone of questions: when to wrap a question in an opener, and what a negative question really means.
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a suffix or a word order. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. Praise the message first ("That recommendation really convinced me."), then the form. At B2, give feedback on accuracy and range: note one error to fix and one upgrade ("You said much cheaper three times. Try considerably or nowhere near as expensive.").
  • Dominant speakers. All three freer tasks have built-in turns: use them. Give every group a talking-turn token (you speak only when you hold it), give the strongest speaker a job (the Race referee, the "client" who must be convinced, the role-play observer with the checklist), and use "your partner's idea first" when groups report back. Say it kindly: "Thanks, Omar. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Each lesson has a Stretch box with C1-edge language (less transparent suffixes, by far / every bit as… as, Would you happen to know…? / I don't suppose you could…). Offer it as an option, not a requirement; the class target is solid B2.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British forms (recognise, modernise, economise, practise, behaviour, flat, motorway, queue, film, loo, full stop, skilfully). Teach recognize, modernize, economize, practice (verb and noun), behavior, apartment, highway, line, movie, restroom, period, skillfully. Accept the British forms when learners use them. Lesson 6.1 itself says that -ise and -ize are both correct: use this to make the point.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

The right form → the right size → the right shape. In C17 learners reshape one idea into four jobs (decide → decision → decisive → decisively) and use the forms to tell a short "comeback story." In C18 they weigh options precisely (far more reliable, only slightly heavier, nowhere near as cheap, twice as big) and make a recommendation to a "client." In C19 they ask for information at a front desk and in a job interview in a way that sounds considerate (I was wondering whether…), and they check and persuade with negative questions (Isn't it cheaper online? Don't you think the annual plan is better value?). The C18 comparison language comes back naturally in the C19 role-plays. Point this out: it is the moment learners feel their B2 tools working together.

Trauma-informed: stories, comparisons and interviews

Three things in this module need care. (1) Comeback stories (C17) are about "something that went wrong and then went right." Keep them light: a team project, a cake, a garden, a driving test, a small business on a scenario card. Never ask about a personal failure, a loss, or a hard journey. (2) Comparisons (C18): the cities, jobs and products are invented on purpose. Don't ask learners to compare "your country and the US" or "your old life and your life now"; it can bring up grief or politics, and the course avoids immigration topics. Pay also needs care: the job cards give the numbers, so no one discusses their own salary. (3) Interviews (C19): the lesson's role-plays are a job interview and customer-service calls only. Never role-play an immigration, asylum, police, court or benefits interview, and don't let a learner play "the officer." The questions in role-play come from the cards, never "Where are you from?" or "Why did you come here?" For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card (in the classroom materials), or something imagined. Opinion tasks always allow an assigned side ("You're on Team Kestrel today"). Anyone can say "Pass!"

Before the Module

  • Print the flashcards (one set for the board): the suffix and prefix cards (color-code them if you can: nouns blue, adjectives green, adverbs orange, verbs red), the difference-scale cards and the question-opener cards. Card stock helps. Cut them before C17.
  • For C17: one pair of Word Family Grid cards (A + B) per pair; one set of Prefix/Suffix Race cards (20 base cards + 27 affix cards) per team of 3–4 (an envelope per team); one Comeback Story bingo grid per learner (two versions, so neighbors differ) and one set of comeback scenario cards.
  • For C18: one Compare & Recommend fact sheet per learner (A and B sheets for three topics: phones, jobs, towns) and one set of client cards per pair. Put the job and product picture cards on the board.
  • For C19: one set of Polite Enquiry role-play cards per group of 3 (A = customer or candidate, B = staff or interviewer, C = observer with the checklist). Tape the opener cards on the wall where everyone can see them.
  • Print the new identity cards and keep them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking. In C19 they double as the candidate profiles for the interview role-play.
  • Optional: a desk bell or a phone for the role-plays ("Good morning, Riverside Community Center, how can I help?"). Learners enjoy the realism, and it makes the register clear.

Word Formation — Prefixes, Suffixes & Word Families

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • recognize and build word families: the noun, verb, adjective and adverb from one base (decide → decision → decisive → decisively)
  • use a suffix to identify a word's job: -tion / -sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -er / -or (nouns) · -ful, -less, -able / -ible, -ive, -ous, -al (adjectives) · -ly (adverbs) · -ize, -en, -ify (verbs)

If you run long

This class has two games and a story task. If time is tight, cut Race Round 2 (Build it) and move Workbook Ex 4 to homework. Never cut the Comeback Story: it is where learners use the forms in connected speech instead of just recognizing them. With a very strong class, shorten the prefix presentation to 5 minutes; they will know most negatives already.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: a word family. One idea, several grammatical jobs. The idea stays the same; the ending changes the job in the sentence.

"She made a quick decision. She's very decisive. She acted decisively." Is the idea the same in all three? (Yes, deciding.) Which one is a thing, a noun? (decision) · Which describes a person? (decisive, an adjective) · Which describes how she acted? (decisively, an adverb) · So what changed? (Only the job, not the idea.)

Meaning: the suffix tells you the job.

"Kindness", "agreement", "ability": nouns or adjectives? (Nouns.) How do you know without a dictionary? (The endings: -ness, -ment, -ity.) · "Hopeless": full of hope or without hope? (Without.) · "Widen": a verb or an adjective? (A verb: make wider.) · "Carefully": how many l's? (Two: careful + ly.)

Meaning: prefixes. A prefix usually changes the meaning, not the job.

"Happy" and "unhappy": both adjectives? (Yes.) · "Overcook": too much or too little? (Too much.) · "I misread the email." Did I read it correctly? (No, wrongly.) · "Rebuild": for the first time? (No, again.) · "Her help was invaluable." Was her help useless? (No! Extremely valuable.)

Meaning: choosing the form for a gap.

"It was a ___ launch." (SUCCESS) What comes after the gap? (A noun, launch.) So what job does the gap need? (An adjective.) The answer? (successful) · "They finished it ___." What does the gap describe? (How they finished: a verb.) So? (An adverb: successfully.) · "The first attempt was ___." (SUCCESS) The story says it failed. So? (unsuccessful: adjective + negative.)

Pronunciation. Teach one powerful rule: -tion / -sion, -ic and -ity pull the stress to the syllable just before them: eDUcate → eduCAtion · eCOnomy → ecoNOmic · creAtive → creaTIvity · PHOtograph → phoTOgraphy → photoGRAPHic. Endings like -ful, -less, -ness, -ment, -ly, -able usually don't move the stress: CAREful, CARElessly, HAPpiness, deVELopment. Sound changes to model: decide /dɪˈsaɪd/ → decision /dɪˈsɪʒən/ (the vowel changes and d → /ʒ/) · succeed → success · strong → strength /strɛŋkθ/ (hard: let learners say "streng-th" slowly) · -tion is always weak /ʃən/, never "tee-on." Prefixes are normally unstressed (unHAPpy, imPOSsible), but we stress them to contrast: "I said POSsible, not IMpossible!"

Drill script
  1. Family chain (hold up a base card): T: "decide" → S1: "decision" → S2: "decisive" → S3: "decisively" → S4: "indecisive." Then succeed, create, strong, care. Choral first, then around the room.
  2. Clap the stress: T: "PHOtograph" (clap on PHO) → S repeat. "phoTOgraphy" → "photoGRAPHic" → "phoTOgrapher." Then "eCOnomy → ecoNOmic," "comPETE → compeTItion → comPEtitive." Learners clap with you.
  3. What's the job? T says a word; learners hold up a colored card (or say N / V / ADJ / ADV): "happiness" → N · "widen" → V · "dangerous" → ADJ · "easily" → ADV · "agreement" → N · "simplify" → V.
  4. Flip it: T: "possible" → S: "impossible." "legal" → "illegal." "regular" → "irregular." "honest" → "dishonest." "correct" → "incorrect." "fair" → "unfair." Fast, then individuals.
  5. Fit it (open pairs → closed pairs): S1 says a base; S2 says a sentence frame; S3 fills it: "CARE" + "Please drive…" → "Please drive carefully."
Word Family Grid — how to run it
  • Pairs sit face to face. A holds Grid A, B holds Grid B. Both grids have the same 10 bases (decide, succeed, create, strong, compete, rely, care, attract, employ, responsible) with columns Noun · Verb · Adjective · Adverb · Negative. Each card shows about half the answers; the missing cells are on the partner's card.
  • Learners ask, answer and spell: "What's the noun from compete?" "Competition: C-O-M-P-E-T-I-T-I-O-N." They write the answers in Workbook Ex 1. No showing the card. (Spelling aloud is useful: many learners have never spelled -tion or -ible aloud.)
  • The Negative column is empty on both cards: pairs must agree on the right prefix together. This is where discussion happens ("Is it unresponsible?" "No, r, so ir-!").
  • Two cells are marked ✱ on both cards: families with two nouns (creation / creativity; employer / employee / employment). Ask pairs what the difference is.
  • Dominant speakers: A asks all the Noun and Verb questions, B all the Adjective and Adverb questions, so both partners talk equally.
Prefix/Suffix Race — how to run it
  • Teams of 3–4. Each team has an envelope: 20 base cards (possible, legal, regular, honest, agree, fair, correct, understand, cook, pay, write, view, care, hope, help, use, kind, create, rely, danger) and 27 affix cards (prefixes un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, dis-, non-, re-, over-, under-, mis-, pre- and suffixes -tion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -er, -ful, -less, -able, -ive, -ous, -al, -ly, -ize, -en, -ify).
  • Round 1 · Flip it (3 min): T reads a base; the first team to hold up the correct negative prefix card and say the word ("Illegal!") scores 1. Wrong prefix = the next team can steal.
  • Round 2 · Build it (4 min): teams lay out as many real words as they can with base + affix cards (up to two affixes, e.g., un + help + ful). 1 point per real word. Then teams walk around and challenge words they don't believe: a phone dictionary settles it. An invented word (beautiless, unpossible, helpness) = −1 point. The answer key has the full list of accepted words.
  • Round 3 · Fit it (4 min): T reads the 8 sentences in Script 6.1-B, saying "BLANK" for the gap and the base in capitals. Teams write the form on paper and hold it up. 2 points for the right form, 1 for the right job with a spelling slip.
  • Give the strongest speaker the referee job for Round 2 (with the answer list): they check challenges and add scores. It keeps them busy and gives the quieter learners the building.
Comeback Story — how to run it
  • The model is the online lesson's passage: "Despite careful preparation, the first launch was a complete failure. The team felt disappointed, but they responded decisively…". Read it aloud and ask: "How many forms can you hear?"
  • Scenario cards give a story frame with invented people: the night-market stall that got no customers in the first month, the community garden that flooded, the soccer team that lost every game, the bake sale with burned cakes, the neighborhood app that nobody downloaded, the second driving test. Learners can also tell a real, light story, or use their identity card.
  • Each teller must use at least six forms from two or more families (e.g., fail, failure, disappointed, decision, decisively, improvement, succeed, successful). Listeners mark every form they hear on their bingo grid. Three in a row = say "Bingo!" quietly after the story, not during it.
  • Talking-turn token: only the teller speaks; after the story each listener asks one follow-up question. The learner who talked longest in the last round tells their story last.
ICQs before the Grid, the Race and the Story
Grid: Can you show your card? (No.) · What do you do after you say the word? (Spell it.) · Who decides the negative? (Both of us, together.)
Race: Round 1: what do you hold up? (The negative prefix card.) · Round 2: do invented words score? (No, minus one!) · Who checks a challenge? (The dictionary / the referee.) · Round 3: what do you write? (The form that fits the sentence.)
Story: How many forms from the families? (Six or more.) · What do listeners do? (Mark the forms on the bingo.) · When do you say "Bingo"? (After the story.) · Does it have to be your real life? (No: a card, real, or imagined.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on four families (decide, succeed, care, strong) and four endings (-tion, -ful, -ly, un- / im-). Give the colored suffix cards as a strip on the desk: blue = thing, green = describes a person or thing, orange = describes how. In the Grid, the learner asks and their partner spells, then the learner copies. In the Race, pair them with a patient speller and let them do Round 1 (listening, no writing). Target: 4 correct gap-fills and a 3-sentence comeback story with fail → decided → successful. Spelling reminders: -ful has one l, -fully two.

Stretch

Push toward C1: less transparent suffixes (-ance / -ence, -hood, -ship, -dom: reliance, childhood, leadership, freedom); words that change job with no suffix but move the stress (a REcord / to reCORD, a PREsent / to preSENT, an INcrease / to inCREASE); and collocations with the family (make a decision, reach an agreement, a significant improvement). In the Race, they may use two affixes only. In the story, they add one -ance / -ence noun and one stress-shift pair. Early finishers write a 6-gap word-formation exercise for a classmate.

L1 watch-outs
  • Wrong part of speech: "a success project," "He explained very clear," "a decide leader." Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai words don't change form for their job, so the ending feels optional. Ask the two-step question every time: "What job does the slot need?"
  • Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian cognates are a gift (-ción → -tion, -dad / -ité → -ity, -oso → -ous) and a trap: sensible ≠ sensible (Spanish = sensitive), actually ≠ actualmente (= currently). The prefix des- leads to "disfortunate" (desafortunado), "discomfortable," "disagradable": English uses un- (unfortunate, uncomfortable, unpleasant).
  • Arabic and Hebrew build words from roots and patterns, so learners understand families quickly, but the English suffixes are unfamiliar: expect "responsibleness," "successness." Praise the logic; give the real word.
  • Stress in the same place every time: French speakers stress the last syllable (photograPHY), Polish the second-last, Czech, Hungarian and Finnish the first. Learners from these languages often keep one stress position across the family. Clap the stress; it is a listening skill as much as a speaking one.
  • Spelling: "carefull," "successfull," "hopefuly," "responsable" (Spanish, French, Portuguese responsable). Point at the -ful / -fully rule and the -ible in responsible, possible, visible.
  • -ed / -ing adjectives slip in: "The team was disappointing" (meaning disappointed). Many languages use one form for both. Quick check: "Who felt it? → -ed. What caused it? → -ing."

6.2 · C18

Advanced Comparison

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • show how big a difference is: far / much / a lot / considerably / significantly + comparative (big) · slightly / a bit / a little / marginally + comparative (small), and never "very + comparative"
  • use not nearly as… as and nowhere near as… as (much less) and not quite as… as (almost as)
  • compare with as… as, multiples (twice / three times / half as… as), the same as, similar to, different from, choosing than or as correctly
  • link two changes with the + comparative…, the + comparative (The earlier we leave, the less traffic we'll hit.) and describe a trend with comparative and comparative (more and more expensive)
  • compare two products, jobs or towns and recommend one to a "client," using six or more different structures
Materials
  • Workbook 6.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Compare & Recommend notes)
  • Slides 8–12 · Difference-scale flashcards · Job and product picture cards · Compare & Recommend fact sheets (A and B for phones, jobs and towns) + client cards · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 6.2-A (how big is the difference?) and 6.2-B (the highway or the coast road?)

Left: the difference line, a long arrow from tiny to huge: marginally · slightly · a bit · a little | … | a lot · much · far · considerably · significantly · (the other way: nowhere near as / not nearly as). Under it, crossed out in red: very cheaper · more better · more faster.

Center: two columns: THAN after -er / more / less · AS with as… as / the same as / twice as… as. Examples: far cheaper than · twice as big as · the same as · similar to · different from.

Right: The more you practice ↗ | the better you get ↘ (two halves, two "the"s, two comparatives). Under it: better and better · more and more expensive (a trend).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the size of the difference.

"The Kestrel is far more expensive." Is the difference big or small? (Big.) · "The Nova is slightly heavier." Would you notice it in your hand? (Probably not; it's a small difference.) · Can I say "very more expensive"? (No! much / far more expensive.) · Can I say "very expensive"? (Yes: no comparative, so very is fine.)
"The movie was nowhere near as good as the book." Which was better? (The book.) A little better or a lot? (A lot.) · "The bus is not quite as fast as the train." Is the bus much slower? (No, only a little.) · Which is the bigger gap: not quite as or not nearly as? (Not nearly as.)

Meaning: multiples and "the same".

"Rent in the city is twice as high as in the town." The town: $900. The city? ($1,800.) · On the fact sheets: $1,500 and $950. Twice as high? (Not quite: almost twice as high.) · "The trip took half as long as we expected." We expected 4 hours. It took? (2 hours.) Faster or slower than we thought? (Faster.) · "Her salary is the same as his." Is one higher? (No, equal.)

Meaning: the parallel comparative.

"The more you practice, the better you get." Two things change: what are they? (Practice and skill.) Do they change together? (Yes: more practice → more skill.) · "The older I get, the less I worry." Getting older → worrying more or less? (Less.) · "The sooner, the better." What's missing? (…we leave / it happens. The listener knows.)
"Prices are getting higher and higher." One change or a continuing trend? (A trend: it keeps going.) · "more and more expensive": why not "expensiver and expensiver"? (Long adjective → more and more + adjective.)

Form.

Big differencefar / much / a lot / considerably / significantly + comparative + thanThis laptop is far more powerful than my old one.
Small differenceslightly / a bit / a little / marginally + comparative + thanThe blue jacket is slightly cheaper than the black one.
Much lessnot nearly / nowhere near as + adjective + asThe sequel was nowhere near as good as the original.
Almost asnot quite as + adjective + asThe bus isn't quite as fast as the train.
Equal / less(not) as + adjective / adverb + asHe works as hard as anyone. · She isn't as tall as her sister.
Multiplestwice / three times / half + as… asTheir house is three times as big as ours.
Same / similarthe same as · similar to · different fromYour phone is the same as mine.

Pronunciation. Stress the intensifier, because it carries the message: "It's FAR more expensive." · "It's only SLIGHTly heavier." · "It's NOWhere near as good." than and as are weak: /ðən/, /əz/: "as tall as" = /əz ˈtɔl əz/, "cheaper than" = /ˈtʃipər ðən/. Twice as links: /ˈtwaɪsəz/. The parallel comparative has two tone units: the voice rises at the end of the first half and falls at the end of the second: "The more you PRACtice ↗ | the BETter you get ↘." A short pause at the comma helps listeners. In American English, better and better uses the flap: /ˈbɛɾər ən ˈbɛɾər/.

Drill script
  1. Scale drill (point at a card on the difference line): T: "cheaper, big" → S: "far cheaper / much cheaper." T: "cheaper, small" → S: "slightly cheaper." T: "good, much less" → S: "nowhere near as good." T: "fast, almost" → S: "not quite as fast." Choral, then individuals, stressing the intensifier.
  2. Very? No! T: "very cheaper" → S: "much cheaper!" T: "very expensive" → S: "OK!" T: "very more reliable" → S: "far more reliable!"
  3. Numbers → multiples: T: "$300 and $600" → S: "twice as expensive as." "100 and 300" → "three times as big as." "4 hours and 2 hours" → "half as long as."
  4. Than or as? T: "bigger…" → S: "than." T: "as big…" → S: "as." T: "the same…" → S: "as." T: "similar…" → S: "to." T: "different…" → S: "from."
  5. Rise–fall: choral "The sooner we leave ↗ | the better ↘." Then open pairs: S1 starts, S2 finishes: S1 "The more you read ↗…" S2 "…the more you learn ↘."
Compare & Recommend — how to run it
  • Three topics, each with an A and a B fact sheet: phones (Nova S vs Kestrel 8), jobs (Warehouse Team Lead at Brightline vs Delivery Driver at QuickRoute), towns (Riverton vs Millbrook). All invented. The numbers are designed so learners need the target language: $320 vs $640 (twice as), 180 g vs 185 g (slightly), 45 minutes vs 10 minutes (far longer / nowhere near as short).
  • Partners don't show their sheets. They tell each other the facts and compare aloud. Each sheet has a checklist of six structures at the bottom: learners tick one each time they use it.
  • Then the pair draws a client card (e.g., "a student on a tight budget," "a nurse who works nights," "a retired couple who don't drive") and must agree on one recommendation, ending with "So overall, I'd recommend… because…". If they disagree, they can each argue a side; that's good B2 practice.
  • Keep the comparisons neutral: invented products, invented job ads, invented towns. Nobody compares their own salary, their own country or their own town.
  • Dominant speakers: the partner with the quieter voice reads the client card and makes the final decision ("You've convinced me" or "Not yet!"). The talkative partner must persuade them.
ICQs before Compare & Recommend

(Who we are recommending for.) · How do you finish? ("So overall, I'd recommend…") · Do we talk about our own salary? (No, only the card.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with three frames only: "X is much ___er than Y." · "X is slightly ___er than Y." · "X is twice as ___ as Y." Stretch

Push toward C1: by far the best, every bit as good as, nothing like as expensive as, a great deal more, not remotely as, and the parallel comparative with a noun (the more money you earn, the more tax you pay). Ask them to hedge the recommendation (On balance… / If budget is the main concern…). Early finishers describe the trend table on their town sheet in four sentences (Rents have become more and more expensive: they're now almost twice as high as in 2018.).

L1 watch-outs
  • than / that / as: Spanish and Portuguese use que for both "than" and "that," so learners write "bigger that." French uses que for both "than" and "as" (plus grand que, aussi grand que): "as big than." Use the board rule: -er / more → than; as / same → as.
  • "the same than" / "the same like": Spanish lo mismo que, German gleich wie, Russian такой же, как. Drill "the same as" as one chunk.
  • Dropping the "the"s: Spanish cuanto más… más, French plus… plus, German je… desto, Russian чем… тем, Chinese 越…越 and Korean -ㄹ수록 have no article: "More you practice, better you get." Point at the two "the"s on the board.
  • "two times more expensive": very common for Russian (в два раза дороже), Chinese, Spanish and Arabic speakers. It is heard in real English and is understood; accept it in speech, and teach twice as expensive as as the clear, standard form.
  • Double comparatives: "more better," "more cheaper," "very cheaper": Arabic, Tagalog and many other speakers. Also Chinese and Japanese learners may skip -er (比 / より do the job): "It's cheap than mine."
  • "The more you practice, the more you get better": logical, but English prefers "the better you get." Recast gently.

Asking Skillfully — Indirect & Negative Questions

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • turn a direct question into a polite indirect question: opener + statement word order, no do / does / did (Do you know what time it opens?)
  • use if / whether for yes/no questions, with or not in the right place (whether or not…, if… or not)

  • sound polite with their voice (wide pitch, a gentle rise, a soft fall-rise) in a customer-service call and a job interview

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: why indirect? The opener softens the question. It adds a little distance and courtesy: ideal with strangers, at work, on the phone, in emails. With friends, direct questions are normal and not rude.

"Could you tell me where the station is?" Is the speaker asking if you can tell them? (No, they want the information: where the station is.) · Is this more polite than "Where's the station?" (Yes, better for a stranger.) · Would you say it to your brother at home? (Probably not. It would sound funny, too formal.)
"Do you know what time it opens?" What was the direct question? ("What time does it open?") Where did does go? (Gone. The -s moved to opens.) · "I was wondering if the bus stops here." Is this a yes/no question or a wh- question? (Yes/no: "Does the bus stop here?") So we add…? (if / whether.)

Meaning: negative questions. They usually don't ask for new information. They show what the speaker already thinks or feels.

"Isn't it beautiful? ↘" Does she really want to know if it's beautiful? (No, she thinks it is; she wants me to agree.) · "Didn't you get my message? ↗" What did he expect? (That I got it.) How does he feel? (Surprised, maybe a little worried.) · "Don't you think we should wait? ↘" Is it really a question? (Not really: a suggestion; he wants to wait.)
"Didn't you get my message?" You did get it. Yes or no? (Yes, I did.) · You didn't get it. Yes or no? (No, I didn't.) · So what does "yes" always mean in English? (It's true / it happened.) · Why add "I did / I didn't"? (It removes any doubt.)

Meaning: subject questions.

"Who broke the window?" Do we know who broke it? (No, that's the question.) Is who the doer? (Yes.) So: did? (No.) · "What did she break?" Who's the doer? (She is.) So: did? (Yes.) · Answer both: "Maria broke the window." · "She broke the window." Where does the answer go? (Front = subject question; after the verb = object question.)

Form.

Wh- indirectopener + wh- word + subject + verb (no do / does / did)Where is the station? → Could you tell me where the station is? · How much did it cost? → Do you know how much it cost?

OpenersDo you know…? · Could you tell me…? · Do you have any idea…? · Would you mind telling me…? · I was wondering if / whether… · I'd like to know…Statement openers end with a period (a question mark is common too).

Answersby the facts: Yes = true / happened · No = not true / didn't happen"Didn't you go?" (I went.) → Yes, I did. · (I didn't.) → No, I didn't.

Pronunciation. In English, politeness lives in the voice as much as in the words. (1) Indirect questions with a question opener (Could you tell me…? Do you know…?) are grammatically yes/no questions: a gentle rise at the end sounds polite and open ("Could you tell me where the STAtion is? ↗"); a fall is also normal and sounds more businesslike. Start a little higher than usual and use a wide pitch range: a flat, low voice sounds bored or annoyed, even with perfect grammar. (2) Statement openers (I was wondering if…) often take a soft fall-rise ↘↗ at the end: tentative and very polite ("I was wondering if you had any OPEnings ↘↗"). (3) Negative questions: stress the negative auxiliary (DIDn't you…, ISn't it…, DON'T you think…). A fall means "I'm sure; agree with me" (Isn't it beautiful? ↘ · Don't you think we should wait? ↘); a rise means "I thought so; is it true?" or surprise (Isn't the office closed on Mondays? ↗ · Didn't you get my message? ↗). Link to B1 question tags: same rule. (4) Weak forms and linking: Could you /ˈkʊdʒə/, Would you /ˈwʊdʒə/, Do you know /dʒəˈnoʊ/, Didn't you /ˈdɪdn̩tʃə/, Don't you /ˈdoʊntʃə/; whether = weather.

Polite Enquiry role-play — how to run it
  • Six customer-service cards: the Riverside Community Center gym front desk, a hotel front desk (phone), Lakeview Community College admissions, the public library, a furniture store (delivery), an internet provider (slow Wi-Fi). Card A (customer) lists 6 things to find out, including one yes/no question (→ if / whether) and one belief to check with a negative question (Isn't it cheaper online?). Card B (staff) has all the answers, plus one upsell to try with Don't you think…? or Wouldn't it be better to…?. This brings back the C18 comparisons ("The annual plan is far better value").
  • Two job-interview cards: café shift supervisor and office assistant at a dental clinic. The interviewer (B) asks at least four indirect questions (Could you tell me what you enjoy about working with customers?) and checks two things from the candidate's profile with negative questions (Didn't you say you'd trained new staff? Haven't you worked weekends before?). The candidate (A) answers from an identity card, never from their real life, and answers the negative questions by the facts. At the end the candidate asks three indirect questions (I was wondering whether there's any paid training.).
  • Observer (C) has the checklist: 6 indirect questions? statement order? no do / did? if / whether? one negative question? polite voice (rise, wide pitch)? They give the feedback: one thing that was great, one thing to fix. Give the observer job to the strongest speaker in Round 1; it is a real, demanding job.
  • Keep every scenario routine: memberships, check-in times, delivery windows, a slow connection, a job interview. No debt, eviction, police, immigration, medical or benefits calls. The interview is always for an invented job, and the candidate always uses an identity card.
  • Planning time (2 minutes) is important at B2: learners write the key words of their questions, not full sentences, then speak. Writing full sentences turns the role-play into reading aloud.

(For a yes/no question.) · What does B try to do? (Sell something with "Don't you think…?") · What does the observer do? (Listen, tick, and say one great thing and one thing to fix.) · In the interview, do you talk about your real life? (No, the identity card.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use two openers only, Could you tell me…? and Do you know if…?, on a strip, with the "sandwich" picture (bread = opener, filling = where it is). Give the gym card (the simplest) with picture cues (🕘 hours, 💲 price, 🏊 pool, 🅿️ parking). The learner plays the customer first, with a supportive partner as staff. Skip subject questions if needed. Stretch

Push toward C1: Would you happen to know…? · I don't suppose you could tell me…? · I'd be grateful if you could let me know whether… (email) · What I'd really like to know is…; whether + to-infinitive (I'm not sure whether to upgrade); negative questions with a softener (Wouldn't it make more sense to…?). In the interview, they combine comparison and questions: Could you tell me whether the evening shift is much busier than the morning? Early finishers write the follow-up email in formal register (a preview of C20).

L1 watch-outs
  • Keeping the question order: "Could you tell me where is the station?" / "Do you know what time does it open?" Almost every learner does this, because the ear expects a question. Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian and Arabic speakers may also transfer their own embedded-question order (in Spanish ¿Sabe dónde está la estación? the verb comes before the subject). Use the red pen and the sandwich.
  • No if: "Do you know is the shop open?" Russian (ли after the verb), Arabic (hal), Chinese and Vietnamese (a question particle) have no joining word like if. Drill "Do you know if…" as one chunk.
  • Answering negative questions: Japanese, Korean, Mandarin and Cantonese speakers (and many others) answer to agree with the question: "Didn't you go?" "Yes" = "Yes, you're right, I didn't." French (si) and German (doch) have a special word for "yes, I did," which English doesn't. Always teach the full answer: "Yes, I did / No, I didn't."
  • Politeness in the words, not the tune: learners whose languages show respect with verb endings, particles or pronouns (Japanese -masu, Korean -yo, Tagalog po / opo, Spanish usted, French vous, Russian вы) may rely on "please" alone and a flat voice. English puts politeness in the opener and the intonation. Model the difference without judging.
  • Auxiliary in subject questions: "Who did break the window?" / "What did happen?" Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese and many others. The quick test: "Is the question word the doer?" (Note: "Who DID break it?" with stress is correct but emphatic; don't teach it here.)
  • Negative questions can sound like complaints: "Didn't you read my email?" with a falling, heavy tone sounds like criticism. Learners who translate directly may sound rude by accident. Model the light, surprised rise.
