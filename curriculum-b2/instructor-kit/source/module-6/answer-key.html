Module 6 — Words & Questions: Answers

Answers to every Module 6 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (Word Family Grid, Prefix/Suffix Race word list, Comeback bingo, Compare & Recommend, Polite Enquiry role-plays, listening scripts), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 6.1–6.3Classes C17–C199 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, then range. A word-formation answer is correct when it is the right part of speech and the right meaning (positive or negative, singular or plural) and is spelled correctly; give half credit for the right job with a small spelling slip (succesful). A comparison is correct when the size of the difference matches the facts and than / as is right. An indirect question is correct when it has statement word order and no do / does / did. Be generous about options: if and whether are both correct (except directly before or not); far / much / a lot are interchangeable, as are slightly / a bit / a little; a question mark after I was wondering if… is acceptable; contractions are fine. American English is the model; British forms (-ise spellings, practise, flat, motorway, film, queue) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

6.1 · Word Formation (C17)

👂 Where's the stress? (Script 6.1-A)

1 PHOtograph · phoTOgraphy · photoGRAPHic · phoTOgrapher · 2 eCOnomy · ecoNOmic · eCOnomist · 3 creATE · creAtion · creAtive · creaTIvity · 4 EDucate · eduCAtion · 5 comPETE · compeTItion · comPEtitive · 6 deCIDE · deCIsion · deCIsive.

The rule: -tion, -ic and -ity put the stress on the syllable just before them. Accept any wording that says this ("the syllable before -tion is strong"). Note that create and creative are already stressed on the second syllable (creATE, creAtive); only -ity moves it.

Exercise 2 — Form the correct word

1 success (N) · 2 creative (ADJ) · 3 clearly (ADV) · 4 strengthen (V) · 5 failure (N) · 6 information (N) · 7 helpful (ADJ) · 8 modernize (V; modernise accepted).

Watch for: 1 "successful" (the gap follows a huge, so a noun) · 5 "fail" (a verb after a complete) · 6 "informations" (uncountable). In 7, "helpless" is a real word but the wrong meaning.

Exercise 3 — Negative prefixes

1 impossible (before p) · 2 illegal (before l) · 3 disagree · 4 unreadable (illegible is also a real word for handwriting; mention it to strong learners) · 5 irresponsible (before r) · 6 incorrect · 7 non-smoker · 8 unreliable.

Exercise 4 — Other prefixes

1 misunderstood · 2 undercooked · 3 rewrite · 4 overworked · 5 preview · 6 misspelled (misspelt is British) · 7 underpaid.

In 6, the double s in misspelled is a common spelling error: mis + spelled.

Exercise 5 — A comeback story

1 preparation (N) · 2 failure (N) · 3 confusing (ADJ) · 4 unreasonable (ADJ, negative) · 5 disappointed (ADJ) · 6 decisively (ADV) · 7 improvements (N, plural) · 8 patiently (ADV) · 9 success (N) · 10 employees (N, plural).

Common errors: 3 "confused" (the customers were confused; the menu was confusing) · 4 "reasonable" (the text says far too high, so the meaning is negative) · 5 "disappointing" · 7 "improvement" (several needs a plural) · 10 "employers" (they are the employers; the workers are employees).

Dialogue & speaking — Comeback Story, bingo, word family map

Dialogue: modernize (V after should), decisive / indecisive (ADJ before a noun), successfully (ADV: how we finished). Comeback Story (open): check six or more family forms, each in the right job. Model: "The community garden was a complete failure the first year: the heavy rain flooded everything, and we felt really disappointed. But our organizer acted decisively. She made a quick decision to build raised beds, and the improvement was amazing. The second year was a huge success: we successfully grew enough tomatoes for the whole street." Bingo: no fixed answers; a mark counts only if the form was used correctly. Word family map (open): check each column for the right part of speech and a real negative.

Prefix/Suffix Race (classroom materials, set D)

Round 1 · Flip it: (T reads possible, legal, regular, honest, agree, fair, correct, kind) impossible · illegal · irregular · dishonest · disagree · unfair · incorrect · unkind. Round 2 · accepted words (1 point each): impossible, possibly, possibility · illegal, legally, illegally, legality, legalize · irregular, regularly, irregularly, regularity · dishonest, honestly, dishonestly · disagree, agreement, disagreement, agreeable, disagreeable · unfair, fairly, unfairly, fairness, unfairness · incorrect, correctly, incorrectly, correction, correctness · misunderstand, understandable · overcook, undercook, precook, cooker · repay, overpay, underpay, prepay, payment, repayment, overpayment, payable, nonpayment · rewrite, writer · review, preview, viewer, reviewer · careful, careless, carefully, carelessly, carefulness, carelessness · hopeful, hopeless, hopefully, hopelessly, hopelessness · helpful, helpless, unhelpful, helpfully, helplessly, helper, helpfulness · useful, useless, usefully, user, reuse, misuse, overuse, usable, reusable, unusable, usefulness · unkind, kindness, kindly, unkindly, unkindness · creation, creative, creatively, creativity, recreate, uncreative · reliable, unreliable, reliably, reliability · dangerous, dangerously. Round 3 · Fit it (Script 6.1-B): 1 improvement · 2 unreliable · 3 creativity · 4 strengthen · 5 competitive · 6 misunderstood · 7 carelessly · 8 employees.

Self-check
Q1
"She handled the crisis very ___."
A) decision
B) decisive
C) decisively
Why: the gap describes how she handled it (a verb), so it needs an adverb (-ly). Decision is a noun; decisive is an adjective and would describe a noun.
Q2
Which negative form of responsible is correct?
A) unresponsible
B) irresponsible
C) imresponsible
Why: in- becomes ir- before an r: irregular, irresponsible. im- is only for m and p.
Q3 · new
"Your help was ___." (= extremely useful)
A) valueless
B) invaluable
C) unvaluable
Why: invaluable is a "surprise" word: the in- does not mean "not"; it means too valuable to measure. Valueless means worthless (the opposite), and "unvaluable" doesn't exist.
Errors you will hear most (6.1)

"a success project" · "He explained very clear" · "unpossible," "inlegal," "unresponsible" · "disfortunate," "discomfortable" (Spanish / Portuguese des-) · "carefull / successfull / hopefuly" · "informations," "advices" · "The team was disappointing" (= disappointed) · the same stress for the whole family (PHOtography, eCOnomic) · invented forms ("helpness," "beautiless"). Correct the part of speech every time; treat stress as a listening focus first.

6.2 · Advanced Comparison (C18)

Exercise 1 — Complete the comparison

1 cheaper · 2 as (or so) · 3 as · 4 as · 5 warmer · 6 harder · 7 to … from · 8 more carefully.

Also correct: 7 different than (common in American English; different from is the safe standard). Wrong: 1 "more cheap" · 4 "than" · 7 "similar with" · 8 "carefuller" / "more careful" (an adverb is needed: how to drive).

Exercise 2 — The more…, the more…

1 the more · 2 The harder · 3 better · 4 less · 5 bigger · 6 less.

4 "more" is grammatical but changes the meaning; accept it only if the learner means it. 6 traffic is uncountable, so less (not "fewer"). Personal endings are open: check the two thes and two comparatives ("The more I cook, the more I enjoy it.").

Exercise 3 — How big is the difference? (Script 6.2-A)
123456
big (far)small (slightly)much less (nowhere near as)almost the same (not quite as)big (considerably)equal (as… as)
Exercise 4 — Rewrite it
  1. The new model is far / much / a lot / considerably better than the old one.
  2. The blue jacket is slightly cheaper than the black one.
  3. The more you sleep, the better you feel.
  4. The movie was not nearly as good as the book. (or: wasn't nearly as good as)
  5. The Kestrel is twice as expensive as the Nova. (or: The Nova is half as expensive as the Kestrel.)
  6. Rent is getting more and more expensive (every year). (or: higher and higher)

Wording may vary; the structure must match. In 1, "very better" is wrong. In 5, "two times more expensive" is heard in real English but is not the target here.

Exercise 5 — Find the mistake
  1. This one is much / far cheaper.
  2. Your bag is the same as mine.
  3. He runs faster than his brother. (or more quickly)
  4. Millbrook is smaller than Riverton.
  5. The more you try, the better it gets.
  6. The Kestrel is twice as expensive as the Nova.
Dialogue — the comparisons

far quicker (big difference) · nowhere near as pretty (much less) · twice as long (multiple) · The earlier we leave, the less traffic we'll hit (parallel) · more and more beautiful (trend) · the farther south you go (the second half of a parallel: accept further) · slightly slower (small) · a lot more · a little more · not quite as cheap (almost) · the same car, the same trip · a much better view · The earlier we set off, the better. That's 10+ structures; any count of 8 or more is a good answer.

Speaking — Compare & Recommend (classroom materials, set E)

Target sentences the numbers are built for: Phones: The Kestrel is twice as expensive as the Nova ($640 / $320). The Kestrel is only slightly heavier (185 g / 180 g). The Kestrel has twice as much storage. The Nova's battery lasts a bit longer (48 h / 46 h). The Kestrel's camera is far better (48 MP / 12 MP: four times as many megapixels). Jobs: The warehouse job pays slightly more ($21.00 / $20.50). The delivery job's commute is far shorter; the warehouse commute is more than four times as long (45 / 10 min). The delivery job has nowhere near as much paid training (3 days / 2 weeks). Towns: Riverton is fifteen times as big as Millbrook (900,000 / 60,000). Rent in Riverton is considerably higher but not quite twice as high ($1,500 / $950). Buses in Millbrook are nowhere near as frequent (every hour / every 10 minutes). Rent in Riverton has become more and more expensive ($900 in 2018 → $1,500). Recommendations are open: mark the reasoning and the language, not the choice. Model: "For a student on a tight budget, I'd recommend the Nova. It's half as expensive as the Kestrel, and it's only slightly smaller. The Kestrel's camera is far better, but the more you pay, the less you have for books. So overall, the Nova is much better value."

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) This one is very more expensive.
B) This one is far more expensive.
C) This one is more expensive as that.
Why: far / much intensify a comparative; very can't. C mixes up than and as: after more, use than.
Q2
"___ you practice, the more confident you become."
A) The more
B) More
C) The much
Why: both halves need the + a comparative. B drops the; much in C is not a comparative.
Q3 · new
$320 vs $640. Which sentence is correct?
A) The Kestrel is twice more expensive than the Nova.
B) The Kestrel is twice as expensive than the Nova.
C) The Kestrel is twice as expensive as the Nova.
Why: multiples go with as… as. A is common in speech but unclear (twice more could mean three times the price); B mixes as and than.
Errors you will hear most (6.2)

"very cheaper" / "very more expensive" · "more better," "more faster" · "bigger that" (Spanish / Portuguese que) · "as big than" (French que) · "the same than / the same like" · "More you practice, better you get" · "two times more expensive than" · "It's cheap than mine" · "The more you practice, the more you get better" (acceptable meaning; recast to the better you get) · fewer vs less with uncountable nouns (less traffic). For range, notice learners who use only much: push considerably, slightly, nowhere near as.

6.3 · Indirect & Negative Questions (C19)

Exercise 1 — The front-desk call (Script 6.3-A)

The caller wants to join the gym (and find out about a beginners' class). Questions: 1 what time the gym opens · 2 how much a monthly membership costs · 3 whether there are any classes for beginners · 4 if towels are provided · 5 where I can park (/ where he or she can park) · 6 whether I can try a class for free.

Accept the full question with its opener. Accept if / whether either way. The receptionist's negative question at the end, "Isn't the annual plan better value, though?", is an upsell (persuading, falling tone).

Exercise 2 — Make it indirect

1 is · 2 opens (drop does) · 3 cost (drop did; cost is the same in the past) · 4 if / whether · 5 whether / if · 6 left (drop did; the past moves onto the verb).

Exercise 3 — Rewrite as polite questions
  1. Could you tell me where the nearest bank is?
  2. Do you know what time the movie starts?
  3. I was wondering if / whether this seat is free. (question mark also accepted)
  4. Would you mind telling me how long the trip takes?
  5. Do you have any idea when my sofa will arrive?
  6. Could you tell me whether or not you deliver on weekends? / …if you deliver on weekends or not?

Wrong: "…where is the nearest bank?" · "…what time does the movie start?" · "…if or not you deliver" (with if, or not goes at the end). In 5, will stays (it's not do / does / did); only the order changes.

Exercise 4 — Negative questions

A. 1 Didn't · 2 Isn't · 3 Wouldn't it be easier to take the train? (also: Isn't it easier…? / Don't you think it would be easier…?) · 4 Doesn't the store close at six? (also: Isn't the store closing… / Doesn't it close…)

B. 5 Yes, I did. · 6 No, I haven't. · 7 No, it isn't. · 8 Yes, I do. (I love it!)

Intonation for the pair work: 1 ↗ (surprise) · 2 ↘ (admire, agree with me) · 3 ↘ (suggestion) · 4 ↗ (checking a belief). The key teaching point in B: yes = true / happened, whatever the question looks like. "No, I did" and "Yes, I didn't" are always wrong.

Exercise 5 — Subject or object? (Script 6.3-C)
  1. Who opened the fridge at nine? (subject: no did)
  2. What did the team buy? (object)
  3. What happened at twelve? (subject)
  4. Who did Ms. Tran call? (object; Whom is very formal: not needed)
  5. Who called a meeting? (subject)
  6. broke · 7 happened

Wrong: "Who did open the fridge?" · "What did happen?" · "What the team bought?" (object questions need did and question order).

Dialogue — Lakeview Community College

Indirect questions: I was wondering if you still had places… · Could you tell me when the class starts? · do you know how much it costs? · Would you mind telling me whether I need to take a test first? Negative questions: Didn't you see the price on our website? ↗ (surprise; answered by the facts: "No, I didn't.") · Don't you think it would be easier to register online? ↘ (suggestion) · isn't it better to pay in person? ↘ or ↗ (the caller is persuading or checking: both readings are fine). Note: had in "if you still had places" is a polite past, not a real past time; mention it only if learners ask.

Speaking — Polite Enquiry role-play (classroom materials, set G)

Open task: mark with the observer checklist (6 indirect questions, statement order, no do / did, if / whether, a negative question, polite voice). Model for the gym card: "Hi, I was wondering if you could help me. Could you tell me what time the pool opens on Saturdays? … Do you know whether there are lessons for adults? … Would you mind telling me how much a family membership costs? … Isn't it cheaper if we pay for the year? … Do you have any idea where I can buy a lock for the locker? … And could you tell me if I need to bring a photo?" Model interview lines: Interviewer: "Could you tell me what you enjoy most about working with customers?" · "Didn't you say you'd trained new staff at your last job?" (Candidate: "Yes, I did. I trained four new servers.") · "Haven't you worked weekends before?" (per the card: "No, I haven't, but I'm happy to."). Candidate: "Could you tell me what a typical shift looks like?" · "I was wondering whether there's any paid training." · "Do you know when you'll make a decision?"

Self-check
Q1
Which indirect question is correct?
A) Could you tell me where does the station is?
B) Could you tell me where is the station?
C) Could you tell me where the station is?
Why: after the opener, use statement order (subject + verb: the station is). B keeps the question order; A adds a does that doesn't belong.
Q2
Which is a correct subject question?
A) Who broke the window?
B) Who did broke the window?
C) Who did break the window?
Why: who is the doer, so there is no auxiliary: the past verb broke is enough. B doubles the past. C is only possible as a strong, emphatic question ("Well, who DID break it, then?"); it is not the normal form.
Q3 · new
"Didn't you get my message?" You did get it.
A) "No, I did."
B) "Yes, I did."
C) "Yes, I didn't."
Why: English answers by the facts. You got it, so yes, and the short answer must match: Yes, I did. A and C mix a yes/no with the opposite short answer.
Errors you will hear most (6.3)

"Could you tell me where is the station?" · "Do you know what time does it open?" · "Do you know is the shop open?" (no if) · "…if or not they're open" · "Who did call you?" / "What did happen?" · "Didn't you go?" "Yes." (meaning I didn't) · "No, I did" · a flat, falling voice on a polite question (sounds impatient) · negative questions used as complaints ("Didn't you read my email?" ↘). Correct word order every time; give tone feedback as a model ("Listen: Could you tell me when it closes? ↗"), never as a judgment of the learner's personality.
