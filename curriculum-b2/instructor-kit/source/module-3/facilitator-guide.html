Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 3 — Advanced Structures

Three classes that take structures learners met at B1 (the passive, relative clauses, reported speech) and turn them into tools for precision and economy. First, the passive across every tense and modal, plus the impersonal reporting passive of news and formal writing (It is said that… · He is thought to have…) and a first look at the causative (I had my car repaired). Then relative clauses reduced to participle clauses (the man standing there · a letter written in haste · Having finished, she left) and the dangling-participle trap. Last, precise reporting verbs and the pattern each one demands (suggested going · insisted on paying · denied taking · refused to sign).

3 lessons Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C7–C9 Weeks 4–5 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (a real communicative task) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-advanced-structures.html. Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C7. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Newsroom A/B story cards, the Editor's Desk drafts and combine-and-reduce strips, the Spot the Difference scene cards, the Meeting role cards and minutes sheet, the Report It! board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. At B1 learners met the basic passive (B1 Module 4), relative clauses with commas (B1 3.3) and reported speech with backshift (B1 3.1–3.2). Don't re-teach those. Check them fast in each lead-in and spend the time on what is new at B2: modal-perfect and impersonal passives, reduced clauses and participle clauses, and verb + pattern reporting. The causative gets a short first look in C7. Lesson 5.2 (C15) teaches it fully, so keep it to five minutes here.
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes in two columns: accuracy (errors to fix) and range (good, ambitious language to praise and spread). Don't stop a newsreader or a meeting to fix a pattern. In the feedback stage, write 2–3 errors and 1–2 "range" examples on the board without names. At B2, feedback on range matters as much as feedback on errors: "Someone said It is widely believed that…. Excellent. Who else can use widely?"
  • Dominant speakers. All three tasks have built-in roles. Use them. Give the strongest speaker the job that needs listening rather than talking: the editor in the newsroom (checks, doesn't read), the word-counter in the compression workshop, the minute-taker in the meeting. Use a talking-turn token (a pen: you speak only when you hold it) in the meeting. Say it kindly: "Great point, Omar. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Each lesson has a stretch box. The C1 direction for this module is choice and register, not new forms: why the passive here, when not to reduce a clause, which reporting verb carries the most exact meaning (claimed vs admitted vs insisted). Ask strong learners to explain the choice, not only produce the form.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and words (analysed, apologise, organise, memorise, signalling, cancelled, queue, resit, film). Teach analyzed, apologize, organize, memorize, signaling, canceled, line (up), retake, movie. Also teach the American pattern suggest / insist / recommend that + base verb (She suggested that we meet on Friday · He insisted that we stay for dinner). Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Shift the focus → cut the words → name the act. In C7 learners move the doer out of the spotlight and speak in the calm voice of the news: The tortoise is thought to be 120 years old. In C8 they compress: two or three short sentences become one tight one (Built in 1890, the station is the oldest building in town.). In C9 they compress other people's words: a whole line of speech becomes one exact verb (She refused to sign. He admitted forgetting.). Every class ends with a real product that other learners hear or read: a radio bulletin, an edited notice, a set of meeting minutes.

Trauma-informed: light news, low stakes

The language of this module lives close to hard topics: news of disasters and crime, He is believed to have been killed, She had her identity stolen, accusations, denials, interviews. In class, keep everything light and invented. Use only the invented stories on the Newsroom cards (a 120-year-old tortoise, a piano in the train station, a library book returned 50 years late). Don't bring in real headlines, and don't ask learners to report news from their home countries. In the causative, prefer services (had my hair cut, got my phone fixed) over misfortunes; if you teach the misfortune meaning, use had my flight canceled, not theft or violence. The meetings in C9 are about a laundry room, a café schedule and a community garden. Never role-play a police interview, a court, an immigration or asylum interview, or a family argument. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card, or something invented. Opinion roles in the meeting are assigned: learners play the card, not themselves.

  • For C8: one Editor's Desk draft per group of 3 (4 different drafts), one envelope of combine-and-reduce strips per pair, and one Spot the Difference A/B pair per pair of learners.

The Passive — Advanced & Impersonal

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the passive in any tense or with any modal by changing only be: is done, was being done, has been done, will be done, must be done, should have been done
  • report a general belief without naming who believes it, in two ways: Pattern A It is said / believed / expected that… and Pattern B She is said to be…

  • choose the passive on purpose for a formal or news register, and switch back to the active when the doer matters

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: why the passive. The passive moves the doer out of the spotlight. We use it when the doer is unknown, obvious or unimportant, or when we want to keep the topic in the subject position. It is the normal voice of news, science and official notices.

"The road has been closed." Do we know who closed it? (No / not important.) What's the important thing? (The road: you can't use it.) · "The novel was written by a teenager." Why add "by a teenager"? (It's surprising: real information.)

Meaning: the modal perfect passive.

"The form should have been sent yesterday." Was it sent? (No.) Is this about the past or the future? (The past.) Is it a criticism? (Yes, a mild one.) · "The package might have been delivered to the wrong address." Are we sure? (No, it's a possibility.)

Meaning: impersonal reporting passives. We report a general belief or claim without naming who believes it. It sounds neutral and careful: the reporter doesn't say it's true.

"The tortoise is thought to be 120." Who thinks so? (People / experts: we don't say.) Is the reporter 100% sure? (No.) Is it a fact or a belief? (A belief.) · "It is expected that the station will reopen in May." Has it reopened? (No.) Is it certain? (Not completely: it's the plan.)

Meaning: timing in Pattern B.

"He is said to be rich." Is he rich now? (Yes, people say so now.) · "He is said to have been rich." Now or before? (Before: he was rich in the past.) · "She is thought to be living in Florida." Right now? (Yes, in progress.) · "The piano is believed to have been left by a musician." Did the leaving happen before people started believing it? (Yes.) Did the musician leave it, or did the piano leave? (The musician left it: passive.)

Meaning: the causative.

"I had my hair cut." Did I cut it? (No, a hairdresser did.) Did I pay / arrange it? (Yes.) · "I had cut my hair." Who cut it? (I did, before another past moment. Different meaning!) · "I got my phone fixed." Same meaning as "had"? (Yes, more informal.)

Form.

Any tenseform of be + past participle (only be changes)It has been done. · It was being done.
Modalsmodal + be + pp · modal + have been + ppIt must be done. · It should have been done.
Pattern AIt + passive reporting verb + that + clauseIt is believed that she is honest.
Pattern BSubject + passive reporting verb + to-infinitiveShe is believed to be honest.
B · timingsame time: to be / to do · in progress: to be doing · earlier: to have done / to have beenHe is thought to have left the country.
Causativehave / get + object + past participleWe had the roof repaired. · I'm getting my hair cut.

Pronunciation. In the passive, be and been are weak and the past participle carries the stress: "It's been closed" /ɪts bɪn ˈkloʊzd/, "It should've been done" /ʃʊdəv bɪn ˈdʌn/ (the have is only /əv/: never write "should of"). In Pattern A, It is is often "It's": "It's said that…"; in formal news reading, keep it full and slow. Pattern B: stress the reporting verb and the key word: "He's thought to be a millionaire." Newsreader voice: steady pace, falling intonation at the end of each sentence, a short pause after the place and time ("In Riverside today | a mystery piano…").

Drill script
  1. Up the ladder (point at the board): T: "They clean the room. Present perfect passive?" → S: "The room has been cleaned." T: "Past continuous?" → S: "The room was being cleaned." T: "Should, past?" → S: "The room should have been cleaned." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. A to B: T: "It is said that the castle is haunted." → S: "The castle is said to be haunted." T: "It is believed that the economy is recovering." → S: "The economy is believed to be recovering." T: "It is known that she was a brilliant scientist." → S: "She is known to have been a brilliant scientist."
  3. Now or earlier? T gives only the timing clue: T: "He's rich. Now." → S: "He is said to be rich." T: "He stole the money. Earlier." → S: "He is said to have stolen the money." T: "She's living in Florida. Right now." → S: "She is thought to be living in Florida."
  4. Newsroom voice: T reads a chatty line, S says it on air: T: "Everyone thinks the tortoise is about 120." → S: "The tortoise is thought to be about 120 years old." / "It is thought that…"
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 gives a chatty rumor about an invented person or place ("People say the new bakery uses a secret recipe."); S2 reports it on air in Pattern A; S3 in Pattern B. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
News Report Rewrite — how to run it
  • Pairs. Give each pair one Newsroom story: learner A gets the "What people are saying" card (chatty rumors: "Everyone says…", "Apparently…", "People think…"), learner B gets the "Official facts" card (what the town, the zoo or the library has confirmed, with plans and dates). Don't let them show the cards.
  • Share (3 min): A and B tell each other their half, in normal spoken English. They take notes.
  • Rewrite (7 min): together they write a six-sentence bulletin on the frame: a headline, then the story. Required: at least 2 impersonal passives (one Pattern A, one Pattern B), at least 1 modal passive (must be, will be, should have been) and at least 1 other tense (has been, was being). The frame has a tick-box for each. Rumors become Pattern A / B; facts become ordinary passives.
  • On air (6 min): each pair reads its bulletin in a newsreader voice (one sentence each, alternating). Ring a bell or say "You're on air." Listeners ask one follow-up question, which the pair answers in character.
  • Roles: in a group of 3, the third learner is the editor: checks the tick-boxes and the to be / to have been timing but doesn't read on air. Give this job to a dominant speaker.
  • All the stories are invented and light. Say before you start: "These are not real places. Nobody gets hurt in these stories."
ICQs before the News Report Rewrite
Can you show your card to your partner? (No, tell them.) · How many sentences in the bulletin? (Six.) · How many "It is said that / is said to" sentences? (At least two.) · The rumors: passive reporting verb or ordinary passive? (Reporting verb: "is said to…") · Who reads on air? (Both, one sentence each.) · Are these real stories? (No, invented.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on Pattern B with to be and three verbs only: said, thought, believed. Give a sentence strip: "The ___ is said to be ___. · The ___ is thought to be ___." In the newsroom, pair the learner with a supportive partner and make them the reader of 2 sentences (after practicing them with you). Fewer items in the workbook: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 3 items 1–2. Target: 2 correct impersonal passives, said aloud.

Stretch (toward C1)

Add adverbs of attitude and hedging: widely believed, generally accepted, reportedly, allegedly. Use Pattern B with the passive infinitive: The station is expected to be reopened in May. Ask: "Is the passive the best choice in every sentence of your bulletin? Change one back to the active and say why." Early finishers turn their bulletin into a two-sentence "spoken" version for a friend and compare the registers.

L1 watch-outs
  • No dummy "It": "Is said that he is rich." Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and Arabic don't need a subject here (se dice que, diz-se que, si dice che, yuqāl inna). Point at the empty subject position: "English needs a subject: It is said…"
  • Mixing A and B: "It is said him to be rich." / "He is said that he is rich." Common for everyone. Show the two patterns as two different doors: It… that or He… to, never both.
  • "They say" / "One says" everywhere: Russian and Polish (govoryat, mówią), German (man sagt) and French (on dit) report beliefs with an active impersonal subject. That's fine in speech. In the bulletin, ask for the passive.
  • Missing be / been: "The bridge built in 1890." (as a whole sentence) · "It should have done." Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers often drop the auxiliary. Underline be in a different color on the ladder.
  • The passive = something bad: Chinese (被 bèi), Korean and Japanese passives often suggest an unpleasant event, so some learners avoid the passive for neutral news. Show neutral examples ("The results are expected to improve").
  • "I cut my hair" for a haircut: Spanish me corté el pelo, Portuguese cortei o cabelo, Italian, Tagalog and others say "I cut my hair" when a hairdresser did it. In English that means you held the scissors. Teach "I had / got my hair cut."

3.2 · C8

Relative & Participle Clauses

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • tell defining clauses (no commas, that OK) from non-defining clauses (commas, no that) and know which ones can be reduced
  • reduce a defining relative clause to a participle: active → -ing (the man standing there), passive → past participle (the letter written by John), using the test "Does the noun do the action or receive it?"
  • join two sentences with a participle clause that shows time (Walking home, I…), reason (Feeling tired, … · Not knowing…, …) or sequence (Having finished, …)
  • find and fix a dangling participle by checking that the participle and the main clause have the same subject
  • decide when not to reduce: single finished past actions, non-defining clauses in careful writing, and relaxed speech
Materials
  • Workbook 3.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Sentence Compression Workshop)
  • Slides 8–12 · Participle flashcards (-ing = does · -ed / 3rd form = receives · having + pp · Not + -ing) · Place picture cards · Editor's Desk drafts (4) · Combine-and-reduce strips (1 envelope per pair) · Spot the Difference A/B scenes · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 3.2-A (Old Town audio tour) and 3.2-B ("Dangler or not?")

Left: reduce it. The man who is standing there · The woman who lives living next door · The letter which was written by John · Test: does the noun DO it? → -ing · RECEIVE it? → past participle. The books selling ✘ sold here ✔.

Center: join it. ⏱ time: Walking home, I saw… · 💡 reason: Feeling tired, he went to bed. · Not knowing the way, we got lost. · Made of glass, it breaks easily. · ➡️ sequence: Having finished, she left.

Right: the subject rule. Walking home, the rain started. ✘ (the rain walked?) · Walking home, I got caught in the rain. ✔ · Check the first noun after the comma. Don't reduce: the man who called me yesterday (one finished action) · My sister, who lives in Ohio, … (careful writing).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: reduction. A reduced clause says exactly the same thing as the full clause, with fewer words. Neither is "more correct." The reduced one is tighter and more written; the full one is more relaxed and more spoken.

"The man standing by the door is my uncle." Which man? (The one by the door.) Is he standing there now? (Yes.) Full version? (The man who is standing by the door.) · "The car parked outside has been there all week." Did the car park itself? (No, someone parked it: passive → parked.) Full version? (The car which is / was parked outside.)
Does or receives? "The people ___ outside look cold." (wait: they do it → waiting) · "The paintings ___ in 2004 were found." (steal: they received it → stolen) · "The books ___ here are second-hand." (sell: do the books sell? No, they are sold → sold.)

Meaning: when not to reduce. -ing reduction works for something in progress, a habit or a state (the woman living next door), not for one finished action in the past.

"The man who called me yesterday was angry." Can I say "the man calling me yesterday"? (No: one finished call. Keep who called.) · "Passengers who are traveling with children may board first." Reduce? (Yes: Passengers traveling with children.) · "My sister, who lives in Ohio, is a nurse." Reduce in careful writing? (No: keep the commas and who.)

Meaning: participle clauses for time, reason and sequence.

"Feeling tired, he went to bed." Why did he go to bed? (Because he felt tired.) · "Walking home, I saw an accident." When did I see it? (While I was walking home: same time.) · "Having finished work, she went home." Which happened first? (Finishing work.) Could we say "After finishing work"? (Yes.) · "Not knowing the way, we got lost." Did we know the way? (No.) Where does not go? (At the start.)

Meaning: the subject rule.

"Reading the letter, tears filled her eyes." Who is reading, grammatically? (The tears!) Can tears read? (No: it's a dangler.) How do we fix it? ("Reading the letter, she began to cry." or "While she was reading the letter, tears filled her eyes.")

Form.

Active → -ingwho / which / that + verb → verb-ingThe train that leaves at noon → The train leaving at noon
Passive → ppwho / which / that + be + pp → ppThe bridge which was built in 1890 → The bridge built in 1890
Time / reason-ing clause, comma, main clause (same subject)Driving to work, he heard the news.
Reason (passive)pp clause, comma, main clauseMade of glass, it breaks easily.
SequenceHaving + pp, main clause · After + -ingHaving lost the map, they turned back.
NegativeNot + participleNot having a plan, we improvised.

Pronunciation. A fronted participle clause is its own tone unit: pause at the comma and let the voice rise slightly, then fall at the end of the main clause: "Feeling tired ↗ | he went to bed ↘." The -ed of reduced passives follows the usual rule: /t/ parked, finished · /d/ closed, delivered · /ɪd/ painted, located, surrounded; irregular forms (built, written, stolen, sold) need practice. In "Having finished," having is short and the participle takes the stress: /hævɪŋ ˈfɪnɪʃt/.

Drill script
  1. Cut it: T: "The man who is standing there." → S: "The man standing there." T: "The woman who lives next door." → S: "The woman living next door." T: "The letter which was written by John." → S: "The letter written by John." T: "Products that are made locally." → S: "Products made locally."
  2. Do or receive? (point at the -ing / pp cards): T: "books · sell · here" → S: "books sold here." T: "people · wait · outside" → S: "people waiting outside." T: "a car · park · outside" → S: "a car parked outside." T: "students · take · the test" → S: "students taking the test."
  3. Join it: T: "She didn't feel well. She stayed home." → S: "Not feeling well, she stayed home." T: "They had finished dinner. They went for a walk." → S: "Having finished dinner, they went for a walk."
  4. Dangler doctor: T: "Running for the bus, my bag fell open." → S: "Running for the bus, I dropped my bag." / "As I ran for the bus, my bag fell open."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 gives two short facts about a place in town (real or invented): "The market is near the river. It is open on Sundays." S2 joins them: "Located near the river, the market is open on Sundays." Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Sentence Compression Workshop — how to run it
  • Round 1: strips (pairs, 6 min). Each envelope has 12 strips; each strip holds 2–3 short sentences. Pairs join each set into one sentence with a reduced clause or a participle clause, and say it aloud before they write it. The target sentence is on your answer key (materials, set D); accept any correct version.
  • Round 2: Editor's Desk (groups of 3, 10 min). Each group gets a wordy draft of about 100 words (A: a community center notice, B: a museum label, C: a mini-biography of an invented local baker, D: an apartment ad). The "editor-in-chief" says: 60 words or fewer, no fact lost, at least 4 participle clauses, no danglers. Roles: a writer, a word-counter (counts after each sentence and says "over / under"), a fact-checker (ticks each fact on the draft as it survives). Give the word-counter job to a dominant speaker.
  • Round 3: read-out (4 min). Each group reads its version. Class votes on "clearest," not "shortest." Anyone who hears a dangler says "Dangler!" and the group fixes it.
  • Honesty rule: if a reduced sentence sounds worse, un-reduce it. "Learning when not to reduce is a B2 skill too." Praise a group that explains why they kept one full clause.
  • If time is short, skip Round 1 and go straight to the Editor's Desk. Spot the Difference (A/B scenes, set E) is the spoken extension: A describes a street scene using reduced clauses ("a man wearing a red hat standing at the bus stop"), B finds 5 differences. Use it for early finishers or as the C9 warmer.
ICQs before the workshop
Strips: how many sentences at the end? (One.) · Say it first or write it first? (Say it first.) · Editor's Desk: maximum words? (Sixty.) · Can you delete a fact? (No! Every fact stays.) · How many participle clauses? (At least four.) · Who counts the words? (The word-counter.) · Which version wins: the shortest or the clearest? (The clearest.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on the reduction move only (no fronted participle clauses yet): who is -ing → -ing, which was + pp → pp. Give the does / receives cards to hold during Ex 2. In Round 1, give the learner 6 strips instead of 12 and let them cross out words with a pencil instead of rewriting. In the Editor's Desk, make them the fact-checker (tick the facts) rather than the writer. Target: 4 correct reductions, said aloud.

Stretch (toward C1)

Use participle clauses in the middle and end of sentences: "The storm hit the coast, leaving thousands without power" (result). Use having been + pp: "Having been told the news, she called her sister." In the Editor's Desk, aim for 50 words and justify one clause they chose not to reduce. Early finishers rewrite the "Old Town" script as a relaxed spoken version and compare the registers.

L1 watch-outs
  • -ing for passive meaning: "the books selling here," "a house building in 1900." Very common for all L1s. Always ask the does / receives question.
  • Loose participle clauses (danglers): Spanish and Portuguese use the gerundio / gerúndio more freely, with a different subject ("Llegando a casa, el teléfono sonó"), and French en + participe is sometimes used loosely too. Danglers are also common in native-speaker writing, so present them as a writing check, not a sign of a weak learner.
  • Modifier before the noun: Chinese, Japanese and Korean put the whole describing phrase before the noun ("the at-the-door-standing man"), so learners may avoid post-noun participles. Praise every attempt.
  • Extra pronoun: "The man standing there he is my uncle." Arabic, Hebrew, Persian and some Chinese speakers add a resumptive pronoun. Cross it out.
  • Irregular past participles: "the bridge builded," "the paintings stealed," "the letter wrote by John." Keep an irregular-verb list handy; Spanish and Tagalog speakers may pronounce -ed as an extra syllable (/ˈpɑrkɛd/).

Reporting Verbs & Patterns

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • report the function of what someone said (an offer, a refusal, an apology, a denial, a suggestion) with one precise verb instead of said / told
  • use each verb with its pattern: + to-infinitive (agree, refuse, offer, promise), + object + to-infinitive (advise, warn, remind, persuade), + (that) clause (admit, explain, insist, deny), + -ing (deny, admit, suggest, recommend), + preposition + -ing (apologize for, accuse sb of, insist on, blame sb for)
  • avoid the classic B2 errors: suggested me to…, apologized to be…, accused me to…, reminded to call (no object)
  • keep the backshift inside that-clauses, and use suggest / insist that + base verb for proposals and demands
  • write and read aloud accurate meeting minutes with at least six different reporting verbs
Materials
  • Workbook 3.3 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Meeting Minutes sheet)
  • Slides 13–18 · Reporting-verb cards (color-coded by pattern) · Meeting role cards (3 meetings × 4 roles) + minutes sheets · Report It! board game (early finishers / C10 warmer) · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 3.3-A (the Riverside Apartments residents' meeting) and 3.3-B (dictation)

Left: vague → precise. She said she'd help. → She offered to help. · He said he didn't take it. → He denied taking it. · She said sorry for being late. → She apologized for being late.

Center: five patterns (five colors). ① + to: agree, refuse, offer, promise, threaten · ② + person + to: advise, ask, warn, remind, persuade, invite · ③ + (that): admit, explain, claim, deny, insist, complain · ④ + -ing: deny, admit, suggest, recommend, mention · ⑤ + prep + -ing: apologize for, accuse sb of, insist on, blame sb for, congratulate sb on, warn sb against.

Right: the meeting six. suggested going / suggested (that) we go · insisted on paying / insisted (that) she had paid · denied taking · admitted forgetting / (that) he had forgotten · agreed to help · refused to sign. Trap: suggested me to ✘ · recommended me to ✘.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: reporting the function. A reporting verb tells the listener what the speaker was doing with the words (offering, refusing, admitting), so we don't repeat the words themselves. It also shows the reporter's interpretation, so choose carefully: claimed suggests you doubt it; admitted suggests it was hard to say.

"I'll carry that for you." Is he giving an order or an offer? (An offer.) → He offered to carry it. · "No, I won't sign." → She… (refused to sign.) Do we need "not"? (No: refuse is already negative.) · "OK, it was my mistake." Was that easy to say? (No.) → He admitted (that) it was his mistake / admitted making the mistake.

Meaning: the meeting six.

suggest: "Why don't we change the day?" Is it an order? (No, an idea.) Who will change it? (We, all of us.) → She suggested changing the day / suggested (that) we change the day. Can I say "suggested us to change"? (No!)
insist: "No, really, I'll pay. I insist." Did he accept a "no"? (No.) → He insisted on paying. · "I paid! I definitely paid." → She insisted (that) she had paid. Is this about a future action or a fact? (A fact: she is sure it's true.)
deny vs refuse: "I didn't take the key." → He denied taking the key. Is it about the past or the future? (The past: he says it didn't happen.) · "I won't give you the key." → He refused to give me the key. Past or future? (Future: he says no to doing it.) Can I say "He denied to give"? (No: deny is about facts; refuse is about actions.)
admit: "Yes, OK, I forgot to order the soap." → He admitted forgetting / admitted (that) he had forgotten to order the soap. · agree: "Fine, I'll do the Saturday shift." → She agreed to do the Saturday shift. Can I say "She was agree"? (No! agree is a verb: she agreed.)

Form.

① + to-infinitiveagree, offer, promise, refuse, threaten, claim, decideShe refused to sign.
② + object + toadvise, ask, tell, warn, remind, encourage, persuade, invite, orderHe reminded us to lock the door.
③ + (that) clauseadmit, explain, claim, deny, insist, complain, promise, agree (backshift)She admitted (that) she had forgotten.
④ + -ingadmit, deny, suggest, recommend, mention, regretHe denied taking the key.
⑤ + prep + -ingapologize for, accuse sb of, insist on, blame sb for, congratulate sb on, warn sb againstHe insisted on paying.
suggest / insist thatfor a proposal or demand: that + subject + base verb (American) · should + base (also OK)She suggested (that) we meet on Friday. · He insisted that we stay.

Pronunciation. The reporting verb carries the meaning, so stress it: "She refused to sign." "He denied taking it." Word stress to check: suggest /səgˈdʒɛst/ (not SUG-gest), insist, admit, refuse (verb; REF-use /ˈrɛfjus/ is the noun "trash"), apologize, recommend. Past -ed: /d/ in apologized, refused, denied, agreed, accused, offered, warned, blamed · /t/ in promised. The ones to drill are the verbs that add a syllable, /ɪd/: suggested, insisted, admitted, recommended, reminded. Learners often swallow that syllable ("He suggest going"), and then the listener hears the present tense.

Drill script
  1. Pattern snap (hold up a verb card): T: "refuse" → S: "refuse to do." T: "suggest" → S: "suggest doing." T: "remind" → S: "remind someone to do." T: "apologize" → S: "apologize for doing." T: "deny" → S: "deny doing." T: "insist" → S: "insist on doing / insist that…" Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Name the act: T (acting): "Sorry, sorry, I'm late!" → S: "She apologized for being late." T: "I didn't eat your sandwich!" → S: "He denied eating my sandwich." T: "Let's order pizza." → S: "She suggested ordering pizza." T: "OK, I'll bring the drinks." → S: "He agreed to bring the drinks." T: "No. I won't work on Sunday." → S: "She refused to work on Sunday." T: "Yes, I broke the printer." → S: "He admitted breaking the printer."
  3. Two ways: T: "He admitted breaking it." → S: "He admitted that he had broken it." T: "She suggested going out." → S: "She suggested that we go out."
  4. Trap check (T says a wrong one, learners shout the fix): T: "She suggested me to go." → S: "She suggested going! / suggested that I go!" T: "He apologized to be late." → S: "He apologized for being late!"
  5. Chain: S1 says a line of direct speech with a clear function; S2 reports it with a precise verb; S3 names the pattern (① to ⑤). Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Meeting Minutes — how to run it
  • Groups of 4 (or 5 with a minute-taker). Each group gets one meeting and four role cards (A = chair, B, C, D). Each card has the situation, the role and 3 moves, each labeled with a function (SUGGEST · REFUSE · ADMIT · DENY · INSIST · AGREE plus one free move). Learners say the move in their own words in the meeting; they must not say the function word ("I suggest…" is fine; "I refuse!" is fine too, but encourage natural lines like "No, I'm sorry, I can't do that").
  • The meeting (8 min + 4 min buffer): the chair opens ("OK, let's start. The problem is…"), makes sure everyone speaks and closes with a decision ("So we agree that…"). The chair's card has a talking-turn rule: nobody speaks twice before everyone has spoken once.
  • The minutes (10 min): everyone writes their own minutes on the sheet (date, present, the problem, what each person did, the decision). No said or told. At least 6 different reporting verbs. The five-pattern cheat-sheet is on the sheet.
  • Peer check: swap minutes; the partner ticks each verb whose pattern is right and circles any suggested me to. Then one reader per group shares.
  • Minute-taker: in a group of 5, give the fifth learner (or a dominant speaker) the minute-taker job during the meeting. They don't speak until the end, when they read the draft and the group corrects it ("No, I didn't refuse! I agreed to do it on Tuesday!"). This correction round is excellent practice.
  • All roles are assigned. Nobody has to share their own opinions. The stakes are small on purpose (a laundry room, a café schedule, watering tomatoes).

(The chair, card A.) · Can someone speak twice before everyone has spoken once? (No.) · In the minutes, can you write "said"? (No!) · How many different reporting verbs? (At least six.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on four verbs and two patterns: agree to / refuse to (①) and suggest -ing / deny -ing (④). Give a card with four sentence frames: "___ agreed to ___. · ___ refused to ___. · ___ suggested ___-ing. · ___ denied ___-ing." In the meeting, give the learner card D (the shortest moves) and let them read their moves from the card. For the minutes, target 4 sentences with the frames. Fewer items in Ex 3–4.

Stretch (toward C1)

Report attitude as well as function: claimed (the reporter doubts it), conceded, acknowledged, urged, proposed, objected to -ing. Use the passive infinitive and perfect -ing: "She denied having been told." "He admitted having forgotten." In the minutes, add one sentence with an impersonal passive from C7 ("It was agreed that…") and one participle clause from C8 ("Having discussed the options, the committee decided…"). That's the whole module in one text.

L1 watch-outs
  • "suggested me to" / "recommended me to": French suggérer à qqn de, Spanish sugerir a alguien, Italian, Portuguese, German (jdm vorschlagen) and Russian (predlozhit' komu + infinitive) all allow a person + infinitive. The most common error in this lesson for almost everyone. Write it on the board crossed out and keep it there.
  • deny vs refuse: Spanish negarse a and Portuguese negar-se a mean refuse, while negar means deny, so learners say "He denied to pay." Use the past / future CCQ every time.
  • "I am agree" / "She was agree": Spanish estar de acuerdo, French être d'accord, Portuguese estar de acordo. Drill "I agree / She agreed" as a verb.
  • "explained me the problem": Spanish me explicó, French il m'a expliqué, Italian mi ha spiegato. Teach "explained the problem to me" / "explained (to me) that…". Same for "suggested me."
  • Wrong preposition: "insist in" (Spanish insistir en), "accuse for" / "blame of" (mixing), "apologize to be" (French s'excuser de + infinitive). Teach the verb + preposition as one chunk, and the rule "after a preposition → -ing."
  • No backshift in that-clauses: "He admitted that he forgets." Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian and Vietnamese keep the original tense. This is B1 language; recast gently.
