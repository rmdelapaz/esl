Module 1 — Narrating & Looking Back

Three classes that open the B2 level. Learners already know the past simple, the past continuous and the past perfect from B1, plus the first and second conditionals and wish + past simple. The B2 work is layering and control: the four narrative tenses together, including the new past perfect continuous; the third conditional, could have / might have and mixed conditionals; and the "unreal past" family: wish / if only + past perfect, would rather and it's (high) time. Class 1 also welcomes a new group and gives you a quick B1 diagnostic. By the end of the module learners can tell a layered anecdote, trace imagined consequences, and voice regrets and preferences with the right tense.

3 lessons Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-1-narrating.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the flashcards and tense-job cards, the welcome question cards and the B1 diagnostic grid, the new identity cards, the Story Reconstruction strips and retell cards, the Chain of Consequences start and ending cards, the "Would You Rather?" cards, the Wish List Meeting scenario and role cards, a "Find Someone Who" game, and all the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons (with a UK or US voice and a speed control), so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. B1 Lessons 5.1–5.2 taught the past perfect and narrative tenses; B1 2.2–2.3 taught the second conditional and wish + past simple. Go fast through what they know and spend the time on what is new: the past perfect continuous (1.1), the whole third conditional and mixed conditionals (1.2), and wish + past perfect, would rather + someone + past, it's time + past (1.3).
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a tense. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. At B2, give feedback on two things: accuracy (the form was wrong) and range (the form was right, but a richer one was available: "You said I was tired because I worked all day. Correct! Can you make it B2? …because I'd been working all day."). Praise the story or the idea first, then the language.
  • Dominant speakers. B2 classes often have one or two very fluent talkers. Give every group talking tokens (3 each; you spend one per turn), give the strongest speaker a job (the checker, the note-taker, the devil's advocate), and use "retell your partner's idea, not yours." Say it kindly: "Great point, Omar. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Every lesson has a Stretch box. Typical C1 moves in this module: a wider range of linkers (no sooner… than, as soon as, it wasn't until…), hedged results (might well have, could easily have), should have / needn't have for criticism and wasted effort, and I'd sooner / I'd prefer it if you… as alternatives to would rather. Stretch is for range; don't teach it to the whole class.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons often use British forms (film, flat, flatmate, motorway, queuing, neighbours, behaviour, realised, recognised, travelled, organised, practise, and got where American English says gotten). Teach movie, apartment, roommate, highway, waiting in line, neighbors, behavior, realized, recognized, traveled, organized, practice, gotten. Two to point out: British wash up = American do the dishes (in American English wash up means "wash your hands and face"), and British shattered = "exhausted." Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

What happened → what could have happened → what we wish had happened. Every lesson in this module looks back. In C1 learners tell the past in layers: the main events, the background, the earlier events, and how long something had been going on (By the time I arrived, I'd been traveling for six hours and the party had finished). In C2 they rewrite that past: If I'd left earlier, I would have caught the train, and link it to now (…and I wouldn't be so tired today). In C3 they add the feeling: I wish I'd set an alarm. I'd rather you didn't call so late. It's high time we fixed the printer. Each class ends with a longer speaking task: a story retold from a new point of view, a group consequence chain, then a meeting where people argue for their wishes.

Trauma-informed: this module is about regret — keep it small

The third conditional and wish + past perfect are the grammar of regret, and the online lessons invite learners to talk about turning points and regrets (a move is offered as one optional turning point). For many adult learners, the biggest turning points are leaving a country, losing someone, family separation, war, or a missed goodbye. In class, never invite those topics. Don't ask "What do you regret most?", "How would your life be different if you hadn't come here?", "What would have happened if you had stayed?" or "What do you wish you had told your family?" Keep every personal task in everyday territory: small choices (a haircut, a purchase, a movie, a recipe, being late), habits (sleep, phones, exercise), and wishes for the city, the building, the workplace or the classroom. Most freer tasks use invented people and scenarios on purpose. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life (small things), a new identity card, or something invented. Anyone can say "Pass!" Say it every time, so passing is normal.

If a learner brings up something painful anyway: listen, say something short and human ("Thank you for telling us. That sounds really hard."), don't correct their grammar, don't ask follow-up questions, and move the group on gently ("Let's go back to our invented friend Priya…"). Check in privately after class if it feels right. The homework prompts in the workbook are rewritten to stay small; tell learners they don't need the online project's "a move" option.

  • For C2: one set of Chain of Consequences start and ending cards per group of 4, and a stack of blank slips (or sticky notes) for the links.

Welcome to B2 · Narrative Tenses — Past Perfect & Continuous

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • get to know the group and show the teacher their B1 range (narrative tenses, conditionals, discussion skills) in a friendly mingle
  • name the job of each narrative tense: past simple = the main events · past continuous = background in progress · past perfect = an earlier event · past perfect continuous = the duration of an activity before a past moment

If you run long

The welcome mingle is the most important part of C1. Don't cut it: the group needs it, and you need the diagnostic. Cut instead: Ex 4 goes to homework, and the dictation (Script 1.1-B) moves to the C2 warmer. Keep at least 12 minutes for the Story Reconstruction: the retell (step 2) is where the past perfect continuous gets used, so if you must, shorten the class sharing instead.

Running the diagnostic — what to listen for

The mingle is a friendly first conversation, not a test. Learners must never feel watched. Hold the grid on a clipboard, stand near a pair for 30–60 seconds, and join in sometimes ("Really? What had happened before that?"). Mark each learner ✓ (easy and accurate) · ~ (understands, some errors) · ✗ (can't produce it yet) in four columns:

  • NARRATIVE TENSES (the STORY cards): past simple for events, was / were + -ing for background, had + pp for an earlier event. Listen for "When I arrived, the bus already left," "I was knowing," irregular past forms, and stories that stay in the present tense.
  • CONDITIONALS (the IF cards): first (If it rains, I'll…) and second (If I had a free day, I'd…), and I wish I had / could…. Listen for "If I would have…," "If I will…," and the present simple after would ("I would to go").
  • DISCUSSION SKILLS (the OPINION cards): Does the learner give a reason and an example? Agree or disagree politely (I see your point, but…)? Ask the partner a question back? Let the partner finish?
  • RANGE & FLUENCY: long turns (4+ sentences), varied vocabulary and linkers, keeping going when they don't know a word, self-correction. At B2, this column matters as much as accuracy.

What to do with the results: mostly ✓ and ~ = ready for B2. Many ~ in the NARRATIVE column is normal: that is exactly what C1 teaches. Many ✗ in the first two columns and short turns = check the placement privately (the toolkit's oral placement interview takes 5 minutes); the learner may do better in the B1 course, or may need extra support from Day 1. Lots of ✓ and strong range = give the Stretch tasks in every class and consider the C1 course later. Never share the grid with learners. Keep it; compare it with Progress Test 1 in C13.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: four tenses, four jobs. Past simple = the main events, in order (the story's timeline). Past continuous = background in progress around a moment (the scenery). Past perfect = an event before the story moment (the "past of the past"). Past perfect continuous = how long an activity had been going on up to that moment, often explaining a result.

"By the time I arrived, the train had already left." Did I see the train? (No.) Which happened first? (The train left.) · "When I arrived, the train left." Did I see it? (Yes, maybe: I arrived, then it left.) So what does had left tell us? (The order: it left before I got there.)
"His eyes were red. He'd been crying." Is he crying now, at the story moment? (No, or maybe just stopped.) Was the crying long or short? (Long: an activity over time.) Why are his eyes red? (Because of the crying: it explains the result.)
"She'd written three emails." / "She'd been writing emails all morning." Which one counts the emails? (The first.) Which one is about the activity and how long? (The second.) Can I say "She'd been writing three emails"? (Not naturally: a number = finished results = simple.)
"I'd known him for years." Why not "I'd been knowing him"? (Know is a state, not an activity. States stay simple: know, be, have (own), want, like, believe.)
when vs while: "While I was cooking, the phone rang." Which action was longer? (Cooking.) Which interrupted? (The phone.) Which tense for the long one? (Past continuous.) · "After I finished, I left." Do I need had finished? (No, after already shows the order. Both are OK.)

Form.

Past simpleverb + -ed / irregularShe opened the door.
Past continuouswas / were + -ingThe rain was falling outside.
Past perfecthad + past participle · hadn't · Had you…?Someone had broken the window.
Past perfect continuoushad been + -ing · hadn't been · Had you been…?They'd been arguing for hours.
With for / sincehad been + -ing + for (a length) / since (a start point)By 2020 she'd been teaching for ten years.
Sequencingby the time + past simple → had done · while / as + past continuous · when + past simple · until · already / just between had and the participleI'd just sat down when he called.

Pronunciation. In speech, had becomes 'd: "I'd been waiting" /aɪd bɪn/, and been is weak /bɪn/ in American English (not "bean"). "She'd been working" sounds like "sheed-bin-working." Teach learners to decode 'd: 'd + past participle or been = had (she'd gone, she'd been); 'd + base verb = would (she'd go). In a negative, hadn't is stressed: "I HADn't seen her for years." Storytelling: slow down and drop your voice for the background ("I'd been waiting for an hour…"), then speed up for the main event ("…when suddenly the lights went out!").

Drill script
  1. Why? (T gives a result; learners explain with had been + -ing): T: "He was exhausted. Why? (run)" → S: "Because he'd been running." T: "Her hands were covered in flour. (bake)" → S: "Because she'd been baking." T: "The ground was wet. (rain)" → S: "Because it had been raining." T: "Their eyes were red. (cry / watch a sad movie)" → S: "They'd been watching a sad movie."
  2. Weak forms: choral "I'd been waiting" ×3 (/aɪd bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/), then "She'd been working for hours" ×2, then "We hadn't eaten" (stress on HAD).
  3. Simple or continuous? T: "three books / write" → S: "She'd written three books." T: "all night / write" → S: "She'd been writing all night." T: "for years / know him" → S: "I'd known him for years." (Stop them if they say "been knowing.")
  4. By the time chain: T: "By the time I got to the party…" → S1: "…everyone had left." T: "By the time the pizza arrived…" → S2: "…we'd been waiting for an hour." T: "By the time she called back…" → S3: "…I'd already fallen asleep."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 gives a result ("I was so happy yesterday."); S2 asks "Why? What had happened?"; S1 answers with a past perfect or past perfect continuous (real, invented or from a card). Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Story Reconstruction — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4. Half the groups get Story A · The Wedding Cake (Priya the baker, a changed hotel, a van that dies, a helpful taxi driver); half get Story B · Locked Out (Daniel's first day, a power cut, keys left inside, a helpful neighbor). Each envelope has 8 strips, printed with letters, in a jumbled order.
  • Step 1: order (6 min). Learners lay out the strips and find the order. Tell them the grammar is the clue: By the time… can't be first; had been + -ing often looks back from a later moment; while / when join background and interruption. They read the story aloud in order when they finish. Answers are in the classroom materials and the answer key.
  • Step 2: retell (8 min). Strips face down. The group takes the retell card for their story: Story A from the taxi driver's point of view ("I'd been driving since six that morning when I saw a woman pushing a van…"); Story B from Mrs. Ortiz, the neighbor's point of view ("I was watering my plants when I heard a door slam…"). They take turns, one part each, and use the card's target: two had been + -ing and two sequencing words. The listener on their left ticks the targets on the card.
  • Step 3: share (4 min). One A-group and one B-group retell to the class. The other groups listen for one detail they hadn't heard before.
  • Dominant speakers: in the retell, each learner tells one part at a time (use the order of the strips: 2 strips each). Give the strongest speaker the job of checker (they hold the retell card and tick the targets) in step 2.
  • Both stories are invented and cheerful on purpose. The optional personal step is in the homework ("My anecdote planner"), where learners choose a small real story, a new identity card story, or an invented one.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on two jobs: was / were + -ing (background) and had + pp (earlier). Give a colored strip with two frames: "I was ___ing when ___." / "By the time I ___, ___ had ___." In the reconstruction, give only the 5 strips marked ★ in the materials (the story still works). In the retell, the learner tells the first part (the easiest) and uses the pictures on the retell card. Target: 3 layered spoken sentences, one with had been + -ing using the drill frame "…because I'd been ___ing."

Stretch (toward C1)

Use a wider range of sequencing: as soon as, it wasn't until… that…, no sooner had… than… (recognize it; don't push the inversion on the class). Add emphasis and reaction to the retell: "What I hadn't realized was that…," "To make things worse…". Early finishers do Ex 5 twice: once calm, once dramatic, and compare the verbs they chose. In the retell, the strong learner adds one detail the other character couldn't have known ("…though I found out later that she'd been baking since five").

L1 watch-outs
  • No past perfect at all: "When I arrived, the train already left." Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian/Malay and Japanese mark the order with time words or context, not with the verb. Learners understand the idea; they just don't need it in their language. Draw the timeline every time and ask "Which happened first?"
  • Overusing the past perfect: "Yesterday I had gone to the market and I had bought…" Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian learners sometimes use the pluperfect for any "far" past, and some Arabic and Persian speakers use it for a completed action. Remind them: the past simple is the story's backbone; had done only steps back from it.
  • "I was knowing / I had been knowing": Spanish, Portuguese and Italian imperfect (conocía, sabía) and the Indian-English style "I was having a car" push states into the continuous. Keep the list of state verbs on the board.
  • Slipping into the present perfect inside a past story: "By the time I arrived, the train has left." German, Dutch, French and Italian use their "have + participle" tense for ordinary past narration. The lesson's rule: stay in the past: had left.
  • Aspect confusion: Russian, Polish and Ukrainian speakers map the past continuous onto the imperfective and may use it for completed or repeated actions ("Every summer I was going to the lake"). Show that a finished action in the story line is past simple.
  • /bɪn/: learners who say "bean" /biːn/ for been are understood, but the weak form helps listening. Tagalog, Chinese and Turkish speakers may also mix he / she in the retell: point at the character on the retell card.

1.2 · C2

The Third Conditional — Past Hypotheticals

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the third conditional: If + had + past participle, … would have + past participle, with either clause first (comma after the if-clause)
  • "flip" a third conditional back to reality (If you'd told me, I'd have helped = you didn't tell me; I didn't help) and use it for regret, criticism and relief
  • choose would have (certain), could have (possible) or might have (maybe) in the result, and use You could have…! as a criticism
  • choose the second or third conditional by asking "Can it still change?"
  • build mixed conditionals: past cause → present result (If I'd taken that job, I'd be rich now) and present trait → past result (If I were more careful, I wouldn't have lost it)
  • say would've / could've naturally and write would have, never "would of"
Materials
  • Workbook 1.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "Small turning points")
  • Slides 10–13 · modal cards (would have · could have · might have) and "can it still change?" cards (Set A) · Chain of Consequences start and ending cards (Set E, 1 set per group of 4) + blank slips · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 1.2-A (The picnic that wasn't) and 1.2-B (dictation)

Left: REALITY: I didn't leave early. → I missed the train. · FLIP ↕ · If I had left early, I would have caught the train. Under it: If + had + pp, … would / could / might have + pp · ✘ If I would have known · ✘ would of → would've /ˈwʊdəv/.

Center: "Can it still change?" YES → 2nd: If I had money, I'd travel. · NO → 3rd: If I'd had money, I'd have traveled.

Right: MIXED: (a) past → now: If I had taken that job, I would be rich now. (b) always → past: If I were more careful, I wouldn't have lost my keys. Below: the chain: If A hadn't…, B wouldn't have… → If B hadn't…, C wouldn't have… → … → …and she wouldn't be… today!

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: an imagined past. The third conditional imagines the opposite of what really happened, so it is impossible to change. The real story is the "flip" of both halves.

"If you had told me, I would have helped." Did you tell me? (No.) Did I help? (No.) Can we change it now? (No, it's finished.) How do I feel? (Disappointed / a little annoyed: it's a criticism.) · "If she hadn't studied medicine, she would never have met him." Did she study medicine? (Yes.) Did she meet him? (Yes.) Is this regret? (No, it's reflection: "Life is funny.")
"If we'd left earlier, we might have caught the train." Are we sure? (No, maybe.) · "…we could have caught it." (It was possible.) · "…we would have caught it." (Certain.) · "You could have called!" Is there an if? (No.) What does it mean? (It was possible, and you didn't. I'm annoyed.)
Second or third? "I don't speak Japanese." Can it still change? (Yes, I can still learn.) So: If I spoke Japanese, I'd read that book. · "I didn't save the file." Can it change? (No, the work is lost.) So: If I had saved it, I wouldn't have lost my work.
Mixed (a): "If I had taken that job, I would be rich now." When was the job offer? (In the past.) Am I rich now? (No.) Why would be, not would have been? (Because the result is now.) · Mixed (b): "If he weren't so stubborn, he would have apologized." Is he stubborn now? (Yes, always.) Did he apologize? (No, in the past.)

Form.

Third conditionalIf + had + pp, … would have + pp · comma only when if comes firstIf I'd known, I would have called you. · I would have called you if I'd known.
Negativeshadn't + pp · wouldn't have + ppIf they hadn't helped, we wouldn't have finished.
Other modalscould have / might have + ppIf we'd left earlier, we might have made it.
2nd vs 3rd2nd: past simple, would + base (unreal now) · 3rd: past perfect, would have + pp (unreal past)If I knew, I'd tell you. · If I'd known, I'd have told you.
Mixed (a)past cause → present result: If + had + pp, … would + baseIf I'd gone to bed earlier, I wouldn't be so tired today.
Mixed (b)present trait → past result: If + past simple / were, … would have + ppIf I were more organized, I wouldn't have forgotten the meeting.

Pronunciation. Both halves contract: "If I'd known, I'd have called" /aɪd əv/. would have → would've /ˈwʊdəv/, could have → /ˈkʊdəv/, might have → /ˈmaɪtəv/, wouldn't have → /ˈwʊdntəv/ ("wouldn't-uv"). The have sounds exactly like "of," which is why even native speakers write "would of." Stress falls on the main verbs and on any negative: "If I'd KNOWN, I'd have CALLED." · "If you HADN'T told me…". Regret intonation: a slow, falling "If only I'd checked…" ↘.

Drill script
  1. Flip it (T gives reality; learners give the third conditional): T: "I didn't study. I failed." → S: "If I'd studied, I would've passed." T: "She missed the bus. She was late." → S: "If she hadn't missed the bus, she wouldn't have been late." T: "It rained. We didn't go to the beach." → S: "If it hadn't rained, we would've gone to the beach."
  2. Back-chain the reduced form: "called" → "would've called" → "I'd have called" → "If I'd known, I'd have called." ×3, choral, then fast individuals. Then "wouldn't have come" /ˈwʊdntəv/ ×3.
  3. Dial the confidence: T: "If we'd left earlier… (sure)" → S: "…we would've caught it." T: "(possible)" → "…we could've caught it." T: "(not sure)" → "…we might've caught it."
  4. Can it still change? T: "I don't have a car." → S: "If I had a car, I'd drive." T: "I didn't have a car last year." → S: "If I'd had a car, I'd have driven."
  5. Chain preview: S1: "If I hadn't overslept, I wouldn't have missed the bus." S2 starts with S1's result: "If I hadn't missed the bus, I wouldn't have…" Keep going around the room for 6 links; the last learner ends with "…now."
Chain of Consequences — how to run it
  • Groups of 4. Each group gets one start card (face up) and one ending card (face down), plus blank slips. The 8 start cards are small, invented everyday events (a missed alarm, a wrong order at a café, a lost umbrella, a spilled coffee). The 8 ending cards are surprising "present" results (running a food truck, being the best dancer at the office party, owning a very large dog).
  • The rule: each new link starts with the result of the last link, turned into an if-clause. S1: "If Ana's alarm had gone off, she wouldn't have missed the bus." S2: "If she hadn't missed the bus, she wouldn't have walked through the park." S3: "If she hadn't walked through the park, she wouldn't have smelled the tacos…" Write each link on a slip and lay the slips in a line on the desk.
  • After 4 links, turn over the ending card. Now the group must steer the chain to the ending in 3–4 more links. The last link must be a mixed conditional: "…and if she hadn't tasted those tacos, she wouldn't be running a food truck today!"
  • Scoring (optional): 1 point per correct link (both halves right), 2 bonus points for a correct mixed-conditional ending, 1 point for a link that uses could have or might have. Everyone checks: "Is the if-half had + pp? Is the result would have + pp?"
  • Dominant speakers: strict turns: the slip goes clockwise, and you may not add a link out of turn. Give the strongest speaker the job of checker in round 1 (they check every slip before it goes down). Quieter learners may read their link from a slip they have written first.
  • Every chain is about invented people and small events. Don't let groups use classmates' real lives or big life events. If a chain drifts somewhere sad, steer it back with a silly link.
ICQs before the Chain of Consequences
What does your link start with? (The result of the last link, as an if-clause.) · What's the form of the if-half? (had + past participle.) · And the result? (would have + past participle.) · When do you turn over the ending card? (After 4 links.) · What's special about the last link? (It's a mixed conditional: …would be… now / today.) · Can you add a link when it isn't your turn? (No.) · Is the story about real people in our class? (No, only the card people.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with one frame on a strip: "If ___ had ___, ___ would have ___." and the 10 most useful participles written under it (gone, left, missed, seen, met, known, told, taken, bought, eaten). In the chain, the learner writes the link first, then reads it aloud, and may copy the result of the last slip to start their if-half. Skip mixed conditionals for now. Target: 4 correct spoken third conditionals, including one negative.

Stretch (toward C1)

Add precision to the result: would probably have, might well have, could easily have. Use should have / shouldn't have for criticism and needn't have for wasted effort ("I needn't have brought an umbrella"). In the chain, make every other link a different modal, and end with a type (b) mixed conditional ("…if she weren't so curious, she'd never have tried them"). Recognize (don't teach) the inverted form: Had I known, I would have called.

L1 watch-outs
  • "If I would have known…": German (wenn ich gewusst hätte uses the same "would" mood in both halves), Dutch, Swedish and many Slavic languages put the conditional in both clauses (Russian если бы я знал, я бы…). Also common in casual American speech. Cross out would in the if-clause every time: if + had.
  • Second conditional for the past: "If I knew, I would tell you" (meaning yesterday). Spanish and Portuguese learners know the difference (si hubiera sabido), but Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Japanese mark "unreal" and "past" with context, so the two English forms feel the same. Use "Can it still change?" as the key question.
  • Mixed-up halves: "If I had taken the job, I would have been rich now." Learners who have just learned the third conditional apply it everywhere. Point at now: "Is now in the past?"
  • "would of / could of": a spelling error, not a grammar one. Native speakers make it too. Every learner who writes it gets the "it sounds like 'of'" explanation once; after that, just underline.
  • Stress and the reduced have: Spanish, Italian, Japanese and Korean speakers, who give every syllable full weight, may say "would HAVE" slowly and stressed, which sounds emphatic or angry. Drill the reduced /əv/. Arabic speakers may add a vowel: "would-uh-have."
  • Word order in questions: "What you would have done?" Chinese and Vietnamese keep statement order. Drill "What would you have done?" as a chunk.

Past Regrets — Wish, If Only, Would Rather & It's Time

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • contrast a present wish (wish / if only + past simple: I wish I had more time) with a past regret (wish / if only + past perfect: I wish I'd set an alarm)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: one trick. In all these structures a past form doesn't mean past time. It means "unreal" or "not yet."

"I wish I had a car." Do I have a car? (No.) Is this about the past? (No, about now.) · "I wish I had bought that car." Did I buy it? (No.) When? (In the past.) Can I change it? (No, it's a regret.) · "If only I'd bought it!" Same meaning? (Yes, but stronger, more emotional.)
"I wish my roommate would wash the dishes." Does he wash them? (No.) Am I annoyed? (Yes.) Who has to change? (He does.) · Can I say "I wish I would go to the gym"? (No, you can't be annoyed with yourself this way.) What can I say? (I wish I went to the gym more. / I wish I could find the motivation.)
"I'd rather walk." Who walks? (Me.) What does 'd rather mean? (Would prefer.) · "I'd rather you didn't call after ten." Who calls? (You.) Is didn't about the past? (No! About now and the future.) · "I'd rather you hadn't told him." Did you tell him? (Yes, already. I'm sorry you did.)
"It's high time we fixed the printer." Have we fixed it? (No.) Should we do it now? (Yes, it's overdue.) Is the speaker happy? (No, a little annoyed.) · "It's time we left." Did we leave? (No, we should leave now.) · "It's time to go." Who? (Not said: everybody.)

Form.

Present wishwish / if only + past simple · could · were (all persons, more formal)I wish I could drive. · If only it weren't raining.
Past regretwish / if only + past perfectI wish we'd booked earlier. · If only I hadn't said that.
Annoyancewish + someone / something else + would + baseI wish it would stop raining. · I wish you wouldn't interrupt.
would rather (me)'d rather + base (+ than + base) · 'd rather notI'd rather walk than wait for the bus.
would rather (someone)'d rather + someone + past simple (now / future) · + past perfect (already done)I'd rather you came tomorrow. · I'd rather you hadn't told him.
it's timeit's (high / about) time + subject + past simple · it's time to + baseIt's about time they fixed that road. · It's time to go.

Pronunciation. I'd rather = /aɪd ˈræðər/ (American: the a in rather as in cat; British /ˈrɑːðə/). Learners often don't hear the 'd: drill "I'd rather" as one chunk. If only carries the regret: high, slow start, falling end: "If ONly I'd known… ↘." wish is stressed, and so is the contrast word: "I wish I HAD more time" vs "I wish I HAD set an alarm" (the second had is often weak: "I wish I'd set…"). It's high time is stressed on HIGH, with an annoyed, falling tone.

Drill script
  1. Reality → wish (T gives a present fact): T: "I don't have much time." → S: "I wish I had more time." T: "I can't cook." → S: "I wish I could cook." T: "It's cold." → S: "I wish it weren't so cold."
  2. Now or then? (T gives a fact; learners decide): T: "I didn't bring an umbrella." → S: "I wish I'd brought an umbrella." T: "I don't have an umbrella." → S: "I wish I had an umbrella." T: "I ate too much cake." → S: "If only I hadn't eaten so much cake!"
  3. Annoyed: T (pointing at an imaginary noisy neighbor): "music!" → S: "I wish they'd turn the music down!" T: "the bus / late" → S: "I wish the bus would come on time!"
  4. Would rather two ways: T: "walk / take the bus" → S: "I'd rather walk than take the bus." T: "you / not call after ten" → S: "I'd rather you didn't call after ten." T: "we / leave now" → S: "I'd rather we left now."
  5. It's high time: T points at pictures or mimes: a clock → "It's time we left." · a broken elevator sign → "It's high time they fixed it." · a messy desk → "It's about time I cleaned it!" Choral with the annoyed tone, then individuals.
"Would You Rather?" — how to run it
  • Pairs, one set of 16 cards face down. Every card is light and everyday (food, weekends, chores, phones, work habits, travel around town). No card asks about the past, the family or the home country.
  • A reads the choice. B answers in two sentences: "I'd rather wash the dishes than cook every night. Cooking takes too long." A reacts, then asks the card's follow-up, which needs the "someone else" pattern: "So if we were roommates, would you rather I cooked or I washed the dishes?" → B: "I'd rather you cooked, definitely!"
  • "I'd rather not say!" is always a correct answer. It's also useful grammar.
  • Stretch: answer with a reason and a condition: "I'd rather live without my phone for a day than without coffee, but if I had a job interview, I'd rather have my phone."
Wish List Meeting — how to run it
  • Groups of 4. Each group gets one scenario card: A · Maple Street apartments (the elevator, the laundry room, noise, the trash area) · B · Brightside office (the printer, the kitchen, meetings, the air conditioning) · C · Riverside Park & the bus stop (lights, benches, the bus schedule, dogs) · D · Our English classroom (the room, the timetable, homework, the coffee). All are about shared places, never about anyone's private life.
  • Roles: the Chair opens and closes the meeting and makes sure everyone speaks; the Note-taker writes the top 3 changes and reports; the Devil's advocate argues against at least two ideas ("I'd rather they didn't spend money on that…"); the Timekeeper gives warnings at 5 and 2 minutes. Give the strongest speaker the Chair or the Note-taker role, not the Devil's advocate.
  • Talking tokens: 3 each. You put one in the middle every time you speak. When yours are gone, you listen until everyone has used theirs, then everyone takes them back. This stops one person running the meeting.
  • Language targets (on the role card): 2 present wishes (I wish the elevator worked), 1 regret about a past decision (I wish they hadn't moved the bus stop), 1 It's high time…, 1 I'd rather they…, and one polite disagreement (I see your point, but…).
  • The meeting speaks about decisions and places, not people: no complaints about real named neighbors, coworkers or classmates. In scenario D, learners may complain about the classroom and the teacher's homework. Take it with a smile.

Stretch (toward C1)

Early finishers write three sentences where wish + past simple and wish + past perfect use the same verb with different meanings (I wish I knew / I wish I'd known).

L1 watch-outs
  • "I wish I would…" / "I wish I will…": Spanish (ojalá + subjunctive), Portuguese, Arabic (atamannā an…) and Chinese (希望 xīwàng = both "hope" and "wish") use one structure for hopes and wishes, so learners say "I wish you will pass" (= hope). Contrast: hope = possible (I hope you pass); wish = unreal (I wish you'd told me).
  • Present tense after would rather you / it's time we: "I'd rather you don't go." · "It's time we go." Almost every learner does this, because the meaning is present. Keep repeating "past form, present meaning."
  • Now / then mix-up: "I wish I had spoken French" (meaning "I wish I could speak it now"). Russian, Polish and Czech speakers use one conditional form (бы) for both, and Spanish and Portuguese speakers may map had spoken to their imperfect subjunctive. Ask: "Is it about now or about a finished moment?"
  • "I'd rather to stay": Spanish prefiero quedarme, French je préfère rester and Arabic ufaḍḍil an pull in to. Compare: I'd prefer to stay but I'd rather stay.
  • Not hearing 'd: "I rather walk." Vietnamese, Cantonese and Thai speakers, whose languages don't end words in -d clusters, often drop the 'd in I'd rather and I'd set. Exaggerate it in the drill, then accept it in fast speech.

Recap on slide 17, "three ways to look back": what happened (By the time I arrived, I'd been traveling for hours and the party had finished) → what could have happened (If I'd left earlier, I would've caught it… and I wouldn't be so tired now) → what we wish (I wish I'd set an alarm. I'd rather you didn't call so late. It's high time we fixed the printer.). One idea joins 1.2 and 1.3: a past form can mean "unreal." Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C4 warmer and again in the C13 progress-test catch-up.

Watch for in Module 2: gerunds and infinitives (stop doing / stop to do, remember doing / remember to do) meet this module in regret: "I regret not studying" = "I wish I'd studied." Past modals of deduction in 2.2 (must have, might have, can't have) use the same modal + have + pp form as the third conditional, so learners will mix "might have" (maybe, in a conditional) with "might have" (a guess about the past). That's useful: link them explicitly. Keep a 2-minute "flip it" warmer (one third conditional, one wish) in C4–C6.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it tests: the four narrative tenses and their jobs, past perfect simple vs continuous (including state verbs), by the time / when / while; the third conditional (form and the "flip"), would / could / might have, second vs third, both types of mixed conditional, would have spelling; wish / if only + past simple vs past perfect, wish + would, would rather (both patterns) and it's (high) time + past simple.
