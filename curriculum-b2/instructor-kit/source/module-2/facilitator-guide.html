Module 2 — Verb Patterns & Modality

Three classes about the small words that carry a lot of meaning. First, gerunds and infinitives: which verbs take -ing and which take to, the rules for prepositions and purpose, and the verbs whose meaning changes with the form (remember, forget, stop, try, regret, go on, mean). Then past deduction: must have / can't have / might have + past participle to reason about what happened, plus should have and could have for judging past choices. Last, the famous look-alikes used to / be used to / get used to: a past habit, a familiar state, and the process of adapting.

3 lessons Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C4–C6 Weeks 2–3 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-verb-patterns.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C4. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Pattern Path board game and referee sheet, the "Two minutes about me" interview cards, the Mystery of Room 12 clue cards and detective notes, the "Adjusting to something new" change cards and role-plays, the Find-someone-who grid and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. B1 taught all three topics at a basic level: verb patterns and stop to / stop -ing (B1 6.2), present deduction must be / can't be (B1 4.2), and used to and would for past habits (B1 1.3). Test first in each lead-in and move fast where the class is strong. The new B2 work is precision: the whole meaning-change set, to as a preposition, past deduction on a certainty scale, the two jobs of could have, and the state / process contrast of be used to vs get used to.
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a verb pattern. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. At B2, feed back on accuracy and range: write up two or three errors and two or three good, ambitious phrases you heard ("I can't afford to waste time" · "They can't possibly have eaten it"). Praise the risk-taking as much as the correct forms.
  • Dominant speakers. All three freer tasks are built to share the floor: the board game has a rotating referee, the mystery is an information gap (each learner holds clues nobody else has, so everyone must speak), and the "adjusting" discussion uses a talking-turn token. Give your strongest speaker a job that makes them listen: referee, note-taker, or "the person who summarizes last." Say it kindly: "Great ideas, Omar. Let's hear Lin's clues first."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Each lesson has a stretch box with C1-flavored options (perfect and passive gerunds, may well have, needn't have, be accustomed to). Offer them as options for early finishers, not as new content for the whole class.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British forms (practise, memorise, apologise, travelling, holiday, neighbour, flat, cinema, at the weekend, binned, half four, ill) and the British present perfect I've got used to. Teach practice, memorize, apologize, traveling, vacation, neighbor, apartment, movie theater, on the weekend, threw it away, four-thirty, sick and I've gotten used to. Accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

What we do → what must have happened → what we're used to. In C4 learners talk about their habits, goals and likes with exactly the right verb pattern: I've stopped staying up late. I'm hoping to run a 10K. In C5 they turn detective and reason about the past with the right amount of certainty: They must have left in a hurry. They can't have eaten the cake. In C6 they describe change over time: I used to work in an office. I'm used to the early start now, but I'm still getting used to the new app. Every class ends with a real communicative task: a two-minute talk, a group verdict on a mystery, and a discussion about adjusting to something new.

Trauma-informed: mysteries, regrets and "big changes"

Three places in this module need care. (1) Deduction. Keep deduction scenarios harmless. In class, use the Mystery of Room 12 instead: a surprise birthday party that moved to the park. Nobody is hurt, no police, no crime. Don't add crime or disappearance stories. (2) Regret. Should have / shouldn't have can open heavy memories. Keep examples small and everyday (the umbrella, a late night, a missed bus), and never ask "What do you regret most in your life?" (3) "A big change." The online lesson 2.3 project lists "moving country" as one optional choice. In class, the "adjusting" discussion is about neutral changes: a new job, a new schedule, new technology, a new neighborhood across town, a new hobby. Never ask "What was it like when you came to this country?" or "What do you miss about home?" If a learner chooses to talk about moving countries, listen warmly and don't ask probing follow-ups. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card (in the classroom materials), or something imagined. Anyone can say "Pass."

Objectives — learners can…

If you run long

This lesson has a lot of form. If the Error Hunt test shows the class already controls the basic verb lists, cut "Clarify: the patterns" to 6 minutes and go straight to the meaning-change verbs, which are the heart of the lesson. Never cut the Pattern Path or the two-minute talk: that is where the patterns start to become automatic. Workbook Ex 5 can move to homework.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the "feel" behind the lists. Many -ing verbs describe your relationship to an activity as a whole thing (enjoy it, avoid it, finish it, miss it). Many to verbs point forward to something that hasn't happened yet (want, hope, plan, decide, promise). It is a helpful feel, not a perfect rule.

"I'm considering taking a course." Have I decided? (No, I'm thinking about it.) Is it considering to take? (No, consider + -ing.) · "We managed to finish." Was it easy? (No, but we did it.) Did we finish? (Yes.)

Meaning: prepositions and the to trap.

"I'm looking forward to the weekend." Can a noun come after to here? (Yes.) So is this to an infinitive or a preposition? (A preposition.) So: looking forward to… (seeing you, not see.) · "I want to the weekend"? (No! So want to is an infinitive: want to go.)

Meaning: purpose.

"I went out to buy milk." Why did I go out? (To buy milk.) Can I say "for buying milk"? (No, not for the reason of an action.) · "This knife is for cutting bread." Is that OK? (Yes, it's what the thing is for, its function.)

Meaning: the meaning-change verbs.

"I remember locking the door." Did I lock it? (Yes.) Is this a memory or a job to do? (A memory.) · "Remember to lock the door." Have you locked it yet? (No.) Memory or job? (A job, a duty.)
"He stopped smoking." Does he smoke now? (No, he quit.) · "He stopped to smoke." Does he smoke? (Yes!) Why did he stop? (To have a cigarette. He paused his walk or his work.)
"Try adding salt." Is it hard to add salt? (No.) Why add it? (To see if the soup tastes better: an experiment.) · "I tried to open the jar." Was it easy? (No.) Did I open it? (Maybe not: an attempt.)
"I regret to tell you the class is canceled." Is the speaker sorry about the past or about news now? (News now: formal.) · "I regret telling him." Did I tell him? (Yes, and I'm sorry.) · "This job means traveling a lot." = intend or involve? (Involve.) · "I meant to call you." = intend or involve? (Intend: I wanted to, but I didn't.)

Pronunciation. to before a verb is weak /tə/: "I want /tə/ go" (and in fast American speech, want to → wanna; recognize it, don't write it). But to as a preposition before -ing is also weak: "looking forward /tə/ seeing you." The -ing ending is /ɪŋ/ with no hard /g/ at the end: "cooking" /ˈkʊkɪŋ/, not "cooking-guh." Stress in the meaning-change pairs carries the meaning: "I stopped TO buy coffee" (contrastive, when you correct someone) · "He STOPPED smoking." can't stand: Americans say /kænt/ with a clear vowel and a short, soft t. Listen for the vowel, not the t.

Drill script
  1. Picture + verb card: hold up the swim picture and the enjoy card → S: "I enjoy swimming." cook + want → "I want to cook." dance + can't stand → "I can't stand dancing." hike + decide → "We decided to hike." play the guitar + good at → "She's good at playing the guitar."   2. Purpose chain: T: "Why do you go to the library?" → S: "To borrow books." T: "Why did you call the clinic?" → S: "To make an appointment." (Short answers with to are natural English: praise them.)
  3. Memory or duty? (point ⬅️ or ➡️ on the board) T: "remember / lock" + ⬅️ → S: "I remember locking it." T: + ➡️ → S: "Remember to lock it." T: "stop / buy coffee" + ⬅️ → "I stopped buying coffee." + ➡️ → "I stopped to buy coffee."
  4. Pronunciation: "I'm looking forward /tə/ seeing you." ×3 (weak to, clear -ing); "I can't afford /tə/ wait." ×2.
  5. Open pairs: S1 asks "What have you stopped doing this year?" S2 answers with -ing; S3 asks "What do you have to remember to do this week?" S4 answers with to. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Pattern Path — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one board, one coin (heads = move 1, tails = move 2) or a die, and one referee sheet face down. Each player uses a small object as a counter (a coin, an eraser, a paper clip).
  • Land on a square and do the task. Complete: say the whole sentence with the right form. Meaning: explain the difference between the two sentences in your own words ("The first one is a memory, the second is a duty"). Error Hunt: find the mistake and say the correct sentence. Talk: speak for 20 seconds using the pattern on the square.
  • The referee is the player to the right of the speaker. They check the sheet and say "Correct, stay!" or "Not quite, go back to where you were." Then the sheet moves one person to the left. Everyone referees, so a strong speaker can't take over.
  • A Talk square is correct if the speaker keeps going for 20 seconds and uses the pattern at least once. Fluency counts; small slips are OK.
  • First to FINISH wins, but keep playing until time is up. Groups that finish early start again from square 1 and must answer the Talk squares with a different example.
  • While they play, write down errors and good phrases for the feedback stage. Don't correct during the game unless the referee asks you.
ICQs before the game and the talk
Game: What does heads mean? (Move 1.) Tails? (Move 2.) · On an Error Hunt square, is it enough to find the mistake? (No, you fix it too.) · Who checks the answer? (The referee: the person on your right.) · What happens to the sheet after each turn? (It moves to the left.) · Wrong answer? (Go back to where you were.)
Talk: How long does A talk? (Two minutes.) · Can A read a script? (No, only key words.) · What does B do while A talks? (Listens and ticks the patterns.) · And after? (Asks two follow-up questions.) · Must it be your real life? (No, a new identity card or invented is fine.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with three frames only, color-coded on a strip: I enjoy + -ing (blue) · I want to + verb (green) · I remember + -ing / Remember to + verb (both). Use the activity pictures as prompts rather than written sentences. In the Pattern Path, pair the learner with a partner who reads the square aloud; they answer orally. In the talk, one minute is enough, from picture notes. Target: 6 spoken sentences with the right form and one meaning-change pair.

Stretch (toward C1)

Perfect and passive forms: I remember being told… · She denied having taken it · He seems to have left · I hate being interrupted. More meaning-change verbs: need doing (= need to be done: "The car needs washing") and be afraid of falling vs be afraid to ask. In the talk, use at least one perfect or passive gerund. Early finishers write three Pattern Path squares of their own for another group.

L1 watch-outs
  • Base verb after a preposition: "without say goodbye," "before eat," "interested in learn." Spanish (sin decir, antes de comer), Portuguese, French, Italian and Arabic use the infinitive after prepositions. Keep saying: "After a preposition: -ing. Always."
  • for + -ing / for to for purpose: "I came for asking…," "I went for to buy…" (Spanish para comprar, French pour acheter, Portuguese para comprar, Tagalog para). Teach to as the purpose word.
  • "I want that you come" / "I want you come": Spanish quiero que vengas, Russian хочу, чтобы ты пришёл, Arabic ureed an, Chinese 我要你来. Drill "want you to come" as one chunk.
  • "She suggested me to go" / "suggested to go": very common across languages (French suggérer de, Spanish sugerir que). suggest takes -ing or a that-clause: "She suggested going / that I go."
  • "They made him to wait" / "Let me to help": Spanish, Arabic, Russian and Chinese learners often add to. Cross it out on the board every time.
  • Infinitive as subject: "To learn English is difficult" (Spanish Aprender inglés es difícil, French, Italian, Portuguese). It's grammatical but stiff; recast: "Learning English is difficult."
  • No -ing form: Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers have no verb endings and may say "I enjoy cook." Tap the board where -ing goes.

Modals of Deduction in the Past

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • make deductions about the past with modal + have + past participle: must have (almost sure it happened), can't / couldn't have (almost sure it didn't), might / may / could have (possible), might not / may not have (possibly not)
  • avoid the two classic errors: mustn't have for a negative deduction and could of / must of in writing
  • use should have / shouldn't have to criticize, regret or give too-late advice
  • tell the two jobs of could have apart: a past possibility (He could have gone home) and a missed chance (I could have gone, but I didn't)
  • turn a present deduction into a past one (must be → must have been) and reason aloud with a group to a verdict on a mystery

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: deduction about the past. We use evidence to reach a confident guess about something that already happened. The modal shows how sure we are.

"The ground's wet. It must have rained." Did I see the rain? (No.) How do I know? (The evidence: the wet ground.) How sure am I? (Almost sure, about 95%.) · "He can't have finished already." Do I think he finished? (No, I'm almost sure he didn't.)
"She might have missed the bus." Am I sure? (No, it's one possibility.) Can I say may have or could have? (Yes, the same meaning.) · "She mustn't have seen us." Correct? (No! "She can't have seen us.") Why? (mustn't means "it's not allowed.")

Meaning: should have.

"You should have called me." Did you call me? (No.) Was that a good idea? (No, a mistake.) · "I shouldn't have eaten that." Did I eat it? (Yes.) Do I feel good about it? (No, I regret it.) · Is should have about how sure I am? (No, it judges a choice.)

Meaning: could have (two jobs).

"Where's Sam? He could have gone home." Do I know where Sam is? (No: it's a possibility.) · "I could have gone to the concert, but I stayed home." Did I go? (No.) Was it possible? (Yes, a missed chance.) · "You could have warned me!" Did you warn me? (No.) Am I a little annoyed? (Yes.)

Form.

Sure it happenedmust have + past participleEveryone's talking about it. It must have been a great night.
Possiblemight / may / could have + ppThey might have taken a wrong turn.
Possibly notmight not / may not have + ppBen may not have gone. He was sick.
Sure it didn'tcan't / couldn't have + ppIt can't have finished early. The photos are from 2 a.m.
Judging a choiceshould / shouldn't have + ppI should have checked the forecast.
Missed chancecould have + pp (but didn't)We could have won, but we missed the penalty.
Now → pastmodal + be / verb → modal + have + ppmust be → must have been · might know → might have known
Continuousmodal + have + been + -ingShe looked exhausted. She must have been working late.

Pronunciation. In speech, have is weak and joins the modal: must've /ˈmʌstəv/, might've /ˈmaɪtəv/, could've /ˈkʊdəv/, should've /ˈʃʊdəv/. That's why native speakers sometimes write "could of": it's always wrong in writing. Stress the modal and the main verb, not have: "She MUST've LEFT." In American English, can't have /ˈkænt əv/ and can have sound close: the difference is the stressed, longer vowel in can't, so teach learners to say "CAN'T have" with strong stress, or use couldn't have, which is easier to hear.

Drill script
  1. Weak "have": must've · might've · could've · should've · can't've: choral ×2 each, then in a phrase: "She must've left." "He can't've known."
  2. Clue → verdict (T gives evidence, S gives the deduction): T: "The street is wet." → S: "It must have rained." T: "He was at work in Denver until nine. The party was in Chicago at nine." → S: "He can't have been at the party." T: "She didn't answer my call." → S: "She might have been busy."
  3. Now → past: T: "He must be tired." → S: "He must have been tired." T: "She can't know." → S: "She can't have known." T: "They might be lost." → S: "They might have been lost."
  4. Fix it: T: "She mustn't have seen us." → S: "She can't have seen us." T: "He must has forgotten." → S: "He must have forgotten."
  5. Judge it: T: "I didn't study and I failed." → S: "You should have studied." T: "He ate three pieces of cake and felt sick." → S: "He shouldn't have eaten so much."
The Mystery of Room 12 — how to run it
  • Setting (read aloud, slide 13): "It's Saturday, 9:15 a.m. You arrive early at the Maple Street Community Center for your 9:30 English class in Room 12. The door is open, nobody is there, and something strange has happened. What happened here?"
  • Groups of 4. Each learner gets one clue card (A, B, C or D) with three text clues. They read their clues aloud but don't show the card: this is an information gap, so every learner must speak. The others take notes on the detective notes sheet.
  • Then the group discusses. Every deduction needs a modal and a reason: "The coffee is still warm, so they can't have left long ago." Push for the full scale: at least one must have, one can't have, one might have and one should have.
  • Roles (useful for dominant speakers): a secretary writes the verdict; a skeptic must challenge every must have: "Are you sure? It might have been someone else." Give the skeptic role to your strongest speaker: it makes them listen and gives others the floor. The secretary is not the strongest speaker.
  • Verdict: 3–4 sentences, from most sure to least sure. Then read Script 2.2-B. Score: 1 point for each part of the verdict that matches the voicemail (the surprise party · Rosa's note · Plan B at the park · they left in a hurry · the keys were dropped). Celebrate the best reasoning, not only the right answer.
  • Groups of 3: one learner holds cards C and D. Groups of 5: two learners share card A and take turns reading.
  • It's a happy mystery on purpose: a surprise party, a change of plan, a forgotten umbrella. Don't turn it into a crime.
ICQs before the mystery
Can you show your card to the group? (No! Read it aloud.) · How many deductions do you write? (At least six.) · Is "They left" a deduction? (No, it needs a modal: "They must have left.") · What does the skeptic do? (Says "Are you sure?" and suggests another possibility.) · Is it a crime story? (No! Something happy happened.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on two forms: must have (sure yes) and can't have (sure no), with thumbs up / thumbs down. Give a strip with 5 past participles they will need (been, left, eaten, seen, gone). In the mystery, give card D (the shortest clues) and read it quietly with the learner first; their job is to say the key deduction about Rosa's note. Target: 4 spoken deductions with must have / can't have.

Stretch (toward C1)

Degrees of certainty: may well have (likely), can't possibly have, is bound to have. Continuous and passive forms: must have been waiting · might have been taken. needn't have (did it, but it wasn't necessary: "We needn't have brought the cake knife") vs didn't need to. In the verdict, rank six deductions by certainty and use at least two of these.

L1 watch-outs
  • "mustn't have" for "can't have": learners generalize the negative of must. German (muss nicht = doesn't have to) and Dutch speakers find it especially confusing. Use the "opposite pair" card: must have ↔ can't have.
  • Missing or wrong participle: "It must have rain," "She must have went." Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian have no participle endings; many other learners mix the past simple and the participle. Keep a list of irregular participles on the board (been, gone, seen, eaten, taken, left, forgotten).
  • "must has / must had": agreement habits from the present perfect. After a modal, have never changes.
  • A different structure: Spanish debe (de) haber salido and Portuguese deve ter saído map well; but Arabic, Russian (должно быть, он ушёл = "must be, he left") and Japanese (…はずだ) use an adverb + past tense. Learners may say "Maybe he must left" or "Probably he left." Accept probably as good English, then offer the modal.
  • could of / should of: learners who learn by ear (and many native speakers) write of. Always have in writing.
  • "can" vs "can't" in American English: learners (and listeners) miss the t. Teach stress, and accept couldn't have as the clearer choice.

2.3 · C6

Used to / Be Used to / Get Used to

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use used to + base verb for past habits and states that are no longer true, with didn't use to and Did you use to…? (no d)
  • use be used to + noun / -ing for something that is familiar and normal for them, in any tense
  • use get used to + noun / -ing for the process of adapting (I'm getting used to…, I got used to…, You'll get used to…)
  • explain why the form changes (in be / get used to, to is a preposition) and apply the "it" test
  • talk about adjusting to something new (a job, a schedule, technology, a neighborhood, a hobby) using all three structures
Materials
  • Workbook 2.3 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "Adjusting to something new" notes)
  • Slides 14–19 · The three used to cards + the "it" test card · Find someone who grid (1 per learner) · change cards (1 set per group of 3) · role-play cards A/B · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 2.3-A (Dana's new shift) and 2.3-B (dictation)

Left: a timeline. PAST (gone): I used to work in an office. (base verb) · NOW (a state): I'm used to the early start / getting up early. · CHANGING (a process, an arrow getting bigger): I'm getting used to the new app / using the scanner.

Center: used to + BASE VERB · be used to + NOUN / -ING · get used to + NOUN / -ING. The "it" test: I'm used to it ✔ → to = preposition → -ing · I used to it ✘ → base verb.

Right: questions and negatives: Did you use to…? · I didn't use to… (no d) · Present habit? I use to jog → I usually jog. · US English: I've gotten used to it (UK: I've got used to it).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: used to (past). A habit or state in the past that is not true now.

"I used to take the bus." Do I take the bus now? (No.) Did I take it once or many times? (Many times: a habit.) · "There used to be a bakery here." Is it still here? (No.) · Can I say "I use to take the bus" about now? (No! "I usually take the bus.")

Meaning: be used to (a state) vs get used to (a process).

"I'm used to the hills." Are the hills difficult for me now? (No, they're normal.) Were they difficult before? (Yes, maybe.) · "I'm getting used to the rain." Is the rain normal for me yet? (Not yet, but it's getting easier.) Is it finished or still happening? (Still happening.)
"She got used to working nights." Was it easy at first? (No.) Is it OK now? (Yes, the change is finished.) · "You'll get used to it." When? (In the future.) · "I'm used to work long hours." Correct? (No: "used to working".) Why? (to is a preposition. Test: "I'm used to it" ✔.)

Form.

used toused to + base verb · past only · didn't use to · Did you use to…?She used to play tennis. · He didn't use to like coffee.
be used toam / is / are / was / will be + used to + noun / pronoun / -ingI'm used to the noise. · She's used to working nights.
get used toget / am getting / got / will get / have gotten + used to + noun / -ingI'm getting used to the commute. · You'll get used to living alone.
Useful chunksIt took me (a month) to get used to… · I still can't get used to… · It takes some getting used to.It took me months to get used to the new system.

Pronunciation. In all three structures, used to is /ˈjuːstə/: an s sound, not a z, and a weak to. Compare used = "utilized" /juːzd/: "I used /juːzd/ a knife." In speech, used to and use to sound the same, so the d problem is a writing problem: say it, then check the spelling after did / didn't. The contraction does a lot of work: "I'm used to it" vs "I used to…" — the 'm is small but essential. Drill it with a clear /m/.

Drill script
  1. /ˈjuːstə/: "used to" ×3 (hiss the s), then "I used to…" · "I'm used to…" · "I'm getting used to…" (clear 'm).
  2. Switch (T says a situation, S chooses): T: "In the past: I lived near the beach. Not now." → S: "I used to live near the beach." T: "Early meetings are normal for me." → S: "I'm used to early meetings." T: "The new app is still strange, but it's getting easier." → S: "I'm getting used to the new app."
  3. Base or -ing? T: "used to / drive" → S: "used to drive." T: "be used to / drive" → S: "I'm used to driving." T: "get used to / wake up early" → S: "I'm getting used to waking up early."
  4. Did you use to…? Chain: S1 asks S2 "Did you use to play a sport?" S2 answers ("Yes, I used to play volleyball." / "No, I didn't use to…"), then asks S3 a new question.
  5. Advice: T: "I just started the night shift. It's terrible!" → S: "Don't worry, you'll get used to it." T: "It took me two months." → S: "It took me two months to get used to it."
Adjusting to something new — how to run it
  • Put the change cards face up on each table. Each card names a neutral change and three prompts: How did things use to be? What are you used to now? What are you still getting used to? Learners may pick a card, use a change from their own life, or use their new identity card's change. All are equal.
  • Notes, not a script: 2 used to + 2 be used to + 1 getting used to + 1 piece of advice for someone starting the same change.
  • Groups of 3, one talking-turn token (a pen). Only the person holding it talks; after 2 minutes, the listeners each ask one follow-up question ("How long did it take you to get used to…?" · "What do you miss about how it used to be?"), then give one piece of advice ("You'll get used to…" · "Try…ing").
  • Dominant speakers: the token helps; also give the strongest speaker the job of speaking last and summarizing the group's best advice. Quiet learners: they can talk from the card prompts, and 1 minute is fine.
  • Keep it neutral. The changes on the cards are everyday (a new job, a night shift, a new phone or app, self-checkout, a new neighborhood across town, a new hobby). Don't add "moving to a new country" as a card and don't ask about it. If a learner chooses it themselves, that's their choice: listen and respond warmly, without probing.
  • Early finishers: role-play cards (A = two weeks into a change, B = a friend who went through it). They act it out for 3 minutes, then swap cards.
ICQs before the mingle and the discussion
Mingle: How many names on each line? (One.) · Do you say "Find someone who is used to…"? (No, make a question: "Are you used to…?") · Can someone say "Pass"? (Yes.) · What do you write next to the name? (One detail.)
Discussion: Do you have to talk about your real life? (No: a card, a new identity, or invented.) · Who can talk? (Only the person with the pen.) · How long? (About two minutes.) · What do the listeners do after? (Ask a question and give advice.) · Which structure is for "still changing"? (getting used to.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the learner a change card with one sentence head for each color. In the mingle, give a grid with 5 lines instead of 10. Target: one spoken sentence with each structure, with -ing after be / get used to.

Stretch (toward C1)

would for past habits in a story (We would walk to school together) and why it can't do states (I would have long hair); be accustomed to + -ing (more formal); grow used to; It takes some getting used to; I've never gotten used to…. In the discussion, use all of these once and narrate the change as a mini-story. Early finishers write the dialogue from their role-play.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I use to get up early" for a present habit: Spanish suelo, Portuguese costumo, Italian di solito and Tagalog kadalasan have a present "habit" verb or adverb, so learners invent a present use to. Teach usually for now.
  • "I'm used to work": Spanish estoy acostumbrado a trabajar, French être habitué à travailler, Portuguese estou acostumado a trabalhar all use the infinitive. The "it" test is the fix.
  • "I didn't used to": a spelling error that everyone makes (native speakers too). Drop the d after did.
  • Mixing state and process: many languages (Chinese 习惯了, Japanese 慣れた, Korean 익숙해졌다) use one expression for both "be used to" and "got used to." Use the timeline: a dot (state) vs an arrow (process).
  • Dropping "be": "I used to the cold" (Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Haitian Creole speakers may drop be). This accidentally makes a different structure. Point at the missing 'm.
  • /juːzd tuː/: learners who read the spelling say "use-d to" with a z and a strong to. Model /ˈjuːstə/.

Closing the Module

End of C6: Module 2 wrap-up

Recap on slide 19, "three tools for precision": I stopped to… / I stopped …ing (verb patterns that change meaning) → They must have… / can't have… / might have… (how sure you are about the past) → I used to… / I'm used to… / I'm getting used to… (past, now, changing). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C7 warmer and again in the C13 progress-test catch-up.

Watch for in Module 3: the advanced passive (It is believed that…, He is said to have left) uses the perfect infinitive (to have + pp), which looks a lot like the past modals from C5. Learners who just mastered must have left may say "He is said to must have left." Also, the reporting verbs in 3.3 are verb-pattern verbs (suggest -ing, refuse to, advise someone to): recycle the Pattern Path board as a C9 warmer.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it tests: verb + -ing vs verb + to, preposition + -ing (including look forward to), the infinitive of purpose, let / make + base verb, the meaning-change verbs (remember, forget, stop, try), past deduction on the certainty scale (must have / can't have / might have, never mustn't have), should have and the two jobs of could have, and the three used to structures with the right form after each.
