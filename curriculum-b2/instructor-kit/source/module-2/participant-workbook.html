Verb Patterns & Modality

Small words, big differences. I stopped to buy coffee and I stopped buying coffee mean opposite things. They must have left and They might have left show how sure you are. I used to…, I'm used to… and I'm getting used to… describe the past, the present and change. In this module you'll learn to choose exactly the one you mean.

2.1

Gerunds & Infinitives — Choosing the Right Form

Class C4
📖 🗣️ New words — habits, goals and feelings about activities
  • I enjoy cooking.
  • We decided to go hiking.
  • I'm learning to play the guitar.
  • I've stopped swimming.

consider = think about doing · can't afford = don't have enough money or time · manage = succeed in doing something difficult · refuse = say no to doing · can't stand = hate · look forward to = feel happy about something in the future · be committed to = be seriously dedicated to

🗣️ Useful phrases for talking about yourself:

I really enjoy…ingI've stopped…ingI keep…ingI can't stand…ingI'm hoping to…I'm saving up to…I'm good at…ingI'm looking forward to…ing
Grammar box
verb + -ingenjoy, avoid, finish, mind, suggest, imagine, consider, keep, practice, miss, can't standI don't mind waiting.
verb + towant, decide, hope, promise, agree, offer, refuse, manage, learn, seem, affordWe can't afford to wait.
preposition + -inggood at, interested in, tired of, instead of, before, after, without, look forward toHe left without saying goodbye.
-ing as subjectthe activity is the subjectLearning a language takes time.
adjective + tohappy, difficult, easy, ready, surprisedIt's difficult to explain.
purposeto + verb (not for + -ing)I went out to buy milk.
verb + person + towant, ask, tell, advise, allow, encourageShe encouraged him to apply.
no "to"after modals and let / make + personLet me help. · They made him wait.

Is to a preposition? Try a noun after it. "I'm looking forward to the weekend" works, so use -ing: "looking forward to seeing you."

Same verb, different meaning: -ing looks back (a memory, the activity itself). to looks forward (a duty, a purpose, an attempt).

+ -ing+ to
rememberI remember locking the door. (a memory)Remember to lock the door. (a duty)
forgetI'll never forget meeting her. (a memory)Don't forget to call me. (a duty)
stopHe stopped smoking. (he quit)He stopped to smoke. (he paused in order to)
tryTry adding salt. (an experiment)I tried to open it. (an attempt, it was hard)
regretI regret saying that. (sorry about the past)We regret to tell you… (bad news now)
go onShe went on talking. (continued)She went on to talk about money. (next topic)
meanThis job means traveling. (involves)I meant to call you. (intended)

Little or no change: like, love, hate, prefer, begin, start, continue. "I like swimming" ≈ "I like to swim."

2.2

It Must Have Been… — Deduction About the Past

Class C5
📖 🗣️ New words — thinking like a detective

evidence = the facts you can see · a clue = one small piece of evidence · a deduction = a confident guess from evidence · a verdict = your final decision · rule out = decide something is not possible · in hindsight = looking back, now that you know more · in a hurry = very quickly, with no time

🗣️ Detective phrases:

It must have been…Someone must have…They can't have…That couldn't have…She might have…He could have…You should have…I shouldn't have…Are you sure?That rules out…
Grammar box

Every past deduction: modal + have + past participle. The modal never changes. have never becomes has or had.

~95% sure it happenedmust have + ppThe ground's wet. It must have rained.
possible (~50%)might / may / could have + ppShe might have missed the bus.
possibly notmight not / may not have + ppBen may not have gone. He was sick.
~95% sure it didn'tcan't / couldn't have + ppHe can't have finished already.

❌ She mustn't have seen us. → ✅ She can't have seen us. · ❌ must of / could of / should of → ✅ must have

NowHe must be tired. · She can't know.
PastHe must have been tired. · She can't have known.

Judging past choices:

should have + ppthe right thing, but it didn't happenYou should have called me.
shouldn't have + ppthe wrong thing, but it happenedI shouldn't have eaten that.
could have + pp ①a possibilityWhere's Sam? He could have gone home.
could have + pp ②a missed chanceI could have gone to the concert, but I stayed home.

📖 🗣️ Dialogue — the office cake mystery

Mia: Look, the cake's gone from the fridge, and there are crumbs all over Dan's desk. He must have eaten it.

Theo: Hold on. Dan left at four for the dentist. He can't have taken it. He wasn't even here at snack time.

Mia: Good point. So someone else might have left those crumbs. Who was here after four?

Theo: Only Priya. And her coffee mug's still warm, so she could have been here five minutes ago.

Mia: But wait, Priya's allergic to chocolate. She couldn't have eaten a whole chocolate cake.

Theo: True. Honestly, we shouldn't have left it in a shared fridge. You should have put your name on it.

Mia: …The cleaner! He comes at four-thirty. He must have thought it was leftovers and thrown it out. Case closed.

2.3

Used to, Be Used to, Get Used to — Past, Now & Changing

Class C6
📖 🗣️ New words — adjusting to something new

adjust / adapt = change to fit a new situation · a routine = what you usually do every day · a commute = the trip to work or school · a shift = your working hours (day shift, night shift) · settle in = start to feel comfortable in a new place or job · a learning curve = the time it takes to learn something new · feel at home = feel relaxed and comfortable

🗣️ Useful phrases:

I used to…I didn't use to…I'm (not) used to…ingI'm getting used to…It took me a while to get used to…I still can't get used to…You'll soon get used to it.It takes some getting used to.
Grammar box
used to + base verba past habit or state, not true now · past onlyI used to swim every day.
be used to + noun / -ingfamiliar, normal for you · any tenseI'm used to swimming in cold water.
get used to + noun / -ingbecoming familiar, adapting · any tenseI'm getting used to swimming at dawn.
NegativeI didn't use to like coffee. (no d after did)
QuestionDid you use to live here?
Now?❌ I use to jog. → ✅ I usually jog.
get used toI'm getting used to it. · I got used to it. · You'll get used to it. · I've gotten used to it.

The "it" test: can you say "I'm used to it"? Yes → to is a preposition → use a noun or -ing. "I used to it"? No → base verb.

📖 🗣️ Dialogue — read with a partner

Grace: So, how's the new job at the warehouse going?

Luis: Good, but it's a big change. I used to work in an office, so I used to sit all day. Now I'm on my feet for eight hours!

Grace: Wow. Are you used to it yet?

Luis: I'm getting used to it. My legs hurt the first week, but now it's fine. The hardest part is the early start. I'm still not used to getting up at four-thirty.

Grace: You'll get used to it. When I started at the bakery, I couldn't get used to going to bed at eight. Now I don't even need an alarm.

Luis: Really? Did you use to be a night owl?

Grace: Totally! I used to stay up until one every night. Now I'm used to being in bed before the news starts.

Luis: That gives me hope. Oh, and they gave us scanners with a new app. It takes some getting used to.

Grace: Give it a month. You'll be so used to it, you'll forget how the old way worked.
