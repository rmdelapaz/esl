Module 2 — Verb Patterns & Modality: Answers

Answers to every Module 2 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (Pattern Path, the Mystery of Room 12, Find someone who), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 2.1–2.3Classes C4–C69 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B2, mark meaning first, then form, but hold learners to the form that changes the meaning: stopped to buy vs stopped buying, must have vs can't have, used to vs be used to. Be generous about genuine alternatives: might / may / could have are interchangeable for possibility; can't have and couldn't have are both correct; like / love / hate / start take either form; contractions (must've, I'm, 've) are fine; American I've gotten used to and British I've got used to are both correct. American English is the model; British spellings and words (apologise, practise, flat, cinema, at the weekend) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

2.1 · Gerunds & Infinitives (C4)

Exercise 1 — Kai's new routine (Script 2.1-A)
123456789
to changestayingto beto cleargettingjoiningmeetingbuyingto charge

Why: 1 decide + to · 2 stop + -ing = quit the habit · 3 try + to = make an effort · 4 purpose · 5 hate takes either form: also accept to get · 6 preposition about · 7 look forward to (preposition) · 8 avoid + -ing · 9 remember + to = a duty. Wrong: 3 "trying being" · 4 "for clearing" · 7 "to meet."

Exercise 2 — -ing or to?

1 cooking · 2 to study · 3 solving (preposition at) · 4 to buy · 5 seeing (to is a preposition here) · 6 apologize (bare infinitive after make) · 7 closing · 8 to pay · 9 helping (committed to + noun works, so to is a preposition) · 10 to explain (adjective + to).

Also accept apologise (British spelling). Wrong: 6 "to apologize" (the most common error: no to after make + person) · 9 "to help" (very common, even among advanced learners: mark it, but explain gently).

Exercise 3 — Which meaning?

1 b (Remember to meet him = a duty) · 2 b (tried to = an attempt that failed) · 3 a (forget seeing = a memory) · 4 b (regret to inform = bad news now, formal) · 5 b (went on to = moved to a new activity) · 6 b (meant to = intended) · 7 a (stopped to get gas = paused in order to).

Exercise 4 — Error Hunt
  1. ✗ I'm looking forward to seeing you on Friday.
  2. ✗ She suggested taking a taxi. (or: She suggested that we take a taxi.)
  3. ✗ We went to the store to buy some bread.
  4. ✓ correct (let + person + base verb)
  5. ✗ Learning a language takes time. (The infinitive is possible but stiff and formal; the gerund is the natural subject.)
  6. ✓ correct (preposition about + -ing)
  7. ✗ They made us wait for an hour.
  8. ✗ I want you to call me tomorrow.
  9. ✓ correct
  10. ✗ She's good at cooking. (The gerund was right; the preposition was wrong.)
  11. ✗ I don't mind working on Saturdays.
  12. ✓ correct (would like + to)

8 wrong, 4 correct. If learners use this as the test in C4, count how many of the 12 each pair got right; most classes score 6–8 before the lesson.

Exercise 5 — Build the sentence

1 Instead of giving up · 2 to borrow a book · 3 interested in volunteering · 4 to see better · 5 to travel next year · 6 without saying a word.

Also accept: 2 and 4 in order to borrow / so as to see (more formal). Wrong: 2 "for borrowing" · 3 "interested to volunteer" (possible in a different meaning, "curious to," but not the target here) · 5 "for traveling."

Dialogue — patterns

Verb patterns: 've stopped going · 've started running · keep meaning · meaning to exercise · put off starting · Try going · remember hating · look forward to running · goal is to feel · need to remember · remember to set · want to come · can't stand getting up · promise to try. Memory: I remember hating it at first. Duty: I need to remember to set my alarm. Note: Try going with a friend = an experiment (try it and see if it helps).

Speaking — Two minutes about me, and Pattern Path

Two minutes about me is open: listen for at least three -ing verbs, three to verbs, one preposition + -ing, one purpose and one meaning-change verb used correctly. Model: I really enjoy cooking, but I can't stand washing the dishes. I've stopped buying lunch at work, because I'm saving up to take a trip. I'm hoping to run a 10K this year, so I go running to clear my head. I'm not very good at getting up early. I remember hating running at school, so this is a big change! Pattern Path: the full referee sheet is in the classroom materials (set C).

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) I'm tired of to wait.
B) I'm tired of waiting.
C) I'm tired for wait.
Why: a verb straight after a preposition (of) always takes -ing. C also has the wrong preposition.
Q2
"On the way home, he stopped ___ a coffee." (He paused in order to get one.)
A) to buy
B) buying
C) for buying
Why: stop to do = pause in order to do something. Stopped buying would mean he quit the habit. For + -ing doesn't express the purpose of an action.
Q3 · new
Which sentence means "I have a memory of doing it"?
A) I remembered to call my mom.
B) I remember calling my mom on her birthday.
C) I remember to call my mom every Sunday.
Why: remember + -ing looks back at something you did. A and C are about a duty (not forgetting to do it).
Errors you will hear most (2.1)

"I'm looking forward to see you." · "I went there for buying…" · "She suggested me to go." · "They made me to wait." · "Let me to help." · "I want that you…" · "I enjoy to cook." · "without say" / "before eat" · "To learn English is difficult." (stiff, not wrong) · "I stopped to smoke" when the learner means they quit. Correct the meaning-change errors every time (they say the opposite of what the learner means); treat look forward to see as a high-priority fix because it is so frequent in emails.

2.2 · Modals of Deduction in the Past (C5)

Exercise 1 — Read the clue
  1. must have lived
  2. can't have graded (or couldn't have graded)
  3. might have gone … might have fallen (may / could have also correct)
  4. must have enjoyed
  5. must have been
  6. can't have left (couldn't have left)
  7. must have studied

Accept might have lived in 1 if the learner explains that excellent English could also come from years of study (good reasoning; the target is must have = strong evidence). In 5, might have been is acceptable if the learner reads it as "maybe she was lucky, maybe she was clever." Wrong: "mustn't have graded" · "must have grade" · "must had enjoyed."

Exercise 2 — How sure are you?
  1. She must have forgotten about the meeting.
  2. He might / may / could have taken the earlier bus.
  3. They can't / couldn't have seen the sign.
  4. She might not / may not have gotten my message. (British: got)
  5. It must have been a mistake.
  6. He can't / couldn't have finished the report in one hour.

In 4, not couldn't have gotten (that is "sure it didn't," too strong for "it's possible"). Explain the difference: might not have = maybe not · can't have = surely not.

Exercise 3 — Correct the error
  1. He can't have heard the alarm. (or couldn't have heard)
  2. You could have told me the meeting was canceled!
  3. She must have been exhausted after that long flight.
  4. They can't have received the invitation, because nobody came.
  5. I should have booked the tickets earlier.
  6. He must have forgotten my number.
  7. It must have rained last night.

Contractions (could've, must've, should've) are correct in informal writing. mustn't = prohibition, never deduction.

Exercise 4 — React (sample answers)
  1. I should have checked the forecast. / I should have taken my umbrella.
  2. You shouldn't have lent him the money. / You should have said no.
  3. I shouldn't have stayed up so late. / I should have gone to bed earlier.
  4. She shouldn't have sent it. / She should have waited and calmed down first.
  5. I could have gone to the concert, but I stayed home. (a missed chance; also: I should have gone!)
  6. You could have told me! / You should have told me. (both correct: could have sounds a little annoyed; should have is direct criticism)

Open answers: check for should(n't) have + past participle and a sensible judgment. Wrong: "I should took…" · "You mustn't have lent…".

Exercise 5 — Three puzzles (Script 2.2-A), model answers
  1. Ana's kitchen: Her roommate must have made soup and gone out. She can't have left long ago (the soup is still warm). She might have gone to the store.
  2. Ben's desk: Ben must have gone on vacation. He can't have left in a hurry (his desk is tidy and he left a note about the plant). He might have gone to the beach.
  3. The empty classroom: The class must have moved to Room 8. The teacher might have written the note. They can't have gone home (you can hear them laughing).

Accept any logical deduction with modal + have + past participle.

Dialogue — the office cake

Sure yes: He must have eaten it · He must have thought it was leftovers and thrown it out. Sure no: He can't have taken it · She couldn't have eaten a whole chocolate cake. Possible: someone else might have left · she could have been here. Judgment: we shouldn't have left it · You should have put your name on it. Past participles: eaten, taken, left, been, eaten, left, put, thought, thrown. The online lesson has "threw it out" and "four-thirty" (British English: "binned it", "half four").

Speaking — The Mystery of Room 12

What happened (Script 2.2-B): the Saturday Walking Club (Maria, Joe, Ahmed, Lin) came in at 7:30 to prepare a surprise 70th birthday party for Rosa, the club leader. Lin bought the cake at 7:50 in the rain. They saw Rosa's note (she was coming in early at 9:30), so they changed to Plan B: Riverside Park at 10:30. They took the cake and left in a hurry, leaving the banner half up, the umbrella, the charger and Ahmed's keys.

Model deductions: They must have been planning a surprise party for Rosa. (A3, C1) · Rosa can't have known about it. (C1, D1) · Someone must have bought the cake before the party. (B1) · They can't have eaten the cake: the plates and knife are clean. (B2) · They must have taken the cake with them. (B1, B2) · Four people must have been here: four cups, four names. (A2, C1) · They must have seen Rosa's note and changed their plans. (D1, D2) · They must have left in a hurry. (A3, C2, C3) · They can't have left long ago: the coffee is still warm. (A1) · Whoever brought the umbrella must have arrived before 8:00. (B3) · Someone might have dropped the car keys; the owner might have walked to the park. (C3, D3) · They should have checked the notice board / booked the park in the first place.

Scoring the verdict: 1 point each for the surprise party, Rosa's note, Plan B at the park, leaving in a hurry, the dropped keys. Reward reasoning: a verdict with "might have" for the right uncertain parts is better than an overconfident one.

Self-check
Q1
"The kitchen was spotless when I got home. Someone ___ cleaned it." (You're almost sure.)
A) must have
B) mustn't have
C) can't have
Why: almost sure it happened → must have. mustn't have doesn't exist as a deduction; can't have says it didn't happen.
Q2
Your friend missed a deadline because you never reminded them. Which sentence expresses your regret?
A) I should have reminded you.
B) I must have reminded you.
C) I could of reminded you.
Why: should have = the right thing, but it didn't happen. B is a deduction (and it's false); C has the "of" spelling error.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) She mustn't have seen us.
B) She can't have seen us.
C) She can't has seen us.
Why: the negative of must have is can't have; after a modal, have never changes to has.
Errors you will hear most (2.2)

"She mustn't have known." · "must of / could of / should of" (writing) · "He must has gone." · "It must have rain." · "She must have went." · "Maybe he must left." · "He can't have left" heard as "He can have left" (American /kænt/: listen for the vowel). Also a meaning error: using must have for everything, with no sense of certainty. In feedback, ask "How sure are you?" before correcting the form.

2.3 · Used to / Be Used to / Get Used to (C6)

Exercise 1 — Choose the structure

1 used to have · 2 get used to it · 3 is ('s) used to seeing · 4 get used to driving · 5 used to be

Wrong: 3 "used to see" (that would mean she saw blood in the past but not now) · 2 "be used to it" (grammatical, but the situation is adapting, a process).

Exercise 2 — Base verb or -ing?

1 play (base: used to) · 2 working (be used to) · 3 living (get used to) · 4 walk (Did … use to) · 5 getting (get used to) · 6 like (didn't use to) · 7 using (get used to).

Exercise 3 — Correct the error
  1. I'm used to getting up early these days. (be used to + -ing)
  2. She didn't use to like her boss. (drop the d after didn't)
  3. It took me a month to get used to eating with chopsticks. (get used to + -ing)
  4. I used to live in Denver ten years ago. (past: keep the d) · Also correct: I lived in Denver ten years ago.
  5. Are you used to working on weekends now? (be used to + -ing)

Accept "She never used to like her boss" in 2 (also correct). Note on 4: used to with "ten years ago" is fine for a period of time, but many teachers prefer the simple past with a specific time. Accept both.

Exercise 4 — Dana's new shift (Script 2.3-A)
123456
startsleepinggot used tois used to / 's used toget used toget used to

Also accept 4 has gotten used to (the meaning is the same: the process is finished). Wrong: 1 "starting" · 2 "sleep" · 4 "used to" (without is).

Exercise 5 — Say it with used to, be used to or get used to
  1. I'm used to early meetings. / I'm used to having early meetings.
  2. I'm getting used to the new software. / I'm getting used to using the new software.
  3. Did you use to have a car when you lived downtown?
  4. He got used to the cold weather (after a year). / After a year, he was used to the cold.
  5. My grandmother didn't use to wear jeans. / My grandmother never used to wear jeans.
Dialogue — sort the used tos

Past (gone): I used to work in an office · I used to sit all day · Did you use to be a night owl? · I used to stay up until one. Now (familiar): Are you used to it yet? · I'm still not used to getting up · I'm used to being in bed · You'll be so used to it. Changing (adapting): I'm getting used to it · You'll get used to it · I couldn't get used to going to bed at eight · It takes some getting used to. Note: You'll be so used to it is a future state; couldn't get used to is a past process that didn't finish (at that time).

Speaking — Adjusting to something new, and Find someone who

Adjusting (open): check for 2 used to + base verb, 2 be used to + noun / -ing, 1 get used to, and advice. Model (new technology): I used to pay for everything with cash, and I didn't use to check my bank balance very often. Now I'm used to paying with my phone, and I'm used to getting a notification every time I spend money. I'm still getting used to the banking app: I can never find the "transfer" button! My advice: you'll get used to it, but ask someone to show you the first time. Find someone who: check the question forms: Are you used to getting up before six? · Did you use to play an instrument? · Are you getting used to a new phone or app? · Did you use to have a different job? · Didn't you use to like coffee? / Did you use to dislike coffee?

Self-check
Q1
"When I first moved here the silence felt strange, but now I ___ it."
A) used to
B) am used to
C) used to be
Why: the silence is normal for me now: a present state → am used to + noun / pronoun.
Q2
Which sentence uses the correct verb form?
A) I'm used to work long shifts.
B) I'm used to working long shifts.
C) I'm getting used to work long shifts.
Why: after be / get used to, to is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing.
Q3 · new
"She ___ drive a small car, but now she drives a truck for work."
A) is used to
B) used to
C) got used to
Why: a past habit that isn't true now, followed by a base verb (drive) → used to. A and C would need driving.
Errors you will hear most (2.3)

"I'm used to work nights." · "I use to drink tea every morning." (present habit) · "I didn't used to…" (writing) · "I used to the cold." (missing be) · "I'm used to it" for "I'm getting used to it" (state vs process) · "used" pronounced /juːzd tuː/. Correct the base-verb-after-be used to error every time: it changes the structure. Treat the missing d / extra d as a writing-only point.
