Module 7 — Communicating with Style: Answers

Answers to every Module 7 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model answers for the open tasks (the manager email, the Register Switch Workshop, the anecdote plan, the pitch), the classroom-materials answers (register-sort strips, listening scripts, Persuasion Bingo), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Marking principle

This module is about effect: does the message fit the reader, does the story hold the listener, does the case persuade? Mark appropriacy first, then accuracy, then range. A formal email with one small grammar slip but a consistent register and a clear request is a success; a grammatically perfect email that closes with "Cheers!" is not. For open tasks, accept any answer that performs the function (a reaction that shows interest, a sentence that really is a contrast). American English is the model. Accept British forms when a learner uses them (enquire / enquiry, Mr without a period, Yours sincerely / Yours faithfully, full stop, primary school, practise, colour). In the US, Sincerely, (or Best regards,) is correct after any greeting; don't mark "Yours faithfully" wrong, but tell the learner it's the UK convention.

7.1 · Register — Formal vs Informal English (C20)

Exercise 1 — Turn the dial up
123456789
requestpostponediscoverrequirereceiveinformassisthowevertherefore

Also accept: 3 establish, ascertain · 5 obtain (more for things you make an effort to get: "obtain a permit") · 6 notify · 8 nevertheless · 9 consequently, as a result. Not accepted: 2 "delay" (possible, but different: a delay is usually not planned) · 7 "support" (a different meaning in most contexts).

Exercise 2 — Informal, neutral or formal?

1 I (gimme) · 2 N (polite could, but everyday words) · 3 F (set phrase, at your earliest convenience) · 4 I · 5 F (unfortunately, full forms, did not match the description) · 6 I (Just checking, are we still on) · 7 N (polite but contracted: typical manager email or spoken) · 8 F (do not hesitate, should you require).

Accept 2 as N or F, and 7 as N or F, if the learner gives a reason ("I'm afraid is polite, but the contractions make it less formal"). The discussion matters more than the letter.

Exercise 3 — The inquiry email

1 Dear · 2 inquire · 3 available · 4 Unfortunately · 5 grateful · 6 attached · 7 hearing · 8 Sincerely

Also accept 2 enquire (UK) and 8 Yours faithfully (UK: the greeting has no personal name). Point out the five parts: greeting (1) · reason (2) · detail (3–4) · request (5–6) · closing + sign-off (7–8).

Exercise 4 — Soften the request (model answers)
  1. Would it be possible to take Friday off? / …for me to have Friday off?
  2. Could you (possibly) send me the schedule?
  3. Would you mind explaining this form to me?
  4. I was wondering whether I could leave early today. / …whether it would be OK if I left early today.
  5. Would it be possible to be seated near a window? / …to have a table near the window?

Wrong: 3 "Would you mind to explain…" (mind + -ing) · 4 "I was wondering whether can I leave" (statement word order after whether: C19!) · 1 "Would it be possible that you give me…" (awkward; use to + verb). Also check the voice: a gentle fall-rise, not a flat order.

Exercise 5 — Ana's email to Mr. Patel (model)

Subject: Saturday shift, May 10

Dear Mr. Patel,

I am writing to let you know that I am unable to work my shift on Saturday, May 10, due to a family commitment.

Maria Lopez has kindly agreed to cover the shift for me. I would be grateful if you could update the schedule accordingly, and I would appreciate it if you could also inform Dan.

Thank you for your understanding.

Sincerely,
Ana Silva

Wording will vary. Check: a greeting with a title and surname (or "Hi Mr. Patel," in a friendly workplace: acceptable, one step down the dial), a clear reason in the first line, no contractions, no emoji or abbreviations (u, thx, tho), a polite request frame, and a sign-off. "Family stuff" → a family commitment / a personal matter: learners don't need to explain further. Also accept Best regards, or Kind regards,.

Dialogue — three things that make the second conversation more formal

Any three: an opening that asks permission (Excuse me, Mr. Patel. Do you have a moment? vs got a sec?) · a title + surname · the tentative frame I was wondering whether it might be possible to… · full sentences instead of fragments (Deal.) · Certainly instead of Yeah · Thank you very much instead of You're a lifesaver! · no slang (got a sec, any chance). Note: Ana still uses contractions (He's, I'll): that's normal in polite speech.

Listening — Script 7.1-A (two voicemails) & 7.1-B (dictation)

7.1-A differences (any three): Hey Sam, it's me / Hello, Mr. Patel, this is Ana Silva from the evening team · I can't make it Saturday / I'm afraid I won't be able to work my shift on Saturday · can you cover? / I was wondering whether it would be possible to swap with Sam · you're the best / thank you for your understanding · Lemme know! / Please let me know if that works for you · speed and reductions in the first; slower, full forms in the second. 7.1-B: see the script for the four sentences; check grateful (not "greatful"), convenience, hesitate, inquire.

Self-check
Q1
You're writing to a British company and you began with "Dear Ms Ford,". Which sign-off follows the British rule?
A) Yours sincerely,
B) Yours faithfully,
C) Cheers,
Why: in UK letters, a named greeting → Yours sincerely; "Dear Sir or Madam" → Yours faithfully. In US English, Sincerely, works after any greeting. Cheers is informal.
Q2
Which sentence is the most appropriate for a formal email?
A) Hi, I just wanna know if you've got any spots left.
B) I am writing to inquire whether any places are still available.
C) Gimme the info when you can, thanks!
Why: full forms, a formal verb (inquire) and a complete, polite sentence. A and C use contractions and slang (wanna, gimme).
Q3 · new
Which message mixes registers?
A) Hi Jo! Running late, save me a seat?
B) Dear Mr. Lee: I am writing to confirm my appointment on June 4. Sincerely, Mina Cho
C) Dear Ms. Ortiz, I am writing to request a copy of my contract. Thanks a bunch, see ya!
Why: C opens formally and closes with slang. A is consistently informal and B is consistently formal: both fit their readers.
Errors you will hear most (7.1)

"I am writing for ask / for asking…" · "I would be grateful if you can…" · "Would you mind to send…" · "Dear Mr. Daniel" (title + first name) · "Dear Sir or Madam … Cheers" · contractions in the formal letter · "Hi" + "Yours faithfully" · a formal letter that never says what it wants until the last line · "I want a refund" (too direct) · stiff over-formality in the text to a friend ("I would be grateful if you could save me a seat"). Also "greatful" in writing. Praise every learner whose three versions are clearly different.

7.2 · Telling Stories & Anecdotes (C21)

Exercise 1 — Order the anecdote

a 5 · b 2 · c 6 · d 1 · e 3 · f 4

So the story reads: d (hook) → b (scene: was carrying) → e (starting situation: At first) → f (Just as) → a (the twist: All of a sudden) → c (the ending: To cut a long story short… that's why). Also accept b and d swapped only if the learner notices that "The funniest thing…" works better as the hook.

Exercise 2 — The best connector

1 At first · 2 Just as · 3 A few minutes later · 4 All of a sudden · 5 before I knew it · 6 In the end

In 4, Suddenly, is also correct. Never both ("Suddenly, all of a sudden…").

Exercise 3 — Past forms
12345678
was rainingwas walkinghad just boughtgotransteppedhad thought / thoughtlaughed

1–2 are the scene (background, in progress); 3 is the backstory (before the scene); 4–6 and 8 are the events. In 7 both are correct: had thought emphasizes that the mistake came before he stepped back. Also accept 8 were laughing (describing the journey). Wrong: 1 "rained" (acceptable, but loses the "scene" effect: mark with a note) · 3 "just bought" (US speakers do say this informally; the target is had just bought) · 4 "was getting."

Exercise 4 — absolutely + extreme adjective

1 absolutely starving · 2 absolutely thrilled · 3 absolutely freezing · 4 absolutely exhausted · 5 absolutely terrified · 6 absolutely furious

really is also fine with extreme adjectives (really furious). Wrong: "very furious," "absolutely angry."

Exercise 5 — Listener reactions (samples)

1 "Oh no! What did you do?" / "You're kidding!" · 2 "No way! How embarrassing!" / "You must have been mortified!" · 3 "No way! What are the chances?" / "What a small world!" · 4 "That's hilarious! You have to tell me the whole thing!"

Any short, genuine reaction that shows interest and invites the teller to continue. Not accepted: flat or formal responses ("I see. That is interesting.") or changing the subject.

Dialogue — Priya & Tom

Hook: "You'll never guess what happened to me on the way to the interview this morning." · Scene: "I was running a bit late, and I was wearing my best suit." · Historic present: "All of a sudden I'm absolutely soaked… this old man just looks at me and goes…" · Direct speech: "Lovely weather for it!" · Ending: "To cut a long story short, I got the job. And that's why I always carry an umbrella now. Lesson learned!" Tom's reactions: Go on. · Oh no! · No way! What did you do? · You're kidding!

Listening — Script 7.2-A (flat vs shaped) & 7.2-B (reactions)

7.2-A: the shaped version adds a hook (You'll never guess…), a scene (I was walking across town…), backstory (I'd been dreaming about…), just as, direct speech (the owner goes, "Sorry, we're closed!"), emphasis (absolutely starving, right in my face) and an ending with a point (And that's why I always call first now). 7.2-B reactions at each ⏸ (any natural one): ⏸1 "Go on!" / "What happened?" · ⏸2 "Oh no!" · ⏸3 "No way!" / "You're kidding!" · ⏸4 "What did you do?" / "Then what?" · ⏸5 "That's hilarious!" / "What a story!"

Self-check
Q1
Which phrase best opens a spoken anecdote?
A) In the end, I went home.
B) You'll never guess what happened to me.
C) And that's why I was late.
Why: B is a hook: it tells the listener a story is coming. A and C are ending phrases.
Q2
Your friend says, "…and when I looked in my bag, my wallet was gone." What's the most natural reaction?
A) Oh no! What did you do?
B) I see. That is interesting.
C) Anyway, what are you having for lunch?
Why: A shows sympathy and invites the teller to continue. B sounds cold (wrong register!), and C ignores the story.
Q3 · new
Which sentence sets the scene for a story?
A) In the end, we all went home.
B) No way! That's hilarious!
C) I was waiting for the elevator when the fire alarm went off.
Why: the past continuous (was waiting) gives the background, and when + past simple brings in the first event. A is an ending; B is a listener's reaction.
Errors you will hear most (7.2)

"When I walked home, it started to rain." (scene → was walking) · a whole story in the present simple with no past forms (not the same as the chosen historic present) · "and then… and then… and then…" · "Suddenly, all of a sudden" · "very furious / very exhausted" · "He said me, '…'" · "I was so embarrassing" (→ embarrassed) · "Before I knew" (→ Before I knew it) · no ending ("…so, yeah"). Correct the -ed / -ing adjective and the past continuous scene every time; treat the rest as range upgrades.

7.3 · Making a Case — Presentations & Persuasion (C22)

Exercise 1 — Presentation phrases

1 cover · 2 begin · 3 brings · 4 Take · 5 show · 6 sum

Also accept: 1 discuss, look at, focus on · 2 start · 5 suggest, indicate. Wrong: 5 "shows" (plural subject figures) · 1 "talk" (needs about).

Exercise 2 — Name the technique

1 RQ · 2 C · 3 3 · 4 CC · 5 A · 6 C

5 "Imagine…" speaks directly to the listener (appeal). 6 sets two choices against each other (contrast); accept A if the learner points to we. 1 is a rhetorical question: nobody is expected to answer.

Exercise 3 — Concede and counter

1 d · 2 e · 3 c · 4 a · 5 b

Punctuation: after a semicolon, the counter starts with lowercase however / that said / nevertheless; after a period, with a capital (4 → a). While it's true that…, takes a comma and no second connector (2 → e).

Exercise 4 — Handling questions

1 B · 2 A · 3 B · 4 C

1: never invent a figure; offering to follow up is professional. 2: checking your understanding buys time and avoids answering the wrong question. 3: concede, then counter. A is combative, C gives up the case. 4: check back politely before moving on.

Dialogue — Leila's moves

Tripling: "greener, safer and friendlier" · Rhetorical question: "Wouldn't we all rather look at vegetables than trash?" · Concede–counter: "Admittedly, summer is the hardest time. That said, eighteen families have already signed up…" Also: signpost (To sum up), handling phrases (That's a good question · I'll send the details · Does that answer your question?), and honesty about the figure.

Speaking — The Pitch Panel (model, topic card 1: bike parking at work)

S1: Good morning. Today we'll cover a small problem with a simple solution. First, the problem: twelve of us cycle to work, and there's nowhere safe to lock a bike. Last month, two bikes were stolen.

S2: Our proposal is a covered bike rack for twenty bikes next to the side entrance. The main advantage is health: research suggests regular cyclists take fewer sick days. Take, for example, our coworker Sam: since he started cycling, he hasn't missed a single day. It's healthier, cheaper and greener.

S3: Admittedly, it costs about $1,500. That said, that's less than we spend on parking permits in two months. It's not a cost; it's an investment. So to sum up: safer bikes, healthier staff. We're asking you to approve the rack this month. Thank you. We're happy to take questions.

The pitch is not marked with a score. Use the Persuasion Bingo grids and your notes for feedback: signposts, a claim with evidence, at least one technique, one concede–counter, a call to action, and a calm first line to each question.

Listening — Script 7.3-A (model pitch) & Persuasion Bingo

Signposts: Today I'd like to… · First,… · Moving on to… · For instance… · To sum up… · Argue: The main advantage is… · Research suggests… · Rhetorical question: Wouldn't it be great to arrive at work already awake? · Tripling: healthier, cheaper and greener · Contrast: A safe place to park isn't a luxury; it's basic. · Concede–counter: Admittedly, it isn't free. That said,… · Appeal: Think about… · Call to action: So we're asking you to… · Every square on bingo version A and 7 of 9 on version B are heard in 7.3-A (B's "Take, for example" and "That's a good question" come only in 7.3-B).

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence best concedes and counters an objection?
A) Admittedly, it's expensive; however, it saves money long-term.
B) This is simply the best option, period.
C) Yes, it's expensive, and that's a real problem.
Why: A admits the objection, then pushes back with a stronger point. B ignores the objection; C concedes but never counters.
Q2
Someone asks a question and you're not sure of the answer. What's the most professional response?
A) Make up a confident-sounding figure on the spot.
B) "That's a good question. I don't have the exact figure, but I'll follow up."
C) Ignore it and move straight to your conclusion.
Why: honesty plus a promise to follow up keeps your credibility. Bluffing (A) and ignoring (C) lose the audience's trust.
Q3 · new
"It's not a cost; it's an investment." Which technique is this?
A) Tripling
B) Contrast
C) A rhetorical question
Why: two ideas are set against each other to sharpen the point. Tripling needs three items; a rhetorical question is a question.
Errors you will hear most (7.3)

"Research suggest…" / "The figures shows…" · "Although it is expensive, but…" · "Admittedly…, but however…" · "I will cover about…" · "In conclusion" used as the first signpost · rhetorical questions with a rising (real-question) tune · no pause at signposts · reading a script at top speed · "Any questions? No? OK, bye." · answering a question with only "Yes" or "It's a good question." and silence. Fix agreement and the double connector every time; treat the rest as delivery coaching.
