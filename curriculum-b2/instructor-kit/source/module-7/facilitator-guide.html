Module 7 — Communicating with Style

Three classes about how we say things, not just what we say. First, register: the four features that shift between casual and formal English (word choice, contractions, sentence length, set phrases), polite spoken requests, and the five-part formal email, practiced in a workplace register-switching workshop. Then storytelling: hooks, scene-setting, narrative connectors, the historic present, direct speech and listener reactions, practiced in an anecdote circle. Last, persuasion: signposting, claim + reason + evidence, rhetorical techniques, concede-and-counter and handling questions, practiced in a problem–solution pitch with audience Q&A.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (the big communicative task) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes. In this module the freer task is long (20–22 minutes) on purpose: it is the lesson. Protect it; cut controlled practice to homework before you cut the task.
  • Present from slides/module-7-style.html. Print materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html before C20. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Register Switch situation cards and register-sort strips, the anecdote prompt cards, listener-reaction cards and story-arc planner, the pitch topic cards, objection cards and pitch planner, Persuasion Bingo, and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home and slow them down.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. B1 Module 7 practiced complaints at a counter and job interviews; B1 Module 5 and B2 Lesson 1.1 taught the narrative tenses; B2 Lesson 4.3 used a few presentation phrases; Lesson 6.3 (C19) taught indirect questions (Could you tell me whether…?), which are politeness and therefore register. Check what learners know in each lead-in and move faster where they are strong. The new B2 work is control: choosing a register on purpose and holding it, shaping a story for a listener, and building an argument that survives a question.
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a tense or a preposition. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. At B2, give feedback on two tracks: accuracy (what was wrong) and range (what could be richer). Keep a two-column notebook page: "Fix it" and "Upgrade it" (e.g. very angry → absolutely furious; and then… and then → just as… before I knew it; I think it's good → The main advantage is…). Praise the effect first ("Everyone laughed at your ending. That's a landed story.") and then the form.
  • Dominant speakers. All three tasks give one person the floor, so structure the turns. Use a talking-turn token in the anecdote circle, a time limit with a visible timer for pitches, and jobs for strong speakers: the range spotter (notes one great phrase per speaker), the timekeeper, or the panel chair who must call on a quiet person first. Say it kindly: "Great question, Mateo. Let's hold it and hear from someone who hasn't asked one yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Every lesson has a stretch box. The C1 direction in this module is subtlety: a register between neutral and formal (the everyday workplace email), a story with a well-timed pause and an understated ending, and persuasion that anticipates the objection before anyone raises it ("You might be thinking…"). Don't add new grammar; ask for more precise choices.
  • American English throughout. The online lessons and this kit both use American English and American letter conventions: Mr. with a period, inquire / inquiry, the sign-off Sincerely, (or Best regards,) after any formal greeting, period, elementary school, a technician to fix the heating. The online lesson mentions the British Yours faithfully / Yours sincerely rule as something learners may meet in UK letters; accept British forms when learners use them (Mr without a period, enquire / enquiry, Yours faithfully / Yours sincerely, mate, cheers, sorted, primary school, full stop).
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Fit the reader → hold the listener → move the audience. In C20 learners take one message and dress it three ways: a text to a friend, an email to a manager, a formal letter. The skill is choosing. In C21 they keep a small group listening to a two-minute story, and they learn to be a good listener too. In C22 they stand up (or sit tall) and persuade a panel to fund a community or workplace improvement, then defend it under questions. Each class uses the one before it: the pitch needs the neutral-to-formal register of C20, and a short anecdote ("Take my coworker Ana…") is one of the best pieces of evidence in a pitch.

Trauma-informed: whose story, whose opinion?

Anecdotes (C21) invite personal memories, and some learners' most "memorable" stories are about migration, danger, loss or officials. Keep every story light: a mix-up, a coincidence, a cooking disaster, a lost-and-found, a kind stranger, a funny misunderstanding at work. Offer three choices out loud: a true story, a story from a prompt card or new identity card, or an invented story. Listeners never ask "Is it true?" (put it on the board as a rule). Never ask for the story of the journey here or a first day in the country. Register (C20): the workplace situations include calling in sick. Teach that in English you don't have to explain ("I'm unwell and unable to come in today" is complete and professional); no one describes real symptoms. Persuasion (C22): pitch topics are everyday community and workplace improvements, drawn from cards. Teams argue for their card, not their personal beliefs, and panelists ask questions from objection cards, so nobody has to reveal an opinion. No politics, religion or immigration topics, even if a learner suggests one; say "Great energy. Let's keep that one for outside class" and give them a card.

7.1 · C20

Register — Formal vs Informal English

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain register as a dial, not a switch, and place a sentence as informal, neutral or formal
  • shift the four features: word choice (ask for → request, put off → postpone), contractions (I'm → I am), sentence length (short and linked with and / so → longer with as, although, therefore), and set phrases (Just wondering… → I am writing to inquire about…)
  • soften a spoken request for a stranger or a manager with could, would, I was wondering whether, would it be possible to
  • build a five-part formal email: greeting → reason for writing → detail → request/action → sign-off, and match the sign-off to the greeting (US Sincerely,; UK Yours sincerely / faithfully)
  • take one workplace message and write it three ways (text to a friend, email to a manager, formal letter) without mixing registers

If you run long

Move Workbook Ex 3–4 to homework and do only Ex 1–2 in class. Never cut the Workshop's "read aloud and guess the reader" stage: it's where learners hear register, which is the whole point. With a strong class, shorten the register-sort to 2 minutes.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: register = fit, not quality. All registers are good English. The question is who the reader or listener is, how well you know them, and what the message is for.

"I would be grateful if you could reply at your earliest convenience." Is this better English than "Can you get back to me soon?" (No, just more formal.) Would you text it to your sister? (No, it would sound cold or sarcastic.) Would you write it to a bank? (Yes.) So what decides? (The reader and the situation.)

Meaning: the four features.

"find out" and "discover": same meaning? (Yes.) Which is more formal? (discover.) Is "find out" wrong in an email? (No, but it leans informal; in a formal letter choose the single verb.) · "I haven't received it" in a formal letter: OK? (Better: "I have not received it.") In a text to a friend? (Contractions are normal.)
"Sorry to bother you" and "I hope you don't mind my writing": which one for a company you've never contacted? (The second.) · A letter starts "Dear Sir or Madam" and ends "Cheers!" What's the problem? (Mixed registers.) What's the rule? (One register from greeting to sign-off.)

Meaning: polite spoken requests. Formal speech isn't about long words; it's about distance: past forms and modals give the other person an easy way to say no.

"I was wondering whether it might be possible to move our reservation." Is the guest talking about the past? (No! Now.) Why "was wondering"? (It's softer, more polite.) Is the guest sure the answer is yes? (No, "might" and "possible" leave room for no.) Would you say this to a close friend? (No, too stiff.)

Meaning: sign-offs, US and UK.

You're writing to a company in Chicago. How do you end? (Sincerely, or Best regards,.) A company in London, and you started "Dear Sir or Madam"? (Yours faithfully,.) You started "Dear Ms. Ford"? (Yours sincerely,.) Will an American reader mind "Yours sincerely"? (No. It's fine, just a little old-fashioned.)

Form.

Wordsphrasal verb / everyday word → single, often Latin-based verbask for → request · get → obtain / receive · find out → discover · put off → postpone · need → require · tell → inform · help → assist · but → however · so → therefore
Contractionsformal writing spells them outI'm → I am · haven't → have not · won't → will not
Sentencesshort + and / but / so → longer, linked with as, although, which, thereforeThe heater's broken. It's cold. → As the heating is no longer working, the apartment has become extremely cold.
Spoken requestsCould you…? · Would you mind + -ing? · I was wondering whether / if + past · Would it be possible to…? · …if that's not too much troubleI was wondering whether I could leave an hour early on Friday.
Email skeleton① Dear… ② I am writing to inquire about / complain about / request… ③ detail ④ I would be grateful if you could… / I would therefore request… ⑤ I look forward to hearing from you. + sign-offUS: Dear Mr. Clarke: (or ,) … Sincerely, · UK: Dear Mr Clarke, … Yours sincerely, · Dear Sir or Madam, … Yours faithfully,

Pronunciation. Register lives in the voice too. Casual speech is fast with heavy reductions (gonna, wanna, can ya, lemme know); polite speech is slower, with full forms and a gentle fall-rise on requests: "I was wondering whether it might be possible… ↘↗". A flat, fast "Could you move my appointment" sounds like an order; the same words with a softer, rising end sound like a request. In I would be grateful, would is weak /wəd/ and be is short; stress grateful. Inquire /ɪnˈkwaɪər/: stress on the second syllable. Contractions are fine in formal speech ("It's under Whitfield"); the no-contractions rule is for formal writing.

Drill script
  1. Flip the card (word-pair flashcards, informal side up): T: "ask for" → S: "request." T: "put off" → S: "postpone." T: "find out" → S: "discover." T: "need" → S: "require." T: "tell" → S: "inform." Then reverse: T: "assist" → S: "help." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Turn the dial up: T (casual voice): "Can you send me the form?" → S (polite): "Could you possibly send me the form?" → S2 (formal writing): "I would be grateful if you could send me the form."
  3. Soften it: T: "Move my shift." → S: "I was wondering whether I could move my shift." T: "Give me a day off." → S: "Would it be possible to take a day off on Friday?" Model the fall-rise; learners copy the tune, not just the words.
  4. Set-phrase ping-pong: T: "Hi…" → S: "Dear…" · T: "Let me know." → S: "I look forward to hearing from you." · T: "Thanks!" → S: "Thank you for your assistance." · T: "Talk soon." → S: "Please do not hesitate to contact me."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 says a casual request to S2 ("Can I borrow your charger?"); S2 turns to S3 and "asks the manager" the same thing politely. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Register Switch Workshop — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Each group takes one situation card (set C): e.g. You can't work your Saturday shift and a coworker has agreed to cover it · The printer at work has been broken for a week · You'd like to take a first-aid course your employer mentioned · Your new washing machine was delivered with a dent. Every card has the core facts, so nobody has to invent personal details.
  • Roles: A writes a text to a friend or close coworker (1–3 lines), B an email to a manager (neutral-to-formal, 50–70 words), C a formal letter or email to an organization or company (five parts, 70–90 words). Rotate roles if the group does a second card.
  • Pass the sheet to the right. The checker uses the four features as a checklist (words · contractions · sentences · set phrases) and upgrades one line in a different color. They may not rewrite the whole thing.
  • Read-aloud round: the group reads its three versions in random order. The class says who the reader is and which feature gave it away ("It's the manager: no contractions but 'Hi Dan,' not 'Dear Mr.'"). This is the key learning moment: learners discover that the manager email is in the middle of the dial, and that's the one they'll write most in real life.
  • Dominant speakers: give the strongest writer role C (the hardest) and the job of reader for the read-aloud round only if a quieter group member doesn't want it. The quietest learner may choose role A: short, and a real skill. Everyone reads at least one line aloud.
  • Calling-in-sick card: remind the group that the message says "I'm unwell and unable to come in today", with no details. That is complete and professional in English.
ICQs before the Workshop
How many versions does your group write? (Three.) · Who writes the formal letter? (C.) · Does the text to a friend need "Dear"? (No!) · When you check a partner's version, do you rewrite everything? (No, upgrade one line.) · When you read aloud, do you say "This is the email to the manager"? (No, the class guesses.) · Do you need to use your real job or your real boss's name? (No, the card has everything.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with two ends of the dial only (text to a friend ⇄ formal email) and one feature at a time: first the set phrases, which carry most of the register. Give a phrase strip with the five email parts already started ("Dear…, / I am writing to… / I would be grateful if you could… / I look forward to hearing from you. / Sincerely,") so the learner fills in the facts. In the Workshop, take role A or B and copy the frame from the strip. Target: one text and one framed formal email, read aloud.

Stretch

Aim for the middle of the dial: the warm-but-professional email to a manager ("Hi Dan, I hope your week's going well. I wanted to check whether…"). Use would appreciate it if, further to our conversation, at your earliest convenience, and a formal passive ("The item was delivered damaged"). C1 step: rewrite one sentence three ways and say why each word changed. Early finishers write the company's polite reply to the complaint.

L1 watch-outs
  • Over-formal everywhere: learners from languages with strong formal address (Spanish/Portuguese usted/você/o senhor, Korean and Japanese honorifics, Arabic, Russian вы, Vietnamese kinship pronouns) often assume "formal = always safer" and write stiff texts to friends. Praise the formal version, then show the casual one is equally correct in its place.
  • Too direct in requests: Russian, Polish, German, Dutch, Arabic and Hebrew speakers may use a plain imperative or "I want" where English expects softening ("I want to change my shift"). It isn't rude in their L1; in English it can sound demanding. Teach I was wondering whether… as a chunk.
  • Over-long greetings and closings: Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Spanish and Tagalog letters often open with good wishes and close with blessings or long thanks. In English formal email, the purpose comes in the first line ("I am writing to…"). A short "I hope you are well" is fine in a manager email, not in a complaint.
  • "I am writing for ask" / "I would be grateful if you can": Spanish para preguntar, Portuguese para perguntar → "for ask"; teach writing to + verb. Keep would … could together: "I would be grateful if you could…".
  • Honorific without a surname: "Dear Mr. Daniel" (Tagalog, Arabic, Vietnamese, Thai and many African Englishes use a title + first name). In US formal email: Mr. / Ms. + last name, or the first name alone if the workplace uses first names.
  • Ms. vs Mrs. vs Miss: teach Ms. /mɪz/ as the safe default for any woman in business; learners from many backgrounds use Mrs. for all women.

7.2 · C21

Telling Stories & Anecdotes

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • shape a spoken anecdote with an arc: open (a hook) → build (scene, then events) → climax → land (the point or punchline)
  • open with a hook (You'll never guess what happened… · So the other day… · This reminds me of the time…) and set the scene with the past continuous
  • control the pace with varied connectors (at first, then, a few minutes later, just as, all of a sudden, before I knew it) instead of "and then… and then"
  • bring the climax alive with the historic present, direct speech (he goes / she was like, informal speech only) and emphasis (absolutely furious, the worst… ever)
  • react as a listener (No way! · You must have been mortified. · What happened next?) and land the ending (In the end… · To cut a long story short… · And that's why…)
Materials
  • Workbook 7.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, Priya & Tom dialogue, story-arc planner)
  • Slides 9–13 · Connector and opener flashcards · Extreme-adjective picture cards (angry → furious, excited → thrilled, cold → freezing, hot → boiling, nervous → terrified) · Anecdote prompt cards (12) · Listener-reaction cards (1 set per circle) · Story-arc planners · Talking-turn tokens · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 7.2-A (the same story told flat, then shaped) and 7.2-B (a story with pauses for listener reactions)

If you run long

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: a hook signals "a story is starting." Without it, the listener doesn't switch into listening mode.

"You'll never guess what happened to me this morning." Is this a real question? (No.) Does the speaker want you to guess? (No, to listen.) What's a good answer? ("What?" / "Go on!") · "This reminds me of the time…" When do you use it? (When the story links to what someone just said.)

Meaning: scene vs events.

"I was waiting for the bus when a dog ran off with my sandwich." Which action was in progress, the background? (Waiting.) Which one was short and sudden? (The dog ran.) Which tense for the background? (Past continuous.) · "Just as I opened the door, the phone rang." One thing after the other, or at the same moment? (The same moment.)

Meaning: the historic present.

"So I'm standing there, and this guy walks up and goes, 'Is this your car?'" Is this happening now? (No, it's a past story.) Why present tense? (It pulls the listener into the moment.) Is "goes" about walking? (No, here it means "says," informal.) Would you write this in a formal report to your manager? (No, spoken stories only.)

Meaning: extreme adjectives.

"I was absolutely furious." Stronger than "very angry"? (Yes.) Can I say "very furious"? (No, absolutely or really.) Can I say "absolutely angry"? (No, very angry.) · Freezing = very… (cold). Thrilled = very… (excited, happy).

Pronunciation. A story is a performance. Teach three tools: (1) Pace: slower for the scene, faster at "all of a sudden," then a pause just before the punchline. (2) Pitch: a higher, wider pitch range for direct speech ("and he goes, 'Is this your car?'"). (3) Stress on the intensifier: "I was absolutely soaked." Hooks have a rising, inviting tune: "You'll never guess what happened ↗." No way! is a high rise then fall; Oh no… is low and falling. In fast speech, was is weak /wəz/: "I wəz walking home…"

Drill script
  1. Hook it (suspense tune): T: "I lost my keys." → S: "You'll never guess what happened: I lost my keys!" T: "A strange thing at work." → S: "The strangest thing happened at work…" Choral, then individuals with a dramatic face.
  2. Set the scene: T: "walk home · rain start" → S: "I was walking home when it started to rain." T: "cook dinner · power go out" → S: "I was cooking dinner when the power went out."
  3. Just as chain: S1: "Just as I sat down, …" S2 finishes: "…the phone rang." S2 starts a new one: "Just as I opened the fridge, …" Around the room.
  4. Switch to now: T: "He walked up to me and said, 'Is this your dog?'" → S: "So he walks up to me and goes, 'Is this your dog?'" Two or three more.
  5. Absolutely! (picture cards): T holds up angry: "Were you very angry?" → S: "Angry? I was absolutely furious!" · cold → "absolutely freezing" · excited → "absolutely thrilled" · nervous → "absolutely terrified."
Anecdote Circle — how to run it
  • Choosing a story. Spread the 12 prompt cards face-up (e.g. The wrong bus, A cooking disaster, The small world coincidence, Lost and found, The misunderstood message, A kind stranger, The pet that…). Each card has three story-starter facts, so an invented story is easy. Say: "True, card, or invented: all equal. Nobody will ask which."
  • Planning. The story-arc planner has five boxes (hook · scene · 2–3 events · climax with one line of direct speech · ending). Key words only; collect any full-sentence scripts before the telling. Learners talk from notes, not read.
  • Circles of 4. The teller holds the token. Listeners hold the reaction cards and must "spend" at least two during the story and ask one follow-up question at the end ("What did your boss say?"). The fourth person in each round is the range spotter: they write down one great phrase the teller used and read it back ("You said 'before I knew it'!").
  • Second telling. Research on fluency (the 4/3/2 technique) shows that telling the same story again, shorter, makes it faster and more accurate. Ask each circle's chosen teller to retell in 90 seconds in a new circle. Watch how much better the second version is and tell them so.
  • Dominant speakers: the 2-minute limit is firm (a phone timer in the middle of the circle). A strong storyteller becomes the range spotter in the first round so they listen first. A learner who dominates as a listener (interrupting) gets the job of asking the one follow-up question only.
  • Quiet learners may tell a 4-sentence version (hook, scene, one event, ending). That's a complete anecdote. Anyone can say "Pass" once and tell their story to you at the end instead.
ICQs before the Anecdote Circle
Does your story have to be true? (No: true, card, or invented.) · Do you write full sentences on the planner? (No, key words.) · How long is each story? (Two minutes.) · Listeners: how many reactions? (At least two.) And at the end? (One follow-up question.) · Can we ask "Is that true?" (No!) · Who holds the token? (The teller.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use a picture prompt card and a 4-box planner with sentence starters printed: "You'll never guess… / I was ___ing when… / All of a sudden… / In the end…". Let the learner draw in the boxes instead of writing. In the circle, the learner tells the 4-sentence version and uses the reaction cards (read aloud from the card) as a listener. Target: one complete short anecdote and three reactions.

Stretch

Use the past perfect for a backstory twist ("What I didn't know was that she'd already…"), What I didn't realize was… clefts (from 4.2), and a planned pause before the punchline. C1 step: understatement for humor ("It wasn't my finest moment.") and an ending that ties back to the hook. Early finishers retell a classmate's story in reported speech for the class, keeping it short.

L1 watch-outs
  • Tense avoidance in stories: Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Malay speakers mark time with words, not verb forms, and may tell a whole story in the present simple ("Yesterday I go…"). That's different from the chosen historic present. Teach the contrast: past for the story, present only at the climax, on purpose.
  • Past continuous for everything: some Spanish, Portuguese and Italian speakers transfer the imperfect and write "I was going to the store and I was seeing my friend." Past continuous is for background; the events are past simple.
  • "Suddenly, all of a sudden" and "very furious / very exhausted": common everywhere. Upgrade in feedback, not during the story.
  • He / she in direct speech: Tagalog (siya), Chinese (spoken tā), Turkish, Persian, Finnish and Hungarian speakers mix he and she, which confuses who said what in a story. Point to the character on the planner.
  • Listening culture: in some cultures listeners stay quiet to show respect, and loud reactions feel rude; in others, overlapping talk is warm; and Japanese listeners give frequent small responses (aizuchi) that English speakers can mistake for agreement. Present English reactions as a tool, not a personality change, and model a quiet "Oh no…" as well as a big "No way!".
  • Final consonants in past-tense verbs (walked, stopped, laughed): Vietnamese, Cantonese, Thai and Korean speakers may drop /t/ or /d/, which makes the story sound present. Accept it in the circle; drill it briefly in feedback.

7.3 · C22

Making a Case — Presentations & Persuasion

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • signpost a short talk so the audience always knows where they are: Today I'll cover… · First, let's look at… · Moving on to… · To sum up…
  • build an argument: a claim + reasons + evidence (a figure or a vivid example): The main advantage is… · Research suggests… · Take, for example…
  • use persuasive techniques: rhetorical questions, tripling, contrast (It's not a cost; it's an investment.), and appealing to the listener (you, we, together)
  • concede and counter an objection: Admittedly… However… · That said… · While it's true that…, …
  • handle questions calmly: That's a good question. · If I understand you correctly, you're asking… · I don't have the exact figure, but I'll follow up., and close with a call to action

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: signposts are for the listener. A reader can look back; a listener can't. Signposts tell them where they are.

"Moving on to my second point…" Is the speaker starting, changing topic, or finishing? (Changing to the next point.) What should you do right after a signpost? (Pause for a beat.) · "To sum up…" Will the talk go on for a long time? (No, it's ending.)

Meaning: an argument = claim + support.

"Bike parking is better." Opinion or argument? (Opinion only.) "Bike parking is better because twelve people already cycle and there's nowhere safe to lock a bike." (An argument: a claim + a reason + evidence.) · "Take my coworker Ana…" Is a story allowed as evidence? (Yes, an example makes it real.)

Meaning: the techniques.

"Wouldn't we all like a safer walk home?" Does the speaker want an answer? (No, it makes you think and agree.) · "It's not a cost; it's an investment." How many ideas are set against each other? (Two: that's contrast.) · "It saves time, saves money and saves stress." How many? (Three: tripling. Why three? It's memorable, rhythmic.)

Meaning: concede and counter.

"Admittedly, it costs more at first. That said, it pays for itself within a year." Does the speaker agree that it costs more? (Yes, they admit it.) Do they change their mind? (No.) Which part is stronger, the first or the second? (The second.) Why admit the weakness? (It builds trust; you look fair, not naive.)

Pronunciation. Pause after every signpost (count "one" silently). Tripling has a list tune: rise, rise, fall: "cheaper ↗, faster ↗, and greener ↘." Contrast stress falls on the two contrasting words: "It's not a cost; it's an investment." Rhetorical questions usually fall (they're really statements): "Isn't that worth it? ↘". Admittedly /ədˈmɪtɪdli/: four syllables, stress on mit. Chunk the talk into thought groups and slow down: nerves make everyone fast; a pitch at 80% speed sounds confident.

Drill script
  1. Signpost and pause: T shows a stage card (START · NEXT · EXAMPLE · END) → S: "Today I'll cover two points. (pause)" · "Moving on to my second point… (pause)" · "Take, for example,… " · "To sum up… (pause)." Choral; exaggerate the pause.
  2. Rule of three: T: "cheaper, faster…" → S: "…and greener!" T: "save time…" → S: "save time, save money and save stress." Keep the rise-rise-fall tune.
  3. Not X; Y: T: "cost / investment" → S: "It's not a cost; it's an investment." T: "problem / opportunity" → S: "It's not a problem; it's an opportunity." T: "a luxury / a necessity."
  4. Concede in one breath: T: "expensive" → S: "Admittedly, it's expensive. That said, it pays for itself." T: "takes time" → S: "While it's true that it takes time, the benefits are huge."
  5. Buy time: T asks a hard question fast ("How much will it cost exactly?") → S: "That's a good question. I don't have the exact figure, but I'll follow up." Then S asks S: open pairs → closed pairs.
The Pitch Panel — how to run it
  • Topics (set F, 8 cards): bike parking at work · a staff language-buddy program · a community tool library · better lighting in the parking lot · a shared-shift swap board · an after-school homework club at the library · a community garden on an empty lot · water refill stations at the community center. Each card has the problem, the solution, the cost, and two facts for evidence. Teams may add an invented anecdote as an example.
  • Teams of 3, one part each (see the planner). A pair is fine; a solo learner can pitch with you as the "team." Everyone speaks. The strongest speaker in a team takes Part 3 (concede–counter + close), which is the hardest.
  • Pitch: 2 minutes, strict. Show the timer. At 1:45 hold up a "wrap up" card. Speakers talk from the planner, not a script.
  • Q&A: 2 minutes. The panel chair (rotate the job; give it to a dominant speaker, with the rule "call on someone who hasn't asked yet first") picks panelists. Panelists read or adapt an objection card ("Who's going to maintain it?" · "What if nobody uses it?" · "Has this worked anywhere else?"). Because the questions come from cards, nobody is attacking or revealing their own view, and nobody has to be against a classmate's idea.
  • Persuasion Bingo keeps the panel listening: tick each technique or signpost you hear. A line of three = a quiet "Bingo!" at the end of the pitch (not during). The team gets the credit.
  • Voting: one sticky note per panelist on the board under the most persuasive pitch, not their own. Award the "best answer to a question" too, so a team that pitched quietly but answered well is recognized.

L1 watch-outs
  • "Research suggest" / "Studies shows": third-person -s slips appear under pressure for almost everyone, especially Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Haitian Creole speakers (no verb agreement in L1). Put research suggests · studies show on the board.
  • Double connector: "Although it is expensive, but it saves money." / "Admittedly…, but however…". Chinese (suīrán… dànshì), Vietnamese (tuy… nhưng), Korean and Arabic use a pair of linkers. English uses one: Although…, … or …, but….
  • Hedging vs over-claiming: some learners (e.g. from Japanese, Korean, Thai and some East African contexts) soften so much that there is no claim ("Maybe perhaps it could be a little good"); others state opinion as fact ("Everybody knows this is the best"). Aim for a clear claim + a fair concession.
  • Rhetorical questions answered: learners from classrooms where every question must be answered may reply to the presenter's rhetorical question. Explain the falling tune and the "don't answer" rule; enjoy the laugh.
  • "I will cover about two points" / "I will talk about the bike": Spanish/Portuguese hablar sobre → "talk about" is fine, but "cover about" isn't. cover / discuss / explain + object directly.
  • Stress on long words: adMITtedly, inVESTment, opporTUnity, eVIdence. Speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Tagalog, Yoruba) may give every syllable equal weight, which flattens the rhetoric. Tap the stressed syllable on the desk.
