Module 5 — Precision Grammar

Three classes about the small choices that make English exact. First, the future continuous and future perfect place an action precisely on a future timeline: still happening (This time next year we'll be running evening classes) or already finished (By June we'll have painted every classroom). Second, the causative shows exactly who did the work: I fixed the sink vs I had the sink fixed, plus the "misfortune" meaning and get someone to / have someone do. Third, determiners and quantifiers say exactly how much of a group you mean: all / whole, every / each, both / neither / either, most (of), none of, with correct agreement. The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

3 lessons Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 90 min per class CEFR B2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-5-precision.html. Print materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html before C14. It holds the service picture cards and grammar cards, the project and character cards with the timeline frame, the Errand Week information gap, the Services & Errands role-play cards, the class survey cards with the tally sheet and report frame, the "Precision Path" board game, and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. B1 Lesson 5.3 taught the future continuous (This time tomorrow I'll be flying) and time clauses with the present (when I land). B1 learners also met some / any / much / many / a few / a little. Check what they know in each lead-in and move quickly where they are strong. The new B2 work is the future perfect and the continuous-vs-perfect choice against one reference point, the causative (new for almost everyone), and the near-neighbor contrasts in 5.3 (all / whole, every / each, both / neither / either, most / most of) with their verb agreement.
  • Precision is the theme, but fluency still comes first. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix will have or every people. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. Praise the message first ("Your timeline was so clear, I could picture opening day"), then the form. At B2, give feedback on accuracy and range: one thing to fix, and one "upgrade" (a better word or structure the speaker could have used). Write the upgrades in a separate column on the board ("You said: a lot of us → Try: the majority of us").
  • Dominant speakers. All three freer tasks have built-in roles. Use them: the strongest speaker is the timekeeper or the "council member" who must ask questions (C14), the service provider who has to explain problems (C15), or the data checker who verifies the tally before the pair reports (C16). Use talking chips (3 per learner; you spend one each time you speak) in group discussions. Say it kindly: "Thanks, Omar. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • Stretching strong learners toward C1. Each class has a "Stretch" box with C1-leaning language (the future perfect continuous, be due to / be set to, the stress contrast in service vs misfortune causatives, the vast majority of / hardly any / each and every / none of which). Offer it; don't require it.
  • American English throughout. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (apartment, movie, pants, tires, neighbor, the subway, detailed, water heater, taking an exam, make an appointment, traveling, schedule, inquiries, recognize, memorize). Accept British forms when learners use them (flat, film, trousers, tyres, the underground, valeted, boiler, sitting an exam, book it in, timetable, enquiries).
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

When exactly → who exactly → how many exactly. In C14 learners place actions precisely on a future timeline, against one reference point: still happening, or already done? In C15 they say precisely who did the work: me, or someone I paid (or someone I didn't want to)? In C16 they say precisely how much of a group they mean, and make the verb agree. Each class ends with a task where one small grammar choice changes the message: a project timeline presented to a "town council," a service-counter role-play where the customer must be clear about what they need done and by when, and a class survey reported as data.

Trauma-informed: futures, misfortunes and surveys

Futures. For some adults the future is uncertain: a visa or asylum decision, a job search, family far away, health. The online lesson's project and journal ask learners to picture "your life in five years." In class, don't press personal futures. The C14 timeline task is about work and community projects (a community center, a food co-op, a bike-share launch) or a fictional character on a card. The only personal option is small and safe: "this time tomorrow" or "by the end of this week." Never ask "Where will you be living next year?", "Will you have gotten your papers by then?" or "When will you see your family?"

Misfortunes. The causative also describes bad things that happen to you (She had her bag stolen). Keep every misfortune light and invented (a canceled flight, a car towed, a phone screen cracked) and on the cards. Never ask "Has anything ever been stolen from you?" Keep "documents" in C15 to a print shop (flyers, copies, photos); don't use immigration, court or benefits paperwork as examples.

Surveys. The C16 survey questions are light on purpose (coffee or tea, bus or bike, mornings or evenings). No questions about age, family, income, home country, religion or health. "Pass" is a real answer and gets its own column in the tally. Reports use the group, not names ("Only one of us…", never "Only Ana…"), and anyone can ask to take their answer out. Offer these choices every time, so choosing a card or passing is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Future Perfect & Future Continuous

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • reflect on their Progress Test 1 results and set one personal goal
  • use the future continuous (will be + -ing) for an action in progress at a future moment: At ten tomorrow the mayor will be cutting the ribbon.

Returning Progress Test 1 (12 min) — keep it warm

1. Celebrate first (3 min). Before any paper comes out, write "We can…" on the board and list three things the whole class did well (for example: tell a story with the past perfect continuous · imagine a different past with the third conditional · report what people said with suggest, deny, admit · add emphasis with What I need is…). Say it out loud.

Four modules of B2! You can tell stories with layers of time, imagine a different past, report exactly how people said things, and argue a point in a discussion. That's upper-intermediate English. Give yourselves a hand.

2. Return privately (2 min). Hand papers back face-down or in folders. Never read scores aloud and never compare learners. Everyone gets a written comment: one strength + one thing to practice.

3. "Let's fix it!" (5 min). Show 4 anonymous errors from the tests. Use your own learners' errors if you can; typical ones at this point: If I would have known, I would have called you. · I wish I didn't say that yesterday. · She suggested me to apply for the job. · I'm used to get up at five. (Also common: She must have forget her keys. · Despite of the rain, we went. · Never I have seen such a mess.) Pairs fix them. Take answers. Praise the fixing, not the mistake-free papers.

4. One goal (2 min). Each learner writes one goal in the workbook box ("My goal after Progress Test 1"): "I want to practice ___. Every week I will ___." Give two examples: "I want to practice the third conditional. Every week I'll write three 'If I had…' sentences about my week." / "I want to speak more in discussions. I'll listen to one English podcast a week and summarize it to a friend."

A test is a map. It tells us where we are. It doesn't tell us who we are.

Some adults carry hard memories of school and tests. Keep your voice light and move on quickly. Offer a 1:1 chat at the break to anyone who struggled (below about 60%), and plan catch-up with the Module 1–4 online lessons. Don't let the test return grow past 12 minutes. The best encouragement is a good, successful lesson straight after it, and today's grammar is kind: the forms are completely regular, and learners already know will, -ing and past participles.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: one reference point, two camera angles. Fix a future moment first (a time, a date, an event). Then ask: is the action still happening at that moment (continuous), or already finished by then (perfect)?

"At 8 p.m. we'll be eating dinner." Will we be at the table at 8? (Yes.) Did dinner start before 8? (Yes, probably.) Will it continue after 8? (Yes, probably.) · "At 8 p.m. we'll have eaten." Are we at the table at 8? (No, finished.) Are the plates clean? (Yes, cleared.)
"This time next year Maya will be working at the clinic." Is she working there now? (No, not yet.) Next year, is the job finished or in progress? (In progress.) · "By next June she'll have finished her training." Will she still be training in July? (No, finished before June.)

Meaning: neutral, polite inquiries. The future continuous asks about someone's plans, not for a decision, so it sounds softer.

"Will you use the car tonight?" Could this sound like I want the car? (Yes, a bit.) · "Will you be using the car tonight?" Am I asking for a decision or just asking about your plans? (Just plans: softer.) · Where do you hear "Will you be paying by card?" (Hotels, stores, restaurants.) Is it rude? (No, very polite.)

Meaning: by vs until.

"I'll have finished by six." Can I finish at five? (Yes: no later than six.) · "I'll be working until six." Do I stop before six? (No, I work all the time up to six.) · "I'll have finished until six." Correct? (No! by.)
"By the time you arrive, we'll have left." Which happens first: you arrive, or we leave? (We leave.) Why not "you will arrive"? (Time clause: present tense, like when and before.)

Pronunciation. In speech will have is almost always contracted: I'll have /ˈaɪləv/, we'll have /ˈwiləv/, won't have /ˈwoʊntəv/. Learners often hear it as "I'll of" and some write "will of": show the written form next to the sound. I'll be is /aɪl bi/: make sure the dark /l/ is there, or it becomes "I be." by the time is one chunk /baɪðəˈtaɪm/. Neutral inquiries sound friendly with a gentle rise at the end: "Will you be paying by card? ↗" Main stress falls on the main verb, not on will / have / be: "By June we'll have painted every classroom."

Drill script
  1. 8 p.m. gesture drill (point at the board dot; wavy hand = in progress, flat "done" hand = finished): T: "eat" + wavy hand → S: "At 8 we'll be eating." T: "eat" + done hand → S: "By 8 we'll have eaten." Repeat with work, watch the game, clean up, leave. Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Contraction drill: T: "I will have finished" → S: "I'll have finished" /ˈaɪləv/. T: "They will not have arrived" → S: "They won't have arrived." Back-chain: "…finished" → "have finished" → "I'll have finished" → "By Friday I'll have finished."
  3. Make it polite: T (as a receptionist): "Pay by card?" → S: "Will you be paying by card?" T: "Stay long?" → S: "How long will you be staying?" T: "Use the projector?" → S: "Will you be using the projector?" Model the gentle rise.
  4. By the time: T: "you / arrive · we / leave" → S: "By the time you arrive, we'll have left." T: "the movie / start · I / buy the popcorn" → S: "By the time the movie starts, I'll have bought the popcorn."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 names a future moment ("This time tomorrow…" / "By Sunday night…"); S2 finishes it with the right form, real or imagined. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Project Timeline — how to run it
  • Groups of 3. Put two piles of cards on the front table: projects (a community center renovation, a food co-op opening, a city bike-share launch, a new clinic wing, a grocery store's new app, a school library makeover) and characters (Maya, a trainee nurse; Dev, who is starting a food truck; Rosa, a new bus driver; Sam, who is building a small web-design business). Each card gives real-sounding milestones and dates.
  • Groups fill in the timeline frame (key words only): next month (what will be happening?) · by the time… (a milestone) · this time next year · in five years' time. Each point needs one will be + -ing idea and one will have + p.p. idea. They may invent details.
  • Two groups meet. Group 1 gives a "status forecast": each learner presents one or two points on the timeline, in order. Group 2 is the town council (for a project) or the character's friends (for a character) and must ask at least two neutral questions: "Will you be hiring local people?" · "Will you have finished the parking lot by opening day?" Then they swap.
  • Dominant speakers: give the strongest speaker in each group the council chair job: they may not present, but they must ask the most questions and give the other group a "vote" at the end ("We approve your project!"). Quieter learners present the first and last points, which are easiest to prepare.
  • The personal option stays small and safe: a learner who wants to may add one sentence about this time tomorrow or the end of this week. No one talks about their own long-term future.
ICQs before the Project Timeline

(Two: one "will be -ing," one "will have done.") · Do you write full sentences on the frame? (No, key words.) · What does the council do? (Listens and asks at least two "Will you be…?" questions.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with two frames on a strip: "At ___, we'll be ___ing." and "By ___, we'll have ___ed." Highlight be in one color and have in another on the board and on the strip. Choose the project card with the shortest text (the bike-share launch) and read it aloud to the group. In the presentation, the learner takes the first point only. Target: 2 sentences of each kind, spoken; in writing, Workbook Ex 2 items 1–4 only.

Stretch

Add the future perfect continuous for duration ("By June we'll have been working on it for two years") and C1-style planning language: be due to, be set to, be on track to ("The center is due to open on June 14th"). Use by which time to join ideas ("We open in June, by which time we'll have hired six teachers"). As council members, ask the harder questions: "Will you have secured the funding by then?" Early finishers rewrite Lena's voicemail as a formal email to volunteers.

L1 watch-outs
  • "By the time you will arrive": French (quand tu arriveras), Russian, Polish and Ukrainian (future perfective in time clauses), Greek, and Portuguese (future subjunctive quando chegares, which feels "future") often put a future in the time clause. Spanish speakers use the subjunctive (cuando llegues) and are usually fine. Keep pointing at "time clause = present."
  • by / until: German bis, Dutch tot, Russian and Polish do, Arabic ḥattā and Turkish -e kadar can mean both "by" and "until," so "I'll have finished until six" is very common. Use the "no later than" test: if it fits, it's by.
  • Dropping have or be: "By 2030 I will finished," "This time tomorrow I will flying." Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers (no verb inflection) and learners who "hear" only the main verb in contracted speech. Show the three-part form with three colors: will + have + finished.
  • Overusing the continuous: Indian and Pakistani English and many Hindi, Urdu and Tamil speakers use will be + -ing for simple future promises ("I will be sending it tomorrow"). It's understood and often fine, but show when plain will or the perfect is clearer.
  • Present for future: Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Japanese and Korean speakers may use the present where English needs a future form ("At 8 we eat dinner" for "we'll be eating"). The meaning is often clear; recast with the camera image.

5.2 · C15

The Causative — have / get something done

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • tell the difference between doing something yourself (I cut my hair) and arranging for someone else to do it (I had my hair cut)
  • form the causative have / get + object + past participle in any tense and after modals (You should get your eyes checked), keeping the object before the participle
  • choose have (neutral) or get (informal, sometimes "finally managed")
  • understand and use the misfortune meaning (They had their flight canceled) and tell it apart from the past perfect (He had stolen a wallet)
  • use get someone to do and have someone do, and recognize have someone doing
  • arrange everyday services in a role-play and report a week of errands
Materials
  • Workbook 5.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "My services" table)
  • Slides 9–13 · Service picture cards (12) · causative pattern cards · Errand Week A/B cards (1 pair per 2 learners) · Services & Errands role-play cards (8 scenarios, A and B)
  • Listening scripts 5.2-A (Marco's busy Saturday) and 5.2-B (dictation)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: who actually does it? The causative keeps me as the subject, but someone else does the work, usually a professional I arrange and pay.

"I had my car repaired." Did I repair it? (No.) Who did? (A mechanic, a garage.) Who arranged it? (I did.) Do we know the mechanic's name? (No, and we don't need it.) · "I repaired my car." Who had the tools in their hands? (I did.)
"I'm getting my hair cut this afternoon." Is it finished? (No, later today.) Will I use the scissors? (No, the hairdresser.) · "You should get your eyes checked." Is this advice? (Yes.) Who checks the eyes? (An eye doctor.)

Meaning: have vs get. The same meaning; get is more informal and common in speech, and can suggest effort.

"It took weeks, but I finally got the heater repaired." Was it easy? (No, it took effort.) · A formal email to a customer: "We will ___ your order delivered by Friday." have or get? (have: more neutral / formal.)

Meaning: misfortune, and the past perfect trap.

"She had her bag stolen on the train." Did she arrange this? (No!) Did she steal it? (No, someone stole it from her.) · "He had stolen a wallet." Who was the thief? (He was.) What changed? (The order: object before the participle = causative.)

Meaning: the person patterns.

"I got my brother to help me move." Did my brother want to help at first? (Maybe not; I persuaded him.) Is there a to? (Yes, with get.) · "I'll have the porter bring your bags up." Who says this? (A hotel receptionist.) Is there a to? (No, with have.) · "She had everyone laughing." Did she ask them to laugh? (No, she made it happen: funny story.)

Form.

Recipehave / get + object + past participleI had my car repaired. · We got the carpets cleaned.
Tenseschange only have / get: have · am having · had · have had · will have · am going to haveShe's having her hair cut right now. · I've just had my eyes tested.
Modalsmodal + have / get + object + p.p. · should have had + object + p.p.You should get your teeth checked. · We should have had it fixed sooner.
Misfortunesame form; something bad happened to youThey had their flight canceled at the last minute.
Person: persuadeget + person + to + verbI got him to help me move the sofa.
Person: arrangehave + person + verb (no to)The manager had her assistant reschedule the meeting.
Recognizehave + person + -ing (cause an ongoing action)She soon had everyone laughing.

Pronunciation. The past participle ending is easy to lose: fixed /fɪkst/, serviced /ˈsɝvɪst/, checked /tʃɛkt/, cleaned /klind/, painted /ˈpeɪntɪd/. If the ending disappears, "I had my car fix" sounds like a mistake, so drill the final sound. Linking: had it /ˈhædɪt/, got it /ˈgɑɾɪt/ (American flap t), get it done /ˈgɛɾɪt dʌn/. Stress usually falls on the thing: "I had my hair cut." Stretch: in a misfortune, the stress often moves to the participle because it's the surprising news: "She had her bag stolen!"

Drill script
  1. Picture cards (hold up a service card): car → S: "I had my car serviced." · sink → "I had the sink fixed." · keys → "I had a key copied." · pants → "I had my pants shortened." · phone → "I had the screen replaced." Choral, then individuals.
  2. Tense ladder (T says a time word; learners change only have): T: "every year" → S: "I have my car serviced every year." T: "right now" → "I'm having my car serviced right now." T: "last week" → "I had…" T: "just" → "I've just had…" T: "next week" → "I'll have / I'm going to have…" T: "should" → "I should get my car serviced."
  3. Pronunciation: choral fixed /fɪkst/, serviced /ˈsɝvɪst/, painted /ˈpeɪntɪd/; then "I had it fixed" /aɪ ˈhædɪt fɪkst/ ×3, fast.
  4. Me or them? T describes; learners choose: T: "My neighbor painted my kitchen for money." → S: "You had your kitchen painted." T: "I painted my kitchen on Sunday." → S: "You painted it yourself!" T: "Someone broke my window." → S: "You had your window broken." (misfortune)
  5. Get him to / have him: T: "My landlord / fix the heater (persuade)" → S: "I got my landlord to fix the heater." T: "The hotel / a porter / bring the bags (arrange)" → S: "The hotel had a porter bring the bags."
Errand Week — how to run it
  • Pairs. Card A is Sam's to-do list for the week (8 jobs: oil change and brakes, flyers for the block party, the dripping kitchen faucet, a haircut before Saturday's wedding, new suit pants that are too long, a spare key for his mom, photos for a new work ID badge, a flat bike tire). Card B has Sam's receipts, texts and calendar notes for the same week. Neither partner can see the other card.
  • A asks about each job with the causative: "Did Sam get the flyers printed?" B answers from the evidence: "Yes, he had 200 flyers printed on Tuesday." Some jobs Sam did himself (B's text says "Fixed the faucet myself! YouTube!"), one went wrong (a misfortune: "Got my car towed from the store parking lot. Ugh."), and some are still open.
  • Together, pairs sort the 8 jobs into four columns on the back of card A: did it himself · had it done · something went wrong · still needs to get it done. Then they write 3 sentences for the last column: "He still needs to get his pants shortened. He should have them done before the wedding on Saturday."
  • Check with the model answers on the materials sheet. A pair that gets the meaning right with one word-order slip has succeeded; fix the slip at feedback.
Services & Errands role-play — how to run it
  • Eight scenarios, each with a customer card (A) and a service card (B): the auto shop, the building manager (apartment repairs), the hair salon / barber shop, the print & copy shop, the phone repair kiosk, the alterations shop, the dry cleaner, and the key counter at the hardware store.
  • A (customer) must: (1) explain two things they need done, with the causative ("I'd like to have the oil changed and get the brakes checked"); (2) ask when it will be ready, with the future perfect or continuous ("Will you have finished by five?"); (3) ask one neutral question ("Will you be calling me when it's ready?"). B (service provider) uses the card: price, time, and one small problem to explain ("We'll have to order the part, so we won't have finished until Thursday").
  • Rounds of 4 minutes. After each round, the customer swaps cards with another pair. Swap roles every round, so everyone is a customer at least once.
  • Finish with a 1-minute report to a new partner: "I'm getting my jacket dry-cleaned. They'll have done it by Saturday, and they're having a new button sewn on too."
  • Dominant speakers make good service providers: give them the cards with a "problem" and the job of explaining it clearly. Quieter learners start as customers, with the phrase bank on the card.
ICQs before Errand Week and the role-play
Errand Week: Can you show your partner your card? (No.) · Who asks, A or B? (A.) · What do you find out? (What Sam did himself, what he had done, what went wrong, what he still needs to get done.) · How many sentences do you write? (Three, for the last column.)
Role-play: Customer, how many things do you need done? (Two.) · What else do you ask? (When it will be ready, and one "Will you be…?" question.) · Service provider, where's your information? (On my card.) · When do you swap? (After 4 minutes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with the past simple only and three picture cards: "I had my ___ fixed / cleaned / cut." Give a strip with the three boxes (have / thing / done) and let learners build sentences by putting the picture card in the middle box. In the role-play, take the customer role with the auto shop or hair salon card (picture on the card) and one required sentence plus "When will it be ready?" Target: 4 spoken causative sentences; in writing, Workbook Ex 2 items 1–4.

Stretch

Use the passive-like and C1 forms: get + object + -ing ("I finally got the engine running"), have something done with the future perfect ("I'll have had the roof repaired by winter"), and need + -ing for services ("The car needs servicing" = needs to be serviced). Contrast stress in service vs misfortune. In the role-play, handle a complaint politely: "I had my suit dry-cleaned here, but the stain is still there. Could you have it looked at again?" Early finishers write a short, polite complaint email.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I cut my hair" for a salon haircut: Spanish me corté el pelo, Portuguese cortei o cabelo, Russian я подстригся, Chinese 我剪头发了 and Arabic use the plain verb (or a reflexive) for both meanings. Note: American speakers also say "I cut my hair" casually for a salon visit, but I got my hair cut / I got a haircut is clearer. Accept it in speech; teach the causative for precision.
  • Word order: "I had repaired my car" / "I got fixed my phone." French j'ai fait réparer ma voiture, Italian ho fatto riparare, and Spanish mandé arreglar el coche put the verb before the object, so learners write the past perfect by mistake. Point at the three boxes: have → thing → done.
  • "make" for "have / get": French faire, Japanese -saseru, Korean -게 하다, Chinese 让 and Turkish -dır causatives often come out as "I made the mechanic repair my car," which sounds like force in English. Show: make = force · get … to = persuade · have = arrange.
  • "have someone to do": very common across languages. have + person + verb (no to); get + person + to + verb.
  • Lost endings: Vietnamese, Cantonese, Thai and Korean speakers often drop final /t/ /d/ /st/: "I had it fix." Drill the final sound and link it to the next word ("fixed_on Monday").

Determiners & Quantifiers — all, every, both & more

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • choose all or whole (all the books · the whole book · all day · the whole day) and avoid "all the day"
  • choose every (the group as a whole) or each (one by one, often two), with a singular noun and verb, and avoid "every people"
  • use both (plural verb), neither and either (singular verb) for two things, and both … and, neither … nor, either … or
  • use most, all, some, none with and without of: most people (general) vs most of the people here (specific) · most of them
  • run a class survey and report the results with a range of quantifiers and correct agreement

Left: a vertical scale from 100% to 0%: all of us · almost all / nearly every one of us · most of us · about half of us · some of us · a few of us · only one of us · none of us. Next to it: 2 people → both (2/2) · either (1 of 2) · neither (0/2).

Center: all + the + plural / uncountable · the whole + singular · all the day → all day / the whole day. every / each + singular noun + singular verb: Every room has… · Each of the rooms has… · every people → every person / all people.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: all vs whole. whole = one complete, unbroken unit; all = everything in a set or an amount.

"I ate the whole cake." How many cakes? (One.) Is any left? (No, every piece.) · "I ate all the cakes." How many? (More than one.) · "It rained all the day." Correct? (No: "all day" or "the whole day.") · Where does whole go: before or after the? (After: the whole day.)

Meaning: every vs each. Same verb (singular), different view of the group.

"Every student passed." Am I thinking of the group together or one by one? (Together: all of them.) · "Each student gets a personal report." One report for the whole class? (No, one for each person, individually.) · "She had a bag in every hand." Correct? (No, two hands: each.) · "Every people needs water." Correct? (No: every person or all people.)

Meaning: both / neither / either. Only for two.

"Both restaurants are good." How many restaurants? (Two.) Are they both good? (Yes.) · "Neither restaurant is cheap." How many are cheap? (Zero.) · "Either day works." Can I choose Monday? (Yes.) Tuesday? (Yes, one or the other.) · "Neither of my three brothers…" Correct? (No! Three → none of.)

Meaning: general vs specific (the "of" rule).

"Most people like music." People in this room or people in general? (In general.) · "Most of the people here like jazz." General or specific? (Specific: the people here.) · "Most of teachers work hard." Correct? (No: no the, no of. "Most teachers" or "most of the teachers.") · "All us are here." Correct? (No: "all of us.")

Form.

all / wholeall (+ the) + plural / uncountable · the / my + whole + singular · all day (no the)all the books · all the information · the whole book · It rained all day / the whole day.

Pronunciation. whole sounds exactly like hole /hoʊl/: the w is silent. each /itʃ/ is a long vowel, not itch /ɪtʃ/. neither and either can be /ˈniðər/ or /ˈnaɪðər/; most Americans say /ˈniðər/, and both are correct. none /nʌn/ rhymes with fun, not phone. Linking in the "of" phrases: all of us /ˈɔləvəs/, most of them /ˈmoʊstəvðəm/ (often /ˈmoʊsəvəm/ in fast speech), none of them /ˈnʌnəvəm/. Stress the quantifier when it's the news: "None of us drink coffee!"

Drill script
  1. Scale drill (with the class, true facts): T: "Who has a pen? Hands up." Count. S: "All of us have a pen." / "Most of us have a pen." T: "Who is wearing a hat?" → "None of us is / are wearing a hat." / "Only one of us is…" Choral, then individuals.
  2. Two pens (hold up two objects): T: "blue?" → S: "Both pens are blue." T: "red?" → "Neither pen is red." T: "can I use one?" → "Either pen is fine." T: "Neither of them…" → S: "Neither of them is red."
  3. Of or no of? T: "most / people" → S: "most people." T: "most / the people here" → "most of the people here." T: "all / us" → "all of us." T: "none / them" → "none of them." T: "all / children" → "all children."
  4. Singular check: T: "every room / have" → S: "Every room has…" T: "each of the rooms / have" → "Each of the rooms has…" T: "both rooms / have" → "Both rooms have…"
  5. whole: T: "all the day" → S: "all day / the whole day." T: "the whole cakes" → S: "all the cakes." T: "the whole team" → S: "Correct!"
Class Survey & Report — how to run it
  • Pairs. Each pair gets one survey card with a light question and 2–4 answer options (e.g. "Do you drink coffee, tea, both, or neither?" · "How do you usually get to class?" · "Have you ever had your hair cut by a friend or family member?"). Twelve cards, so every pair has a different question. The tally sheet has a column for each answer and a "Pass" column.
  • Mingle: pairs split up and ask everyone, including the teacher. Tally marks only, no names. Then they meet and add their tallies. The data checker (the stronger learner in the pair) checks that the total equals the number of people in the room.
  • The report (4–5 sentences) uses the frame on the tally sheet: "We asked every person in the class… · Most of us… / None of us… / All of us except two… · Each of us… · Both of us (the two in our pair)… / Neither of us… · The whole class…" Encourage the upgrade phrases from the workbook: the majority of us, just over half of us, only a handful of us, not a single person.
  • Reports go to the class. Listeners note one fact that surprised them. Then each pair writes its best sentence on the "Our class in numbers" poster. Keep the poster on the wall for the C17 warmer.
  • Anonymity: no names in reports. If only one person gave an answer, say "one of us," never who. Anyone can ask a pair to take their answer out. "Pass" is counted and reported like any other answer: "Two of us preferred not to say."

(Yes, and we count it.) · What's the data checker's job? (Check the numbers add up and the verbs agree.) · How many sentences in the report? (Four or five.) · Which words must you use? (One all / most / none of us, one every / each, one both / neither / either.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Stretch

Use a wider range: the vast majority of us, hardly any of us, not one of us, each and every one of us, all but two of us. Use neither … nor with proximity agreement ("Neither my partner nor I am… / Neither I nor my partner is…", then rephrase to sound natural). Try relative quantifiers (C1): "We asked fourteen people, most of whom drink coffee, none of whom drink it black." Early finishers write the class report as a short, neutral paragraph for a newsletter.

L1 watch-outs
  • "all the day": Spanish todo el día, French toute la journée, Italian tutto il giorno, Portuguese o dia todo put "all" before the article with a singular noun. Teach all day and the whole day as chunks.
  • "every people / each students": Arabic kull means all, every and each and takes plural nouns in some uses; Chinese 每…都 and Korean 모든 / 마다 don't mark number the same way. Point out that people is already plural.
  • "the most of people / most of people": Spanish la mayoría de la gente, French la plupart des gens, Portuguese a maioria das pessoas, Italian la maggior parte delle persone have an article or "of the" in every case. No the in English → no of; never "the most" for quantity.
  • Plural verbs after every / each / neither: many speakers follow the meaning (many people) rather than the grammar. "Every student have…" is very common. Mark it in writing; in speech, neither of them are is normal English, so don't over-correct.
  • both / neither for more than two: languages without a "two" form (Tagalog, Vietnamese, Chinese, Japanese) may use both for three or more ("both of my three kids"). Hold up two fingers every time.
  • "none" vs "no one" vs "nobody": "None came to the party" (for people) sounds odd in everyday English; nobody / no one came or none of them came.
