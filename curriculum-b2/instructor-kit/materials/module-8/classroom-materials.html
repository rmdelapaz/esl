C · Decode It — A/B Cards (8.1)

Pairs, one A and one B card. The speaker says each sentence naturally (the gray line shows how). The listener writes the full form, then answers the sentence like a real reply. Swap after six. Fold the cards so the listener can't see them.

Decode It · Card A

Say these naturally

  1. Do you want to grab lunch? "D'you wanna grab lunch?"
  2. What are you going to do tonight? "Whatcha gonna do tənight?"
  3. I've got to go to the bank. "I've gotta go tə the bank."
  4. Let me know if you need a hand. "Lemme know if you nee-da hand."
  5. Did you see the game last night? "Didja see the game las(t) night?"
  6. It's kind of cold. Can you close the window? "It's kinda cold. C'n you close the window?"
Decode It · Card B

Say these naturally

  1. Give me a minute. I'm looking for my keys. "Gimme a minute. I'm looking fər my keys."
  2. Don't you want to come with us? "Doncha wanna come with us?"
  3. I don't know what time it starts. "I dunno what time it starts."
  4. Tell them I'll be there at six. "Tell 'em I'll be there ət six."
  5. We were going to call you, but we ran out of time. "We wər gonna callya, bud we ra-nouda time."
  6. Would you like a cup of coffee? "Wouldja like a cuppa coffee?"

D · "Appropriate or sloppy?" — Sort Cards (8.2)

Groups of 3–4. Put the two header cards on the table. Read each card aloud, agree ✅ NATURAL or ❌ SLOPPY, and fix every ❌ card out loud with a precise version. The small gray line is the key (cut it off or fold it back before class).

✅
NATURAL
vague language that fits the situation
❌
SLOPPY
too vague for the situation, or too many placeholders

F · Game — Roughly Speaking (8.2)

How to play (groups of 3–4 · 12 minutes)

Roll a die (or flip a coin: heads = 1, tails = 2) and move. Answer the square's prompt for about 30 seconds using the language it asks for: ≈ approximate · 🧺 general noun · 🪶 hedge. The player on your right times you. The group gives a thumbs-up if you used the right kind of language. 🎯 PRECISE squares flip the rule: give exact facts, with no vague language (if you say "or something," go back 2 squares). ⏸ STOP: the player on your left asks you a follow-up question ("Roughly how far is it?"). Don't want to answer a personal prompt? Say "Pass" and answer the next square's prompt instead. New identity cards are fine.

G · Debate Kit — Side Cards & Role Cards

Side cards: print 2 of each (one per team). Teams draw face-down. Role cards: one per learner; each card says what to do and gives ready phrases.

🟦
PROPOSITION
You argue FOR the motion. It's your card, not your opinion.
🟥
OPPOSITION
You argue AGAINST the motion. It's your card, not your opinion.
Role · Chair 🪑

Run the debate. Stay neutral.

Follow the chair's script. Call each speaker by name. Keep time with the timekeeper. Take floor questions one by one, alternating teams. Never give your opinion.

I now call on… · Thank you, time is up. · We'll now take questions from the floor.

Role · Timekeeper ⏱

Be fair to both sides.

Start the timer when each speaker starts. Show 1 MINUTE LEFT, 15 SECONDS, TIME. Fill in the speech log. Collect and count the votes.

Time, please.

Role · Opening speaker 🎤 (2 min)

State your case.

Say your side, then 2–3 arguments, each with a reason and an example or consequence. End with a clear call.

We are here to argue that… · Our case rests on… · First of all… · What's more… · For these reasons, we urge you to support / oppose the motion.

Role · Rebuttal speaker 🔁 (1½ min)

Answer what they actually said.

Listen and take notes during their opening. Pick their 2 strongest points. Concede what's fair, then counter. Add a new point if there's time.

My opponent claimed that… · Admittedly… but… · While I accept that…, I still believe… · That argument ignores…

Role · Summary speaker 🏁 (1 min)

Weigh the debate. No new arguments.

Remind the floor of your best points and the other side's weakest. Say why your side wins, on balance.

Let's look at what we've heard tonight. · The other side argued…, but… · Weighing it all up… · So we urge you to vote…

Role · The floor 🙋

Ask one short question.

Write your question during the opening speeches. Ask a named team. Keep it short. Then vote in secret: which team argued better?

My question is for the proposition. · You said that… Could you explain…? · What would happen if…?

Role · Note-taker 📝 (teams of 4)

Be your team's ears.

During the other team's speeches, write their key words (the stressed words!) and pass them to your rebuttal speaker. You may answer one floor question.

They said… · Their strongest point was…

Everyone · the rules

Respect in a debate

  • Attack ideas, never people.
  • Speak only when the chair calls you.
  • Key words only, no full scripts.
  • When the timekeeper shows TIME, finish your sentence.
  • Your side is your card, not your opinion.

G · Debate Kit — The Chair's Script

One copy per debate, for the chair. About 16–17 minutes in all.

Chair's script
  1. Open (1 min): "Good evening, everyone, and welcome to tonight's debate. The motion is that . Speaking for the motion, the proposition: . Speaking against, the opposition: . Our timekeeper is . The rules: speakers must stop when time is up, the floor may ask one question each, and we attack ideas, not people. First, a secret vote: on your slip, write P or O. Which side do you expect to argue better?"
  2. "I call on to open the case for the proposition. You have two minutes." → "Thank you."
  3. "I now call on to open the case for the opposition. Two minutes, please." → "Thank you."
  4. "Teams, you have one minute to prepare your rebuttals."
  5. ", the proposition's rebuttal, please. A minute and a half." → ", the opposition's rebuttal." → "Thank you both."
  6. "We'll now take questions from the floor. Please say which team your question is for, and keep it short." (Alternate teams. About 4 minutes. "We have time for one more question.")
  7. "To sum up, first the opposition: , one minute." → "And finally, the proposition: , one minute."
  8. "Thank you. Time for the second secret vote: which team made the stronger case tonight? Write P or O." (The timekeeper counts.) "The result is… The proposition gained ___ votes and the opposition gained ___. Please thank both teams!"

I · Teacher Observation Checklist · C25 → C26

How to use it

Observe during team planning, rehearsal, the debates and the floor questions. Mark ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help, or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You don't need a mark in every box. After class, use it to (1) plan each learner's C26 assessment. The B2 final assessment is an individual speaking interview (about 8–10 minutes) plus short listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's B2 final assessment; match these notes to its speaking criteria and decide where each learner should start, (2) choose the two weakest items for a short C26 warm-up, (3) name one real strength for each learner, and (4) note who needs encouragement. This is not a grade.

  • 1 Structures a turn: signposted opening, points, conclusion
  • 2 Supports points with reasons, examples, consequences
  • 3 Understands the other side at natural speed and answers what was actually said
  • 4 Concedes and counters politely (Admittedly… but…)
  • 5 Conditionals to predict results (If…were…, …would…)
  • 6 Passive / impersonal passive / reporting verbs to sound objective
  • 7 Comparison, cleft or inversion for emphasis
  • 8 Register: formal backbone + appropriate hedging / vague language
  • 9 Interaction: asks / answers a floor question, or chairs / times well
  • 10 Fluency & pronunciation: from notes, keeps going, clear stress

J · Listening-Lab & Listening Scripts (Teacher)

How to read these scripts

Natural speed = your normal conversational speed, with the reductions and links you'd really make. The gray "sounds like" line is a guide; don't exaggerate it and don't slow down "to help." Slow version = clear and careful, every word full, but not robotic. In the listening lab, always read the natural version first and use the slow version as the check.

8.1-A · Careful vs natural (lead-in: read the careful version, then the natural one)

Careful (dictionary English): Sam: What are you doing this weekend? Do you want to grab a coffee? · Jo: I would love to, but I have got to finish this report. It is kind of due on Monday. · Sam: Ah, no worries. Let me know when you are free. I am going to be around all week. · Jo: Will do. Fish and chips on Friday? My treat. · Sam: Sounds great. See you then!
Natural: Sam: Whatcha doing this weekend? D'you wanna grab a coffee? · Jo: I'd love to, but I've gotta finish this report. It's kinda due Monday. · Sam: Ah, no worries. Lemme know when you're free. I'm gonna be around all week. · Jo: Will do. Fish 'n' chips on Friday? My treat. · Sam: Sounds great. See ya then!

Sounds like: "WHATcha DOing this WEEKend? D'you wanna GRA-ba COFfee?" Ask: "Which words disappeared?" (are you, do you, to, have, of, on, let me, going to, and, you.) "Which words stayed clear?" (doing, weekend, grab, coffee, finish, report, Monday…: the content words.)

8.1-B · Can or can't? (Workbook 8.1, Ex 1: natural speed first, then the slow check)

Natural: 1. I CAN'T come on Friday. · 2. She kən drive a truck. · 3. We kən meet after work. · 4. They CAN'T find the keys. · 5. You kən park behind the building. · 6. He CAN'T speak Spanish, but he kən speak Portuguese. · 7. I kən help you with that. · 8. We CAN'T hear you. Kən you speak up?

Say can as a quick, unstressed /kən/ and can't as a long, stressed /kænt/ with an almost silent t (don't release it). Slow version: read the same sentences with the full vowels. Answers: 1 can't · 2 can · 3 can · 4 can't · 5 can · 6 can't, can · 7 can · 8 can't, Can.

8.1-C · Micro-dictation (Workbook 8.1, Ex 4: each sentence twice at natural speed, then once slowly)

1. I was going to call you, but I ran out of time. ("I wz gonna callya, bud I ra-nouda time.") · 2. Do you want a cup of tea? ("D'you wanna cuppa tea?") · 3. What are you going to do about it? ("Whatcha gonna do abou-dit?") · 4. I don't know. Let me think about it. ("I dunno. Lemme think abou-dit.") · 5. Tell them to turn it off at the end of the day. ("Tell 'em tə tur-ni-doff ət thee-yend ə the day.") · 6. We've got to leave at six, so we can't stay long. ("We've gotta leave ət six, so we CAN'T stay long.")

Word counts (contractions = 1 word): 1 twelve · 2 seven · 3 eight · 4 eight · 5 eleven · 6 eleven. Note 2: here "wanna" = want a, not want to. Great discussion point.

8.1-D · Dictogloss: a voicemail from Dana (Workbook 8.1, Ex 5: twice at natural speed; slow version at the end)

Text (slow version = read this clearly): Hi, it's Dana from work. Listen, I was going to call you yesterday, but I ran out of time. The meeting has been moved to next Tuesday, and they want us to bring a copy of the report. I don't know about you, but I've got to finish my part tonight. Do you want to get together on Monday and check it over? We could meet at the café next to the station at around ten. Let me know what you think. Oh, and don't forget: it's Sam's last day on Friday, so we're going to take him out for lunch. Talk to you soon!
Natural (sounds like): Hi, it's Dana from work. Lissen, I wz gonna callya yesterday, bud I ra-nouda time. The meeting's bin moved tə nex(t) Tuesday, 'n' they want us tə bring a copy ə the report. I dunno aboutchu, bud I've gotta finish my par(t) tənight. D'you wanna get təgether on Monday 'n' check i-dover? We could meet ət the café nex(t) tə the station ət around ten. Lemme know whatcha think. Oh, 'n' don't forget: it's Sam's las(t) day on Friday, so we're gonna take 'im out fə lunch. Talk tə ya soon!

About 105 words. The six facts: (1) the meeting has moved to next Tuesday · (2) bring a copy of the report · (3) Dana must finish her part tonight · (4) meet on Monday to check it · (5) at the café next to the station, around ten · (6) Sam's last day is Friday; lunch with him. Most-missed words: to, of, has been, and, at, him.

8.3-B · Your model opening speech and a model rebuttal (team planning: read once, about 3 minutes in all)

Opening (proposition), motion: "Phones should be switched off during family meals." Madam Chair, ladies and gentlemen. We are here to argue that phones should be switched off during family meals. Our case rests on two points: connection and example. First of all, connection. For many families, dinner is the only time of day when everyone is in the same room. It is widely accepted that regular family meals help children feel secure and do better at school. But what really damages those meals isn't the food or the time. It's the constant checking. If phones were switched off for just thirty minutes, people would actually listen to each other. Our second point is example. Children copy what adults do, not what adults say. Not only does a phone-free table teach children to pay attention, but it also shows them that people matter more than notifications. Now, the other side will say that some people need their phones for work. Admittedly, a nurse on call can't switch off completely, and emergencies are, of course, a different matter. We're talking about most meals, most days. On balance, the cost is tiny: thirty minutes without a screen. The benefit is by far the greater: a family that actually talks. For these reasons, we urge you to support the motion. Thank you.
Rebuttal (opposition): Thank you, Madam Chair. My opponent claimed that what really damages family meals is the constant checking. That may be true in some homes, but it's sort of a picture of the worst case. In many families, phones bring people together at the table: a video call with a grandparent, photos from a child's school trip, a quick check of tomorrow's weather. My opponent also admitted that people on call can't switch off. Well, that's a lot of people: nurses, drivers, parents with a sick child, anyone waiting to hear about a shift. A rule with that many exceptions isn't really a rule at all. What we need is not a ban but good manners: phones face-down, and used together, not alone. For these reasons, we oppose the motion.

Ask the teams: "How did the rebuttal use what the opening actually said?" (It quoted "the constant checking" and turned the opening's own concession, people on call, into an argument.) "Find the toolkit items." (Opening: impersonal passive, cleft, second conditional, not only inversion, by far, on balance. Rebuttal: that may be true, but, sort of, cleft what we need is…)
