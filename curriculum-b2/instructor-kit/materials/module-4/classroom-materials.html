E · Set the Record Straight — A/B Cards (4.2)

Pairs. A reads the wrong "facts" as confident questions. B corrects each one with an it-cleft, then adds the what-cleft on the card. Swap roles with a new pair of cards. All situations are invented.

Card A · 1 · The office party

You think…

  • Sam organized the party.
  • It was on Saturday.
  • The pizza was the problem.

Ask: "So Sam organized the party, right?"

Card B · 1 · The office party

The facts

  • Nadia organized it.
  • It was on Friday.
  • The music was the problem.

Add: "What Sam did was bring the music!"

Card A · 2 · The broken machine

You think…

  • The washing machine broke.
  • Your partner called the repair person.
  • It was fixed on Monday.

Ask: "So the washing machine broke?"

Card B · 2 · The broken machine

The facts

  • The dryer broke.
  • The upstairs neighbor called the repair person.
  • It was fixed on Tuesday.

Add: "What I did was hang everything outside to dry."

Card A · 3 · The family trip

You think…

  • Your partner's mom planned the trip.
  • They went by car.
  • Everyone loved the beach.

Ask: "So your mom planned the trip?"

Card B · 3 · The family trip

The facts

  • Your brother planned it.
  • You went by train.
  • Everyone loved the museum.

Add: "What we didn't expect was the rain."

Card A · 4 · The new manager

You think…

  • Ms. Diaz got the manager job.
  • The new manager started in January.
  • The first thing they changed was the dress code.

Ask: "So Ms. Diaz got the job?"

Card B · 4 · The new manager

The facts

  • Mr. Park got the job.
  • He started in March.
  • The first thing he changed was the schedule.

Add: "What everyone likes is the new break room."

H · C12 Speaking Checklist — Present, Argue & Discuss (feeds C13)

Score each learner's whole performance across the presentation, the Q&A and the discussion: five criteria, 0–3 points each, total /15. 0 = no response · 1 = not yet B2 · 2 = B2 · 3 = strong B2 (moving toward C1). Score what a patient listener understands, not an accent. Tick M1–M4 when you hear a structure from that module used correctly. Write one sentence you heard in the notes column; it makes the slip easy to write.

Rules

  2. Roll and move. Follow the numbers: 1–6 left to right, 7–12 right to left, and so on.
  3. Answer the square in full sentences. The group says "Good!" or helps you fix it. If the group helps, stay on the square this time.
  4. Personal squares: answer about you, a persona card, or invent. Anyone can say "Pass!" and move back 1.
  5. First to FINISH, or the player nearest to it when the teacher stops the game, wins.

J · Listening & Dictation Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

Read at a natural speed, with pauses at the punctuation. Read each script twice: once for the main idea, once for detail. At B2, keep the natural features learners meet outside class: contractions, weak forms (that /ðət/, was /wəz/) and the small pause after a sentence connector. For dialogues, change your voice or ask a confident learner to read one part. The 🔊 buttons in the online lessons give learners extra listening at home.

4.1-A · Two speakers (C10 lead-in: which one is easier to follow?)

Speaker 1: Remote work is convenient. It can feel lonely. Companies save money. Some people get less done. · Speaker 2: Although remote work is convenient, it can feel lonely. Companies save money because they need less office space; however, some people get less done as a result.

Ask: "Same facts?" (Yes.) "Which speaker told you in advance what kind of idea was coming?" (Speaker 2: although = contrast, because = reason, however = contrast, as a result = result.)

4.1-B · City or countryside? (Workbook 4.1, Ex 1: write the ten linkers)

Today I'd like to weigh up life in the city and life in the countryside. A lot of people love the city because there's always something to do, and since public transportation is everywhere, you don't really need a car. However, city life has a price. Rents are so high that many young people share an apartment well into their thirties. In the countryside, life is slower and cheaper, whereas in the city everything moves fast. Despite the lower cost of living, people often leave rural areas due to the lack of jobs. My cousin moved back to her village last year. It was such a peaceful place that she started sleeping better within a week. Even though she earns less, she says she's happier. Therefore, I'd say the right choice depends on the stage of life you're in.

Answers: 1 because · 2 since · 3 However · 4 so · 5 whereas · 6 Despite · 7 due to · 8 such · 9 Even though · 10 Therefore. Then ask: "What came after Despite? A clause or a noun?" (a noun: the lower cost of living).

4.1-C · Dictation (C10 end or C11 warmer; read each sentence 3 times)

1. Despite the heavy rain, the outdoor concert went ahead as planned. · 2. The flight was delayed because of a technical problem. · 3. It was such a long meeting that nobody remembered the first point. · 4. Sales fell sharply last quarter; therefore, the launch was postponed.

Check punctuation too: the comma after rain in 1, and the semicolon + comma in 4. Then learners rewrite 1 with Although and 2 with because + clause.

4.3-A · Should we try a four-day week? (Workbook 4.3, Ex 2: signposts, structures, the objection)

Presenter: Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to talk about the four-day work week, and I'd like to make the case that it's worth trying, at least as an experiment. I'll cover three points: the benefits for staff, the benefits for the company, and one common objection. Let me start with staff. It is widely believed that longer hours mean more work gets done, but the evidence suggests otherwise. Tired people make more mistakes. What most people need is not more time at their desks but more time to recover. Moving on to the company. One key advantage is that people are less likely to leave. Having spoken to several managers who tried it, I'm convinced it saves money on hiring. Finally, the objection. It could be argued that customers will suffer if the office is closed on Fridays. That's a fair point. However, staff can take turns, so the phones are always answered. To sum up, if we had tried this a year ago, we might already be seeing the results. Not only would it make people happier, but it would also make us more efficient. Thank you. I'm happy to take questions.

Dana: I take your point about tired people, but surely some jobs just can't be done in four days? Nurses, for example? · Presenter: You're right to an extent. Hospitals would need a different model. Even so, I'd argue that most office jobs could make it work. · Leo: What worries me is the cost. Wouldn't the company have to hire more people? · Presenter: Granted, but we shouldn't forget that we're already paying for overtime. The bottom line is, a rested team costs less.
