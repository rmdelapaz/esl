Final Test — B2 Exit Check

The end-of-course check on all eight modules, taken on the last day, which is also a celebration. It shows each learner what they can now do in English: tell a layered story, argue a case and handle push-back, switch between formal and informal English, follow fast natural speech, write a formal email and a balanced argument, and use the precision grammar of B2. It also gives you the evidence for the next step: Advanced English (C1), C1 with a review plan, another round of B2 with more support, or a specialist track (IELTS or Business English). It is never a pass/fail verdict on a person.

⏱ ~60 min written + 8–10 min interview each Written /85 · Speaking /15 · Total /100 Covers Modules 1–8
Frame it as "show what you can do"

By Class 26 your learners have met you 25 times, and yesterday they held a structured debate. Use that: "Thirteen weeks ago we were working on the past perfect. Yesterday you argued a case, conceded a point, and won the room, all in English!" Do every example item together, keep the room calm and unhurried, and plan the celebration so it happens whatever the scores are. Every learner gets a certificate of attendance. The test decides the next class, not who gets to celebrate.

Literacy-adapted version

Same paper, different delivery. Read all instructions and items aloud, enlarge the paper, and give extra time or split it over Classes 25 and 26. Parts 2–3: read the choices aloud and accept a spoken answer (for word formation, the learner can say the word and you check the form). Part 4: read the article and the notice aloud and ask the questions orally. Part 5: the learner says the email and the argument while you scribe, and you score them with the same checklist and rubric. Mark the paper "Adapted". For these learners, the interview and listening are the main evidence for placement. The key explains how to recommend C1 together with literacy support instead of holding someone back for reading and writing alone.

Real or imagined, your side or an assigned side

The interview asks about change, choices, and opinions. For some learners, questions about work and the past touch on a lost profession, a hard journey, or a qualification that isn't recognized here yet. Say before you start: "Real or imagined, no problem. And in the discussion, I'll argue against you on purpose. That's my job today, not my real opinion. You can take any side you like." Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status. If a learner says "I'd rather not talk about that," thank them and move to the next stage.

📖A. Skim the questions first. Then read the article.
Four Days, Same Pay: Does It Work?

When a mid-sized marketing firm in Denver switched to a four-day week last year, many of its clients were skeptical. "Several of them warned us that we would lose business," admits Karen Liu, the company's operations director. "One even asked whether we had lost our minds."

Twelve months later, the results are hard to ignore. Output has stayed the same, sick days have fallen by almost a third, and the company has received three times as many job applications as it did the year before. It is widely believed that the extra day off gives staff time to rest, run errands, and deal with family matters, so they arrive on Monday more focused.

Not everyone is convinced, however. Critics point out that the model works far better in office jobs than in hospitals, schools, or factories, where someone has to be there every day. Others claim that squeezing five days' work into four simply moves the stress around. "Some of my colleagues are exhausted by Thursday," says one designer, who asked not to be named.

Liu accepts that the system isn't perfect. "If we had tried to change everything at once, it would have failed," she says. Instead, the firm ran a three-month trial with one team before rolling it out. Meetings were cut to thirty minutes, and staff were encouraged to block out "quiet hours" for focused work.

So, is this the future of work? Probably not for everyone. But what the Denver experiment does show is that the length of the working week is a choice, not a law of nature. And the more companies experiment, the more we will learn.

A · Ana's presentation (read the whole talk twice, then give 1 minute)
Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to make the case for a mentoring program for new staff. I'll cover three points: the problem, the evidence, and how we could start.
First, the problem. Last year we lost about a third of our new employees within six months. Every time someone leaves, we pay to recruit and train their replacement. That's not just expensive; it's exhausting for the whole team.
Moving on to the evidence. My sister's company introduced mentors two years ago, and far more new staff stayed: almost nine out of ten are still there. Research suggests that people who have someone to ask in their first weeks settle in much faster.
Now, admittedly, mentoring takes time from our most experienced people, and they're already busy. That said, it's an hour a week, and it saves them hours of fixing mistakes later. It's not a cost; it's an investment.
So, to sum up: I'm proposing a three-month trial in one department, starting with the warehouse team. If it works, we roll it out. If it doesn't, we've lost very little. Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.

Individual Speaking Interview · 8–10 minutes

Sit beside the learner, not across a desk, with the grid on your lap. Speak at a natural pace, with natural contractions. Ask the core question first. If there's no answer after about 5 seconds, use the support version (rephrase, give a choice, or give a model). If the answer comes easily, add the stretch question. At B2, listen for developed, connected speech: a long turn that has a shape, an argument that survives push-back, and a real change of register in the role-play. Don't correct during the interview. Note one strength and one next step instead. Before stage 1, let the learner choose a long-turn card so they can think about it quietly (about 1 minute) while you do the warm-up.

1

Warm-up: work, study & change

~1½ min
Hello! How are you today? So, what have you been doing since the course started? Tell me a little about your work or studies. (real or imagined)
What's something you've had to get used to in the last year or two?

Support: "Is it something at work, at home, or in your town?" · Stretch: "What do you think you'll have achieved by this time next year?" (future perfect) · "How is that different from how things used to be?"

2

Long turn (monologue)

~2½ min
Now please talk about the card you chose. About one and a half to two minutes. I'll just listen.

Don't interrupt. Time about 1½–2 minutes; stop kindly at 2½. Then ask one follow-up question about the content. Support: if they freeze, ask "What happened first?" or "What's your main point?" then "Can you give me an example?" · Stretch: "What would have happened if…?" or "Some people would say the opposite. What would you say to them?" Listen for a clear shape (hook or roadmap, development, a landed ending), narrative tenses, and signposting.

3

Discussion with push-back

~2½ min

Read one discussion statement below. Whatever the learner says, take the other side and push back twice, politely, so they must defend, concede, or refine their view. Your push-back is a role, not your opinion.

Here's a statement: [statement]. What's your view? … Why? … Hmm, I'm not sure I agree. Surely [counter-argument]? … But what about [second objection]? … So, on balance, where do you stand?

Support: "Do you agree or disagree?" then "Why?" and one push-back only · Stretch: "What's the strongest argument on the other side?" · "What would need to change for you to change your mind?" Listen for concede-and-counter (That's a fair point, but… · Admittedly…, but…), hedging (arguably, to some extent), and an emphasis structure (What really matters is…).

4

Register role-play

~2 min

The learner chooses one role-play card. Part 1: you play the formal role. Part 2: switch roles. You are now the learner's friend, and they tell you what happened, informally.

(Hotel, you are the receptionist) Good afternoon, Grand Riverside Hotel. How may I help you? … I see. And what name is the reservation under? … Let me check… Is there anything else I can help you with?
(Manager, you are the manager) Come in! What can I do for you? … Next Friday? Hmm, that's a busy day. Why do you need it? … Could anyone cover your shift?
(Switch) OK, now I'm your friend. Hey! So, how did it go? What did they say?

Listen for a real shift: indirect questions, I was wondering whether… / Would it be possible to…? in Part 1; contractions, reporting (She said I could…, He suggested…), and relaxed or vague language in Part 2. Support: model the first line ("Good afternoon, I was wondering…"). Stretch: in Part 1, create a small problem ("I'm afraid we're fully booked that night") and see how they negotiate.

5

Closing + your question

~½ min
Thank you, that's everything. Before you go, do you have a question for me? Anything at all.
