Progress Test 1 — Modules 1–4

A friendly halfway check after Lesson 4.3. It covers the narrative tenses (past perfect and past perfect continuous), third and mixed conditionals, wish / if only, would rather and it's (high) time, gerunds and infinitives (including remember / stop / try), past modals of deduction, used to / be used to / get used to, advanced and impersonal passives, participle clauses, reporting verbs, linkers of contrast, cause and result, cleft sentences and negative inversion, and the language of argument. It tells you what to recycle in Weeks 7–13. It does not rank anyone.

⏱ ~52 min written + 7 min pair speaking Written /55 · Speaking /15 CEFR B2
Why a progress test, and how to frame it

B2 learners have usually taken several English tests already, and many are sitting this one with a job, a family, or a professional license riding on their English. Some still find written tests stressful, especially if English tests have been part of hiring or official interviews. Say it plainly: "This is a check, not an exam. It tells me what to teach in the second half, and it shows you how far you've come since Class 1." The texts are longer than at B1, so tell the class to skim the questions before they read or listen. Do every example item together, and remind them they can ask you to clarify an instruction at any time. Read the listening at a natural pace, with natural contractions and weak forms (I'd have, must've, had been): hearing them is part of B2 listening, so don't over-articulate.

Literacy-adapted version (learners still building reading and writing)

Some B2 learners speak far better than they read and write, for example people who learned English mostly by ear at work, or who had limited schooling in any language. Use the same paper and change how it is done, not what it tests. Read every instruction and item aloud, one at a time, and enlarge the paper if you can. In Parts 2–3, read the choices aloud and accept a spoken answer; for the rewrites (11–15), let the learner say the new sentence and score what they say. Do Part 4 orally: read the essay aloud twice and ask the questions. In Part 5, the learner may say the message and the paragraph while you scribe; score the content, organization, and language they produce, not the handwriting. Give extra time, or split the paper over two sittings. Write "Adapted" at the top and note what changed. Report these learners' listening and speaking as the main evidence. Low literacy is not low ability. It points to a literacy-support need, and the key says how to act on it.

Personal information and opinions are always optional

The speaking task asks about turning points, regrets, and change; the writing asks for an argument. Learners can answer with a "new identity" (the cards on the teacher pages) or an invented story, and in the debate and the paragraph they may argue an assigned side that isn't their own view. Say this before the test starts: "Real or imagined, your side or the other side: no problem. I'm scoring the English, not your life or your opinions." Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status.

Starting Over at Forty-Two

Three years ago, I was sitting in my car outside a community college, trying to find the courage to go in. I had been working in the same warehouse for fifteen years, and although the pay was steady, I had stopped learning anything new a long time before. My supervisor, who had always encouraged me, had suggested applying for the evening nursing-assistant program. I had laughed at the idea. Surely, I told myself, it was too late.

What changed my mind was a conversation with my daughter. She asked me why I always told her to follow her dreams if I wasn't following mine. I had no answer. The following week, I enrolled.

The first semester was hard. I wasn't used to sitting in a classroom, and I had never written an essay in English. Having spent the whole day on my feet, I often fell asleep over my books, and more than once I seriously considered quitting. Not only was I the oldest student in the class, but I was also the slowest typist. However, my classmates, most of whom were half my age, turned out to be incredibly supportive. It was a twenty-year-old called Dev who showed me how to use the online learning system.

Today I work at a local clinic, and I'm still getting used to wearing scrubs instead of a safety vest. People sometimes ask whether I regret waiting so long. Honestly? I wish I had started earlier. But if I hadn't spent those years in the warehouse, I wouldn't be as organized as I am now. It is often said that it's never too late to learn. I used to think that was just a nice phrase. Now I know it's true.

Pair Speaking Task · 7 minutes

Two learners sit with you. Give Card A to one and Card B to the other. Give them one minute to read the cards silently, then check the instructions:

In Part 1, do you just answer, or do you ask follow-up questions too? (Both.) Short answers or developed answers? (Developed: reasons, examples, what happened next.) In Part 2, do you have to argue your real opinion? (No. You can take the side on the card.) Real life or imagined? (Either is fine.)

Part 1 (about 3 minutes): they ask and answer the card questions, which cover turning points, change, regrets, and preferences. Encourage one follow-up question each. Part 2 (about 3 minutes): give them the debate card. They discuss the proposal, each giving reasons, conceding at least one point and countering it. If a pair doesn't want to choose sides, assign them: Student A argues for, Student B against. Part 3 (about 1 minute): ask each learner, "What did your partner argue or suggest?" Listen for reporting verbs with the right pattern (She suggested trying…, He admitted that…, She insisted on…). Step in only to restart a stuck pair ("Now ask your next question"). Don't correct during the task. Score each learner on the grid, using the rubric in the key. Mixed-level pairs: score each person on what they did, not on the pair's result.

After the test: close the loop in Week 7

Score the papers before Class 14 with the key. Add up each section across the class, because the weakest section tells you what to recycle. Warmers in Classes 14–19 are a good place for it: for example, a 5-minute "Rewrite it" relay (active → impersonal passive) if rewrites 11–12 were weak, or a "What must have happened?" picture-clue game if item 9 was weak. Both link naturally to Module 5 (the causative and future perfect). Give each learner their paper back with one thing they did well and one thing to practice, plus the online lesson that practices it. The key has a class analysis and feedback phrases.
