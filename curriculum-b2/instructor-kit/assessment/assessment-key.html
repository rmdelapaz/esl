Scoring principles

Score meaning, not spelling, in Listening and Reading: an answer that shows understanding gets the point, even if it is misspelled ("she was tierd from standing"). In Words & Phrases the form must be right, because the form is what's being tested: strenghten earns the point (a spelling slip), but strong or strength does not. Circle items are right or wrong. Rewrites (Progress Test 3B) earn the point only when the target structure is correct; ignore small spelling slips elsewhere in the sentence. Writing and speaking use the checklists and rubrics below. Half points are not used. The tests use American English; accept British forms everywhere (learners may know them from British materials: flat, holiday, queue, motorway, practise, organise, apologise, enquire, colour, Yours faithfully).

One US/UK difference to know: Lesson 2.2 teaches that mustn't have is wrong for a negative deduction, and the tests follow that (can't have is the answer). In American English, though, "He must not have heard the alarm" is common and correct. If a learner says or writes must not have for a negative deduction in speaking or writing, don't count it as an error. Can't have is still the safer choice in exams and in British English.

Part 5 · Writing /10
  • A. Reporting message (4 × 1 pt). +1 a greeting to Jordan and a friendly close. +1 for each of three different reporting verbs used in the correct pattern (max +3): Sara apologized for being late with the report / apologized that the report was late; Mike suggested hiring a temporary assistant / suggested that we hire…; the manager warned us not to book any vacation in March; everyone agreed to work one Saturday. Said and told earn no points here (the task asked for other verbs). No point for suggested us to hire or apologized to be late.
  • B. Argumentative paragraph (6 pts). Task 0–2: 2 = a clear position + two reasons with support + one opposing point conceded and countered + a conclusion, about 120–150 words; 1 = one element missing (usually the concession) or under about 90 words. Organization 0–1: at least three argument phrases or linkers used correctly, including one contrast linker followed by the right grammar (Although + clause, Despite + noun, However, + sentence). Range 0–1: at least one Modules 1–4 structure used naturally (an impersonal passive, a mixed or third conditional, a participle clause, a cleft or inversion, a reporting verb). Accuracy 0–2: 2 = errors are occasional and never cause misunderstanding; 1 = frequent errors, but the argument is clear; 0 = meaning often lost.

Model answer A (4/4): Hi Jordan, you missed an interesting meeting! Sara apologized for being late with the report. Mike suggested hiring a temporary assistant until June, and the manager warned us not to book any vacation in March. In the end, we all agreed to work one Saturday next month. See you tomorrow!
Model answer B (6/6, 135 words): I'd like to argue that employers should let staff choose their own start and finish times. Firstly, it reduces stress. Workers who can avoid rush hour arrive calmer and more focused, and it is widely believed that less stressed staff take fewer sick days. A further point is that flexibility helps parents and caregivers, who often have to leave early for school pick-ups. It could be argued that flexible hours make teamwork harder, since colleagues are not always in the office together. That's a fair point; however, most teams only need a few shared "core hours" for meetings, for example from ten to three. What really matters is that the work gets done well, not what time someone arrives. To sum up, flexible hours cost employers very little, whereas the benefits for staff are considerable.

Errors to expect (Progress Test 1)
  • Mixed conditionals with two pasts ("I would have lived there now", item 3) and would in the if-clause ("If I would have left", item 2). Both are extremely persistent. Recycle the Lesson 1.2 "decide the time of each half" routine in warmers.
  • Present vs past wish ("I wish I didn't say that yesterday", item 4) and base verb after it's time we / I'd rather you ("It's time we leave", items 5–6). Speakers of Spanish, Portuguese, French, and Italian may reach for a subjunctive-style present here.
  • Base verb after be/get used to ("getting used to drive", item 10) and stop + -ing / to confusion (item 8). Learners whose languages don't mark this contrast (Chinese, Vietnamese, Japanese, Korean) need many meaningful examples, not more rules.
  • Timing in impersonal passives ("was said to resign", item 12) and verb patterns after reporting verbs ("apologized to miss", "suggested me to…").
  • Despite + clause ("Despite she had little experience…", Part 2A): the single most common B2 linker error, especially from Spanish (a pesar de que) and French (malgré le fait que) speakers who translate a full clause.
  • Writing: an argument with no concession, or a concession with no counter ("It's true that it's hard for teams. So it's a problem."). Also However used to join two clauses ("However it is flexible, …"). Praise the argument before you correct the grammar.

Part 5 · Writing /20
  • A. Formal inquiry email (8 × 1 pt). +1 a formal greeting (Dear Ms. Patel,). +1 the purpose in the first line (I am writing to inquire about…). +1 a relevant detail about the writer (job, experience, reason for interest). +1 a first polite question correctly formed (indirect: statement order, no do/does/did). +1 a second polite question correctly formed, ideally with a different opener or if/whether. +1 formal register held throughout: no contractions, no slang, no emojis. +1 a polite request frame or closing line (I would be grateful if you could… · I look forward to hearing from you.). +1 a sign-off that matches (Sincerely, / Yours sincerely, + full name).
  • B. Balanced argument (12). Score with the writing rubric below: four criteria × 0–3.

Model answer A (8/8, 109 words): Dear Ms. Patel, I am writing to inquire about the evening course in Workplace Communication advertised on your website. I currently work as a shift supervisor at a distribution center, and I would like to improve my presentation and email skills. Could you tell me when the next course starts and how many evenings a week it runs? I was also wondering whether participants receive a certificate at the end, as this would be useful for my career. Finally, I would be grateful if you could let me know the total cost and whether my employer can pay directly. I look forward to hearing from you. Sincerely, Leila Haddad

A sign-off note: Lesson 7.1 teaches the American sign-off Sincerely, (standard after any formal greeting) and mentions the British rule (Yours sincerely after a name, Yours faithfully after Dear Sir or Madam). Accept Sincerely, Yours sincerely, and Best regards,. Yours faithfully after a named greeting is the one slip the lesson flagged: no point for the sign-off, and mention it kindly.

Writing B · Model answer (B2+, 12/12, 166 words)

The question we need to consider is whether employers should pay for their staff to take training courses. The strongest argument in favor is that training benefits the company as much as the worker. Staff who learn new skills make fewer mistakes and are far more likely to stay, and replacing an employee is often much more expensive than training one. Not only does training improve performance, but it also shows staff that they are valued. On the other hand, it is often argued that employees may use the new skills to find a better job elsewhere. Admittedly, some will leave. That said, if companies refused to train anyone, they would lose even more people to employers who do. Another concern is cost, especially for small businesses; however, many courses are short, online, or partly funded by the government. On balance, I would argue that employers should pay for training, at least for skills connected to the job. What really matters is that both sides benefit.

Errors to expect (Final Test)
  • Wrong aspect in the future (14: "will cook" for a finished action) and will in time clauses ("by the time you will get home"). Link this to the B1 rule they already know.
  • The causative vs the past perfect (15: "I'm going to have cut my hair"). Ask "Who does the cutting?"
  • Determiner agreement (16–17: "Every employees", "Neither of the plans are"). The plural verb after neither of is normal in relaxed speech, so don't over-correct it in the interview.
  • "very cheaper" (18), one the in parallel comparisons (19), and question order inside indirect questions (20 and Listening 13: "Could you tell me where is…"). Speakers of languages that keep question order in embedded questions (Spanish, Arabic, Tagalog, and others) will do this for a long time. It rarely blocks meaning, but it matters in formal settings.
  • Writing A: a formal opening followed by an informal close ("Thanks! Cheers"), contractions, and direct questions that sound like demands ("What is the price?"). Writing B: arguments listed but never weighed, a missing conclusion, or a conclusion that ignores the concession. Many learners also overuse However at the start of every sentence.
  • Listening B (items 6 and 10): thirty/thirteen and the relaxed forms (gonna, wanna, lemme). Accept only the right answer, but treat misses as a listening next step, not a grammar problem.

Speaking Rubric · Both Tests

Five criteria, each 0–3 (total /15). 0 = no response, or only L1. The bands follow the CEFR qualitative aspects of spoken language (range, accuracy, fluency, interaction, coherence, phonological control) and sociolinguistic appropriateness (register). The B2 column paraphrases the Council of Europe's B2 descriptors (CEFR Companion Volume, 2020). For the progress test, judge against Modules 1–4 language only.

CriterionNot yet B2 (1)B2 (2)B2+ strong (3)
Task & coherence /3 B1 level: tells a story or gives an opinion as a simple list of points; reasons are brief; the long turn has little shape or runs out quickly. Gives clear, detailed descriptions and develops a viewpoint with supporting points and relevant examples; explains the advantages and disadvantages of options. Links ideas with a range of connectors, although a long turn may be a little "jumpy". Develops the long turn and the argument systematically, highlighting the key points; stories have a hook, layers of background, and a landed ending; conclusions follow clearly from the reasons given.
Range & register /3 Enough language for familiar topics, but searches for words often, repeats the same phrases, and can't adjust formality. A sufficient range to describe, give viewpoints, and develop arguments without much obvious searching for words, using some complex sentence forms. Can express themselves appropriately in formal and informal situations and change register when the situation changes. Wide, flexible range (emphasis, hedging, reporting verbs, precise comparison) chosen to suit the point; switches register confidently and naturally, including relaxed, vague language where it fits.
Accuracy /3 Good control of B1 structures, but B2 forms (conditionals, past modals, passives, verb patterns) often break down; errors sometimes cause confusion. A relatively high degree of grammatical control. Errors (articles, prepositions, an occasional tense or pattern slip) do not cause misunderstanding, and the learner corrects many of them. Consistently good control of the course's structures, including mixed conditionals, impersonal passives, and future perfect; slips are rare and usually self-corrected at once.
Interaction /3 Answers and responds, but struggles to keep an exchange going or to respond to disagreement without help. Starts, maintains, and closes discussion naturally, taking turns appropriately. Helps the discussion along, invites others in, and responds to counter-arguments, conceding a point before countering it. Handles push-back with ease: concedes, refines, and defends a position persuasively; asks probing follow-up questions; negotiates a compromise or solution in the role-play.
Pronunciation & fluency /3 Understandable, but long pauses to plan, flat stress, or unclear sounds make the listener work hard in longer turns. Produces stretches of language at a fairly even tempo; may hesitate while searching for patterns and expressions, but there are few noticeably long pauses. Stress and intonation are generally appropriate; accent has little or no effect on intelligibility. Speaks fluently and spontaneously; uses stress and intonation to highlight key points and to sound polite or relaxed as needed; weak forms and linking sound natural.

Writing Rubric · Final Test Part 5B

Four criteria, each 0–3 (total /12). 0 = nothing written, or nothing in English. At B2 the learner can write an essay that develops an argument, giving reasons in support of or against a particular point of view and explaining the advantages and disadvantages of various options, and can synthesize information and arguments from different sources (paraphrased from the CEFR). An assigned side is fine: score the argument, never the opinion.

CriterionNot yet B2 (1)B2 (2)B2+ strong (3)
Task & content /3 Under about 120 words, or one-sided: arguments for or against, with no weighing; no clear conclusion. 150–180 words; the question introduced; two arguments for and two against, with reasons or examples; at least one concession answered; a conclusion that takes a position. A well-developed, balanced argument: points supported with specific examples, the strongest objection answered convincingly, and a conclusion that follows logically from the weighing.
Organization & linking /3 Ideas in a list; linkers repeated (and, but, However, However) or used with the wrong grammar. Clear structure (introduction, for, against, conclusion); a range of linkers used accurately (Although, Despite, whereas, Admittedly… That said, On balance). Paragraphing and signposting guide the reader smoothly; linkers are varied and precise, and the concede-and-counter move reads naturally.
Range /3 Repeats the same words and B1 structures; little formal vocabulary. Enough topic vocabulary; several B2 structures (a conditional, an impersonal passive, a participle clause, a comparison, an emphasis structure); a suitably neutral-to-formal register. Varied vocabulary, including accurate word-formation choices, and B2 structures used naturally for effect (inversion, cleft, mixed conditional); register consistent throughout.
Accuracy /3 Frequent errors that make the reader stop and reread. A relatively high degree of control; errors don't cause misunderstanding; punctuation of connectors mostly correct. Few errors, mostly small slips; complex sentences and punctuation well controlled.

These are guidelines. Your thirteen weeks of observation count as much as one test on one day, especially the Class 12 and Class 25 review projects. A learner who was ill, anxious, or grieving on test day deserves a re-test or a quick re-check with the shared placement test before any "repeat" recommendation. Record the outcome and a one-line reason for your program.

Giving Feedback Kindly

Principles
  • Private, always. Never read scores aloud or return papers in score order.
  • Strength first, then one next step. One concrete step, not a list of errors.
  • Name the progress. Compare with Week 1, not with classmates.
  • Fluency before accuracy. Praise what they achieved (a persuasive point, a smooth concession, a real change of register) before you mention a form.
  • Accuracy and range. At B2, the next step can be a wider choice, not just a correction: "You said good five times. Try effective or worthwhile."
  • Written note to take home, with the lesson number and URL.
  • Adults decide. Offer the recommendation and the reason, and let the learner choose when the program allows it.
Phrases that work at B2
You did a great job. 🌟 Your argument was really persuasive. The way you conceded that point, then came back with a stronger one, was excellent. You switched from formal to informal so naturally. Next step: "I wish I had known", not "I wish I would have known". Do Lesson 1.2 again at home. Click 🔊 Listen and shadow it. Your next class: Advanced English (C1). 🎉 One more B2 class. It'll feel easier the second time. I'm proud of you.
Thirteen weeks ago we were layering past tenses. Yesterday you won a debate!
Your speaking is ready for C1. Reading and writing need more time, so let's find a writing class to go with it.
Avoid

Red ink everywhere, percentages without meaning ("62%"), the word "fail", comparisons ("Maria got 90"), and correcting a learner's accent as though it were an error. Don't grade opinions: a learner who argues the opposite of your view, clearly and politely, or who argued an assigned side, has done exactly what B2 asks. If a learner cries or goes quiet, pause the conversation and come back to it. The number can wait.
