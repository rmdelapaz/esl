🔢 Lesson 5.3: Determiners & Quantifiers — all, every, both & more

Words like all, every, each, both, most, and none look tiny, but they carry a surprising amount of meaning — and a surprising number of traps. Do you say "all the day" or "the whole day"? Is it "every people" or "all people"? Does neither take a singular or a plural verb? This lesson clears up the whole family of determiners and quantifiers, so you can talk about groups — whole, individual, paired, partial — with precision and correct agreement.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish all from whole, and every from each
  • Use both / neither / either correctly for two things, with the right verb agreement
  • Use most, most of, none, none of, and all/some of + the/pronoun accurately
  • Apply subject–verb agreement after each/every/neither/either, and avoid classic errors like "every people"

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe and compare two groups you know well (people, places, options) in at least six sentences, using a range of determiners and quantifiers with correct agreement.

Small Words, Big Precision

Determiners and quantifiers are the words that sit in front of a noun and tell us how much or how many — and, crucially, how we're viewing the group. Are we treating it as one whole block, or picking through it one member at a time? Are we talking about two things, or many? All of it, none of it, or just some?

At B1 you learned the basics — some, any, much, many, a few, a little. At B2 the challenge is precision: choosing between near-neighbours that carry a real difference in meaning, and getting the verb to agree correctly. Consider how much rides on a single word:

Every student has a laptop. (the group as a whole — singular verb)

All the students have laptops. (the group together — plural verb)

Each student has a different timetable. (one by one — singular verb)

Same group of students, three subtly different pictures. That's the skill this lesson builds: seeing the group the way the determiner asks you to, and matching the verb to it.

🧠 Mindset

These words feel fiddly because the differences are small — but small differences are exactly what separate confident B2 English from good B1 English. Don't try to memorise a wall of rules in one go. Learn the contrasts in pairs — all vs whole, every vs each, both vs neither — and lean on the examples until the choice starts to feel right. And when the verb agreement trips you up, remember: it's one of the most common slips even for advanced speakers, so noticing it is already progress. 🌱

All vs Whole

All and whole can both mean "the complete thing," but they sit in different positions and take different articles. The classic contrast:

❌ "all the day"  →  ✅ "the whole day"  or  ✅ "all day"

The key patterns:

WordPatternExample
allall + (the) + nounI've read all the books on the list.
wholethe/a/my + whole + singular nounI've read the whole book.
all (time)all + day/week/year (no the)It rained all day.
whole (time)the whole + day/weekIt rained the whole day.

The big structural difference: whole comes after the article ("the whole day"), while all comes before it ("all the day" is wrong — but "all day" without the is fine). Whole works most naturally with singular countable nouns; for plurals and uncountables, prefer all:

✅ the whole cake / the whole team    ✅ all the cakes / all the information

⚠️ "the whole cakes" and "all the information" show the split — use whole for one unit, all for the collection.

💡 A quick feel

Whole emphasises one complete unit seen as unbroken ("the whole story", "her whole life"). All gathers up everything in a set or an amount ("all the details", "all my energy"). If you can put "the entire…" in front of a single thing, whole fits.

Every vs Each

Both every and each refer to members of a group and both take a singular noun and a singular verb — but they look at the group differently:

everyeach
View of the groupthe group as a whole (all of them together)the members one by one, individually
Typical usegeneralising about all membersfocusing on separate members, often two or a small number
ExampleEvery student passes the entry test.Each student receives a personal report.

Because they treat the group as made of individual units, every and each always take a singular verb, even though the meaning covers many people:

✅ Every room has a view.    ❌ Every room have a view.

✅ Each of the candidates was interviewed.    (each of + plural, but still singular verb)

⚠️ The "every people" trap

Because every and each take a singular noun, you cannot say "every people" or "each students." Use a singular noun, or switch to all for the plural:

  • ❌ "Every people needs water." → ✅ "Every person needs water." or ✅ "All people need water."
  • ❌ "Each students has a locker." → ✅ "Each student has a locker."

Remember: people is already plural, so it can never follow every or each.

Here's the split in a diagram — same group, two ways of looking at it:

graph LR
    A["A group of members"] --> B["every: the whole group at once / singular verb"]
    A --> C["each: one member at a time / singular verb"]

💡 When they're interchangeable — and when they're not

Often either works ("Every / Each room has a number"). But use each when you stress the individuals, especially with two ("She held a bag in each hand" — not "every hand"), and prefer every for near-100% generalisations and with words like almost ("almost every day", not "almost each day").

Both, Neither & Either

This trio is reserved for two things — a pair. Choosing between them is about how many of the two you include, and the verb agreement is where learners most often slip.

WordMeaning (of two)VerbExample
boththe two together (2/2)pluralBoth options are good.
neithernot one and not the other (0/2)singularNeither option is good.
eitherone or the other (1 of 2)singularEither option is fine.

The verb agreement is the headline: both is plural, but neither and either are singular — because they focus on one option at a time:

✅ Both restaurants are excellent.    ✅ Neither restaurant is cheap.    ✅ Either day works for me.

They also pair up with and / nor / or as correlative conjunctions:

both … and … neither … nor … either … or … Neither of them is… Both of us are… Either of you is welcome.

⚠️ "Neither of / either of" + plural noun — still singular verb

When you add of before a plural, the noun goes plural but the verb stays singular in careful/formal English: "Neither of the answers is correct," "Either of the trains gets you there." (In relaxed speech you'll hear a plural verb — "neither of them are" — but for exams and formal writing, keep it singular.)

A: So, the flat in the city or the house by the coast — which are you leaning towards?

B: Honestly, both places have their charms, but neither option is cheap.

A: True. Either choice means a longer commute, though.

B: Exactly. Neither of them is perfect — but both of us agree we need more space.

A: Then let's visit each one again and decide.

Most, None & the "of" Family

Several quantifiers change slightly when you point to a specific group rather than things in general. The rule of thumb: add of before the, a possessive (my, his), or a pronoun (them, us) — but drop it when you generalise.

General (no of)Specific (+ of + the/pronoun)
Most people love music.Most of the people here love jazz.
All children need sleep.All of my children are grown up.
Some advice is useless.Some of the advice was useful.
None (as a short answer)None of the students failed.

So you say "most people" (people in general) but "most of the people in this room" (a specific set). With a pronoun, of is compulsory: "most of them", "all of us", "none of you" — never "most them" or "all us."

⚠️ Two classic errors to bury for good

  • ❌ "all of people" → ✅ "all people" (general) or ✅ "all of the people" (specific). Don't put of before a bare plural noun.
  • ❌ "most of teachers" → ✅ "most teachers" (general) or ✅ "most of the teachers" (specific).

The fix is simple: of needs a the / possessive / pronoun after it. No the? Then no of.

💡 Agreement with none of

None of can take a singular or plural verb. With uncountables it's singular ("None of the water was clean"). With plural nouns, formal English prefers the singular ("None of the students was late"), but the plural ("None of the students were late") is extremely common and widely accepted in everyday use. Either is safe in speech; lean singular for strict formal writing.

Putting it together: "Most of my friends have left the city, but none of them has forgotten it. All of us still meet up, and each visit feels like coming home."

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use a singular verb after every, each, neither, and either — even though the meaning covers several things.
  • Put whole after the article ("the whole day") and all before it or bare ("all the books", "all day").
  • Add of only before the, a possessive, or a pronoun ("most of the class", "all of us") — drop it for general statements ("most people").
  • Reserve both / neither / either for exactly two things.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "every people" / "each students" ❌ → every and each take a singular noun: "every person", "each student".
  • "all of people" / "most of teachers" ❌ → drop of without the: "all people", "most teachers".
  • Plural verb after neither/either (in formal English) ❌ → "Neither of them is…", not "are".
  • "all the day" ❌ → "the whole day" or "all day".
  • Using both/neither for three or more ❌ → those words are only for two; use all / none for larger groups.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • All comes before the article (or bare — "all day"); whole comes after it ("the whole day") and suits single units.
  • Every views the group as a whole; each views members one by one — both take a singular noun and verb.
  • Both (plural verb), neither and either (singular verb) are only for two things.
  • Add of before the, a possessive, or a pronoun ("most of the class", "none of us"); drop it in general statements ("most people").
  • Avoid the classic slips: "every people" ✗, "all of people" ✗, "the whole… → all the day" ✗.

You can now say exactly how much of a group you mean — all, most, none, both, neither — and get the verb to agree correctly, which is one of the most reliable markers of careful, advanced English. ❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is it really wrong to say "every people"?

Yes. Every (and each) must be followed by a singular noun, and people is plural. Say "every person" or switch to "all people" for the plural idea. The same logic blocks "each students" → "each student".

Should "neither of them" take "is" or "are"?

In careful and formal English, use the singular: "Neither of them is ready." In relaxed speech, many native speakers say "neither of them are," and it's widely accepted. For exams and formal writing, play it safe with the singular.

When do I need "of" — "most people" or "most of people"?

Use of only before the, a possessive (my, his), or a pronoun (them, us): "most of the people", "most of them". For a general statement with a bare noun, drop it: "most people". So "most of people" is always wrong.
