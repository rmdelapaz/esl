🎯 Lesson 4.3: Review Project — Present, Argue & Discuss

This is it — the capstone. In this final lesson you'll learn the language of presenting, arguing, and discussing: how to structure a talk, build a case, concede a point and counter it, and persuade with control. Then you'll take everything you've built across the whole B2 level and pour it into one extended speaking project. This lesson doesn't just close B2 — it closes the entire journey from the alphabet to confident, fluent discussion. Let's finish strong.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure and deliver a short presentation or argument with a clear intro, body, and conclusion
  • Use signposting and discourse language to guide a listener through your ideas
  • Agree, disagree, concede, and persuade politely and effectively in discussion
  • Combine the full range of B2 grammar — narrative tenses, conditionals, passives, participle clauses, reporting verbs, emphasis — in extended, fluent speech

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~65 minutes

🎯 Project: Prepare and deliver a 3–4 minute presentation OR structured argument on a topic of your choice, then handle a short discussion — deliberately using a wide range of B2 structures.

The Final Skill: Making Your Case

Across B2 you've built astonishing range — you can layer the past, speculate about the impossible, express regret, hedge, emphasise, and report what others think. This lesson is where all of that goes to work together. The most demanding thing a confident speaker does is stand up (or speak up) and make an argument: present an idea clearly, defend it under pressure, and respond to challenge with grace.

That's a functional skill — it's less about a single grammar point and more about the phrases and moves that hold a discussion together. Compare:

Unstructured: "Um, I think remote work is good. And also it's bad sometimes. People like it. Yeah."

Structured (B2): "I'd like to make the case that remote work benefits both sides. Firstly, it widens the talent pool. On the other hand, it's often argued that it weakens team culture — and that's a fair point. However, the evidence suggests culture depends far more on leadership than on location. To sum up, the real question isn't where we work, but how we're led."

Same opinion — but the second speaker sounds credible, organised, and persuasive. That's what this lesson teaches.

🧠 Mindset

You may feel that "presenting" or "debating" is only for confident native speakers. It isn't — it's a set of learnable moves, and you already own the grammar to power them. Think back to Lesson 1.1, when a four-tense sentence felt like a lot to hold at once. You hold it effortlessly now. Presenting is the same: awkward at first, automatic with reps. You are not starting a new mountain today — you're planting a flag on the summit you've already climbed. 🌱

Part A · Signposting a Talk

Signposting means giving your listener a map: telling them where you're going, when you move on, and when you're wrapping up. It's the single biggest thing that separates a clear talk from a confusing one. Good signposts are short, predictable phrases — that predictability is exactly what makes them useful.

Stage of the talkWhat it doesExample
Open & state topictells them what it's about"I'd like to talk about the future of work."
Preview structuretells them how many parts"I'll cover three points."
Move between pointssignals a transition"Firstly… / Moving on to… / Finally…"
Concludesignals the end & summarises"To sum up… / In conclusion…"

🗣️ Phrase Bank — Signposting

I'd like to talk about… Today I'll cover three points… Let me start by… Firstly, / To begin with, Moving on to… Turning now to… This brings me to… Finally, / Lastly, To sum up, / In conclusion, So, to bring it all together…

💡 Say your structure out loud, early

The most reassuring thing you can do in the first fifteen seconds is announce your plan: "I'll make three points — the cost, the benefits, and one common objection." Now the listener relaxes; they know what to expect. And so do you, which calms the nerves. Signposts help the speaker as much as the audience.

Part A · Building an Argument

Once the structure is clear, you fill it with a case: claims, reasons, and evidence, weighed against the other side. The phrases below let you present reasons, rank them, balance both sides, and hedge a claim you can't fully prove.

MoveUseful language
Give reasons"There are several reasons for this…" · "This is largely because…"
Rank a point"One key advantage is…" · "Perhaps the most important factor is…"
Balance both sides"On the one hand… on the other hand…" · "While X is true, Y matters more."
Make a tentative claim"It could be argued that…" · "There's a strong case that…"
Support with evidence"The evidence suggests…" · "Studies have shown that…"

🗣️ Phrase Bank — Building a Case

There are several reasons for this… One key advantage is… A further point is that… On the one hand… on the other hand… It could be argued that… The evidence suggests… This clearly shows that… The main drawback is…

✅ Notice the grammar already working

Look how naturally your B2 structures live inside these phrases: "It could be argued that…" is an impersonal passive; "The evidence suggests that…" is a reporting verb; "On the one hand… on the other hand…" is contrast linking. You don't have to bolt the grammar on — argument language is B2 grammar in action.

Part A · Discussion Moves

A presentation is a monologue; a discussion is live. Someone pushes back, and you respond. The most sophisticated — and most persuasive — response is rarely a flat "No, you're wrong." It's to concede a genuine part of their point and then counter it. This shows you're listening, keeps the tone civil, and makes your own position look more reasonable.

🤝 Phrase Bank — Concede, then Counter

That's a fair point, but… I take your point; however,… You're right to an extent, though… I see where you're coming from, yet… Granted, but we shouldn't forget that… There's some truth in that; even so,…

🎯 Phrase Bank — Persuade

Surely you'd agree that… The fact is,… What we can't ignore is… The bottom line is… If anything, this proves… Let's be honest —…

🌫️ Phrase Bank — Hedge & Soften

It seems to me that… to some extent arguably I'd tend to think… up to a point as far as I can tell

⚠️ Register in formal discussion & debate

The tone of a debate, a meeting, or an academic seminar is firm but courteous. Push the idea, never the person: say "I'd question that assumption," not "That's silly." Prefer the concede-and-counter move ("I take your point; however…") over blunt contradiction. Hedges like arguably and to some extent aren't weakness — in formal English they signal a careful, credible speaker who isn't overclaiming. Save the strong persuade phrases ("The fact is…") for your one or two most important points, or they lose their force.

Here's the concede-and-counter move in a short debate exchange:

A: The trouble with electric cars is that the batteries are terrible for the environment to produce.

B: That's a fair point — battery production is carbon-intensive, I won't pretend otherwise. However, if you look at the whole lifespan of the vehicle, the evidence suggests they still emit far less overall. So the manufacturing cost, while real, is arguably outweighed by the years of clean driving that follow.

A: Granted, but not everyone has the money to buy one in the first place.

B: I take your point; that's exactly why the policy question matters. Surely you'd agree that the answer is to make them affordable — not to abandon the technology altogether?

Part B · B2 Grammar Review — Your Full Toolkit

Before the project, take a moment to see just how far you've come. Look at the range you now command! Every structure below was once a new lesson; now it's a tool you can reach for mid-sentence to make an argument sharper. Here's your whole B2 toolkit, each item paired with the kind of sentence it lets you produce in a talk or debate:

B2 structureUsed in a presentation / argument
Narrative tenses"By the time the policy was introduced, attitudes had already changed."
Third / mixed conditionals"If we had acted sooner, we wouldn't be facing this crisis now."
Wish / regret"Many now wish they had invested in prevention earlier."
Gerunds & infinitives"We can't afford to ignore this, and we should stop pretending it will fix itself."
Past deduction (modals)"The results must have been affected by the sample size."
used to / be used to"People used to commute daily; now they're used to working from home."
Advanced / impersonal passive"It is widely believed that the shift is permanent."
Participle clauses"Having considered the evidence, I'm convinced the benefits outweigh the risks."
Reporting verbs"Critics have argued that the plan underestimates the cost."
Linkers & discourse markers"Despite this, support has grown. Consequently, the debate has shifted."
Emphasis (cleft & inversion)"What really matters is the long term. Not only does it save money, but it also saves lives."

💡 The goal isn't to use all of it at once

Fluent speakers don't cram every structure into one paragraph. They keep the whole toolkit available and reach for the right tool at the right moment — a conditional to speculate, a cleft to emphasise, a passive to sound objective. In your project, aim to weave in a handful naturally, not to tick every box mechanically. Range means choice, not overload.

Practice & Capstone Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose the discussion move

Objective: Match each situation to the most appropriate phrase.

  1. You want to accept part of an objection before disagreeing:
  2. You want to make your strongest, most decisive point:
  3. You want to soften a claim you can't fully prove:
  4. You're moving from your first point to your second in a talk:
✅ Answer

1. "That's a fair point, but…" / "I take your point; however,…" (concede–counter) · 2. "The fact is,…" / "The bottom line is…" (persuade) · 3. "It seems to me that…" / "arguably" / "to some extent" (hedge) · 4. "Moving on to…" / "Turning now to…" (signpost)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Rewrite for range

Objective: Rewrite each plain sentence using the B2 structure named in brackets.

  1. People think the internet has changed politics forever. (impersonal passive)
  2. We didn't plan ahead, so we have this problem now. (mixed conditional)
  3. The cost is the thing that concerns me most. (cleft for emphasis)
  4. I looked at the data, and then I reached my conclusion. (participle clause)
✅ Answer

1. "It is widely believed that the internet has changed politics forever." · 2. "If we had planned ahead, we wouldn't have this problem now." · 3. "What concerns me most is the cost." · 4. "Having looked at the data, I reached my conclusion." (Other valid versions exist — the key is that the target structure is present and accurate.)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Correct the error

Objective: Find and fix the one error in each argument sentence.

  1. Not only it saves money, but it also saves time.
  2. Having considered, the evidence is clear.
  3. It could be argue that we acted too late.
✅ Answer

1. Inversion after Not only: "Not only does it save money, but it also saves time." · 2. The participle clause needs its own subject-action: "Having considered the evidence, I think the case is clear." (the -ing clause and main clause must share a logical subject) · 3. Passive needs the past participle: "It could be argued that we acted too late."

🎯 Your Capstone Project: Present, Argue & Discuss

This is the big one — the final project of the whole programme. Do it solo (plan, then deliver into your phone's voice recorder and listen back) or with a partner / group (deliver live, then hold a real discussion or mini-debate). Either way, aim for a genuine 3–4 minutes of extended speech.

  1. (3 min) CHOOSE a topic you have a view on — for example: technology & society, the environment, education, the future of work, or the media. Pick a side or a clear angle.
  2. (8 min) PLAN a structure: an intro + signpost ("I'd like to argue that… I'll make three points…"), then 2–3 arguments with support (reason + evidence or example), then a conclusion ("To sum up…"). Jot key phrases, not a full script.
  3. (4 min) DELIVER a 3–4 minute talk. Deliberately use at least: a passive / impersonal passive ("It is widely believed that…"), a conditional ("If we had…, we wouldn't…"), a linker of contrast ("Despite this,…"), and one emphasis structure — a cleft ("What really matters is…") or inversion ("Not only… but also…").
  4. (4 min) DISCUSS. Field one or two questions or counterpoints (have a partner ask them, or write two likely objections yourself and answer them aloud). Concede genuinely, then counter politely: "That's a fair point, but…" · "I take your point; however,…"

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I opened with a clear topic statement and signposted my structure
  • ☐ I made 2–3 arguments, each with a reason or piece of support
  • ☐ I used a passive / impersonal passive
  • ☐ I used a conditional
  • ☐ I used a linker of contrast
  • ☐ I used an emphasis structure (cleft or inversion)
  • ☐ I concluded with a summary ("To sum up…")
  • ☐ I handled at least one counterpoint by conceding, then countering

📝 Lesson Summary & Farewell

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Signposting gives your listener a map: state the topic, preview the structure, mark each transition, and flag your conclusion.
  • Building an argument means claims backed by reasons and evidence, weighed against the other side — with hedges where you can't fully prove a point.
  • The strongest discussion move is concede, then counter: acknowledge the valid part, then respond ("That's a fair point, but…").
  • Your full B2 toolkit — narrative tenses, conditionals, passives, participle clauses, reporting verbs, linkers, and emphasis — lives naturally inside real argument, and it's yours to reach for at will.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's next after B2? Am I "fluent" now?

B2 is real, confident competence — the level at which you can live, work, and study in English and hold spontaneous conversations on a wide range of topics. "Fluent" isn't a finish line you cross once; it's a spectrum, and you're now firmly on the confident end of it. The path onward is C1 and C2, and you reach those less through textbooks and more through immersion and real use: reading widely, watching and listening to authentic material, speaking often, writing regularly — plus the ESL Hub skills courses to sharpen specific areas. You don't need permission to start using English fully. You already can.

How do I keep my English from slipping?

The honest rule is use it or lose it — but "using it" can be enjoyable, not a chore. Speak regularly (even a weekly conversation), read something you like every day, watch shows and follow creators in English, and write a little — messages, a journal, comments. A language you use for the things you already care about doesn't fade; it grows. Little and often beats occasional cramming.

Do I still make mistakes — and is that a problem?

Yes, you still make mistakes, and no, it's not a problem — confident speakers make them constantly and simply carry on. What's changed is that you now notice and self-correct, which is exactly what a strong B2 speaker does. Accuracy will keep improving as a by-product of use. Don't let the fear of a small error stop you from saying the big, interesting thing.

🔭 Looking Ahead

The course ends here, but your English doesn't — this is a beginning as much as an ending. From here you can:

  • Aim for C1 and beyond: richer vocabulary, subtler tone, and near-effortless fluency, built mainly through wide reading and real-world use.
  • Live in the language: books, podcasts, films, news, communities, and — above all — real conversations with real people.
  • Prove it, if you want to: exams like Cambridge B2 First or C1 Advanced, or IELTS, give you a recognised certificate for work, study, or migration — and a concrete next goal to train toward.
  • Keep leveling up your skills with the other ESL Hub courses when you're ready for a new focus.

✅ "Keep Going" Habits

  • Have at least one real English conversation every week — talk about ideas, not just logistics.
  • Read something in English daily, even a single article, and note one new word or phrase.
  • Watch or listen to authentic English (shows, podcasts, talks) for enjoyment, not just study.
  • Write regularly — messages, a short journal, a comment, a review.
  • Keep your Learning Journal going; review it now and then to see how far you've come.
