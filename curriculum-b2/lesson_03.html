😔 Lesson 1.3: Past Regrets — Wish, If Only, Would Rather & It's Time

There's a whole family of English structures for the things we didn't do, wish we hadn't done, or would rather someone did differently. They all share one clever trick: they use a past form to talk about something unreal. Master them, and you can voice regret, preference, and gentle pressure with the fluency of a native speaker.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Express regrets about the past with wish / if only + past perfect
  • Contrast present wishes (wish + past) with past regrets (wish + past perfect)
  • Use would rather to state preferences — for yourself and for other people
  • Use it's (high) time + past simple to say something is overdue

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Project: Reflect on and share six or more real (or invented) regrets and preferences, using the full range of structures from this lesson.

The Unreal Past

In the last lesson you built third conditionals: "If I had studied medicine, I would have earned more." You used a past perfect to describe something that didn't actually happen. This lesson is that same idea, aimed straight at feeling: the language of regret and preference.

Watch how the same trick appears again and again:

Regret about now: "I wish I had a bigger flat." (past form → present meaning)

Regret about the past: "I wish I had bought that flat years ago." (past perfect → past meaning)

Preference for someone: "I'd rather you didn't mention it." (past form → present/future meaning)

Something overdue: "It's time we left." (past form → right-now meaning)

Notice that none of these sentences is really about the past. The past tense is a grammatical signal for "unreal" or "not yet" — one logic, several uses.

🧠 Mindset

Regret gets a bad reputation, but for a language learner it's pure gold: every "I wish I had…" is really a lesson you've already learned, dressed as sorrow. Try reframing each regret as an insight or a goal — "I wish I'd started earlier" becomes "so I'll start today." As you practise these structures, let them point forward. You're not rehearsing what went wrong; you're rehearsing the self-awareness of a confident speaker. 🌱

Review: Wishes About the Present

You met this at B1, so here's a quick recap. To wish a present situation were different, use wish / if only + past simple. (If only is just a stronger, more emotional version of wish.)

Reality (present)Wish (present)
I don't have much time.I wish I had more time.
She lives far away.If only she lived closer.
I can't drive.I wish I could drive.
It's raining.I wish it weren't raining.

💡 was or were?

After wish / if only, were for all persons is the traditional, slightly more formal choice: "I wish I were taller." In everyday speech, "I wish I was taller" is extremely common and perfectly acceptable — but were is safer in writing and exams.

Regrets: Wish + Past Perfect

Here's the new tool. To express a regret about the past — something you're sorry happened or didn't happen — use wish / if only + past perfect (had + past participle).

Reality (past)Regret (wish + past perfect)
I didn't study medicine.I wish I had studied medicine.
I said something cruel.If only I hadn't said that.
We didn't book earlier.I wish we had booked earlier.
You spent all the money.I wish you hadn't spent it all.

This is the emotional twin of the third conditional from Lesson 1.2. In fact they often pair up:

"I wish I had set an alarm. If I had set one, I wouldn't have missed the interview."

Regret (wish + past perfect) → its consequence (third conditional). Same unreal past, two angles.

⚠️ Don't collapse the two wishes

The tense choice carries the whole meaning — pick it deliberately:

  • Present wish: "I wish I spoke French." → I don't speak it now.
  • Past regret: "I wish I had learned French." → I'm sorry about a past choice not to learn.

Saying "I wish I had spoken French" when you mean the general present ability is a classic slip. If the situation is ongoing now, use the past simple; if it's a finished past event or choice, use the past perfect.

Wish + Would (Annoyance)

One more wish pattern, also from B1. Use wish + would + base verb to complain about someone else's (or something's) behaviour — an annoying habit or a situation you want to change.

  • I wish it would stop raining.
  • I wish you wouldn't interrupt me.
  • I wish the neighbours would turn the music down.
  • If only he would listen for once.

💡 You can't wish + would about yourself

Because would here means "please change your behaviour," it needs a different subject. Say ❌ "I wish I would exercise more"; instead use a present wish about your ability or situation: ✅ "I wish I exercised more" (present wish) or ✅ "I wish I could find the motivation." Use wish + would only when someone or something else is doing the annoying thing.

Would Rather

Would rather (often contracted to 'd rather) means would prefer to. It has two very different patterns depending on who does the action.

PatternFormMeaningExample
(a) Same subject'd rather + base verba preference for yourselfI'd rather stay home tonight.
(b) Different subject'd rather + someone + past simpleyou want someone else to do/not do somethingI'd rather you didn't smoke here.

To compare two options in pattern (a), use than + base verb: "I'd rather walk than wait for the bus." The negative is simply 'd rather not: "I'd rather not talk about it."

⚠️ The tricky past tense in pattern (b)

When the subjects are different, English uses the past simple even though the meaning is present or future. It's the unreal-past trick again:

  • "I'd rather you came tomorrow." → about tomorrow, not the past.
  • "She'd rather we didn't tell anyone." → about now.
  • "I'd rather you hadn't told him." → past perfect here does point to the past: you're sorry they already did.

So: past simple = present/future preference; past perfect = a preference about something already done.

I'd rather stay… I'd rather not… I'd rather you didn't… I'd rather walk than… Would you rather…?

It's (High) Time

Use it's time + past simple to say that something should happen now — it's due, even a little overdue. Adding high (it's high time) or about (it's about time) adds mild criticism: it should have happened already.

FormMeaning & toneExample
It's time + past simpleit's the right moment nowIt's time we left — the film starts soon.
It's high time + past simpleoverdue; mild reproachIt's high time you got a proper job.
It's about time + past simpleoverdue; impatientIt's about time they fixed that road.
It's time + to + base verbneutral, no subject namedIt's time to go.

💡 Past form, present/future meaning

"It's time we left" does not mean we already left — it means we should leave right now. Just like would rather + someone, the past simple after it's time carries a present/future sense. Use it's time to + verb when you don't want to name who does it.

Here is the whole family gathered together, so the shared logic is visible at a glance:

graph TD
    A["A PAST FORM used for an UNREAL / not-yet idea"] --> B["wish + past simple = present wish"]
    A --> C["wish + past perfect = past regret"]
    A --> D["would rather + someone + past = preference for now"]
    A --> E["it's time + past = overdue / due now"]

✅ One pattern, many uses

If these felt like four separate rules to memorise, look again at the diagram: they're one idea — a past tense signalling "this isn't real / hasn't happened yet." The same instinct behind the second and third conditionals is doing the work here. You're not learning four things; you're applying one thing four ways. That's the moment grammar starts to feel like logic instead of a list.

In Conversation

Two friends, Nadia and Tom, catch up over coffee. Notice every structure from this lesson working naturally in real speech.

Nadia: You look shattered.

Tom: I am. I wish I hadn't stayed up so late finishing that report. If only I had started it on Monday like you told me.

Nadia: Ha — it's high time you learned to plan ahead.

Tom: I know, I know. Honestly, I wish my manager wouldn't dump things on me at the last minute, though.

Nadia: Fair. So, lunch? There's that new Thai place.

Tom: To be honest, I'd rather just grab a sandwich and sit outside. I'd rather we didn't spend an hour queuing.

Nadia: Deal. And it's about time we caught up properly — I feel like I never see you.

Tom: Same. I wish I had more free weekends. Right — it's time we got going before the good tables fill up.

💡 Spot the shift

Tom's "I wish I hadn't stayed up" is a past regret (past perfect), but "I wish I had more free weekends" is a present wish (past simple). Same speaker, same "I wish" — only the tense tells you which world he's talking about.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Present wish or past regret?

Objective: Decide whether the situation is ongoing now (wish + past simple) or a finished past event (wish + past perfect), and complete each sentence.

  1. I don't earn much. → I wish I (earn) more.
  2. I didn't take that job offer. → I wish I (take) that job offer.
  3. You're not here with me. → If only you (be) here.
  4. We told them our secret. → I wish we (not / tell) them.
  5. I can't play an instrument. → I wish I (can) play the piano.
  6. She missed the last train. → She wishes she (not / miss) it.
✅ Answer

1. earned (present) · 2. had taken (past regret) · 3. were (present; "was" also heard) · 4. hadn't told (past regret) · 5. could (present) · 6. hadn't missed (past regret)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Rewrite as "I wish…"

Objective: Turn each third-conditional idea or plain statement into a regret beginning with I wish or If only.

  1. If I had saved more money, I could have travelled. → I wish
  2. I ate too much, and now I feel sick. → I wish
  3. We didn't book a table, so we couldn't get in. → If only
  4. I forgot her birthday. → I wish
✅ Answer

1. I wish I had saved more money. · 2. I wish I hadn't eaten so much. · 3. If only we had booked a table. · 4. I wish I hadn't forgotten her birthday. (Answers may vary slightly, but must use wish/if only + past perfect.)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Choose the structure

Objective: Pick the correct form of would rather or it's (high) time.

  1. I'd rather (walk) than take the bus.
  2. I'd rather you (not / park) in front of my gate.
  3. It's high time the government (do) something about it.
  4. It's time we (go) — it's getting dark.
  5. Would you rather I (call) you or emailed?
✅ Answer

1. walk (same subject → base verb) · 2. didn't park (different subject → past simple) · 3. did (it's high time → past simple) · 4. left / went (it's time → past simple) · 5. called (different subject → past simple)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Let the tense choose the world: past simple after wish for now, past perfect for the finished past.
  • Reach for if only when you want the regret to sound stronger and more heartfelt.
  • Remember that would rather + someone and it's time both take a past form for a present meaning — the same unreal-past logic.
  • Use 'd rather not and 'd rather + than to keep preferences natural and concise.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wish + would about yourself: ❌ "I wish I would sleep earlier." → ✅ "I wish I slept earlier" / "I wish I could sleep earlier."
  • Present-vs-past mix-up: ❌ "I wish I had known him" (when you mean you don't know him now) → ✅ "I wish I knew him."
  • Base verb after "would rather you": ❌ "I'd rather you don't go." → ✅ "I'd rather you didn't go."
  • Infinitive after "it's time we": ❌ "It's time we leave." → ✅ "It's time we left." (Use it's time to leave only without a subject.)
  • "would" inside the wish: ❌ "I wish I would have studied." → ✅ "I wish I had studied."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Wish / if only + past simple = a wish about the present ("I wish I had more time").
  • Wish / if only + past perfect = a regret about the past ("I wish I had studied medicine") — the emotional twin of the third conditional.
  • Wish + would complains about someone else's behaviour — never about yourself.
  • Would rather + base verb = your own preference; would rather + someone + past simple = what you want someone else to do.
  • It's (high) time + past simple = something is due now or overdue.
  • Several of these use a past form for an unreal present/future — one logic, many uses.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now voice the full emotional range of hindsight and preference: sorrow for what's done, longing for what isn't, and gentle pressure for what's overdue — all with the exact tense a fluent speaker would pick. That's a real leap in subtlety. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is "if only" really the same as "wish"?

Grammatically, yes — both take the same tense patterns. The difference is tone: if only is more emphatic and emotional, so it suits stronger regrets and longing ("If only I hadn't said that!").

Why is the past simple used after "would rather you" and "it's time" if the meaning is present?

It's the same "unreal past" instinct behind the second and third conditionals and wish. English recycles the past tense as a signal for "not real / not yet," not for real past time. So "I'd rather you left" and "It's time we left" both point to now or the future.

Can I ever use the past perfect after "would rather" or "it's time"?

After would rather, yes — to regret something already done: "I'd rather you hadn't told him." After it's time, we normally stick to the past simple for the "do it now" meaning; the past perfect isn't used there.
