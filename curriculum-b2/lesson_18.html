❔ Lesson 6.3: Asking Skilfully — Indirect & Negative Questions

You can already ask a direct question — but the way you ask it changes how you come across. "Where's the station?" gets an answer; "Could you tell me where the station is?" gets an answer and makes you sound considerate and fluent. In this lesson you'll learn the more sophisticated question forms that mark a confident B2 speaker: the indirect question for politeness, the negative question for surprise and confirmation, and the quietly tricky subject question.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form indirect questions with statement word order and no do/does/did
  • Use if / whether to make yes/no questions indirect
  • Ask negative questions to express surprise or seek confirmation
  • Recognise and form subject questions that need no auxiliary ("Who broke the window?")

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Role-play (or script) a real information-seeking situation, asking at least six polite indirect questions and handling the replies naturally.

Why How You Ask Matters

A direct question is efficient, but it can sound abrupt — especially to a stranger, in a shop, at a reception desk, or in an email. English softens the edges by wrapping the question inside a polite opener. Compare:

Direct: "Where is the station?"  (fine with friends; a bit blunt to a stranger)

Indirect: "Could you tell me where the station is?"  (polite, professional, fluent)

Notice something important: the actual question — where the station is — changes its shape once it's tucked inside the opener. The word order goes back to a statement, and the little helper is moves to the end. Learning to make that switch smoothly is the heart of this lesson. Alongside it, you'll meet two more tools — negative questions for reacting with surprise, and subject questions, where a small structural quirk trips up even good learners.

🧠 Mindset

Politeness in English isn't about being formal for its own sake — it's about reading the situation and matching your register to it. The same speaker might say "Where's the loo?" to a friend and "Could you tell me where the toilets are?" to a shop assistant, and both are exactly right. What you're building here is range: the ability to dial your questions up or down to fit the moment. That flexibility is a hallmark of genuine fluency. 🌱

Indirect Questions

An indirect question puts a polite opener in front of the real question — and the real question then follows statement word order. Three things change compared to the direct form:

  • the word order goes back to subject + verb (no inversion);
  • you drop do / does / did entirely (the tense moves onto the main verb);
  • for yes/no questions, you add if or whether.
Direct questionIndirect question
Where is the station?Could you tell me where the station is?
What time does it open?Do you know what time it opens?
How much did it cost?Could you tell me how much it cost?
Why are they closed?Do you know why they are closed?

Look closely at rows two and three. "What time does it open?" loses the does and the tense lands on the main verb: "what time it opens." "How much did it cost?" loses the did: "how much it cost." That's the single most common place learners slip — so it's worth slowing down on.

Here's the transformation as a diagram:

Useful openers

Keep a small bank of openers ready — they're what make the question polite:

Could you tell me…? Do you know…? Do you have any idea…? I was wondering… Would you mind telling me…? Can you remember…? I'd like to know…

⚠️ Keep statement order — don't invert twice

The most frequent mistake is keeping the direct word order inside the opener: "Could you tell me where is the station?" ❌ The inversion belongs only in the direct question. Once it's indirect, the verb goes after the subject: "Could you tell me where the station is?" ✅ And don't put do/does/did back in: "Do you know what time does it open?" ❌ → "…what time it opens?" ✅

💡 A note on punctuation

If the opener is itself a question ("Could you tell me…?", "Do you know…?"), the whole sentence takes a question mark. But if the opener is a statement ("I was wondering…", "I'd like to know…"), it's technically a statement and takes a full stop — though a question mark is very common and perfectly acceptable in practice.

Yes/No Questions: If & Whether

A direct question with no question word — a plain yes/no question — needs a joining word when it goes indirect. Use if or whether:

Direct (yes/no)Indirect with if / whether
Are they open on Sundays?Do you know if they are open on Sundays?
Does the bus stop here?Could you tell me whether the bus stops here?
Has the train left?Do you know if the train has left?

Everything else follows the same rules as before — statement order, no do/does/did. The if / whether simply replaces the missing question word. In most everyday cases if and whether are interchangeable; whether is a touch more formal, and it's the one to use before or not directly ("whether or not they're open") and in writing.

💡 "…or not"

You can add or not to sharpen a yes/no indirect question: "Do you know whether they're open or not?" With whether, "or not" can even sit right after it ("whether or not they're open"); with if, keep "or not" at the end ("if they're open or not"), not directly after if.

Negative Questions

A negative question — built with isn't, don't, didn't, aren't, wasn't… — isn't really asking for new information. It's usually a way of expressing surprise, seeking agreement, or confirming something you already believe.

Negative questionWhat it really does
Isn't it beautiful?Expresses admiration; expects you to agree.
Didn't you get my message?Surprise — I thought you had.
Haven't we met before?Seeking confirmation of a belief.
Wouldn't it be easier to fly?Making a polite suggestion.

The tone is the key. "Isn't it beautiful?" isn't a genuine question about beauty — it's an invitation to share your enthusiasm. "Didn't you get my message?" carries a note of surprise, even mild concern. Native speakers reach for these constantly, and using them makes your English sound warmer and more engaged.

⚠️ Answering a negative question — mind the trap

English answers a negative question by the facts, not by agreeing with the questioner. If someone asks "Didn't you get my message?" and you did get it, you say "Yes (I did)." If you didn't, you say "No (I didn't)." This differs from many languages, where you'd say "yes" to agree with the negative — so it's a genuine trap. Stick to the facts: yes = it's true / it happened; no = it isn't / it didn't.

A: Isn't this view incredible?

B: It really is. Haven't we been here before, actually?

A: I don't think so. Wouldn't you remember that climb?

B: True! By the way, didn't you say the café closes at five?

A: Yes, I did — so we should head back soon.

Subject Questions

Here's a small structural point that catches out even confident learners. Most questions ask about the object and use an auxiliary (do/does/did). But when the question word itself is the subject — the doer — there's no auxiliary and no inversion. The word order is just like a statement.

Object question (with auxiliary)Subject question (no auxiliary)
Who did you see? (you saw someone)Who saw you? (someone saw you)
What did she break?Who broke the window?
What does this mean?What happened next?
Who do you live with?Who lives here?

See the difference on the right: "Who broke the window?" has no did — the verb is already in the past (broke), and who is doing the breaking. Compare "What did she break?", where she is the doer and what is the thing broken, so we need did. Ask yourself: is the question word the doer? If yes, drop the auxiliary.

💡 A quick test

Answer the question with a statement. If the answer replaces the question word at the front ("Maria broke the window"), it's a subject question — no auxiliary. If the answer goes after the verb ("She broke the window"), it's an object question — use do/does/did. The question word simply takes the subject's place, and the verb stays in its normal form.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • In an indirect question, switch to statement order and drop do/does/did — put the tense on the main verb.
  • Use if / whether to make a yes/no question indirect.
  • Reach for negative questions to express surprise or invite agreement ("Isn't it lovely?").
  • Ask "is the question word the doer?" — if yes, it's a subject question with no auxiliary.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Keeping the inversion: "Do you know where is he?" ❌ → "Do you know where he is?" ✅
  • Leaving in do/does/did: "Could you tell me what time does it start?" ❌ → "…what time it starts?" ✅
  • Adding an auxiliary to a subject question: "Who did phone?" ❌ → "Who phoned?" ✅
  • Answering a negative question by agreement, not fact: "Didn't you go?" — if you went, the answer is "Yes," not "No."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Indirect questions use a polite opener + statement word order, and drop do/does/did ("Could you tell me where the station is?").
  • For yes/no questions, add if or whether ("Do you know if they're open?"); whether is a touch more formal.
  • Negative questions (Isn't it…? Didn't you…?) express surprise, seek agreement, or confirm — and are answered by the facts, not by agreeing.
  • Subject questions take no auxiliary when the question word is the doer ("Who broke the window?"); object questions keep do/does/did.
  • Choosing indirect forms lets you match your register to the situation — the core of sounding polite and fluent.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Are indirect questions always more polite? Should I always use them?

They're more polite and more careful, which is ideal with strangers, in service situations, in emails, and at work. But with friends and family, a direct question ("What time's the film?") is perfectly natural and not rude at all. The skill is choosing to match the situation — use indirect forms when a little distance or courtesy helps, direct forms when you're relaxed and familiar.

What's the difference between "if" and "whether"?

In most everyday indirect questions they're interchangeable ("Do you know if/whether they're open?"). Whether is slightly more formal and is preferred in writing, directly before or not ("whether or not…"), and before a to-infinitive ("whether to go"). If is more common in casual speech. When unsure, either is usually fine.

How do I answer a negative question without getting confused?

Ignore the negative and answer by the plain facts. "Didn't you finish it?" — if you did finish, say "Yes (I did)"; if you didn't, say "No (I didn't)." In English, yes always means "it's true/it happened" and no means "it isn't/it didn't," regardless of how the question was phrased. Adding the short tag ("Yes, I did") removes any doubt.
