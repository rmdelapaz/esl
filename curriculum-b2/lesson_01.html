📖 Lesson 1.1: Narrative Tenses — Past Perfect & Continuous

Welcome to B2 — the level of nuance and range. You can already talk about the past. Now you'll learn to layer it: to show what happened before what, what was in progress, and how long it had been going on. These are the tenses that turn a flat list of events into a real, gripping story.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Combine the past simple, past continuous, past perfect, and past perfect continuous in one narrative
  • Use the past perfect to show that one past event happened before another
  • Use the past perfect continuous to describe the duration of an activity before a past moment
  • Sequence events clearly with by the time, before, after, already, when and while

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Tell (or record) a two-to-three-minute anecdote — a memorable or dramatic event — using all four narrative tenses to give it depth and background.

Why Layer Your Past?

At B1 you told stories in a line: this happened, then that happened, then that. That's clear — but real storytelling isn't a straight line. Good narratives constantly step backwards to fill in what came before, and sideways to describe what was going on in the background. The narrative tenses are the tools for exactly that.

Compare these two versions of the same moment:

Flat (past simple only): "I arrived at the station. The train left. I waited two hours."

Layered (narrative tenses): "By the time I arrived at the station, the train had already left. I had been rushing all morning, and now I was standing on an empty platform with two hours to wait."

Same facts — but the second version has depth, cause, and feeling. That's the B2 difference.

🧠 Mindset

You already know every ingredient here — have, been, past participles, -ing. B2 isn't about learning brand-new pieces; it's about combining familiar pieces with more control and subtlety. If a four-tense sentence feels like a lot to hold at once, that's normal — you're building fluency, and fluency is a muscle that grows with reps, not a switch that flips. 🌱

The Four Narrative Tenses

Each tense does a distinct job in a story. Here's the whole toolkit at a glance:

TenseFormJob in a storyExample
Past simpleverb + -ed / irregularthe main events, in orderShe opened the door.
Past continuouswas/were + -ingbackground action in progressThe rain was falling outside.
Past perfecthad + past participlean earlier event (before the story's "now")Someone had broken the window.
Past perfect continuoushad been + -ingduration of an activity before that momentThey had been arguing for hours.

✅ The big picture

Think of the past simple as the timeline of your story. The past continuous paints the scenery around a moment. The past perfect reaches further back to something earlier. The past perfect continuous adds how long that earlier activity lasted. Layering them is what makes you sound like a storyteller, not a reporter.

The Past Perfect in Depth

The past perfect (had + past participle) is the key new tool. It marks an event as happening before another past event — the "past of the past."

Two past eventsWhich came first?
When I got home, the party had finished.The party finished first; then I got home.
When I got home, the party finished.I got home, and then the party finished (my arrival triggered it).
⚠️ Important Note: You don't need the past perfect for every earlier event — only when the order might be unclear or you want to emphasise it. With before and after, the sequence is already obvious, so the past simple is often enough: "After I finished, I left" is fine. Use the past perfect where it adds clarity, not everywhere.

💡 Negatives, questions & contractions

Negative: hadn't (had not) — "I hadn't seen her for years." Question: "Had you eaten before you left?" → "Yes, I had. / No, I hadn't." Contraction: 'd = had — "She'd already gone" (careful: 'd can also mean would — context decides).

Past Perfect Continuous

The past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) emphasises the duration of an activity that was going on before a moment in the past — often explaining a result or a scene.

UseExample
duration up to a past point (often with for/since)By 2020, she had been teaching for ten years.
explaining a past result / evidenceHis eyes were red; he had been crying.
an activity interrupted or just finishedWe had been walking for hours when it started to rain.

⚠️ Simple vs continuous (same logic as the present perfect)

Use the past perfect simple for a completed earlier action or a quantity: "She had written three books." Use the past perfect continuous for the ongoing activity or its duration: "She had been writing all night." And remember: stative verbs (know, be, have, want) stay simple — "I had known him for years," not "had been knowing."

Here it all is, working together in a single narrative:

"When the manager finally called my name, I had been waiting for nearly an hour. I was getting nervous — I hadn't prepared as much as I'd wanted to, because the bus had broken down that morning. Still, I stood up, took a breath, and walked in."

Past perfect continuous (waiting) · past continuous (was getting) · past perfect (hadn't prepared, had broken down) · past simple (stood, took, walked).

Sequencing Words

These linkers make the order of events crystal clear and pair naturally with the narrative tenses.

By the time… before / after already / just when while / as until by then no sooner … than
  • By the time we arrived, it had ended.
  • While she was cooking, the phone rang.
  • I had just sat down when he called.
  • We waited until the rain had stopped.

💡 when vs while

Use while (or as) with a longer background action in the past continuous: "While I was reading…". Use when with the shorter action that interrupts it, in the past simple: "…when the lights went out."

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These sentences layer the past perfect and past continuous the way a good story does.

  • By the time we arrived at the station, the last train had already left.
  • She was reading quietly in the corner when the lights suddenly went out.
  • I recognised him at once, because we had met several times before.
  • They had been driving for hours before they finally found somewhere to stop.
  • While the guests were arriving, the caterers were still setting up the tables.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose the narrative tense

Objective: Put each verb in the best past tense.

  1. When we arrived, the film (already / start).
  2. She was tired because she (work) all day.
  3. While I (drive) home, I saw an accident.
  4. By the time he retired, he (work) there for 30 years.
  5. I (not meet) her before that evening.
✅ Answer

1. had already started · 2. had been working · 3. was driving · 4. had been working · 5. hadn't met

🏋️ Exercise 2: Past perfect or past simple?

Objective: Decide whether the earlier event needs the past perfect.

  1. After she (finish) dinner, she washed up.
  2. When I opened my wallet, I realised I (lose) my card.
  3. The ground was wet because it (rain).
✅ Answer

1. finished (after makes the order clear — past simple is fine; "had finished" is also acceptable) · 2. had lost · 3. had been raining (or had rained)

🎯 Your Project: Tell a Layered Anecdote

Speak out loud — to a partner, in a group, or into your phone's recorder.

  1. (3 min) Choose a memorable or dramatic event (a near-miss, a surprise, a first day, a mix-up).
  2. (3 min) Plan it in three layers: the main events (past simple), the background/scenery (past continuous), and what had already happened before it (past perfect / continuous).
  3. (3 min) Tell the story, aiming to use all four tenses and at least two sequencing words.
  4. (2 min) If you recorded it, listen back: does the past perfect make the order clear? Did you use for/since with the continuous?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I used the past simple for the main events
  • ☐ I used the past continuous for background
  • ☐ I used the past perfect for an earlier event
  • ☐ I used the past perfect continuous for duration
  • ☐ I used at least two sequencing words

🎯 Quick Quiz

Question 1: "When I got to the airport, the plane ___." Which is correct if the plane left before I arrived?

Question 2: "She was exhausted because she ___ all night." Choose the best form to stress duration.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use the past perfect when the order matters and isn't already obvious from before/after.
  • Reach for the past perfect continuous to explain a state or result ("was tired because…").
  • Set your scene with the past continuous before the main action lands.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Overusing the past perfect: not every past-in-the-past needs it — "Before I ate, I washed my hands" is fine. Save it for clarity/emphasis.
  • Stative verbs in the continuous: "I had been knowing him" ❌ → "I had known him" ✅.
  • Mixing time frames: don't slip into the present perfect ("has done") inside a past narrative — stay in the past ("had done").

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The four narrative tenses each have a job: events (past simple), background (past continuous), earlier events (past perfect), duration before a point (past perfect continuous).
  • The past perfect (had + pp) marks the "past of the past" — use it when order matters.
  • The past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) stresses duration and explains results.
  • Don't overuse the past perfect; before/after often make the past simple enough.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've moved from telling stories in a straight line to telling them in layers — reaching back, painting the scene, and controlling exactly what your listener understands about the order of events. That's the storytelling range of a genuinely confident speaker. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I always need the past perfect for the earlier event?

No. Use it when the sequence would otherwise be unclear or when you want to emphasise that something happened first. With before, after, or an obvious context, the past simple is perfectly natural.

How is the past perfect continuous different from the past continuous?

Past continuous = an action in progress at a past moment ("I was cooking when he arrived"). Past perfect continuous = an activity that had been going on before and up to that moment, stressing duration ("I had been cooking for an hour when he arrived").

In speech, "'d" is ambiguous — how do listeners know had vs would?

Context and the following verb. 'd + past participle = had ("she'd gone"); 'd + base verb = would ("she'd go"). Fluent listeners decode it instantly from that pattern, and so will you with practice.
