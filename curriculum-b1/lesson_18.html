⚖️ Lesson 6.3: Degree & Quantity — too/enough, so/such, few/little

How hot is too hot? When is something enough? What's the difference between "a few problems" and "few problems" — and why does so hot and such a hot day mean the same thing but need different grammar? This lesson gives you the precise tools of degree and quantity, so you can say exactly how much, how many, and to what extent.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Contrast too (more than you want) with enough (the right amount)
  • Use so and such for emphasis, with the …that result pattern
  • Choose between (a) few and (a) little — and feel the positive/negative difference
  • Use all / most / some / none of with nouns and pronouns

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Review a real experience (a film, a meal, a trip, a place) in eight or more sentences, using too/enough, so/such, and few/little naturally.

Saying How Much

We're always measuring things in conversation — too expensive, not warm enough, so tired, a few minutes, most people. These small words of degree (how much of a quality) and quantity (how much of a thing) let you be exact instead of vague. Getting them right is what separates rough B1 English from confident, expressive B1 English.

Two ideas run through the whole lesson, so keep them in mind:

📖 Two things to watch

1. Countable or uncountable? Some words go with things you can count (few, many), others with things you can't (little, much).

2. Positive or negative feeling? A tiny change — adding a — can flip the meaning from "enough, don't worry" to "not enough, a problem".

🧠 Mindset

These words are small, but they carry a lot of meaning and a lot of feeling. It's completely normal to mix up few and a few, or so and such, for a while — even advanced learners pause on these. Don't treat that as failure; treat it as the sign of someone reaching for real precision. Learn the contrasts as pairs, say them aloud until the difference feels different, and trust that clarity will come with use. 🌱

Too & Enough

These two are a natural pair: too means more than you want (a problem), and enough means the right amount (no problem). The trick is word order — they sit in different places.

WordPositionExample
too + adjectivebefore the adjectiveThe coffee is too hot. (I can't drink it)
adjective + enoughafter the adjectiveThe coffee isn't cool enough. (still too hot)
enough + nounbefore the nounWe don't have enough chairs.

⚠️ The order flips around enough

enough goes after an adjective but before a noun:

  • adjective: old enough, warm enough, big enough ✅  (not enough old)
  • noun: enough money, enough time, enough people ✅

too, by contrast, always comes before the adjective: too small, too expensive, too late.

Both often lead into a to-infinitive that explains the result:

It's too cold to swim. (so cold that we can't swim)

She's old enough to drive. (old enough that she can drive)

We don't have enough time to finish. (not enough time for finishing)

Too Much / Too Many

When too goes with a noun (not an adjective), it becomes too much or too many — and here the countable/uncountable choice matters.

PhraseUsed withExample
too manycountable (plural) nounsThere are too many cars in the city.
too muchuncountable nounsI drink too much coffee.
too much (alone)after a verbYou worry too much.

✅ Quick check: can you count it?

If you can put a number in front (one car, two cars), it's countable → too many. If you can't (you don't say "two coffees" for the drink in general), it's uncountable → too much. Same split you use for many / much in questions and negatives.

It's too expensive. I'm not tall enough. There's too much noise. We have too many meetings. Do we have enough time?

So & Such … That

To add emphasis — "really, extremely" — English uses so and such. They mean the same thing; the difference is purely grammatical: what comes after them.

WordFollowed byExample
soadjective / adverb (alone)The film was so good. He drove so quickly.
such a(adjective +) singular nounIt was such a good film.
such(adjective +) plural / uncountable nounThey're such kind people. It was such nice weather.

📖 The simple test

Is there a noun after the emphasis word?

No noun → use so: "so hot", "so tired", "so slowly".

Yes, a noun → use such (with a/an before a singular noun): "such a hot day", "such tired eyes".

Both often continue with … that + result — showing what the extreme quality caused:

It was so hot that we left early.

It was such a good film that I watched it twice.

She spoke so quietly that nobody could hear her.

Pattern: so + adjective + that + result, or such (a) + noun + that + result.

Priya: How was the concert last night?

Dan: Amazing! The band was so good that we didn't want it to end.

Priya: Really? I heard it was such a long show, though.

Dan: It was — three hours! But the time went so quickly that I hardly noticed.

(A) Few & (A) Little

Here's where a single letter changes everything. These four expressions all mean "a small amount," but they split two ways — by countable/uncountable, and by positive/negative feeling.

Countable (plural)Uncountable
positive (= some, enough)a few friendsa little time
negative (= not many/much)few friendslittle time

📖 The two splits, side by side

Countable vs uncountable: use few / a few with things you can count (friends, minutes, books); use little / a little with things you can't (time, money, water).

Positive vs negative: add a for the positive, hopeful version; drop it for the negative, "not enough" version.

✅ Feel the difference

"I have a few friends here." → positive: some friends, I'm okay. 🙂

"I have few friends here." → negative: almost none, I'm a bit lonely. 🙁

"We have a little time." → positive: enough to do something. 🙂

"We have little time." → negative: hardly any, hurry! 🙁

In everyday speech, the negative few / little can sound quite formal, so people often say "not many / not much" instead — but you should recognise both.

All / Most / Some / None of

Finally, a scale of quantity from everything to nothing: all → most → some → none. These describe how much of a group you mean.

WordMeaningExample
all100%All my friends came.
mosta large majorityMost people agree.
somea part, not allSome students were late.
none0%None of the shops were open.

⚠️ Add "of" before "the / my / this / them"

For a general group, use no of: "Most people like music."

For a specific group (with the, my, this, these, or a pronoun), add of: "Most of the people here…", "Some of my friends…", "None of them…".

  • ❌ Most of people → ✅ Most people (general)
  • ❌ Most the students → ✅ Most of the students (specific)
  • With a pronoun, of is always needed: all of us, some of them, none of you.

General (no of): Most children love stories, and all animals need care.

Specific (with of): Most of the children in my class love stories, but none of the animals at the shelter had a home.

The moment you point to a particular group — with the, my, or them — the little word of appears.

Most people agree. Some of my friends came. None of them were open. All of us were tired. Most of the time, it works.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. Notice the word order with too/enough, the so/such … that pattern, and the feeling behind few and a few.

  • The coffee was too hot to drink, so I waited a few minutes.
  • She isn't old enough to drive, but she's tall enough to reach the shelf.
  • It was such a good film that I watched it twice.
  • I was so tired that I fell asleep on the sofa.
  • We have a little money, so most of us can share a taxi.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: too, enough, too much or too many

Objective: Complete each gap with too, enough, too much, or too many.

  1. This box is heavy for me to lift.
  2. Sorry, I can't come — I have work today.
  3. She's not old to watch that film.
  4. There were people at the party.
  5. We don't have chairs for everyone.
✅ Answer

1. too (before adjective "heavy") · 2. too much (uncountable "work") · 3. enough (after adjective "old") · 4. too many (countable "people") · 5. enough (before noun "chairs")

🏋️ Exercise 2: so / such, and few / little

Objective: Complete each gap. For 1–3 choose so, such, or such a. For 4–6 choose a few, few, a little, or little.

  1. It was cold that the lake froze.
  2. She's kind person.
  3. They were interesting stories.
  4. Don't worry, we still have time before the train. (positive)
  5. I've made friends at my new job. (positive, countable)
  6. Sadly, people came to the meeting. (negative: almost none)
✅ Answer

1. so (adjective alone) · 2. such a (singular noun "person") · 3. such (plural noun "stories") · 4. a little (positive, uncountable) · 5. a few (positive, countable) · 6. few (negative, countable)

🏋️ Exercise 3: all / most / some / none of

Objective: Complete each sentence with the right quantity word, adding of only where it's needed.

  1. ____ people need water to live. (everyone, general)
  2. ____ my friends live abroad now. (the majority, specific)
  3. I invited ten people, but ____ them came. (zero)
  4. ____ students passed, but a few didn't. (a part, general students in this class → specific)
  5. She ate ____ the cake by herself! (the whole thing, specific)
✅ Answer

1. All people need water. (general, no "of")
2. Most of my friends… ("my" → add "of")
3. …but none of them came. (pronoun → "of")
4. Some of the students passed. (specific group → "of the")
5. She ate all of the cake. (or "all the cake" — both fine)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Put too before the adjective, but enough after the adjective (and before a noun).
  • Ask "is there a noun after it?" — noun → such (a); no noun → so.
  • Add a for the positive meaning (a few, a little); drop it for the negative (few, little).
  • Add of after all/most/some/none only before the, my, this, or a pronoun.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • enough in the wrong place: "enough big" ❌ → "big enough" ✅.
  • too vs very: "The film was too good" (when you mean you loved it) ❌ → "The film was so good / really good" ✅. Too is a problem, not praise.
  • so + noun: "It was so a nice day" ❌ → "It was such a nice day" ✅.
  • a few vs few: saying "I have few friends" (sad) when you mean "I have a few friends" (fine).
  • most of people: "most of people" ❌ → "most people" ✅ (general, no of).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • too = more than you want (before an adjective); enough = the right amount (after an adjective, before a noun). too much (uncountable) / too many (countable).
  • so + adjective/adverb; such (a) + (adjective) noun — both continue with … that + result.
  • a few / a little = positive (some); few / little = negative (not many/much). Use few for countable, little for uncountable.
  • all / most / some / none take of before the, my, this, or a pronoun — but not before a general noun.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now measure the world in English with real precision — how hot is too hot, whether you have enough, how to say something was so good you did it twice, and the delicate difference between a few problems and few problems. These are exactly the touches that make your English sound expressive and exact rather than approximate. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the difference between "too" and "very"?

Very just makes an adjective stronger, with no judgment: "It's very hot" (a fact). Too means more than is acceptable — it signals a problem: "It's too hot" (so hot that I'm uncomfortable / can't do something). If it's a good thing, use very or so, never too.

How do I remember whether it's "so" or "such"?

Look for a noun. If a noun follows, use such (with a/an for a singular noun): "such a nice house." If there's no noun — just an adjective or adverb — use so: "so nice," "so quickly." One quick test answers it every time.

Do people really use "few" and "little" without "a" in real speech?

In conversation, the negative few / little can sound a bit formal, so speakers often prefer "not many / not much" or "hardly any." But you'll meet few and little often in writing and more careful speech, so it's important to recognise the negative meaning even if you sometimes choose a simpler phrase yourself.
