🛠️ Lesson 7.2: Complaints & Getting Things Fixed

Sooner or later, something goes wrong: the coffee's cold, the delivery never arrives, you're charged twice, the room is dirty. Knowing how to complain in English — politely and effectively — is one of the most useful real-world skills there is. And here's the surprising part: the more polite and indirect you sound, the more likely you are to get the problem solved. This lesson gives you the phrases to describe a problem, complain kindly, ask for action, and handle a complaint if you're on the other side of the counter.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe a problem clearly (There's a problem with…, It doesn't work, I was charged twice)
  • Open a complaint politely (I'm sorry to bother you, but… / I'd like to make a complaint)
  • Ask for action indirectly (Could you…? Would it be possible to…?)
  • Respond to a complaint helpfully — apologise and offer a solution

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Role-play a complaint and its resolution — as the customer and as the helpful staff member.

Why Polite Complaints Work

When something goes wrong, it's tempting to be blunt: "This is terrible! I want my money back!" But in English, shouting or being aggressive usually makes staff defensive — and less willing to help. The polite, indirect complainer nearly always gets the better result, because they make it easy for the other person to say yes.

The secret is register — the level of formality and directness you choose. A polite complaint separates the problem from the person: it's not "you did something wrong," it's "there seems to be a problem, and I'd be grateful if you could help." That small shift keeps everyone calm and gets things fixed faster.

📖 A polite complaint has three parts

  • Soften the opening — "I'm sorry to bother you, but…"
  • Describe the problem — "…there's a problem with my order."
  • Ask for action — "Could you look into it for me?"

🧠 Mindset

Complaining in another language can feel intimidating — you're upset and you have to find the right words. So learn the phrases before you need them. When you have "I'm afraid there's been a mistake" ready on your tongue, you can stay calm and clear even when you're frustrated. Being polite isn't being weak — it's being effective. Kindness gets refunds. 🌱

Describing the Problem

Before you can ask for a fix, you need to say clearly what's wrong. Here are the most useful ways to describe common problems.

PhraseWhen to use it
There's a problem with…the all-purpose opener
It doesn't work. / It's not working.a device or appliance
It's faulty. / It's broken.a damaged product
It's the wrong size / colour / item.you received the wrong thing
I was charged twice. / I was overcharged.a billing error
It hasn't arrived yet.a late or missing delivery
It's not what I ordered.a mistake in your order

💡 The passive is your polite friend

Notice "I was charged twice" rather than "You charged me twice." The passive lets you describe the problem without blaming a person directly — which keeps the conversation friendly. Other examples: "My card was declined," "The parcel was left outside," "The room hasn't been cleaned."

There's a problem with… It doesn't work. It's faulty. I was charged twice. It hasn't arrived yet. It's not what I ordered.

Polite Complaint Openers

The first thing you say sets the tone for everything that follows. A soft, respectful opener signals "I'm reasonable — please help me," and it almost always gets a warmer response.

OpenerRegister / situation
Excuse me, I'm sorry to bother you, but…polite, in person
I'm afraid there's been a mistake.gentle, softened with I'm afraid
I'd like to make a complaint.more formal / official
I'm not very happy with…clear but calm
I'm writing to complain about…for an email or letter
I wonder if you could help me.very polite, indirect

✅ "I'm afraid" softens bad news

I'm afraid doesn't mean you're frightened! It's a polite cushion that warns "something you won't like is coming" — for a complaint or a refusal. "I'm afraid there's been a mistake with my bill." / "I'm afraid the food was cold." It makes even a firm complaint sound reasonable.

⚠️ Register matters — match it to the situation

With a friend or a market stall, "There's a problem with this — can you swap it?" is fine. In a hotel, a bank, or a formal email, reach for the softer, fuller forms: "I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid there's been a mistake…" Too casual in a formal setting sounds rude; too formal with a friend sounds cold. Read the room.

Asking for Action

Describing the problem isn't enough — you have to say what you want done. But a direct order ("Fix it!" / "Give me a refund!") sounds aggressive. Instead, use indirect question forms, which are the heart of polite English requests.

RequestHow polite?
Could you…? / Can you…?polite, everyday
Would it be possible to…?very polite, indirect
Would you mind + -ing…?very polite (answer "not at all")
I'd appreciate it if you could…formal, quite firm but courteous
I'd like a refund / a replacement, please.clear statement of what you want
Do you think you could…?soft, tentative

Blunt: "Give me my money back. This is broken."

Polite & effective: "I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid this is faulty. Would it be possible to get a refund, or could you replace it?"

Same request — but the second version makes it easy for the staff member to help you gladly.

💡 The "polite ladder"

The longer and more indirect the phrase, the more polite it sounds. From softest to most direct: Would it be possible to… > Could you… > Can you… > I want… When in doubt, climb one rung higher up the ladder — it rarely does any harm.

Responding to a Complaint

One day you might be on the other side — working in a shop, a hotel, or answering the phone. Handling a complaint well means apologising sincerely and offering a solution quickly. These phrases calm an unhappy customer instantly.

FunctionPhrase
ApologiseI'm terribly sorry about that. / I do apologise.
Show you understandI completely understand. / I see the problem.
Take responsibilityI'll sort that out for you right away.
Offer a solutionWe'll give you a refund / a replacement.
Ask for detailCould you tell me exactly what happened?
ReassureDon't worry, we'll fix this.

✅ Apologise + Act

The winning combination is always apology + action: "I'm terribly sorry about that — I'll sort it out right away." An apology alone can feel empty; an action alone can feel cold. Together they turn an angry customer into a happy one.

I'm terribly sorry about that. I do apologise. I'll sort that out right away. We'll give you a refund. Don't worry, we'll fix this.

A Complaint in Action

Here's a customer complaining about a hotel room — and a helpful receptionist handling it beautifully. Watch how both stay polite, and how the problem gets solved in just a few lines.

Guest: Excuse me, I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid there's a problem with my room.

Receptionist: Oh, I'm sorry to hear that. Could you tell me exactly what's wrong?

Guest: The heating doesn't work, and the room hasn't been cleaned properly. There are towels missing too.

Receptionist: I do apologise — that's not the standard we aim for. I'll sort that out right away.

Guest: Thank you. Would it be possible to move to another room?

Receptionist: Of course. I'll upgrade you to a room on the top floor at no extra charge. And I'll send someone up with fresh towels immediately.

Guest: That's very kind, thank you. I'd appreciate it if you could do it soon — it's quite late.

Receptionist: Absolutely. Here's your new key — room 802. Again, I'm terribly sorry for the inconvenience, and please let me know if there's anything else you need.

🎯 Your Project: Role-Play a Complaint & Resolution

Act out a complete complaint from both sides. Work with a partner if you can; alone, play both roles or record yourself.

  1. (3 min) Choose a situation: a cold meal in a restaurant, a faulty item in a shop, a problem hotel room, or a delivery that never came.
  2. (4 min) As the customer, plan your three parts: a polite opener, a clear problem, and a polite request for action.
  3. (4 min) As the staff member, plan how you'll apologise and offer a solution.
  4. (4 min) Act it out. Keep it calm and polite throughout — no shouting, even when you're "annoyed."
  5. (2 min) Swap roles and do a different situation, so you practise both making and handling a complaint.

Role-play starter: Customer: "I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid there's a problem with…" Staff: "I'm terribly sorry about that. Could you tell me exactly what happened?"

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Soften the opening before you say what's wrong — it sets a friendly tone.
  • Use the passive to describe problems without blaming ("I was charged twice").
  • Ask for action with indirect questions (Could you…? Would it be possible to…?).
  • If you're handling a complaint, always pair an apology with an action.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Being too direct: "Give me a refund!" ❌ → "Would it be possible to get a refund?" ✅.
  • Blaming the person: "You broke it" ❌ → "I'm afraid it's not working" ✅.
  • "I'm afraid" misread: it's a polite cushion, not real fear — use it freely for bad news.
  • Forgetting to ask: describing the problem isn't enough — always say what you want done.
  • Apology with no action (as staff): "Sorry" alone isn't enough — add "I'll sort it out."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Describe the problem clearly: There's a problem with…, It doesn't work, It's faulty, I was charged twice, It hasn't arrived yet.
  • Open politely: I'm sorry to bother you, but…, I'm afraid there's been a mistake, I'd like to make a complaint.
  • Ask for action indirectly: Could you…?, Would it be possible to…?, I'd appreciate it if you could….
  • Respond well (as staff) with apology + action: I'm terribly sorry — I'll sort that out right away, We'll give you a refund.
  • Register matters: the more polite and indirect you are, the more likely the problem gets fixed.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

If I'm really angry, isn't being polite dishonest?

No — being polite isn't hiding your feelings; it's choosing the tone most likely to get results. You can be firm and polite: "I'm afraid I'm not happy with this, and I'd like a refund." Politeness keeps the other person on your side, which is exactly what you want.

What if the staff member is rude back or won't help?

Stay calm and escalate politely: "I understand, but I'd like to speak to the manager, please." Keeping your tone reasonable makes you look better and often gets a better outcome. In writing, "I'm writing to complain about…" and a clear request work well.

Are complaints by email different from in person?

The three parts are the same (open, problem, request), but emails are usually a little more formal: "I'm writing to complain about…", "I would appreciate it if you could…", and a clear statement of what you want (a refund, a replacement, an explanation). Being specific — dates, order numbers — helps a lot.
