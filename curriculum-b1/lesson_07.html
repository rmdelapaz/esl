💬 Lesson 3.1: Reported Speech — Statements

Every time you tell a friend what someone else said — "She said she was tired," "He told me he'd call later" — you're using reported speech. It's one of the most useful skills in real conversation, and once you understand the pattern behind it, you'll be able to pass on anyone's words with confidence.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Change direct speech into reported statements
  • Apply the tense backshift (present → past, will → would, and so on)
  • Change pronouns and time/place references correctly
  • Choose confidently between say and tell

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Project: Report six or more real things people actually said to you recently — a friend, a family member, the news — using reported speech.

Direct vs Reported

There are two ways to pass on someone's words. Direct speech quotes them exactly, inside quotation marks. Reported speech (also called indirect speech) puts their meaning into your own sentence, with no quotation marks.

Direct speech (exact words)Reported speech (your sentence)
He said, "I am tired."He said (that) he was tired.
She said, "We live in Rome."She said (that) they lived in Rome.

Notice three things change when we report: the little word that can appear, the tense moves back (am → was), and the pronouns change (I → he, we → they). We'll take each one in turn.

📖 About the word "that"

The linking word that is optional. Both of these are perfectly correct:

He said that he was tired. — He said he was tired.

In everyday speaking we usually drop it; in writing we often keep it. Either way, the grammar is the same.

🧠 Mindset

Reported speech can look like a huge list of changes to memorise — but it isn't. It's really one idea (step everything back in time and out of "my" point of view) applied consistently. Don't try to swallow the whole table at once. Learn the logic, then let the details settle through use. Every time you tell a friend "she said…", you're practising it for free. 🌱

The Tense Backshift

This is the heart of the lesson. When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told), the tense inside the quote usually moves one step back into the past. This is called the backshift.

Direct speech→ becomes →Reported speech
present simple
"I work hard."		→ past simpleHe said he worked hard.
present continuous
"I am working."		→ past continuousHe said he was working.
present perfect
"I have finished."		→ past perfectHe said he had finished.
past simple
"I saw her."		→ past perfectHe said he had seen her.
will
"I will help."		→ wouldHe said he would help.
can
"I can swim."		→ couldHe said he could swim.
may
"I may come."		→ mightHe said he might come.
must
"I must go."		→ had toHe said he had to go.

Think of it as pressing "rewind" once on the verb. A quick way to picture the direction:

graph LR
    A["present simple / continuous"] --> B["past simple / continuous"]
    B --> C["past perfect"]
    D["present perfect"] --> C
    E["will / can / may / must"] --> F["would / could / might / had to"]

📖 A few tidy details

  • would, could, might, should don't change — they're already back-shifted forms. "I would love to." → She said she would love to.
  • Past perfect can't step back any further, so it stays. "I had eaten." → He said he had eaten.

💡 When backshift is optional

Backshift is normal when the reporting verb is past (said, told). But if the reporting verb is present ("He says he is tired") or the statement is still true right now ("She said she lives in Rome" — and she still does), you can leave the tense unchanged. Keep this light for now: when in doubt with said/told, backshift — it's always safe.

Pronoun Changes

When you report someone's words, you're no longer speaking from their point of view — you're speaking from yours. So the pronouns shift to match who is actually talking now.

Direct (their words)Reported (your words)
"I am ready."He said he was ready.
"We are leaving."They said they were leaving.
"This is my bag."She said it was her bag.
"You are late." (to me)He said I was late.
"Our car broke down."They said their car had broken down.

✅ Quick test

Ask yourself: "From where I'm standing now, who is this person?" If your friend said "I", then to you they are he or she. If they said "you" and meant you, that becomes I. Picture who is pointing at whom, and the pronoun follows naturally.

Time & Place Changes

Because reported speech usually looks back at a moment that has passed, words that mean "now" and "here" often shift too. If your friend said "I'll call you tomorrow" on Monday and you report it on Wednesday, "tomorrow" is no longer tomorrow — it was the next day.

Direct→Reported
now→then
today→that day
tomorrow→the next day / the following day
yesterday→the day before
here→there
this→that
ago→before

Direct: She said, "I saw this film yesterday, and I'll watch another one tomorrow."

Reported: She said she had seen that film the day before, and she would watch another one the next day.

⚠️ Only when it matters: Change time and place words only when the change reflects reality. If you report something on the same day, "today" can stay "today" and "here" can stay "here". These shifts are logical, not mechanical — match them to when and where you're actually speaking.

Say vs Tell

These two verbs both introduce reported speech, but they behave differently — and mixing them up is the single most common error in this whole area. The rule is short:

⚠️ The key card: TELL needs a person

TELL must be followed by a person (the listener): She told me…, He told his boss…

SAY does not take a person straight after it: She said…, He said that…

So:

  • "She said me…" ❌  →  "She told me…" ✅  or  "She said…" ✅
  • "She told to me…" ❌  →  "She told me…" ✅  (no to!)
VerbCorrect patternExample
saysay (that) + statementShe said (that) she was busy.
telltell + someone + (that) + statementShe told me (that) she was busy.
say + tosay + to someone (less common)She said to me she was busy.
She said (that)… He told me (that)… They said they… My mum told me… The news said (that)…

✅ Memory hook

You tell someone something — tell always wants a listener. You just say something — say is happy on its own. If a person's name or "me/him/her" comes next, reach for tell.

Putting It Together

Reported speech is the language of gossip, news and everyday storytelling. Watch how a direct conversation turns into a report. First, the original:

Sam: I'm moving to Berlin next month. I've found a great flat.

Alex: Really? I didn't know that! I'll visit you there.

Sam: Please do. My sister lives there too — she can show you around.

Now here's Alex passing it all on to a friend later — the same words, reported:

"You'll never guess — I saw Sam yesterday. He told me he was moving to Berlin the next month, and that he had found a great flat. I said I would visit him there. He said his sister lived there too and could show me around!"

Every change at work: am moving → was moving, have found → had found, will → would, can → could, next month → the next month, plus the pronoun and say/tell choices.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Backshift the tense

Objective: Rewrite each direct statement as reported speech, applying the backshift. Start each with the verb given.

  1. "I am hungry." → He said …
  2. "We are watching TV." → They said …
  3. "I have lost my phone." → She said …
  4. "I saw the accident." → He said …
  5. "I will pay you back." → She said …
  6. "You can borrow my car." → He told me …
  7. "I must leave early." → She said …
✅ Answer

1. He said (that) he was hungry.
2. They said (that) they were watching TV.
3. She said (that) she had lost her phone.
4. He said (that) he had seen the accident.
5. She said (that) she would pay me back.
6. He told me (that) I could borrow his car.
7. She said (that) she had to leave early.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Say or tell?

Objective: Complete each gap with the correct form of say or tell.

  1. She me that she was leaving.
  2. He that the shop was closed.
  3. They us the good news.
  4. My teacher that the test was easy.
  5. Did you him about the party?
  6. The message the train was late.
✅ Answer

1. told (person: me) · 2. said · 3. told (person: us) · 4. said · 5. tell (person: him) · 6. said

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the pronoun & time-word changes

Objective: Each reported sentence has one or more mistakes in its pronouns or time/place words. Correct them. (Assume you are reporting the words later, on a different day.)

  1. Maria said that I was tired. (Maria was talking about herself.)
  2. Tom told me he would call me tomorrow. (He said this last week.)
  3. They said they had arrived here the day before.
  4. She said she had bought this jacket yesterday.
  5. He said that we were his best friends. (He was talking about himself and his brother.)
✅ Answer

1. Maria said that she was tired.
2. Tom told me he would call me the next day.
3. They said they had arrived there the day before.
4. She said she had bought that jacket the day before.
5. He said that they were his best friends.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Do the changes in a fixed order: (1) tense back one step, (2) pronouns to your viewpoint, (3) time/place words if reality changed.
  • Use tell when you want to name the listener; use say when you don't.
  • Remember would, could, might, should and the past perfect don't move — they're already back.
  • Feel free to drop that in speaking; keep it if it helps clarity.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • say + person: "She said me…" ❌ → "She told me…" ✅ or "She said…" ✅
  • tell + to: "He told to me…" ❌ → "He told me…" ✅
  • Forgetting the backshift: "He said he is tired" (as a report of "I am tired") → "He said he was tired." ✅
  • Pronoun left unchanged: reporting "I'm busy" as "She said I was busy" ❌ → "She said she was busy." ✅
  • Over-changing time words: don't force "today → that day" when you're reporting on the same day.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Reported speech puts someone's words into your own sentence; the linking word that is optional.
  • After a past reporting verb, the tense back-shifts one step: present → past, present perfect / past simple → past perfect, will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to.
  • Pronouns change to match your point of view (I → he/she, we → they, my → his/her).
  • Time/place words shift when reality does: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, here → there, this → that, ago → before.
  • TELL needs a person (told me, no to); SAY doesn't (said).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now take anyone's words and pass them on accurately — the everyday skill behind gossip, news, retelling a conversation, and reporting a message. That's a big step in sounding like a natural, connected speaker. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I always have to back-shift the tense?

No. Backshift is normal and always safe with a past reporting verb (said/told). But if the reporting verb is present ("He says he's tired") or the fact is still true now ("She said she lives in Rome", and she still does), you can leave the tense as it is. When unsure with said/told, back-shift.

Is "that" ever wrong to include?

No — it's always grammatically fine after say and tell someone. It's simply optional, and we usually drop it in casual speech. The one thing to avoid is putting it after tell without a person: "told that…" ❌ needs a listener — "told me that…" ✅.

What if the person is quoting a permanent fact, like "The sun rises in the east"?

General truths often stay in the present because they're still true: "He said the sun rises in the east." Back-shifting it ("…rose in the east") isn't wrong either, but keeping the present sounds more natural for timeless facts.
