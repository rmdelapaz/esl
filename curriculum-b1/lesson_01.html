⏱️ Lesson 1.1: Present Perfect vs Past Simple

Welcome to B1! You already know both of these tenses on their own. The skill that marks an independent speaker is knowing which one to choose — and why. This lesson gives you that judgement, so your English starts to sound natural instead of just correct.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Decide when to use the present perfect and when to use the past simple
  • Use for and since to talk about unfinished time
  • Use already, yet, just, and still naturally
  • Move smoothly from a present-perfect opener into past-simple details in a conversation

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Talk (or record yourself) for two minutes about an experience or achievement — open with the present perfect, then give the details in the past simple.

Two Tenses, One Choice

In A2 you met the present perfect (I have visited Rome) and the past simple (I visited Rome in 2019) as separate tools. At B1, they stop being separate and start competing: for almost any sentence about the past, your brain has to pick one. That choice is one of the biggest things that separates an intermediate speaker from a beginner.

Here's the good news: the choice follows a clear logic. It isn't about how long ago something happened — it's about whether you are connecting the past to now, or placing it in finished time. Once that clicks, dozens of "confusing" sentences suddenly make sense.

🧠 Mindset

If you've been making "mistakes" with these tenses for a while, don't read that as failure — read it as a sign you're now attempting the real subtlety of English. Native speakers make this choice by feel; you're going to make it by understanding, which is just as good and often more reliable. Plateaus like this one are where the biggest gains hide. 🌱

The Core Difference

Learn this one idea and everything else follows:

📖 The key idea

Past simple = a finished action at a finished, specific time. The time is over and often stated (yesterday, in 2019, last week, two hours ago).

Present perfect = a past action connected to now — the time is unfinished or unimportant. We care about the result or experience, not the exact "when".

Present Perfect (connected to now)Past Simple (finished time)
I have lost my keys. (and I still can't find them now)I lost my keys yesterday. (finished time: yesterday)
She has lived here for ten years. (she still lives here)She lived there in 2015. (not any more)
Have you ever eaten sushi? (in your life up to now)Did you eat sushi at the party? (that finished event)
We have just arrived. (result: we're here now)We arrived at 8 o'clock. (stated time)

⚠️ The golden rule: If you say when (a finished time — yesterday, last year, at 6 p.m., in 2010), you must use the past simple. You cannot say "I have seen him yesterday." ❌ → "I saw him yesterday." ✅

For & Since

These two little words are present-perfect signals. They describe a situation that started in the past and is still true now — exactly what the present perfect is for.

WordUsed withMeaningExample
fora period of timehow longI've known her for ten years / for a long time.
sincea point in timethe starting momentI've known her since 2014 / since I was a child.

✅ Quick test

Ask yourself: is it a length (two hours, a week, ages) or a start point (Monday, 9 a.m., last year)? Length → for. Start point → since. Answer the question "How long have you…?" with one of them: "I've worked here for three years." / "…since 2021."

⚠️ Important Note: With for/since + present perfect, we mean the action continues now. Compare: "I lived in Madrid for two years" (past simple = I don't live there now) vs "I have lived in Madrid for two years" (present perfect = I still live there).

Already, Yet, Just & Still

These adverbs live naturally with the present perfect and make your speech sound fluent and current.

AdverbMeaningPositionExample
justa very short time agobefore the past participleI've just finished lunch.
alreadysooner than expected (positive)before the past participleShe's already left.
yetup to now (questions & negatives)at the endHave you eaten yet? / I haven't eaten yet.
stillcontinuing / not happened (surprise)before haven'tI still haven't finished.
I've just got home. Have you finished yet? She's already done it. They haven't replied yet. I still haven't packed.

How They Work Together

Here's the pattern that makes you sound like a real speaker: open with the present perfect (to introduce a topic or experience), then switch to the past simple for the specific details.

"I've been to Japan, actually. I went in 2019 with two friends. We spent ten days there and visited Tokyo and Kyoto. It was amazing."

Notice: present perfect to open the topic (no time) → past simple once we start giving the finished details.

Listen for this shape in a real conversation:

A: Have you ever run a marathon?

B: Yes, I have! I ran one last year in Berlin.

A: Wow. How was it?

B: Really hard, but great. I trained for six months before it.

A: I've never done anything like that. I've just started going to the gym, though!

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. Notice how the present perfect links the past to now, while the past simple points to a finished time.

  • I have visited Paris three times, but I went there last in 2019.
  • She has already finished her homework, so she watched a film.
  • Have you ever eaten sushi, or did you try it on your trip?
  • We have lived here for ten years, and we moved from London.
  • He hasn't called me today, but he called me yesterday.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Scan for a finished time word first — if it's there, use the past simple, no exceptions.
  • Use the present perfect for experiences and news ("I've broken my phone!") where the result matters now.
  • Open a topic with the present perfect, then drop into the past simple for the story.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Present perfect + finished time: "I have seen her last week" ❌ → "I saw her last week" ✅.
  • for vs since: "since three years" ❌ → "for three years" ✅; "for 2019" ❌ → "since 2019" ✅.
  • Past simple for unfinished time: "I live here for five years" ❌ → "I've lived here for five years" ✅ (you still live here).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Past simple = finished action at a finished/specific time; present perfect = past connected to now.
  • A finished time word (yesterday, in 2019, ago) forces the past simple.
  • for + a period, since + a start point — both signal the present perfect for situations still true now.
  • just, already, yet, still live with the present perfect; open with it, then switch to the past simple for details.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've taken two tenses you already knew and learned to choose between them with real judgement. That single skill will quietly improve almost everything you say about the past — and it's exactly the kind of control that defines an independent B1 speaker. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is the present perfect for things that happened "recently"?

Not exactly — recency is a hint, not the rule. The real test is connection to now and no finished time word. "I saw her five minutes ago" is still past simple, because "ago" states a finished time.

American speakers sometimes use the past simple where I'd use the present perfect. Who's right?

Both are accepted. American English often prefers the past simple with just/already/yet ("Did you eat yet?"), while British English prefers the present perfect ("Have you eaten yet?"). Learn the present-perfect version — it's safe everywhere — and don't worry if you hear both.

Why "I've been to Japan" and not "I've gone to Japan"?

Been = went and came back (an experience). Gone = went and is still there. "She's gone to Japan" means she's there now; "She's been to Japan" means she visited at some point.
