💼 Lesson 7.3: Work & Interviews — Describing Your Experience

"Tell me about yourself." Few sentences make people more nervous — but with the right phrases ready, a job interview becomes a chance to shine rather than a test to survive. In this listening and speaking lesson you'll learn how to talk about your job, your experience, and your skills, and how to answer the classic interview questions with strong, confident, natural English.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe your job and responsibilities (I'm responsible for…, I've been working as…)
  • Talk about your experience and skills (I have experience in…, I'm good at…)
  • Use key work vocabulary (apply for, position, colleague, deadline, strengths)
  • Answer common interview questions with strong, structured answers

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Run a mock job interview — playing both the interviewer and the candidate.

Talking About Work

Whether you're chatting at a party, meeting new colleagues, or sitting in an interview, you'll often need to explain what you do. English has a set of natural phrases for this, and many of them lean on grammar you already know — especially the present perfect from Module 1 (I've been working…, I've done…), which is perfect for connecting your past experience to now.

This lesson has two halves. First, the everyday language for describing your job, experience, and skills. Then, the special situation of the job interview — the classic questions and how to answer them well. By the end you'll be able to talk about work confidently in any setting.

📖 Present perfect — your work best friend

Use it to link past experience to the present:

  • "I've been working as a nurse for five years." (started in the past, still true now)
  • "I've worked in three different companies." (life experience up to now)

Remember for + a length of time, since + a starting point: "since 2020."

🧠 Mindset

In an interview, you're not bragging — you're informing. Many learners feel shy about saying "I'm good at…", but an interview is exactly the place to say it clearly and calmly. Prepare a few strong phrases about your skills and practise them out loud until they feel natural. Confidence isn't pretending; it's being ready. The more you rehearse, the calmer you'll be when it counts. 🌱

Describing Your Experience

Here are the core phrases for talking about your job, your duties, and what you're good at. Notice how they let you sound professional without complicated grammar.

PhraseUse it to…Example
I work as a… / I'm a…state your jobI work as a graphic designer.
I've been working as… (for / since)say how longI've been working as a teacher for six years.
I'm responsible for…describe your dutiesI'm responsible for managing the team.
My job involves…describe daily tasks (+ -ing)My job involves dealing with customers.
I have experience in…highlight a field (+ noun / -ing)I have experience in sales and marketing.
I'm good at… / I'm skilled at…name a strength (+ noun / -ing)I'm good at solving problems.

⚠️ After these phrases, use -ing or a noun — not the infinitive

responsible for, experience in, good at, involves are all followed by a noun or the -ing form, because for / in / at are prepositions.

  • "I'm responsible for to train" ❌ → "I'm responsible for training new staff." ✅
  • "I'm good at to cook" ❌ → "I'm good at cooking." ✅
I've been working as… I'm responsible for… My job involves… I have experience in… I'm good at…

Work Vocabulary

These words come up constantly at work and in interviews. Learn them as a set — they'll appear again and again.

✅ "Apply for" vs "apply to"

You apply for a job or position, but you apply to a company or organisation: "I applied for the marketing role" and "I applied to three companies." A small preposition, an easy point to get right.

Tip: When you prepare for an interview, read the job advert and underline the skills and responsibilities it mentions. Then use those exact words when you describe yourself — it shows you're a great fit.

Common Interview Questions

Most interviews use a handful of predictable questions. If you prepare answers to these, you'll be ready for the vast majority of what you'll be asked.

QuestionWhat they really want to know
Tell me about yourself.a short, relevant summary — not your life story
Why do you want this job?that you're genuinely interested and a good fit
What are your strengths?your best skills, with an example
What's your biggest weakness?self-awareness — and how you're improving
Where do you see yourself in five years?your ambition and commitment
Do you have any questions for us?your interest — always have one ready!

💡 The "weakness" trick

When asked about a weakness, don't say "I have none" (sounds arrogant) or name something disastrous. Pick a real but small weakness and show you're working on it: "I used to worry too much about details, but I've learned to manage my time better." Turning a weakness into growth is exactly what interviewers want to hear.

Giving Strong Answers

A strong answer isn't longer — it's structured. A great pattern for "Tell me about yourself" is Present → Past → Future: what you do now, what you've done before, and what you're looking for next.

📖 The Present → Past → Future answer

Present: "I'm currently working as a sales assistant."

Past: "I've been in retail for three years, and I have experience in customer service."

Future: "Now I'm looking for a role where I can take on more responsibility — which is why I applied for this position."

For "What are your strengths?", use the pattern Strength + Example — never just name a quality; prove it:

Weak: "I'm a hard worker and I'm good with people."

Strong: "I'm good at working under pressure. For example, in my last job I regularly met tight deadlines, and I still made sure the quality was high."

The example is what makes the interviewer believe you.

Here's the flow of a confident interview answer:

graph LR
    A["Listen to the question"] --> B["State your point clearly"]
    B --> C["Give an example / reason"]
    C --> D["Link it to the job"]

✅ Buy yourself thinking time

You don't have to answer instantly. Polite fillers give you a second to think: "That's a good question…", "Let me think for a moment…", "Well, I'd say…". They sound natural and confident — far better than a panicked silence or a rushed answer.

Listen: A Job Interview

Here's a short interview for a customer-service position. Read it once, then use it as a listening text. Watch how the candidate, Marco, uses the phrases and patterns from this lesson — and how he stays calm and gives examples.

Interviewer: Thanks for coming in, Marco. So, to start — tell me a little about yourself.

Marco: Thank you for having me. Well, I'm currently working as a customer-service assistant, and I've been in that role for about three years. I'm responsible for handling calls and solving customer problems, so I have a lot of experience in dealing with people.

Interviewer: Great. And why do you want this position?

Marco: That's a good question. I really enjoy helping customers, but I'm looking for a role with more responsibility. Your company has a great reputation for training its staff, and I'd love to grow here.

Interviewer: What would you say your main strengths are?

Marco: I'm good at staying calm under pressure. For example, in my last job I often dealt with difficult calls, and I always tried to find a solution rather than get upset. I'm also good at working in a team.

Interviewer: And a weakness?

Marco: Let me think for a moment. I used to take on too much work myself, but I've learned to ask my colleagues for help and to manage deadlines better.

Interviewer: That's good to hear. Do you have any questions for us?

Marco: Yes — could you tell me what a typical day in this role looks like?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use the present perfect to link experience to now: "I've been working as… for…".
  • Answer "Tell me about yourself" with Present → Past → Future, not your life story.
  • Back every strength with a short example — proof beats adjectives.
  • Prepare one question to ask the interviewer, and use fillers to buy thinking time.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Infinitive after prepositions: "good at to cook" ❌ → "good at cooking" ✅.
  • Wrong preposition: "apply to a job" ❌ → "apply for a job" ✅ (but apply to a company).
  • "I have 5 years" alone: say "I've been working here for five years" or "I've had this job for five years."
  • No example: "I'm a hard worker" — add proof: "…for example, I…".
  • "I have no weaknesses": sounds arrogant — name a small one you're improving.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What if I don't have much work experience yet?

Talk about what you do have: studies, volunteering, part-time jobs, projects, or skills. "I don't have professional experience yet, but I've done volunteer work where I was responsible for…" shows initiative. Everyone starts somewhere, and interviewers value attitude and willingness to learn.

Is it OK to say "I'm good at…" — doesn't it sound like bragging?

In an interview, it's expected and appropriate — that's what the interviewer is asking for. It only sounds like bragging if you give no proof. Pair it with an example ("I'm good at organising — for example…") and it sounds confident and honest, not boastful.

How long should my "Tell me about yourself" answer be?

About 30–60 seconds — three or four clear sentences. It's a summary, not your whole life. Use Present → Past → Future, keep it relevant to the job, and stop. A short, focused answer sounds far more professional than a long, rambling one.
