🎤 Lesson 8.3: Review Project — Presentation & Panel Discussion

This is it — your B1 capstone, and the final lesson of the whole course. Instead of learning something new, you'll bring everything together in one satisfying speaking task: give a short presentation on a topic you care about, then take part in a panel discussion about it. Narrative tenses, conditionals, reported speech, discussion and opinion language, question tags, phrasal verbs — it all comes out to play here. Let's finish B1 in style.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Structure and deliver a short presentation with clear signposting
  • Take part in a panel discussion, agreeing, disagreeing, and responding
  • Listen to a talk and a discussion, and check your understanding
  • Combine the grammar and language of the whole B1 level in real speech

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60–70 minutes

🎯 Project: Prepare and deliver a 1–2 minute spoken presentation on a topic you care about, then take part in (or imagine) a discussion about it.

How to Structure a Presentation

A good short presentation has the same simple shape as a good paragraph: an introduction, a body of two or three points, and a conclusion. Say what you'll talk about, talk about it, then say what you said.

📖 The three-part shape

  1. Introduction — greet, name your topic, say why it matters. "Today I'd like to talk about…"
  2. Body — two or three clear points, each signposted. "First… / Another point is… / Finally…"
  3. Conclusion — sum up and finish. "To sum up… / Thank you for listening."

Here's the flow in one picture — three points held between a clear opening and a clear close:

graph LR
    A["Introduction: name the topic"] --> B["Point 1"]
    B --> C["Point 2"]
    C --> D["Point 3 / optional"]
    D --> E["Conclusion: sum up"]

✅ Keep it small and clear

One to two minutes is short — that's on purpose. Two solid points, well signposted, beat five rushed ones. Pick a topic you genuinely care about (a hobby, a place, a change you'd like to see) and your enthusiasm will carry you.

Signposting Language

Signposting means using set phrases to guide your listener through your talk — like road signs telling them where you are and where you're going. These phrases make you sound organised and confident, and they give you a moment to think.

StageSignposting phrases
StartingToday I'd like to talk about… / I'm going to tell you about…
First pointFirst of all… / To begin with… / The first thing is…
AddingAnother point is… / Secondly… / What's more…
ExamplesFor example… / For instance… / Let me give you an example.
ConcludingTo sum up… / In conclusion… / So, to finish…
Today I'd like to talk about… First of all,… Another point is that… For example,… To sum up,… Thank you for listening.
💡 Tip: Learn three or four signposting phrases really well and use them every time. They act like a frame that holds your ideas in place — so even when you're nervous, your talk still sounds clear and structured.

Taking Part in a Discussion

After a presentation comes the discussion — a panel where people respond, agree, disagree, and ask questions. This is where the opinion language from Module 7 shines. The golden rule: react to what others say, don't just wait for your turn.

📖 Agreeing, disagreeing & responding

Agreeing: That's a good point. / I completely agree. / Exactly.

Disagreeing (politely): I see your point, but… / I'm not sure I agree. / Yes, however…

Giving your view: In my opinion… / I think… / If you ask me…

Bringing others in: What do you think? / You agree, don't you?

⚠️ Disagree with the idea, not the person

In English, we soften disagreement so it stays friendly. Start with something positive, then add your view: "That's an interesting point, but I'm not sure it works in every case." Avoid a flat "No, you're wrong" — it sounds harsh. A little cushioning keeps a discussion warm and productive.

That's a really good point. I see what you mean, but… I'm not sure I agree, to be honest. If you ask me,… What's your view on that?

Listening: A Talk & a Panel

Before you prepare your own, listen to a model. Below is a short talk followed by a small panel discussion. Read it, then listen using the audio tools, and finally check your understanding with the questions underneath.

Host: Welcome, everyone. Today Maya is going to tell us about cycling to work. Maya, over to you.

Maya: Thank you. Today I'd like to talk about why more of us should cycle to work. First of all, it's healthy. Before I started cycling, I had never felt so full of energy in the mornings. Another point is cost — I gave up my expensive bus pass, and now I save money every week. Finally, if more people cycled, our streets would be cleaner and quieter. To sum up: it's good for you, good for your wallet, and good for the city. Thank you for listening.

Host: Thank you, Maya. Let's open it up. Sam, what do you think?

Sam: That's a really good point about health. I completely agree — I feel much better since I started cycling too. But it's not always safe, is it?

Maya: I see what you mean. However, if cities built more bike lanes, it would be much safer. My colleague told me she had stopped cycling because of traffic, and that's a real shame.

Priya: I'm not sure I agree that everyone can do it, though. What about people who live far away?

Maya: That's fair. For those people, public transport is better. I'm just saying that if you live close enough, cycling is worth trying, isn't it?

Host: A great discussion. Let's sort out the details over coffee, shall we?

🔊 Listen & Repeat

Tap the 🔊 Listen button on the talk above, or read each speaker's lines aloud yourself, then use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. Listen once for the general meaning, then again to hear the signposting (First of all… Another point is… To sum up…), the agreeing and disagreeing phrases, and the question tags. Then repeat Maya's lines as if you were giving the talk.

🎧 Comprehension Questions

1. What three reasons does Maya give for cycling to work?
2. What did Maya give up when she started cycling?
3. What is Sam's main concern?
4. According to Maya, what would make cycling safer?
5. Why does Priya think not everyone can cycle?

Answers: 1. It's healthy, it saves money, and it would make streets cleaner and quieter. · 2. Her expensive bus pass. · 3. That cycling is not always safe. · 4. If cities built more bike lanes. · 5. Because some people live far away from work.

🎯 Your Capstone Project: Present & Discuss

This is your B1 finale. Prepare a short spoken presentation, deliver it, then take part in — or imagine — a discussion about it. Do it with a partner or class if you can; if you're working alone, record yourself and play both roles. First, read the model excerpt below.

Model presentation excerpt:

"Today I'd like to talk about learning a musical instrument. First of all, it's wonderful for the mind. Before I took up the guitar, I had never realised how relaxing practice could be. Another point is that it connects you with people — I get on really well with the friends I met at music class. For example, last month we gave a small concert, and it was fantastic. To sum up, if you have a little free time, learning an instrument is one of the best things you can do. Thank you for listening."

Notice the B1 toolkit at work: signposting (First of all… Another point is… To sum up…), past perfect (had never realised), a phrasal verb (took up, get on with), a gerund (learning), and a first conditional (if you have… is).

  1. (10 min) Choose a topic you care about (a hobby, a place, a change you'd like to see) and plan the three parts: introduction → 2–3 points → conclusion.
  2. (10 min) Write notes (not a full script). Build in signposting (First… Another point is… To sum up…) and aim to use at least four different B1 structures (a conditional, a phrasal verb, reported speech, past perfect, a question tag…).
  3. (5 min) Deliver your 1–2 minute presentation aloud. Speak clearly and don't rush.
  4. (10 min) Hold the discussion. Invite (or imagine) two or three questions or comments, and respond using the agreeing / disagreeing language — "That's a good point, but…"
  5. (5 min) If you recorded it, listen back. Note one thing you did well and one thing to improve. Celebrate finishing — this is your B1 capstone!

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My presentation had a clear introduction, body, and conclusion
  • ☐ I used signposting phrases (First… / Another point is… / To sum up…)
  • ☐ I used at least four different B1 structures
  • ☐ I spoke for 1–2 minutes without rushing
  • ☐ I took part in a discussion, agreeing and disagreeing politely
  • ☐ I reflected on one strength and one thing to improve

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use notes, not a full script — it keeps you sounding natural and looking up.
  • Anchor your talk with three or four signposting phrases you know well.
  • In the discussion, acknowledge others first, then add your view.
  • Speak slowly and clearly — a calm pace sounds more confident than a rushed one.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Reading word for word: a memorised script sounds flat — use bullet notes instead.
  • No structure: jumping between ideas — signpost each point so listeners can follow.
  • Blunt disagreement: "No, that's wrong" ❌ → "I see your point, but…" ✅.
  • Rushing: speed hides your good English — slow down and breathe.
  • Trying to be perfect: communication matters more than a flawless sentence — keep going if you slip.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A short presentation has three parts: introduction → 2–3 points → conclusion.
  • Signposting phrases (First of all… Another point is… To sum up…) guide your listener and steady your nerves.
  • In a panel discussion, acknowledge others first, then agree or disagree politely and give your view.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

I still make mistakes when I speak. Am I really at B1 level?

Yes. B1 has never meant "no mistakes" — it means you can communicate independently about familiar topics, handle most everyday situations, and express opinions and plans. Making mistakes while still getting your message across is B1 in action. Fluency and accuracy keep growing together at B2; the mistakes shrink naturally with more practice.

How can I keep my English strong after finishing the course?

Use it a little every day: listen to podcasts or watch shows in English, read something you enjoy, keep a short journal, and speak whenever you can — even to yourself. Revisit the lessons that felt hardest. The single best habit is regular, low-pressure exposure. You've built the foundation; now keep it warm.

Am I ready to move up to B2?

If you can give this capstone presentation and discuss it — even imperfectly — you're ready to start B2. B2 (Upper-Intermediate) will stretch you toward more natural, nuanced, and detailed English, but it builds directly on everything you've done here. Trust the progress you've made.
