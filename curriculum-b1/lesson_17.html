🔧 Lesson 6.2: Gerunds & Infinitives — Verb Patterns & Meaning

When one verb follows another, English makes a choice: I enjoy swimming but I want to swim. Why -ing in one and to in the other? At A2 you met the basics; now you'll build a real command of these verb patterns — including the intriguing verbs whose meaning changes depending on which form you pick. Master this and your English will sound far more natural and precise.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use verb + -ing and verb + to-infinitive after the right verbs
  • Use verb + object + to-infinitive (tell someone to do)
  • Use -ing after prepositions and as the subject of a sentence
  • Handle the meaning-change verbs — stop, remember, forget, try

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–60 minutes

Two Verbs Together

Very often we want to put two verbs side by side: enjoy + read, want + go, decide + stay. When that happens, the second verb has to take one of two forms:

📖 The two forms

The gerund (-ing): the verb acting like a noun — swimming, reading, working.

The to-infinitive: to + base verb — to swim, to read, to work.

Which one you use depends mostly on the first verb. Some first verbs demand -ing, others demand to, and a few allow either.

At A2 you learned the everyday pairs — I like reading, I want to go. At B1 we widen the net: more verbs, new structures (-ing after prepositions and as a subject), and the special verbs that switch meaning when you switch the form. Here's the shape of the whole lesson:

🧠 Mindset

There is no single rule that tells you "this verb takes -ing, that one takes to" — it's mostly a matter of learning the common verbs in groups and hearing them often. That might sound like bad news, but it's actually freeing: you don't have to work anything out, you just have to notice and collect. Learn the frequent verbs, meet them in real sentences, and the patterns settle in through use. Progress here comes from exposure, not from stress. 🌱

Verb + -ing

Some verbs are always followed by the -ing form. A useful group to memorise covers likes, dislikes, and the "start/stop/continue" family:

Verb + -ingExample
enjoyI enjoy cooking at the weekend.
avoidShe avoids driving in the city.
finishHave you finished reading it?
suggestHe suggested going out for dinner.
keepShe keeps forgetting her keys.
mindDo you mind waiting a moment?

Other common -ing verbs worth adding to your collection: admit, imagine, practise, miss, can't stand, feel like, give up, spend time.

💡 A memory trick for the -ing group

Many -ing verbs are about experiences and reactions — enjoying, avoiding, not minding, finishing, giving up. Think of the -ing form as the "activity itself," pictured as a whole: "I enjoy swimming" = the experience of swimming pleases me. That mental picture won't be perfect, but it helps many of these feel natural.

📌 Note: This builds directly on the basic -ing / to patterns from A2. The verbs are new, but the shape — first verb + second verb in -ing — is one you've already seen with like, love, and hate.

Verb + to-infinitive

A second big group of verbs is followed by the to-infinitive. Many of these look forward — to decisions, hopes, plans, and promises:

Verb + toExample
wantI want to travel more.
decideWe decided to stay at home.
hopeShe hopes to pass the exam.
promiseHe promised to help me.
learnI'm learning to drive.
offerThey offered to pay for dinner.
refuseShe refused to answer.
manageWe managed to finish on time.

More to collect: agree, plan, expect, would like, need, seem, prepare, arrange, afford.

💡 A memory trick for the "to" group

Many to-verbs point forward in time — you want, hope, plan, decide, promise, offer to do something in the future. The little arrow of to matches the forward-looking meaning. Again, it's not a rule you can rely on completely, but it's a helpful nudge for a lot of these verbs.

Verb + Object + to

Some verbs need a person (object) in the middle before the to-infinitive. The pattern is verb + someone + to do. These are the verbs of asking, telling, wanting, and advising — where you're getting another person to do something.

Pattern: verb + object + toExample
want someone toI want you to listen carefully.
tell someone toShe told him to wait outside.
ask someone toHe asked me to help him.
advise someone toThe doctor advised her to rest.

More in this group: allow, expect, encourage, remind, invite, warn, teach, persuade.

⚠️ Don't drop the person after tell / ask

These verbs need the object. Compare:

  • ❌ She told to wait.  →  ✅ She told him to wait.
  • ❌ He asked to help him. (changes the meaning — now he wants to help)  →  ✅ He asked me to help him.

This connects to the say/tell work from earlier: tell always wants a listener, and here it wants one before the to-infinitive too.

-ing After Prepositions & as Subject

Two more places always take the -ing form — and they trip learners up because there's no other verb before them signalling the choice.

After a preposition

When a verb comes straight after a preposition (at, in, of, about, before, after, without, instead of), it must be -ing. A preposition is never followed by to + base verb.

Preposition phraseExample
good atShe's good at drawing.
interested inI'm interested in learning Italian.
before / afterAfter finishing work, I went home.
instead ofWe walked instead of taking the bus.
tired ofHe's tired of waiting.

⚠️ The famous "to" trap

In some fixed phrases, to is a preposition, not part of an infinitive — so it takes -ing:

  • I'm looking forward to seeing you. (not "to see")
  • I'm used to getting up early. (not "to get")

Test: can you put a noun after to? "looking forward to the trip" works, so this to is a preposition → use -ing.

As the subject of a sentence

When an action is the subject — the thing the sentence is about — English uses the -ing form:

Swimming is good for you.

Learning a language takes time.

Getting up early is hard in winter.

Here the -ing word behaves like a noun and sits at the front as the subject.

I'm good at cooking. Thanks for helping me. Reading is my favourite hobby. I look forward to meeting you. Before leaving, check the door.

Verbs That Change Meaning

Here's the most fascinating part. A small set of verbs can take either form — but the meaning changes completely depending on which you choose. These are worth learning carefully, because the difference is real and speakers rely on it.

Verb+ -ing+ to
stopstop doing = end the activity
I stopped smoking. (I quit)		stop to do = pause in order to do
I stopped to smoke. (paused for a cigarette)
rememberremember doing = a memory of the past
I remember locking the door.		remember to do = don't forget a task
Remember to lock the door.
forgetforget doing = forget a memory
I'll never forget meeting her.		forget to do = fail to do a task
I forgot to call him.
trytry doing = experiment / test it
Try turning it off and on.		try to do = make an effort
I tried to open the jar.

📖 The clearest pair: stop

stop + -ing = the activity ends. "He stopped working" means he no longer works.

stop + to = you stop one thing in order to do another. "He stopped to work" means he paused (from something else) so that he could work.

The to here means "in order to" — it shows a purpose.

Lena: Did you remember to bring the tickets?

Marco: Yes! And I clearly remember putting them in my bag last night.

Lena: Good. On the way, can we stop to get a coffee?

Marco: Sure. I've been trying to cut down, but I'll never stop drinking it completely!

✅ How to keep them straight

For remember and forget, ask: past memory or future task? Memory of what already happened → -ing. A task still ahead of you → to. For stop and try, ask: am I ending/testing something (-ing), or pausing-for-a-purpose / making an effort (to)?

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the verb in the correct form (-ing or to)

Objective: Type the verb in brackets as either the -ing form or to + base verb, depending on the first verb or the preposition.

  1. I enjoy (read) before bed.
  2. She decided (stay) at home.
  3. Do you mind (wait) a few minutes?
  4. He's very good at (cook).
  5. They offered (pay) for the meal.
  6. I'm looking forward to (see) you soon.
✅ Answer

1. reading (enjoy + -ing) · 2. to stay (decide + to) · 3. waiting (mind + -ing) · 4. cooking (after "at") · 5. to pay (offer + to) · 6. seeing ("to" here is a preposition → -ing)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the meaning (stop, remember, try…)

Objective: Type the verb in brackets as -ing or to + base verb so the sentence matches the meaning in italics.

  1. On the drive, we stopped (have) lunch. (we paused in order to eat)
  2. The doctor told him to stop (eat) so much sugar. (end the habit)
  3. Please remember (post) the letter. (a future task)
  4. I remember (meet) her at a party years ago. (a past memory)
  5. My laptop froze, so I tried (restart) it. (I tested this as a solution)
✅ Answer

1. to have (stopped in order to eat) · 2. eating (end the habit) · 3. to post (future task) · 4. meeting (past memory) · 5. restarting (tested it as a solution)

  3. "You should rest." The doctor advised her… →
  4. "Come to my party!" They invited us… →
  5. "Don't touch the wire." She warned him… →

1. She told me to wait outside.
2. He asked me to help him.
3. The doctor advised her to rest.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Learn the common verbs in groups (-ing verbs, to-verbs, object+to verbs) rather than one by one.
  • After any preposition — at, in, of, before, after, without — always use -ing.
  • Use the -ing form when an action is the subject ("Reading helps.").
  • For stop, remember, forget, try, decide the meaning first, then pick the form.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Wrong form after the verb: "I enjoy to cook" ❌ → "I enjoy cooking" ✅.
  • to + base after a preposition: "good at to draw" ❌ → "good at drawing" ✅.
  • The "looking forward to" trap: "looking forward to see you" ❌ → "looking forward to seeing you" ✅.
  • Missing the person: "She told to wait" ❌ → "She told me to wait" ✅.
  • Confusing meaning-change verbs: "I stopped to smoke" (= paused for a cigarette) when you mean "I quit" → "I stopped smoking" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Verb + -ing: enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, keep, mind — learn them as a group.
  • Verb + to-infinitive: want, decide, hope, promise, learn, offer, refuse, manage — many look to the future.
  • Verb + object + to: want/tell/ask/advise someone to do — don't drop the person.
  • -ing always follows a preposition (good at drawing, looking forward to seeing) and works as a subject (Swimming is good).
  • Meaning-change verbs: stop / remember / forget / try — the -ing and to forms mean different things.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is there a rule to tell whether a verb takes -ing or to?

Not a reliable one, no. There are helpful hints (many to-verbs look forward; many -ing-verbs describe experiences), but the safest approach at B1 is to learn the common verbs in groups and meet them often in reading and listening. With exposure, the right form will start to sound correct.

What about "like" — is it "like doing" or "like to do"?

Both are correct and very common! "I like swimming" and "I like to swim" are nearly the same. A subtle difference: -ing often stresses enjoying the activity, while to can suggest a choice or habit ("I like to check my email in the morning"). For B1, treat them as interchangeable.

How is "to" a preposition in "looking forward to"? Isn't "to" always part of an infinitive?

Not always — that's the trap. In look forward to, be used to, and object to, the to is a preposition. The test: if you can put a noun after it ("looking forward to the weekend"), it's a preposition, so a following verb takes -ing: "looking forward to seeing you."
