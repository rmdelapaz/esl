🔮 Lesson 5.3: Future Forms & Future Continuous

English has several ways to talk about the future — and each one carries a slightly different meaning. Are you making a spontaneous decision, describing a plan, or reporting a fixed arrangement? In this lesson you'll finally sort out will, going to, and the present continuous, then add the elegant future continuous — and learn the neat rule that keeps will out of time clauses.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Choose between will, going to, and the present continuous for the future
  • Form and use the future continuous (will be + -ing) for an action in progress at a future time
  • Use the present simple — not will — in future time clauses after when, as soon as, before, and until
  • Speak naturally about your plans, arrangements, and predictions

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe your plans, arrangements, and predictions for the coming week using at least three different future forms — including one future continuous and one time clause.

Which Future When?

Back at A2 you met going to, will, and might for the future. The tricky part was never the forms — it was knowing which one to choose. That's what this lesson finally makes clear. English doesn't have one "future tense"; it has a small toolkit, and each tool has a job.

📖 The idea behind the choice

All these forms talk about the future. What changes is how you see it right now:

  • Deciding as you speak? → will
  • You already intend or expect it? → going to
  • It's a fixed arrangement with a time and place? → present continuous

This lesson builds directly on the A2 going to / will / might you already know — it doesn't replace them, it organises them and adds two new, natural tools on top.

🧠 Mindset

Learners often worry there's one "correct" future and they'll pick the wrong one. In reality, several forms are frequently possible, and native speakers choose by feel. Your job at B1 isn't to be perfect every time — it's to understand the main differences and let real use sharpen your instinct. Aim for meaning first; the fine-tuning comes naturally with practice. 🌱

Will, Going To & Present Continuous

Here's the "which future when" table — the single most useful reference in this lesson. Read down the middle column and match it to what you mean.

FormUse it for…Example
will + base verbdecisions made now, predictions, promises, offersIt's cold — I'll close the window. / I think it 'll rain. / I 'll help you.
be going to + base verbplans / intentions already decided; predictions from evidenceWe're going to paint the house. / Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.
present continuous (am/is/are + -ing)fixed arrangements with a time / placeI'm meeting Sara at six. / We're flying to Rome on Friday.

💡 Will vs going to — the key feeling

Use will for a decision you make at the moment of speaking, and going to for a plan you already had:

  • (The phone rings) — "I'll get it!" (decided right now)
  • "I'm going to call her tonight." (already planned)

And for predictions: will = your opinion/guess ("I think our team will win"); going to = there's evidence you can see ("It's going to rain — look at the sky").

✅ Going to vs present continuous

Both describe plans, and they often overlap. The difference is small: going to highlights the intention ("We're going to visit Rome someday"), while the present continuous highlights a fixed arrangement — a booking, a date in the diary ("We're flying to Rome on Friday at 8"). If it's in the calendar, the present continuous feels most natural.

I'll give you a hand. I think it'll be fine. We're going to redecorate. I'm seeing the dentist tomorrow. She's leaving on Sunday.

The Future Continuous

Now for a new and rather elegant form. The future continuous describes an action that will be in progress at a particular moment in the future. It's the future's version of the past continuous.

📖 The form

will be + verb-ing — the same for every person.

"This time tomorrow, I will be flying to Rome." At that future moment, the flying will already be happening.

Picture a point in the future. The future continuous shows the action stretched across that point — already started, not yet finished:

graph LR
    A["now"] --> B["future point / this time tomorrow"] --> C["later"]
    B --> D["will be flying / action in progress"]
TypePatternExample
Positivewill be + -ingAt 8pm I 'll be watching the match.
Negativewon't be + -ingDon't call at noon — I won't be working then.
QuestionWill + subject + be + -ing?Will you be using the car tonight?

💡 Two friendly uses

  • An action in progress at a future time: "This time next week, we'll be relaxing on the beach."
  • A polite, no-pressure question about someone's plans: "Will you be joining us for dinner?" — softer than "Will you join us?"
Compare: "At 8pm I 'll watch the film" (I'll start it at 8) versus "At 8pm I 'll be watching the film" (I'll already be in the middle of it at 8). The continuous puts you inside the action at that moment.

Future Time Clauses

Here's a rule that surprises many learners — and fixing it makes your future English sound instantly correct. After certain time words, we use the present tense to talk about the future, not will.

⚠️ No will after these time words

After when, as soon as, before, after, until, and by the time, use the present simple — even though the meaning is future.

❌ I'll call you when I will arrive.

✅ I'll call you when I arrive.

The will goes in the main clause (the result); the time clause stays present.

If this feels familiar, it should — it's the very same logic as the first conditional you learned in Module 2: if and these time words both point at the future while keeping a present-tense verb.

❌ Wrong✅ Correct
I'll text you as soon as I will land.I'll text you as soon as I land.
We'll wait until she will come back.We'll wait until she comes back.
Turn off the lights before you will leave.Turn off the lights before you leave.
When the film will finish, we'll go home.When the film finishes, we'll go home.

📌 The pattern to remember

Main clause (will / going to) + time word + present simple.

I 'll cook dinner (main, will) when I get home (time clause, present).

Either order works — just keep will out of the time clause: "When I get home, I'll cook dinner" is equally correct.

Putting It Together

Listen to two colleagues talking about the week ahead. Notice how they move between the different future forms — a plan here, a spontaneous offer there, an arrangement, a prediction — all in one natural conversation.

A: Are you free on Thursday? I'm having a few people over for dinner.

B: Thursday… I'm meeting a client in the afternoon, but I should be done by six. What time?

A: Around seven. Don't worry — I'll text you the address as soon as I finish work.

B: Great. Should I bring anything?

A: Actually, you know what, I'll make a dessert myself. But if you want to bring some wine, that would be lovely.

B: Done. I'm going to try that new wine shop on the corner — I've been meaning to for ages.

A: Perfect. This time on Thursday, we'll all be sitting round the table, relaxing at last!

B: I can't wait. I'll call you if I'm running late.

Every future form at work: arrangement — I'm having, I'm meeting (present continuous); spontaneous decisions/offers — I'll text, I'll make (will); intention — I'm going to try (going to); action in progress later — we'll be sitting (future continuous); and a time clause — as soon as I finish (present, not will).

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose the best future form

Objective: Fill each gap with will, going to, the present continuous, or the future continuous. Think about the meaning: decision now, plan, arrangement, or action in progress?

  1. That bag looks heavy. I (carry) it for you. (offer, decided now)
  2. We (fly) to Madrid on Saturday morning. (fixed arrangement)
  3. Look at those dark clouds — it (rain). (evidence)
  4. This time next week, I (lie) on a beach. (in progress at a future time)
  5. Have you decided about the course? — Yes, I (sign up) next month. (intention)
✅ Answer

1. 'll carry (offer) · 2. 're flying (arrangement) · 3. 's going to rain (evidence) · 4. 'll be lying (future continuous) · 5. 'm going to sign up (intention)

Note: 2 could also be "'re going to fly," and 3 "will rain," but the forms shown fit the clue best.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Present or will? Future time clauses

Objective: Put the verb in brackets into the correct form. Remember: after when, as soon as, before, until, use the present simple; keep will for the main clause.

  1. I (call) you when I (arrive).
  2. We'll wait here until the rain (stop).
  3. As soon as she (get) the results, she (tell) us.
  4. Turn off the oven before you (leave) the house.
  5. When the film (finish), we (go) for a coffee.
✅ Answer

1. 'll call … arrive · 2. stops · 3. gets … 'll tell · 4. leave · 5. finishes … 'll go

The present simple always goes in the time clause; will stays in the main clause.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Match the situation to the form

Objective: For each situation, decide which future form is most natural and write a sentence.

  1. Someone knocks at the door. You react. (will)
  2. You have a dentist appointment booked for 3pm tomorrow. (present continuous)
  3. You can see your friend is about to drop a glass. (going to)
  4. At midnight tonight, describe what you'll be doing. (future continuous)
✅ Answer

Sample answers: 1. I'll get it! · 2. I'm seeing the dentist at three tomorrow. · 3. Careful — you're going to drop that! · 4. At midnight, I'll be sleeping.
(Your sentences will differ — check the form matches the situation.)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Ask "deciding now, planning, or arranging?" — that picks will, going to, or the present continuous.
  • Use the future continuous (will be + -ing) to place an action mid-progress at a future moment.
  • Keep the present simple after when, as soon as, before, until — the future lives in the main clause.
  • Remember that more than one form is often possible — choose by the meaning you want.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Will in a time clause: "as soon as I will arrive" ❌ → "as soon as I arrive" ✅.
  • Will for a fixed arrangement: "I 'll meet the dentist at 3 tomorrow" (already booked) → more natural: "I'm meeting the dentist at 3" ✅.
  • Forgetting be in the future continuous: "I'll flying" ❌ → "I'll be flying" ✅.
  • Going to → will for evidence predictions: "Look at the sky — it will rain" is weaker → "it's going to rain" ✅ when the evidence is right there.
  • Present simple alone for the future: "I go to Rome next week" ❌ (except timetables) → "I'm going / I'm going to go to Rome" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • will = decisions made now, predictions, promises, offers; going to = plans/intentions and evidence-based predictions; present continuous = fixed arrangements with a time or place.
  • Future continuous = will be + -ing, for an action in progress at a future moment ("this time tomorrow I'll be flying").
  • After when, as soon as, before, after, until, by the time, use the present simple — never will — for future meaning.
  • More than one future form is often possible; choose by the meaning you want to give.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now talk about the future with real precision and confidence — offering, planning, arranging, predicting, and picturing yourself mid-action at a future moment. This is a big leap in sounding natural, because the future comes up in almost every conversation about what's next. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is it wrong to use "will" for a plan I've already made?

It's not exactly wrong, but it's not the most natural choice. For plans you've already decided, going to ("I'm going to visit my aunt") or, for fixed arrangements, the present continuous ("I'm visiting my aunt on Sunday") sound better. Save will for decisions you make as you speak, plus predictions and offers.

Why can't I use "will" after "when" if the meaning is future?

English simply doesn't repeat the future marker in a time clause — the will in the main clause already makes the whole sentence future. So the time clause stays in the present: "I'll rest when I finish." It's the same rule you met with the first conditional's if-clause.

When would I really use the future continuous?

Two everyday times: to say what you'll be in the middle of at a future moment ("At 9 tonight I'll be studying, so I won't answer"), and to ask about someone's plans politely without sounding pushy ("Will you be using the printer later?"). It's a smooth, natural form once you notice it.
