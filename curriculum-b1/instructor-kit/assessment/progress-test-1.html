Progress Test 1 — Modules 1–4

A friendly halfway check after Lesson 4.3. It covers the present perfect vs the past simple (for/since, already/yet/just/still), the present perfect continuous, used to and would, the zero, first, and second conditionals (unless, If I were you…), wish / if only, reported statements, questions, and commands, relative clauses, the present and past simple passive, modals of deduction, and the language of opinions. It tells you what to recycle in Weeks 7–13. It does not rank anyone.

⏱ ~50 min written + 7 min pair speaking Written /55 · Speaking /15 CEFR B1
Why a progress test, and how to frame it

Most of your learners have already taken an A2 course or the placement test, so a test is not new to them. For some adults, though, a written test still brings back job screenings or official interviews. Say it plainly: "This is a check, not an exam. It shows me what to teach next, and it shows you how far you have come." At B1 the texts are longer, so tell the class to read the questions before they read the text. Do every example item together, and remind them they can ask you to repeat an instruction at any time. The listening is read twice at a natural pace. Don't slow down so much that the contractions (I've, I'd, she's been) disappear, because hearing them is part of the test.

What each part checks
  • Listening /12: hearing tense and conditional forms, a short talk about a neighborhood, and who thinks what in a discussion
  • Words & phrases /10: time words and linkers, and what a phrase is doing (advice, opinion, polite disagreement, deduction)

  • Reading /8: a blog post, "My street, then and now"
  • Writing /10: a reply with advice + an opinion paragraph (80–100 words)

Literacy-adapted version (learners still building reading and writing)

Use the same paper and change how it is done, not what it tests. Read every instruction and item aloud, one at a time, and enlarge the paper if you can. In Part 2, read the word box aloud and let the learner say the answer while you mark it. In Part 3, read the choices aloud and let them circle the one that sounds right; for the rewrites (11–15), let them say the new sentence and score what they say. Do Part 4 orally: read the blog post aloud twice and ask the questions. In Part 5, the learner may say the advice and the opinion while you scribe; score what they say. Give extra time, or split the paper over two sittings. Write "Adapted" at the top and note what changed. Report these learners' listening and speaking as the main evidence. Low literacy is not low ability. It points to a literacy-support need, and the key says how to act on it.

Personal information is always optional

The speaking task asks how long people have lived somewhere and how life used to be. The writing asks for opinions about where to live. Learners can answer with a "new identity" (the cards on the teacher pages) or anything they invent. Say this before the test starts: "Real or imagined, no problem." We are testing the English, not the facts. Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status.

My street, then and now

I've lived on Carver Street for twenty-two years. When I first arrived, it was a quiet street with small houses and a few old stores. There used to be a bakery on the corner, and the owner, Mrs. Park, would give a free cookie to every child who walked past. Everyone knew everyone.

A lot has changed since then. The bakery closed in 2015, and a big apartment building was built in its place. Most of the new people work downtown. They leave early and come home late, so I don't see them very often. My neighbor Luis, whose family has lived here even longer than mine, says the street has lost its heart. I don't completely agree. The new park, which opened last spring, is full of families on the weekend, and I've met more people there this year than in the last five years.

Still, I wish the rents weren't so high. My daughter has been looking for an apartment near me for six months, but she hasn't found anything she can afford yet. If rents were lower, she'd move here tomorrow. The city says new homes are planned, but nobody knows when. Maybe this year's street party will bring people together again. I'm organizing it, so I really hope so!

Amir: If you ask me, the town center should be closed to cars.

Lena: Really? I'm not sure I agree. What's the main reason?

Lena: I see what you mean, but a lot of older people can't walk very far. And the stores might lose customers.

Pair Speaking Task · 7 minutes

Two learners sit with you. Give Card A to one and Card B to the other. Give them one minute to read the cards silently, then check the instructions:

In Part 1, do you only answer, or do you ask questions too? (Both.) Do you give short or long answers? (Long: two or three sentences.) In Part 2, do you have to agree with your partner? (No! Agree or disagree, politely.) Real life or imagined? (Real or imagined, no problem.)

Part 1 (about 3 minutes): they ask and answer the card questions, which cover experiences, how long, how life used to be, and imagining. Part 2 (about 3 minutes): give them the discussion card. They discuss it together, giving opinions with reasons and agreeing or disagreeing politely. Part 3 (about 1 minute): ask each learner, "What did your partner tell you?" Listen for reported speech (She told me she'd lived…, He said he would…). Step in only to restart a stuck pair ("Now ask your next question"). Don't correct during the task. Score each learner on the grid, using the rubric in the key. Mixed-level pairs: score each person on what they did, not on the pair's result.

After the test: close the loop in Week 7

Score the papers before Class 14 with the key. Add up each section across the class, because the weakest section tells you what to recycle. Warmers in Classes 14–19 are a good place for it: for example, a 5-minute "Have you ever…? When did you…?" chain if many missed items 1–2, or a "What did she say?" whisper game if the rewrites (11–13) were weak. Both also prepare Module 5's storytelling. Give each learner their paper back with one thing they did well and one thing to practice, plus the online lesson that practices it. The key has a class analysis and feedback phrases.
