Final Test — B1 Exit Check

The end-of-course check on all eight modules, taken on the last day, which is also a celebration. It shows each learner what they can now do in English: tell a story with the right tenses, report what people said, give and defend an opinion, make a complaint that gets results, handle a job interview, and give a short talk. It also gives you the evidence for the next step: Confident English (B2), B2 with a review plan, or another round of B1 with more support. It is never a pass/fail verdict on a person.

⏱ ~60 min written + ~8 min interview each Written /85 · Speaking /15 · Total /100 Covers Modules 1–8
Frame it as "show what you can do"

By Class 26 your learners have met you 25 times. Use that trust: "Thirteen weeks ago you chose between two tenses. Today you can give a talk, defend your opinion, and get a refund, all in English!" Do every example item together, keep the room warm and unhurried, and plan the celebration so it happens whatever the scores are. Every learner gets a certificate of attendance. The test decides the next class, not who gets to celebrate.

Literacy-adapted version

Same paper, different delivery. Read all instructions and items aloud, enlarge the paper, and give extra time or split it over Classes 25 and 26. Parts 2–3: read the choices aloud and accept a spoken answer. Part 4: read the story and the job ad aloud and ask the questions orally (numbers and times can be pointed to). Part 5: the learner says the complaint and the opinion while you scribe, and you score them with the same checklist and rubric. Mark the paper "Adapted". For these learners, the interview and listening are the main evidence for placement. The key explains how to recommend B2 together with literacy support instead of holding someone back for reading and writing alone.

Real or imagined, always

The interview asks about work, a past experience, and change over time. For some learners those are painful or private questions: a lost profession, a hard journey, a job they can't do here yet. Say before you start: "Real or imagined, no problem. You can tell me about a new person." The new-identity cards from the progress test work here too. Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status. If a learner says "I'd rather not talk about that," thank them and move to the next stage.

📖A. Read the questions first. Then read Samira's story.
From Dishwasher to Head Chef

When Samira Haddad arrived in Chicago in 2014, she didn't know anyone. She had studied accounting in her home country, but her degree wasn't recognized, so she took the first job she could find: washing dishes in a busy hotel restaurant.

"It was hard," she remembers. "I was working ten hours a day, and I was so tired that I fell asleep on the bus most evenings." But Samira watched the cooks carefully. After her shift, she would stay for an extra hour and practice cutting vegetables. One night, while the kitchen was preparing for a big wedding, one of the cooks suddenly got sick. The head chef, who had noticed Samira's hard work, asked her to help. By the end of the night, she had prepared 150 salads, and she had found a new passion.

Over the next five years, Samira was promoted three times. She took evening classes at a community college, where she learned about food safety and kitchen management. Today she is the head chef at the same hotel, and she is responsible for a team of twelve people.

"Most of my team started like me," she says. "A few of them didn't speak much English at first, so I always tell them not to give up." Next spring, Samira is opening her own small restaurant. "This time next year, I'll be cooking my grandmother's recipes for my own customers. I can't wait."

Good evening, everyone. Today I'd like to talk about volunteering. First of all, let me tell you how I started. Three years ago, I was walking past the community center when I saw a sign: "Volunteers needed." I had never volunteered before, but I decided to try. At first, I helped in the kitchen on Saturdays. Individual Speaking Interview · ~8 minutes

Sit beside the learner, not across a desk, with the grid on your lap. Speak at a natural pace. Ask the core question first. If there's no answer after about 5 seconds, use the support version (a choice question or a model). If the answer comes easily, add the stretch question. At B1, listen for connected answers: a story in order, a reason for every opinion, and a short talk with a beginning, middle, and end. Don't correct during the interview. Note one strength and one next step instead. Before stage 1, let the learner choose a talk-topic card so they can think about it quietly while you do stage 1 and 2.

1

Warm-up: work & experience

~1 min
Hello! How are you today? How long have you been studying English?
Tell me about your work, or the kind of work you'd like to do. What are you (or would you be) responsible for? (real or imagined)

Support: "Do you work now, or are you looking for a job?" · Stretch: "How is your work life different from five years ago?" (used to … but now …; present perfect)

2

A story

~1½ min
Tell me about a time when something went wrong, or went surprisingly well. Where were you? What were you doing? What happened?

Support: "Was it on a trip? At work? What was the weather like?" · model: "I was waiting for the bus when…" · Stretch: "Had anything like that happened to you before?" (past perfect) · "What did people say to you afterwards?" (reported speech)

3

Short talk (monologue)

~2 min
Now please give me your short talk on the topic you chose. About one minute. Start with "Today I'd like to talk about…"

Let them speak without interrupting. Time about 1 minute; stop them kindly at 1½ minutes. Support: if they freeze, ask "What's your first point?" then "Can you give me an example?" · Stretch: one follow-up question on the talk: "What would happen if everyone did that?" Listen for signposting (First of all, Another point is, For example, To sum up).

4

Discussion

~2 min

Read one discussion statement below as if it were your opinion. Push back gently once, so the learner has to agree, disagree, or defend a view.

Here's my opinion: [statement]. What do you think? … Why? … Some people say the opposite. Do you agree with them? … What would happen if…?

Support: "Do you agree or disagree?" then "Why?" · Stretch: "What's the strongest argument on the other side?" Listen for polite disagreement (I see your point, but…), reasons, and hedging (maybe, I suppose, to be honest).

5

Role-play + your question

~1½ min

The learner chooses one role-play card. You play the other person. Then invite one question.

(Complaint, you are the store worker) Good afternoon, how can I help? … Oh, I'm sorry to hear that. When did you buy it? … Do you have your receipt? … What would you like us to do?
(Interview, you are the interviewer) Thanks for coming in. Tell me a little about yourself. … Why do you want this job? … What's your biggest weakness?
Now, do you have a question for me? Anything at all.
