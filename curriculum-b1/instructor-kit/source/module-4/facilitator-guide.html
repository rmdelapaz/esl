Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 4 — Structures for Fluency

Three classes that finish the first half of B1. First comes the passive (present and past simple) for facts, products and processes: Coffee is grown in Brazil. The bridge was built in 1932. Then modals of deduction (must / might / can't be) to reason from clues, set against the obligation and advice modals learners met at A2. Last, a task-based review: learners hold a mini debate and tell a short story with the grammar of Modules 1–4, while you score a speaking checklist that feeds Progress Test 1 (C13).

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C10 and C11 follow the usual shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C12 is a task-based review: the task comes first and language work comes out of it.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-fluency-structures.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C10. It holds the flashcards, the How It's Made quiz, the process strips, the fact-file information gap, the mystery close-ups, the detective cards, the "Whose bag?" case, the rules-or-advice cards, the mini-debate cards, the story cards, the C12 speaking checklist, the Module 1–4 Review Race board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. Learners met past participles with the present perfect (B1 Module 1, and A2), and have to / must / mustn't / should at A2. Build on this. The new B1 work is the passive voice, must / can't / might for deduction, and the choice between obligation, no obligation, prohibition and advice.
  • Keep B1 honest. Learners produce the present and past simple passive and present deductions only. They may meet other forms (is being repaired, has been sent, must have left) outside class: teach these for recognition ("You'll understand this; you don't need to make it yet"). Past perfect is Lesson 5.1: accept it in C12 stories, never require it.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (downtown, package, mail carrier, (train) car, conductor, nine-thirty, practice, traveling). Accept British forms from learners (city centre, parcel, postman, carriage, guard, half past nine, practise, travelling). Note: Americans rarely say mustn't. For rules they say can't, aren't allowed to or (in writing) must not. Teach mustn't so learners understand it, and give the American forms next to it.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Move the spotlight → measure how sure you are → say what you think. In C10 learners move the focus of a sentence from the doer to the thing (This watch is made in Switzerland.) and describe products and processes. In C11 they reason from clues and show how certain they are (The lights are off, so they must be out.), and keep that apart from rules and advice (You have to… / You should…). In C12 they use everything from Modules 1–4 in two real tasks: argue a point in a small group and tell a short story. Every class ends with a speaking task that is longer and freer than the one before.

Trauma-informed: facts, guesses and opinions

C10: "facts about your country" is an option, never a requirement. Learners can describe any product, any place, or a process like making bread. Don't ask one learner to "represent" their country. C11: deductions are about pictures, objects and card people, never about people in the room ("She must be pregnant," "He must be poor"). Say this rule before every detective task. C12: debate topics are everyday (city life, phones, cooking, transportation), never politics, religion or immigration. Every learner argues from an opinion card, so no one has to share a real opinion. Stories can be true, changed, or completely invented (story cards). A light story is a good story. Say "Real or invented, both are great" every time.

Two B1 habits for every class in this module

Fluency before accuracy. In freer tasks, don't stop speakers to correct. Listen, write down 4–6 sentences you hear (good ones and ones with errors), and give feedback in two steps: first content ("Which group had the most surprising fact? Who changed their mind?"), then language on the board with no names, and the class fixes it together. Correct on the spot only when a mistake blocks meaning.

Dominant and quiet speakers. B1 groups often have one confident speaker who fills the silence. Use structure, not criticism: talking tokens (3 each; you put one down every time you speak; when your tokens are gone, you listen), a chair role that rotates and invites others ("What do you think, Ana?"), think time (30 seconds silent before anyone speaks) and a fixed speaking order for the first round. Give the strongest speaker a job that needs listening: chair, timekeeper, or "question master." Quiet learners rehearse in pairs before they speak to a group.

4.1 · C10

The Passive Voice — How It's Made

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the present simple passive (am / is / are + past participle) and the past simple passive (was / were + past participle), with negatives and questions
  • explain when the passive is the better choice: the doer is unknown, unimportant, obvious or "people in general," or the focus is a process or result
  • add by + agent only when the doer is interesting or important (never "by someone / by people")
  • change active sentences into passive with the 3-step method, and use made of / made from / made in / made by
  • describe a product, a place or a process in 6+ passive sentences

  • Workbook 4.1 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "My 6 facts" plan)
  • Slides 3–7 · Product flashcards (Set A) · 3–4 real labeled objects · How It's Made quiz (Set B) · Process strips (Set C, 1 set per pair) · Fact-file information gap A/B (Set D, 1 page per pair)
  • Listening scripts 4.1-A (How coffee is made) and 4.1-B (News in brief)

If you run long

Cut the quiz to 8 questions and move the fact-file information gap to the C11 warmer (it recycles the passive before deduction). Never cut the be check in the grammar stage: a missing be ("English spoken here") is the top error, and it is easy to fix today and hard to fix later.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the passive moves the spotlight. The thing that receives the action becomes the subject. The doer is not important, not known, obvious, or "people in general." It is not a new tense; it is a new voice.

"This watch is made in Switzerland." Do we know who makes it? (No.) Is that important? (No.) What is the sentence about: the workers or the watch? (The watch.) · "My car was stolen last night." Who stole it? (We don't know.) Present or past? (Past: was.)
"He was arrested on Friday." Who arrested him? (The police: it's obvious.) · "Rice is eaten all over Asia." Who eats it? (People: everybody.) Do we need to say "by people"? (No.)
"Hamlet was written by Shakespeare." Why do we say by Shakespeare here? (It's famous, interesting information.) · "The office is cleaned every evening." Can I say by them? (You can, but it adds nothing. Leave it out.)

Form. Color the be verb in one color and the past participle in another. be shows the tense and agrees with the subject; the participle never changes.

Presentsubject + am / is / are + past participleEnglish is spoken here. · These cars are made in Japan.
Pastsubject + was / were + past participleThe bridge was built in 1890. · The letters were sent yesterday.
Negativeisn't / aren't / wasn't / weren't + participlePlastic isn't recycled here. · The package wasn't delivered.
Question(Wh-) + is / are / was / were + subject + participle?Is this dish made with milk? · When was it built? · Where are they grown?
Agent… + by + doer (only if useful)The Mona Lisa was painted by Leonardo da Vinci.
made…made of (material you can see) · made from (changed) · made in (place) · made by (maker)made of wood · made from milk · made in Italy · made by hand

Pronunciation. In speech, be is weak and linked: "It's MADE in JApan" (/ɪts/), "They're GROWN…", "It was BUILT" /wəz/. The stress falls on the participle and the new information, not on was. -ed endings matter here: invented, painted, designed /ɪd/ adds a beat only after /t/ or /d/; used, opened, delivered end in /d/; picked, packed, cooked in /t/. used in "It is used for…" is /juzd/ (z), not /just/ as in "used to." ground (from grind) rhymes with found.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "in Brazil" → "is grown in Brazil" → "Coffee is grown in Brazil." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Then "was built in 1890" → "The bridge was built in 1890."
  2. Tense switch (hold up a card: NOW or a year): T: "coffee / grow / Brazil" + NOW → S: "Coffee is grown in Brazil." T: "the bridge / build" + 1890 → S: "The bridge was built in 1890." T: "these shoes / make / Italy" + NOW → S: "These shoes are made in Italy."
  3. Subject switch (is / are): T: "English / speak" → "English is spoken…" T: "Spanish and Portuguese / speak" → "…are spoken." Point at the subject every time.
  4. Question chain: S1: "When was the Eiffel Tower built?" S2: "It was built in 1889. Where are bananas grown?" S3: "They're grown in… (Ecuador / India / the Philippines)."
  5. Cut the agent: T says an active sentence; learners say the passive and decide on by: "Someone stole my phone." → "My phone was stolen." "Shakespeare wrote Hamlet." → "Hamlet was written by Shakespeare."
How It's Made quiz — how to run it
  • Teams of 3–4 with a team name. One sheet of paper per team for the score. Read each card and its three options twice.
  • 30 seconds to talk (in English!). Then the team's speaker for that round says the answer as a full passive sentence. The speaker changes every round, so everyone speaks. This is also your fix for a dominant speaker.
  • Scoring: 1 point for the right fact, +1 for a correct passive sentence (right be + participle). A wrong fact in a perfect sentence still gets 1 point. Say this: good English matters as much as knowing the fact.
  • After the answer, give one extra fact from the card's note (in the passive) and let a learner repeat it.
  • Last round: each team writes one question for the other teams about their own town, a product they know, or any country ("Where is ___ grown?", "When was ___ built?"). Nobody has to ask about their home country.
ICQs before the quiz and the information gap
Quiz: Who talks first: the team or the speaker? (The team, 30 seconds.) · How do you give the answer? (A full passive sentence.) · How many points for a correct sentence? (One more.) · Is the speaker always the same person? (No, it changes.)
Fact files: Can you show your card? (No.) · What kind of questions do you ask? (Passive questions: "When was it built?") · When you finish, what do you do? (Present one file to another pair.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with four fixed chunks: It is made in… · It is grown in… · It was built in… · It was made by… Use the product flashcards and labels as prompts. In the quiz, the learner can answer by pointing to the option and then saying the chunk with a partner. In the fact files, give a question strip with the questions already written; the learner reads a question and writes only the answer (a year, a place, a number). Target: 4 spoken passive sentences about real objects.

Stretch

Use sequence words in a process (First… Then… After that… Finally…) and a relative clause (The beans are roasted, which gives them their color.). Recognize other tenses of the passive when you meet them (is being repaired, has been sent) and say what they mean. Rewrite a short news article from a free news site for learners in the active, then back in the passive, and decide which version sounds better.

L1 watch-outs
  • No be: "English spoken here" / "Coffee grown in Brazil": Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic, Russian and Haitian Creole speakers (no be in many present sentences). Point at the be color every time: "No be, no passive."
  • "Here speaks English" / "It sells in the market": Spanish se habla, Portuguese fala-se, Italian si parla, French on parle build the passive idea with se / on. Show the English pattern: "English is spoken here." "It is sold at the market."
  • "An accident was happened" / "He was died": common for Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Turkish speakers, and for anyone who has just learned the passive. happen, die, arrive, go, sleep have no object, so no passive.
  • Passive only for bad news: Chinese 被 bèi and the Japanese / Korean "suffering" passive are mostly used for unpleasant events. Learners may avoid neutral passives ("Rice is eaten…"). Give many neutral examples.
  • "The house becomes built" / "The bridge will built": German and Dutch werden / worden (= become) make the passive. Drill "is / was + participle."
  • Base verb or regular -ed instead of the participle: "are make," "was builded," "was stealed." Everyone. Keep the irregular list from Module 1 on the wall.
  • "made of" for everything (Spanish and Portuguese hecho de / feito de, French fait de). Teach the four: made of wood · made from milk · made in Italy · made by hand.

4.2 · C11

Modals of Deduction & Obligation — Detectives at Work

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use must (sure it's true) and can't (sure it's not true) + base verb to make present deductions from clues
  • use might / may / could (+ not) when they are not sure, and place a guess on the certainty scale
  • link a deduction to its clue with so: The lights are off, so they must be out.
  • use can't, not mustn't, for a negative deduction, and tell deduction-must from obligation-must
  • tell apart have to / must (necessary), don't have to (not necessary), mustn't (not allowed) and should (advice)
Materials
  • Workbook 4.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "My 3 deductions")
  • Slides 8–13 · Clue-object flashcards (Set A) · Mystery close-ups (Set E) · Detective cards (Set F) · "Whose bag?" case (Set G) · Rules or advice? cards (Set H)
  • A real bag with 5–6 objects (see the lead-in) · Listening scripts 4.2-A (Where's Sam?) and 4.2-B (dictation)

Left: the certainty scale, top to bottom: ✅ must (sure YES, ~95%) → 🤔 might / may / could (maybe, ~50%) → ❌ can't (sure NO, ~95%). Beside it: CLUE, so + DEDUCTION: The ground is wet, so it must be raining.

Center: modal + base verb: She must be tired. · He can't be hungry. · They might know. (no to, no -s). A box: can't ✅ / mustn't ❌ for "sure it's not true."

Right: RULES & ADVICE: have to / must = necessary · don't have to = not necessary (your choice) · mustn't (US: can't / not allowed) = forbidden · should = a good idea. Below: must has 2 jobs: 📜 "You must sign here." (rule) · 🕵️ "You must be tired." (deduction)

If you run long

This class has two jobs. If time is short, keep the deduction work whole (it is new) and shorten the obligation review to slide 11 + half the Rules or advice cards: learners met these modals at A2. In the freer stage, do the "Whose bag?" case and only 3–4 detective cards.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: deduction. A guess with a reason. The speaker looks at a clue and says how sure they are. Nobody told them the answer.

"The light's on, so she must be home." Did she tell me she's home? (No.) What's my clue? (The light.) How sure am I? (Very sure, about 95%.) Is it a rule? (No, it's a guess.)
"He can't be hungry. He just ate." Is he hungry? (No.) Am I sure? (Yes, very.) Can I say "He mustn't be hungry"? (No! mustn't means "not allowed.")
"She's not answering. She might be busy." Is she busy? (Maybe.) Can I say "may be busy" or "could be busy"? (Yes, the same.) Can I say "She can be busy" for this? (Not for one situation now; use might / could.)

Meaning: obligation and advice.

"You have to show ID at the bank." Is it my choice? (No, it's a rule.) · "You don't have to come on Saturday." Can I come? (Yes.) Do I need to? (No, it's my choice.) · "You mustn't park here." Is it OK? (No, it's against the rules.) · "You should rest." Is it a rule? (No, it's advice: a good idea.)
"You must be tired after that trip." A rule or a guess? (A guess: deduction.) · "You must sign here." A rule or a guess? (A rule.) How do you know? (The situation. be tired is a state; sign is an action someone asks you to do.)

Form. Modals never change: no -s, no to, no do. The verb after them is the base form, very often be. For "happening now," use be + -ing: It must be raining. They might be sleeping.

Pronunciation. The big one in American English: can't /kænt/ vs can /kən/. In a positive sentence can is weak and unstressed; can't is strong and stressed, and the final t is often not released. Learners must stress CAN'T or the listener hears the opposite. must is weak in deductions ("She /məs/ be TIRED"); stress the new information. mustn't /ˈmʌsənt/: the first t is silent. have to = /ˈhæftə/ ("hafta"), has to = /ˈhæstə/. Deductions often have a thinking, falling tone: "Hmm… it MIGHT be…" with a pause before the guess, which is natural and gives thinking time.

Drill script
  1. Scale drill (point at the scale on the board): T: "be home" + ✅ → S: "She must be home." + 🤔 → "She might be home." + ❌ → "She can't be home." Choral, individuals, then faster.
  2. Clue → deduction (T gives a clue, learners finish with so): T: "He's yawning…" → S: "…so he must be tired." T: "She doesn't have a brother…" → S: "…so that can't be her brother." T: "I don't know who's at the door…" → S: "…so it might be the mail carrier."
  3. can / can't listening: T says "You can park here. / You can't park here. / It can be cold here in winter. / It can't be cold, it's July." Learners show 👍 (can) or 👎 (can't). Then learners test each other with a stressed CAN'T.
  4. Fix the mustn't: T: "He mustn't be at home. His car's gone." → S: "He can't be at home." T: "The shop mustn't be open." → S: "The shop can't be open."
  5. Rule, not necessary, or advice? T: "Seatbelt, car." → S: "You have to wear a seatbelt." T: "Free museum, pay." → "You don't have to pay." T: "Library, phone calls." → "You mustn't / can't make phone calls." T: "Cold, sleep." → "You should go to bed early."
The detective agency — how to run it
  • The rule first: "Detectives make deductions about pictures, objects and card people. Never about people in this room." Say it before every round.
  • "Whose bag?" (Set G): the four owner profiles go face up; the clue cards go face down in order (1–5). Turn over one clue. Every learner makes one deduction about one person with the clue: "It can't be Grace's, because the ticket is to Toronto." Then the next clue. After clue 5 the group writes its answer ("It must be ___'s bag, because…"). The group with the best reasons, not the fastest, wins.
  • Detective cards (Set F): a player reads the situation aloud. Each other player adds one deduction at a different level (must / might / can't), always with the clue: "There are three newspapers at the door, so they can't be at home." The reader adds one piece of advice with should: "We should call them." Next player reads.
  • Talking tokens work well here: 3 each. When your tokens are gone, you listen until everyone has spoken.
  • Early finishers turn two detective cards into a short dialogue like the lost-bag model and read it to another group.
ICQs before the detective agency
Do we make deductions about people in this room? (No! Only pictures, objects and card people.) · In "Whose bag?", do you turn over all the clues at once? (No, one at a time.) · What word goes between the clue and the deduction? ("So.") · With the detective cards, how many deductions? (Three: must, might, can't.) · And the reader? (One piece of advice with "should.")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the three picture symbols ✅ 🤔 ❌ in place of the words, and three fixed frames: It must be… · It might be… · It can't be… The close-ups are ideal: no reading needed. In the detective agency, a partner reads the situation card aloud and the learner chooses a symbol and finishes the frame. Target: 4 spoken deductions, at least one of each level, and one "You should…".

Stretch

Use be + -ing for deductions about now (They must be sleeping.) and give two competing deductions for one clue (She might be at work, or she could be at the gym.). Recognize past deduction (She must have left) in the online lesson and say what it means, but keep your own deductions in the present. Write a short "mystery" (3 clues, one answer) for a classmate to solve.

L1 watch-outs
  • German "muss nicht" = don't have to. German speakers say "You mustn't come" when they mean "You don't have to come." The meaning reverses. Show the pair side by side and drill it.
  • "He mustn't be at home" for a negative deduction: everyone, but especially Spanish and Portuguese speakers (no debe de estar / não deve estar = probably isn't). Fix it every time: "sure NO → can't."
  • "It can be the mail carrier" for a possibility now: Spanish puede ser, Portuguese pode ser, French ça peut être, Italian può essere. In English, can is for general possibility ("It can be cold here in winter"); for one situation now, use might / could.
  • "She must to be tired" / "It must is broken": learners whose L1 uses a full verb for must (Spanish debe, Arabic yajib an). Chant: "modal + plain verb."
  • "He should be at home" as a deduction (Chinese 应该 yīnggāi = should / probably). It is possible English ("I expect so"), but it is not must. Accept it and offer must for "I'm sure."
  • can / can't in sound: Vietnamese, Cantonese and Spanish speakers often drop the final t, so can't sounds like can. Stress and a longer vowel save the meaning: "I CAN'T /kæːnt/."

4.3 · C12

Review Project — Opinions & Storytelling

90 min
A task-based review, not a new-grammar class

There is no new grammar today. The only new language is functional: phrases to give, support, agree and disagree. The class is built around two real tasks: a mini debate in groups of four and a short story (true or invented) told to a small group. Learners plan, rehearse, then perform while you listen with the speaking checklist (Set K). Language work comes from the tasks: short focus-on-form moments when you hear a gap, not presentations. The checklist results feed C13 (Progress Test 1): they are your spoken evidence for Modules 1–4, and they tell you who goes to which catch-up station.

Objectives — learners can…
  • give an opinion with a range of openers (In my opinion… · If you ask me… · As far as I'm concerned…) and support it with a reason and an example
  • agree (That's a good point. · I couldn't agree more. · So do I. / Neither do I.) and disagree politely by acknowledging first (I see what you mean, but…)
  • take part in a 10-minute group mini debate, with a chair who manages turns
  • tell a connected 2–3 minute story, true or invented, using at least four structures from Modules 1–4
Materials
  • Workbook 4.3 (opinion phrases, Exercises 1–5, the debate planner, the story frame, the Module 4 "I can" checklist)
  • Slides 14–18 · Review Race board game (Set L) + dice + counters · Mini-debate topic cards, opinion cards, chair cards, talking tokens (Set I) · Story cards (Set J) · Speaking checklist: one class grid + score slips (Set K)
  • Listening scripts 4.3-A (the discussion) and 4.3-B (the model story: The bag on the train) · optional: phones for recording

Left: an opinion = CLAIM + REASON + EXAMPLE. Openers: In my opinion… · If you ask me… · As far as I'm concerned… · It seems to me that… Support: The main reason is… · For example… · On the one hand… on the other hand…

Center: 👍 That's a good point. · I couldn't agree more. · So do I. / Neither do I. 🙏 I see what you mean, but… · That may be true, but… · I'm not sure I agree. Rule: acknowledge → then disagree.

Right: the story road: ① THEN (used to / was) → ② WHAT HAPPENED (past simple + a relative clause) → ③ WHAT PEOPLE SAID (reported speech) → ④ A GUESS / A FACT (must / might / can't · the passive) → ⑤ NOW & IF (present perfect · second conditional / wish)

Hearing everyone

In 29 minutes of tasks you can score about 8–10 learners well. Choose one of these for the rest, and tell the class which one before the debate: (a) learners record their story on a phone during the story circles and play it for you in the C13 catch-up time; (b) the rest tell their story to you in the C13 catch-up stations; (c) in a large class, score the debate in C12 and the stories in C13. Anyone who prefers to tell the story only to you (not a group) may do so. That is a normal choice, not a problem.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review focus)

Meaning: an opinion is a claim plus support.

"In my opinion, cities are better for young people." Is this a fact or an opinion? (An opinion.) Is it enough? (No, we need a reason.) · "The main reason is that there are more jobs." What does this add? (The reason.) · "For example, my cousin found work in a month." And this? (An example.)
"I see what you mean, but I think exams are useful." Does the speaker agree? (No.) Is it polite? (Yes.) Why? (They show they listened first.) · "You're wrong." Polite? (No, it sounds rude in English.)
"I don't like crowds." — "Neither do I." Does the second person like crowds? (No.) · "I've been there." — "So have I." Why have, not do? (We copy the helper verb.)

Meaning: each structure has a job in the story. Show the story road on slide 17.

"I used to take the train every day." Do I still take it? (No, or not always.) Which step? (① Then.) · "She told me that she was on her way to an interview." What did she say? ("I'm on my way to an interview.") Which step? (③.) · "If I found a bag again, I'd do the same." Did I find a bag again? (No, it's imaginary.) Which conditional? (The second.)

Form: the review table.

Pronunciation. Opinion openers are said as one chunk, with a small pause after: "If you ask me | …", "As far as I'm conCERNED | …". Polite disagreement has a friendly rise on the first part and a fall on the new idea: "I SEE what you mean ↗ | but I think it's too exPENsive ↘." A flat, low "No." sounds rude even with polite words: model the warm version. So do I / Neither do I: stress the last word, I. Neither is /ˈniðər/ or /ˈnaɪðər/; both are correct.

Drill script
  1. Opener chain: T: "public transportation / free" → S1: "In my opinion, public transportation should be free." → S2 (new opener, same idea): "If you ask me, …" → S3: "As far as I'm concerned, …" Round the room; no one repeats the previous opener.
  2. Add the support: T: "Cooking should be taught at school." → S: "…The main reason is that… For example, …"
  3. Acknowledge, then disagree: T gives an opinion; learners answer only with the polite frame: "I see what you mean, but…" / "That may be true, but…" Say the rise-fall together.
  4. So / Neither: T: "I love coffee." → "So do I." "I can't swim." → "Neither can I." "I've been to Canada." → "So have I." "I didn't sleep well." → "Neither did I."
  5. Story upgrade: T says a plain sentence; learners upgrade it with one B1 structure: "I took the train." → "I used to take the train every day." "A woman left her bag." → "A woman who was sitting across from me left her bag."
The mini debate — how to run it
  • Opinion cards make it safe. Deal the four cards face down. Learners argue the card's view, not their own. Say: "Good debaters can argue any side." Learners may swap cards within the group if a topic feels personal.
  • The chair keeps time (a phone timer), opens ("Today's question is… Let's start with you, Omar."), invites quiet speakers ("What do you think, Ana?"), stops long turns kindly ("Thanks, let's hear from someone else"), and sums up at the end. Give the chair card to a dominant speaker: the job needs listening.
  • Talking tokens in round 2: 3 each; put one in the middle every time you speak. No tokens left? Listen until everyone has used theirs, then everyone gets 3 more.
  • Every turn in round 2 starts with a response to the last speaker: agree or acknowledge first, then add a point. This keeps it a discussion, not four speeches.
  • Topics avoid politics, religion and immigration. If a group gets heated, the chair (or you) says: "Great ideas. Let's hear one reason from each side and sum up."
The speaking checklist — how to score it (feeds C13)

Five criteria, 0–3 points each, /15: 0 = no response · 1 = not yet B1 · 2 = B1 · 3 = strong B1. Score the whole performance across the debate and the story. Tick the module boxes (M1–M4) when you hear a structure used correctly. The full grid and descriptors are in the classroom materials (Set K).

CriterionWhat 3 points sounds like
1 · TaskDebate: a clear opinion with 2 reasons and an example. Story: connected, with a beginning, a middle and an end; the listener can follow it.
2 · Range (Modules 1–4)Uses structures from at least 3 of the 4 modules (e.g. used to, a second conditional, reported speech, a passive or a deduction), without being prompted.
3 · AccuracyErrors are few and don't block meaning; tenses are controlled (past simple for the events, present perfect for life experience).
4 · Interaction & opinion languageVaried openers; acknowledges before disagreeing; responds to the last speaker; asks or invites others.
5 · Fluency & pronunciationKeeps going with natural pauses; chunks and stress make it easy to follow; little translating in the head.

Feeding C13. Record each learner's scores and module ticks on the class grid. In C13, send learners to a catch-up station for any module with no tick and to the station for their lowest criterion: no M1 tick → tenses station (present perfect vs past simple, used to); no M2 tick → conditionals and wish station; no M3 tick → reported speech and relative clauses station; no M4 tick → passive and modals station (Sets B, E, F); criterion 4 ≤ 1 → opinion language (Workbook 4.3 Ex 1, 4, 5). If Progress Test 1 has its own speaking task, keep this grid anyway: it is your evidence of extended speaking for Modules 1–4, and it lets you give each learner one kind, specific next step.

ICQs before the mini debate and the story circles
Debate: Do you have to give your real opinion? (No, you use your opinion card.) · What does the chair do? (Keeps time, invites everyone, sums up.) · What do you do with a token? (Put one down every time you speak.) · Before you disagree, what do you say? ("I see what you mean, but…")
Stories: Does it have to be true? (No: true, changed, or invented.) · Can you read your notes? (Only a quick look.) · What do the listeners do? (Ask one question and give one opinion.) · Do you have to tell it to the group? (No, you can tell it only to the teacher.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a phrase strip with 3 lines: "In my opinion, … because …" · "I agree. / I see what you mean, but…" · "For example, …". In the debate, the learner speaks third in round 1 (after hearing two models) and uses the strip. For the story, use the picture version of the frame: draw a small picture in each step. Target: a 5–6 sentence story with used to, the past simple, one "She said…" and one "It must be…". Score on the same checklist: a clear, short story can earn full marks on criteria 1, 3 and 5.

Stretch

Be the chair and sum up both sides fairly. Use On the one hand… on the other hand… and a "hedge" (It seems to me that…). In the story, use all five steps plus a passive (I was given…) and a wish. Tell the story in 2 minutes with no notes. Listeners ask two follow-up questions, not one. Write the story (8–10 sentences) for homework and underline one structure from each module.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am agree" / "Are you agree?": Spanish estoy de acuerdo, French je suis d'accord, Portuguese estou de acordo, Italian sono d'accordo. Very common. Drill "I agree. · Do you agree?"
  • "According to me…" (French selon moi, Spanish según yo, and common in Indian English): according to is for other people ("according to the news"). Offer "In my opinion…"
  • "I think no" / "I think not" (Spanish creo que no, Portuguese acho que não): "I don't think so."
  • "In my opinion, I think…" (double opener, many L1s). Accept in speech; point out in writing.
  • "We discussed about…" (Spanish discutir sobre, Arabic): discuss has no preposition. Also note discutir can mean argue.
  • Directness differs. Dutch, German, Russian and Hebrew speakers may disagree very directly ("No, that's wrong."); Japanese, Korean, Chinese and Filipino speakers may avoid disagreeing at all. Teach the English middle: acknowledge, then disagree. Both styles are normal; English just has its own.
  • Tense drift in stories (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai have no past endings): prompt with the step: "① Then: used to or use to?" Past perfect ("I had never done that") may appear; accept it, it's coming in 5.1.

From this module it can test: present and past simple passive (+ / − / ?), when to use by + agent, active → passive, made of / from / in / by, must / might / may / could / can't for present deduction, can't vs mustn't, have to / don't have to / mustn't / should, and opinion, agreeing and disagreeing phrases.
