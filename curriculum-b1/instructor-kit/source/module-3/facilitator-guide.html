Module 3 — Reporting & Connecting Ideas

Three classes that let learners pass on other people's words and join their own ideas into longer sentences. First comes reported statements: the tense backshift, pronoun and time-word changes, and say vs tell. Then reported questions (statement word order, if / whether) and reported requests and commands (told / asked me (not) to…). Last, relative clauses: who, which, that, where, when, whose, defining vs non-defining clauses, and the commas that change the meaning.

3 lessons Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C7–C9 Weeks 4–5 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-reporting.html. Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C7. It holds the flashcards, the Gossip Chain news cards, the Survey Report cards, the Message Relay cards and "While you were out" notes, the "Who am I? / What is it?" definition cards and bingo, the sentence-combining strips, the new identity cards and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. A2 Lesson 6.2 taught defining relative clauses with who, which, that, where. Many learners also met said and told as everyday past verbs. Check what they know in each lead-in and go faster where they are strong. The new B1 work is the backshift system, reported questions and commands, and in 3.3 non-defining clauses, commas, whose / when and dropping the object pronoun.
  • Fluency before accuracy. In every freer task, listen and take notes; don't stop a speaker to fix a backshift. The feedback stage is where you fix errors, without names. Praise the message first ("Great story! Everyone understood."), then the form. At B1 a report that is clear but has one un-shifted verb is a success.
  • Dominant speakers. Reporting tasks are natural "turn" tasks, so use them: give every group a talking-turn token (a pen or a coin; you speak only when you hold it), give the strongest speaker a job (the checker, the timekeeper, the "last person in the chain"), and use "report your partner, not yourself" so quieter learners get the floor. Say it kindly: "Thanks, Omar. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons sometimes use British forms (film, flat, mum, holiday, neighbour, maths, moved house, memorise, practise, Mr with no period). Teach movie, apartment, mom, vacation, neighbor, math, moved, memorize, practice, Mr. Accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

What they said → what they asked and wanted → who and what we mean. In C7 learners pass on news: She said she was starting a new job. In C8 they report the questions people asked them and the things people asked or told them to do: She asked me whether I liked cooking. The doctor told me not to eat before the test. In C9 they stop reporting and start connecting: they describe people, places and things exactly (the woman who called you) and add extra facts inside commas (My brother, who lives in Chicago, is a nurse.). Every class ends with the same kind of freer task: tell other people something real (or imagined) in longer, joined-up English.

Trauma-informed: whose words, whose questions?

Reported speech can bring back hard conversations: an immigration or asylum interview, a police officer, a court, a difficult doctor's visit, a landlord who wants rent. The online lesson 3.2 project suggests "a police interview" as one option. In class, keep every task everyday: friends, neighbors, work, a routine clinic visit, the building manager fixing a sink. Never ask "What did the officer ask you?", "What did the interviewer ask you at immigration?" or "What did your family say when you left?" The gossip in C7 is always about invented people on the news cards, never about classmates. For every personal task (reporting real things people said, the survey, "the people and places in my life"), offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card (in the classroom materials), or something imagined. Anyone can say "Pass!" to a survey question. Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal.

Before the Module

  • For C7: one set of Gossip Chain news cards (12 cards) and a small piece of paper for each line of learners.
  • For C8: one Survey card per learner (6 different topics, so neighbors have different questions) and one report frame per learner. Tape the 8 Message Relay cards on the walls around the room before class. Print 2 "While you were out" note sheets per pair.
  • For C9: one set of "Who am I? / What is it?" definition cards per group of 3–4, one bingo grid per learner, and one set of sentence-combining strips + comma cards per pair. An envelope per pair keeps the strips together.

  • Optional: a real phone to "play" the voicemail in C7 and C8 (read the script while holding the phone to your ear). Learners enjoy it and it makes the situation clear.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: reported speech. We pass on someone's words later, in our own sentence, not their exact words. Direct speech = the exact words in "quotation marks."

"Mrs. Lee said she was moving to Florida." Who is moving: me or Mrs. Lee? (Mrs. Lee.) Did she say "was"? (No, she said "I'm moving.") Is she moving now, in the past? (No, she's moving soon. "Was" is only because I'm reporting it later.) Are these her exact words? (No, my words.)

Meaning: the backshift. After a past reporting verb (said, told), we usually move the verb one step back, like pressing "rewind" once.

"He said he would help." What were his exact words? ("I will help.") · "She said she had seen the accident." Her exact words? ("I saw the accident." or "I've seen the accident.") · "He said he had to go." Exact words? ("I must go." or "I have to go.")
When is backshift optional? "She said she lives in Rome." Does she still live in Rome now? (Yes, so no change is OK.) · "He says he's tired." Is says past? (No, present: no backshift.) · Not sure? (Backshift. It's always safe after said.)

Meaning: time and place. Change them only if the time or place has really changed.

On Monday Tom said, "I'll call you tomorrow." Today is Wednesday. Is "tomorrow" still tomorrow? (No! It was Tuesday.) So: Tom said he would call me… (the next day). · Your friend said it this morning. Today → that day? (No, it's still today.)

Meaning: say vs tell.

"She told me she was busy." Who did she speak to? (Me.) · "She said she was busy." Do we know who she spoke to? (No, not in the sentence.) · Can I say "She said me…"? (No! "She told me…") · "She told to me"? (No to.)

Form.

Statementsubject + said + (that) + backshifted clauseHe said (that) he was hungry.
With a personsubject + told + person + (that) + clauseShe told me (that) she had lost her phone.
Present → pastam / is → was · are → were · work → worked · am working → was working"We're watching TV." → They said they were watching TV.
→ past perfecthave / has + p.p. → had + p.p. · past simple → had + p.p."I saw it." → He said he had seen it.
Modalswill → would · can → could · may → might · must → had to · would / could / might / should stay"You can borrow my car." → He told me I could borrow his car.
Alsois going to → was going to"I'm going to buy a car." → She said she was going to buy a car.

Pronunciation. that is weak /ðət/ or dropped completely in speech: "She said she was tired" is the normal spoken form. told /toʊld/: say the final d; learners who drop it say "tol' me," which is fine in fast speech but write the d. The past perfect is usually contracted: "He said he'd finished" /hid/, and would too: "She said she'd call." Teach learners to hear "he'd" as either he had or he would (the next verb tells you: he'd finished = had · he'd call = would). Storytelling intonation: a high, excited start: "Guess what! She told me she was getting married!"

Drill script
  1. Rewind drill (point at a backshift card): T: "am" → S: "was." T: "will" → S: "would." T: "can" → S: "could." T: "have seen" → S: "had seen." T: "must" → S: "had to." T: "might" → S: "might! No change." Choral, then fast individuals.
  2. Report it: T (as a character, with a gesture): "I'm hungry!" → S: "He said he was hungry." T: "I've lost my keys!" → S: "She said she'd lost her keys." T: "I'll pay you back." → S: "She said she would pay me back."
  3. Say or tell? T gives a report without the verb and a person or no person: T: "… me she was busy" → S: "She told me she was busy." T: "… she was busy" → S: "She said she was busy."
  4. Time travel (point at the Monday / Wednesday calendar): T: "tomorrow" → S: "the next day." T: "yesterday" → S: "the day before." T: "here" → S: "there." T: "two weeks ago" → S: "two weeks before."
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 says a true or invented line ("I'm going to the dentist tomorrow."); S2 turns to S3: "Ana said she was going to the dentist the next day." Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Gossip Chain — how to run it
  • Lines of 5–6 learners, standing or sitting in a row. The first learner reads a news card (about an invented person in the "Maple Street" apartment building) for 30 seconds, then gives the card back to you.
  • The first learner whispers a report, not the exact words: "Mrs. Lee said she was moving to Florida the next month. She said she had sold her car…" Each learner passes on what they heard, starting "[Name] said…" or "[Name] told me…" (not "you said"). One whisper only; no "Can you say it again?"
  • The last learner writes the report on a paper slip. Hold up the card and read the original. Score: 1 point for each correct backshifted verb in the written report, and 1 bonus point if the news is still basically true. The funniest changes get a laugh, not a penalty.
  • Rotate: the first person moves to the end, so a new learner reads the next card. Give the strongest speaker the job of the last person (writer and checker) in round 1: it keeps them busy and gives the quieter learners the speaking turns.
  • The gossip is about the people on the cards only, never about classmates or real neighbors. Say it before you start: "These people are not real. Please be kind."
  • Last 4 minutes: pairs report 3 real things someone said to them this week ("My daughter told me she wanted a cat."), or 3 lines from a new identity card.
ICQs before the Gossip Chain
Do you say the exact words from the card? (No, you report: "She said she was…") · What are the first words? ("Mrs. Lee said…" / "Mrs. Lee told me…") · How many times can you whisper? (Once.) · Who writes? (The last person.) · Can we gossip about people in our class? (No! Only the card people.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on three changes only: am / is → was, will → would, can → could, plus I → he / she. Give a phrase strip: "He said he was… / She said she would… / She told me she could…". In the Gossip Chain, stand next to the learner and read the news card aloud once, quietly; give them the middle of the line (listen and repeat, not first or last). Target: 3 spoken reports with was / would / could.

Stretch

Use a variety of reporting verbs beyond said / told: explained, mentioned, promised, admitted ("He admitted he had forgotten."). Notice when backshift is optional ("She said she lives in Rome," still true). In the chain, add a reaction: "…and I said I couldn't believe it!" Early finishers rewrite the Sam and Alex dialogue as a report from Sam's point of view.

L1 watch-outs
  • "She said me…" / "She told to me…": Spanish me dijo, Portuguese me disse, French elle m'a dit, Italian mi ha detto, Arabic qāl-li all put the person right after "say." Point at the key card every time: "tell needs a person; say doesn't."
  • No backshift: "He said he is tired." Russian, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Tagalog and Thai keep the original tense in reported speech (Russian он сказал, что устал literally "he said that he is tired"). It is understood. Recast and use the rewind gesture (a hand turning backward).
  • He / she mix-ups in the pronoun change: Tagalog (siya), Chinese (spoken tā), Turkish (o), Persian, Finnish and Hungarian use one word for "he" and "she." "My sister said he was tired" is very common. Point at the person on the news card.
  • Missing was / were: "She said she tired." Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Vietnamese and Haitian Creole speakers may drop be, which also removes the backshift. Color the be verb on the board.
  • Over-changing time words: learners who memorized the list change "today" even when it is still today. Keep asking: "Is it still tomorrow?"

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: a reported question is not a question any more. It is part of a statement, so it has statement word order and a period.

"She asked where I lived." Is this a question? (No, a sentence about a question.) Do we need a question mark? (No.) Do we need did? (No.) What were her exact words? ("Where do you live?")
"He asked if I was ready." What was the question? ("Are you ready?") Was there a wh- word? (No, it was a yes/no question.) So what joins the report? (if or whether.) Is if here about a condition? (No, it means "whether.")

Meaning: commands and requests.

"The doctor told me to rest." Did the doctor ask a question? (No, an instruction.) Who has to rest? (Me.) · "She asked me to close the door." Was it an order or a polite request? (A polite request.) · "He told me not to worry." Should I worry? (No.)
"She reminded me to call the bank." Did I forget? (Maybe, she helped me remember.) · "He warned us not to go there." Is it dangerous? (Yes, maybe.) · "She advised me to rest." Is it an order? (No, a recommendation.)

Form.

Wh- questionasked (+ person) / wanted to know + wh- word + subject + verb (backshifted)"Where do you work?" → She asked where I worked. · "What time does it start?" → He asked what time it started.
Yes/No questionasked (+ person) + if / whether + subject + verb"Can you drive?" → He asked if I could drive. · "Did you call?" → She asked whether I had called.
Commandtold + person + to + base verb"Open your books." → The teacher told us to open our books.
Requestasked + person + to + base verb"Please wait for me." → My friend asked me to wait for her.
Negativetold / asked + person + not to + base verb"Don't be late." → Mom asked me not to be late.
More verbsremind, warn, advise, order + person + (not) to…She reminded me to call the bank. · He warned us not to go there.

Pronunciation. A reported question falls at the end, like a statement: "She asked where I lived. ↘" (no rise). Learners often rise because their ear hears "a question": model the contrast. whether sounds like weather /ˈwɛðər/: tell learners so; they are often surprised and pleased. asked is one syllable: /æskt/ ("askt"); the -sk-t cluster is hard, and many learners say "ask" or "as-ked." Drill it slowly: "ass – k – t → askt." In commands, to is weak /tə/ and not to is stressed on not: "She told me NOT to worry."

Drill script
  1. Question → report (T asks, learners report what T asked, pointing at themselves): T: "Where do you live?" → S: "You asked where I lived." T: "What are you doing?" → S: "You asked what I was doing." T: "Why did you leave?" → S: "You asked why I had left."
  2. Yes/no → if: T: "Are you hungry?" → S: "You asked if I was hungry." T: "Do you speak Spanish?" → S: "You asked whether I spoke Spanish." T: "Have you finished?" → S: "You asked if I had finished."
  3. Pronunciation: choral asked /æskt/ ×3, whether = weather ×2, then the falling report: "She asked where I lived. ↘"
  4. Command or request? T says it in two voices: firm "Sit down!" → S: "You told me to sit down." Polite "Could you sit down, please?" → S: "You asked me to sit down." T: "Don't touch it!" → S: "You told me not to touch it."
  5. Chain: S1 asks S2 a real question ("Do you like coffee?"); S2 answers; S3 reports both: "Ana asked Omar if he liked coffee. He said he did."
Survey Report — how to run it
  • Six survey topics (Food, Weekends, Phones & apps, Learning English, Music & movies, Work & dream jobs), 5 questions each. Give neighbors different topics so the reports are new to the group.
  • Every card mixes wh- and yes/no questions, so learners must choose between the wh- word and if / whether in the report.
  • The mingle is fast: ask 3 people, write 1–3 words per answer. The report is the main event. In groups, each learner reports what they asked and what the person said, and at least one question someone asked them: "Lina asked me whether I had a favorite app."
  • Anyone can answer "Pass!" or answer as their new identity card. The questions are light on purpose; learners may not add personal questions about family, age or home country.
  • Dominant speakers: in the group stage, each learner has two turns (use the talking-turn token). A learner who finishes early listens and checks: "Did she remove the do?" This gives the strong speaker a useful job.
Message Relay — how to run it
  • Before class, tape the 8 message cards on the walls, far from the desks: the boss, the doctor's office, the building manager, the child's school, the pharmacist, the neighbor, the car mechanic, the dentist's office. Each card has 3–4 lines of direct speech: a command, a negative command, a polite request and a question.
  • Pairs: A = runner, B = writer. The runner may not carry the card or a pen. They read one line, walk back and report it: "The pharmacist told you to take one pill twice a day. She asked if you had any allergies." The writer writes a short to-do on the "While you were out" note ("take 1 pill ×2 a day").
  • After 2 cards, swap roles. At the end, pairs walk to their cards and check their notes. A note that is correct in meaning is a success, even if the report had small errors.
  • The messages are everyday and routine on purpose (a repair visit, a lab test, school pick-up time). Keep it that way; don't add eviction, debt or bad medical news.
ICQs before the Survey and the Relay
Survey: How many people do you ask? (Three.) · Do you write long answers? (No, 1–3 words.) · In your group, what do you report? (What I asked, what they said, and one question someone asked me.) · "Where do you live?" is OK on this card? (No! Only the card questions.)
Relay: Can the runner take the card? (No.) · How many lines at a time? (One.) · What does the writer write? (A short to-do.) · What words start the report? ("Your boss told you to… / asked if…") · When do you swap? (After 2 cards.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with two frames only: "She asked me if I…" and "He told me to…". Give a survey card with only 3 yes/no questions and picture cues. In the Relay, pair the learner as runner with a partner who writes, and let them use the picture on each message card (doctor, teacher, mechanic) as a memory hook. Target: 3 reported yes/no questions and 3 reported instructions.

Stretch

Use wanted to know, wondered, whether or not, and the full set of verbs (remind, warn, advise, order, beg, invite). In the Relay, combine lines into one smooth report ("She reminded you to bring your card and asked whether you could come ten minutes early."). Early finishers write a 6-line job-interview dialogue and report it.

L1 watch-outs
  • Question word order in the report: "She asked where did I live?" / "I asked him why was he angry." Almost everyone does this, because the ear expects a question. Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, Arabic and Chinese learners may also keep the question intonation. Use the red pen on the board: cross out do / did and "?" every time.
  • "He asked was I ready" (no if): Russian (li is placed after the verb), Chinese and Vietnamese (a question particle at the end) have no joining word like if. Drill "asked if…" as one chunk.
  • "She told me that I sit down" / "She said me to go": Spanish and Portuguese use decir que + subjunctive (me dijo que me sentara), Italian mi ha detto di, French elle m'a dit de. Show the English chunk: told me to + verb.
  • "The doctor told me don't eat": Chinese, Vietnamese and Tagalog speakers may keep the direct command. Also "told me to not worry." Teach not to as one piece: "told me not to eat."
  • "asked to me" / "asked for me to help" (Spanish le pidió a, Arabic ṭalab min "asked from"). ask takes the person directly: "asked me to…"
  • /æskt/: Spanish, Arabic, Vietnamese, Japanese and Korean speakers find the -skt cluster hard and may say "ask," "as-ked" or "ass-uh-ku-to." Accept "ask" in fast speech early on, but model "askt."

Relative Clauses — Joining Ideas

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • choose the right relative pronoun: who (people), which (things), that (people or things, defining only), where (places), when (times), whose (possession)
  • build defining clauses that say which one, with no commas: The woman who called you is my aunt.
  • build non-defining clauses that add an extra fact, with two commas, no that: My brother, who lives in Chicago, is a nurse.
  • drop the pronoun only when it is the object of a defining clause: The movie (that) I watched was great.
  • define people, places and things in a game, join two short sentences into one, and describe six people, places and things in their life

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: a relative clause describes the noun just before it. It joins two ideas into one sentence.

"That's the woman whose car was stolen." Whose car was stolen? (The woman's. Her car.) · "This is the café where we met." Is where a question here? (No, it means "in this café.") · "I remember the day when we moved." Is when about a time or a place? (A time.)

Meaning: defining vs non-defining.

"My sister who lives in Rome is a lawyer." (no commas) How many sisters do I have? (Two or more.) Why do I say "who lives in Rome"? (To say which sister.) · "My sister, who lives in Rome, is a lawyer." (commas) How many sisters? (One.) Is "who lives in Rome" needed? (No, it's extra.)
"Paris, which is the capital of France, is beautiful." Do we know which Paris? (Yes.) So: commas or no commas? (Commas: it's extra.) · Can I say "Paris, that is the capital…"? (No! No that after a comma.)
Dropping: "The movie I watched was great." Is a word missing? (Yes, that / which.) Is it OK? (Yes, I is the new subject.) · "The man called me was angry." OK? (No! who is the subject: "The man who called me.")

Form.

Pronounswho people · which things · that people / things (defining) · where places · when times · whose possessionThat's the man whose dog barks all night.
Definingnoun + pronoun + clause · no commas · that OKThe keys that open the garage are lost.
Non-definingnoun, + who / which + clause, · two commas · no that · never drop the pronounOur new house, which we bought last year, has a garden.
Drop the pronoundefining + object only (a new subject follows)The people (who) we met were lovely. · ❌ The woman lives here…
where = which … inwhere + subject + verbThe house where I live = the house which I live in.

Pronunciation. In speech, a non-defining clause is a little "aside": a short pause at each comma and a slightly lower, faster voice inside: "My brother | who lives in Chicago | is a nurse." A defining clause has no pause: "The woman who called you is my aunt." Use your hands: push the extra fact to the side. whose and who's sound the same /huz/; only writing shows the difference. that in a defining clause is weak /ðət/. which and witch sound the same; the wh is /w/ in American English.

Drill script
  1. Which pronoun? (hold up a flashcard) T: nurse → S: "a person who…" · fridge → "a thing which…" · post office → "a place where…" · summer → "a time when…"
  2. Join it: T: "I have a friend. She speaks five languages." → S: "I have a friend who speaks five languages." T: "This is the book. It changed my life." → S: "This is the book that changed my life."
  3. Pause or no pause? T reads; learners show ✋ (pause, commas) or 👉 (no pause, no commas): "My mother, who is a nurse, works nights." ✋ · "The bus that goes to town leaves at seven." 👉 · "Venice, which is full of canals, is sinking." ✋
  4. Drop it? T: "The movie that I watched…" → S: "The movie I watched." ✂️ T: "The man who called me…" → S: "Keep it!"
  5. whose substitution: T: "I know a boy. His father is a pilot." → S: "I know a boy whose father is a pilot." T: "That's the woman. Her car was stolen." → S: "That's the woman whose car was stolen."
"Who am I? / What is it?" — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, a set of 24 cards face down: 8 people (jobs), 8 places, 8 things. Each card has a picture, the word and two "Don't say" words.
  • A player takes a card and defines it without saying the word, using a relative clause: "It's a person who… / It's a place where… / It's a thing which (that)… / You use it when…". The first person to guess correctly keeps the card. Most cards wins.
  • Level 1: the "Don't say" words are allowed. Level 2 (stretch): the player may not say the "Don't say" words either ("chef: don't say cook or restaurant").
  • The guessers must answer with a full sentence: "Is it a nurse? A nurse is a person who helps doctors!"
  • Dominant speakers: the guesser who wins a card must wait one turn before guessing again. This gives quieter learners time to think.
ICQs before the game and the sentence strips
Game: Can you say the word on the card? (No!) · What words start your definition? ("It's a person who… / a place where… / a thing which…") · Who keeps the card? (The first person who guesses.)
Strips: How many sentences do you make from two strips? (One.) · When do you use the comma cards? (When the information is extra.) · Can you put that after a comma? (No.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on defining clauses with who, which and where only. In the game, use the picture side of the card and three frames on a strip: "a person who… / a thing which… / a place where…". Give only the first 5 sentence strips (all defining). Target: 4 spoken definitions and 3 joined sentences. The commas can wait.

Stretch

Use whose and when in the game. In the strips, find the two sentences that can be both defining and non-defining and explain the difference in meaning. In "my people, places & things," use two non-defining clauses and one which is why…. Early finishers write 3 sentences that change meaning with commas.

L1 watch-outs
  • The extra pronoun: "The book which I read it" / "The man who I saw him." Arabic, Hebrew, Persian, Turkish (in some forms), Irish and many Chinese speakers use a "resumptive" pronoun. The lesson's "double subject" warning ("The book which it changed my life") is the same error. Cross out the extra pronoun.
  • Clause in the wrong place or avoided: Chinese, Japanese and Korean put the describing clause before the noun (like "the lives-in-Canada friend"), so learners may avoid relative clauses and use two sentences. Praise every attempt; accuracy comes later.
  • who / which mix-ups: "the man which…" or "the car who…": Spanish que, French qui / que, Portuguese que and Tagalog na / -ng use one word for people and things. The board symbols (👤 / 📦) help.
  • "My car, that is red, …": Spanish, Portuguese and Italian allow que / che after a comma. Teach: comma → who / which.
  • Commas everywhere: German (and Russian, Polish, Czech) put a comma before every relative clause, so learners write "The man, who lives next door, is a doctor" for a defining clause. Show that the commas change the meaning in English.
  • whose / who's: same sound for everyone. Spelling only: who's = who is.
