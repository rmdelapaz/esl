Module 2 — If & Imagining (Conditionals)

Three classes that move from the real world to the imagined one. First, the zero conditional for facts, habits and life hacks and the first conditional for real, likely futures (with when and unless). Then the second conditional for dreams, dilemmas and advice (If I were you, I'd…). Last, wish / if only for small, everyday wishes about the present, and wish + would for mild annoyances.

3 lessons Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C4–C6 Weeks 2–3 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-conditionals.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C4. It holds the flashcards, the "imagine a person" identity cards, the listening scripts, the Life Hacks & Superstitions card sort, the conditional-plans role-play, the dilemma cards, the advice role-play, the Small Wishes, Real Fixes cards and the Wish Mingle (Find Someone Who).
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • This is extension, not a first meeting. A2 Lesson 6.1 taught the first conditional (If it rains, I'll stay home). Most B1 learners will recognize it, and many will still say "If it will rain." Check fast in the lead-in and spend your time on the new B1 work: zero vs first, when vs if, unless, the second conditional, were, and wish. Keep models inside B1: no third or mixed conditionals and no wish + had done (past regrets are B2). If a strong learner asks, give one example and say "That's for the next level."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (apartment, roommate, vacation, soccer, movies, gray, neighbor, practice, organized, apologize; temperatures in °F with °C in parentheses). Accept British forms (flat, flatmate, holiday, football, films, grey, neighbour, practise, apologise) when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Real → imagined → wished for. In C4 learners talk about what is always true (life hacks, habits, facts) and what will really happen (If I finish early on Friday, I'll cook something nice). In C5 they step out of reality: If I found a wallet, I'd…, If I were you, I'd…. In C6 they use the same "unreal past" to say what they want to be different right now: I wish it were sunny. I wish I could sing. I wish the bus would come on time. The one big idea across the module: the verb shows how real the situation is. Present = real. Past = unreal (not past time!). Build the "reality line" on the board in C4 and add to it in C5 and C6.

Trauma-informed: keep the "what ifs" and wishes small

Conditionals and wishes can open painful doors for adult newcomers: "What would you do if you could go home?", "I wish my family were here," "If only I had stayed…". Never ask about home countries, family far away, migration, papers, war, illness or money worries, and never use them as examples. Every task in this module uses light, everyday topics: life hacks, superstitions, a found wallet, a lottery win, superpowers, the weather, skills, the commute, the phone, a noisy neighbor. For every personal task offer three choices out loud: your real answer, an "imagine a person" card (in the classroom materials), or an invented answer. Anyone can say "Pass" at any time. Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong. If a learner shares something heavy anyway, thank them simply ("Thank you for telling us"), don't dig, and move the group on to the next card.

B1 speaking: fluency first, and room for everyone

Fluency before accuracy. In every freer stage, don't interrupt to correct. Walk around with a notepad, write down 4–6 sentences you hear (good ones and wrong ones), and use them in the Feedback stage with no names. First give feedback on content ("Which dilemma got the most different answers?"), then on language. Correct on the spot only in the drill and controlled stages. Dominant speakers: give each learner three talking tokens (paper clips, coins) per discussion; each turn costs one, and when yours are gone you listen and ask questions. Give roles: asker (reads the card), timekeeper (1 minute per person), reporter (tells the class). Always go around the circle once before open discussion, and say "Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." Quiet learners can write their answer first and then read it.

Before the Module

  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board): life hacks & superstitions, dreams & dilemmas, small wishes (weather, skills, commute). Card stock helps. Cut them before C4.
  • For C4: one Life Hacks & Superstitions card sort set per group of 3 (32 half-cards + 3 column headers). Cut and put each set in an envelope. Bring sticky notes (or strips of paper and tape) for the class "Life Hack Wall."
  • For C5: one set of conditional-plans role-play cards per pair (warmer), one set of dilemma cards per group of 3–4 + a reaction-phrase strip for each learner, and one set of advice role-play A/B cards per pair.
  • For C6: one set of Small Wishes, Real Fixes cards per pair and one Wish Mingle (Find Someone Who) sheet per learner.
  • Print the "imagine a person" cards and keep them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking.
  • Talking tokens for the discussions: 3 paper clips or coins per learner. For the C4 lead-in, a wooden spoon (or a photo of a spoon across a pot) is a fun prop.

Zero & First Conditional — Life Hacks, Superstitions & Real Plans

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use the zero conditional (If / When + present simple, present simple) for facts, habits and life hacks: If you freeze grapes, they make great ice cubes.
  • use the first conditional (If + present simple, will / won't + base verb) for one real, likely future: If it rains this afternoon, I'll take the bus.
  • keep will out of the if-clause and the when-clause (when I get home, not "when I will get home")
  • choose when (sure) or if (possible), and use unless (= if … not) with a positive verb
  • put a comma after the if-clause when it comes first, and use can / might or an imperative in the result
  • share and discuss life hacks and superstitions, and say 6+ real plans and warnings about their own week

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning 1: zero conditional = always true. Facts, rules, how a machine works, a person's regular habit, a life hack. The result happens every time.

"If you heat ice, it melts." Is this about one day, or every time? (Every time.) Is it a fact? (Yes.) · "When I'm tired, I drink coffee." Is this a habit? (Yes.) Can I say "If I'm tired" here? (Yes, the same meaning.)

Meaning 2: first conditional = one real, likely future. The speaker thinks it can really happen.

"If it rains this afternoon, I'll take the bus." Is it raining now? (No.) Is rain possible this afternoon? (Yes, maybe.) Is this about every day or about this afternoon? (This afternoon.) Is it a plan with a condition? (Yes.)
"If it will rain, I'll take the bus." Is this correct? (No!) Where does will go? (In the result, the other half.) · "When I get home, I'll call you." Will I get home? (Yes, I'm sure.) "If I get the job, I'll celebrate." Will I get the job? (Maybe.)
"I'll be late unless I leave now." Am I leaving now? (We don't know yet.) What happens if I don't leave now? (I'll be late.) Is "unless I don't leave" OK? (No, the "not" is inside unless.)

Form. Color the if-clause in one color and the result in another. Show that the halves can swap.

ZeroIf / When + present simple, present simpleIf you mix blue and yellow, you get green. · When I drink coffee late, I can't sleep.
FirstIf + present simple, will / won't + base verbIf we leave now, we won't be late. · If she calls, I'll tell her.
Other resultscan / might + base verb · imperativeIf you finish early, you can go home. · If you see John, tell him hi.
when / unlesswhen = sure · if = maybe · unless = if … not (+ positive verb)When I get home, I'll call you. · You won't pass unless you study.
CommaIf-clause first → comma · if-clause second → no commaIf I see her, I'll tell her. · I'll tell her if I see her.

Pronunciation. Use the contraction: I'll /aɪl/, it'll /ˈɪtəl/, we'll, won't /woʊnt/. The if-clause rises and waits, the result falls and finishes: "If it RAINS ↗ | I'll take the BUS. ↘". The pause at the comma is the "comma you can hear." unless is stressed on the second syllable: unLESS /ʌnˈlɛs/. Superstitions are often said with drama: "you'll have SEVen years of BAD luck!" — have fun with it.

Drill script
  1. Rise–fall chant: "If it rains ↗ | I'll take the bus ↘." Choral ×3 with a hand going up, then down. Half the room says the if-half, the other half the result. Swap. Then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Finish my sentence (zero): T: "If you heat ice…" → S: "…it melts." T: "When I'm hungry…" → S: "…I eat a snack." T: "If you don't water plants…" → S: "…they die."
  3. Chain (first): T: "If it's sunny on Saturday, I'll go to the park." S1: "If I go to the park, I'll play soccer." S2: "If I play soccer, I'll be tired." … around the room. Stop anyone who says "if I will" with the red ❌ gesture; they self-correct.
  4. Transform with unless: T: "I'll be late if I don't leave now." → S: "I'll be late unless I leave now." T: "You won't pass if you don't study." → S: "You won't pass unless you study."
  5. when or if? T gives a situation; learners choose: "I'm sure I'll get home at six." → "When I get home, I'll call you." / "Maybe I'll see Anna." → "If I see Anna, I'll give her the book."
Life Hacks & Superstitions card sort — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one envelope each. Step 1: match the if-halves (plain border) to the result halves (gray border) to make 16 sentences. The grammar is a clue: a present result or a will result.
  • Step 2: sort the sentences under the three headers: 🔧 Always true (life hacks, facts, habits → zero) · 🍀 Superstition (a belief) · 📅 One real future (a plan or warning → first). Check with the class (answers in the answer key). Ask: "Look at the superstitions. Zero or first? Why?" (First: people say them like predictions: you'll have bad luck.) One superstition uses the zero form (If your ears are burning, someone is talking about you) — a nice discussion point.
  • Step 3: discuss with talking tokens. Roles: the asker reads the questions on the header card, the timekeeper gives each person about a minute, the reporter remembers the best answer. Go around once before open discussion.
  • Step 4: each group writes 2 life hacks they really use (cooking, cleaning, phones, saving money, the bus) as zero conditionals on sticky notes and puts them on the Life Hack Wall. Leave the wall up for C5.
  • Superstitions are beliefs, and some are connected to religion or family. Nobody has to say what they believe, and we never laugh at a belief. Say: "You can talk about a superstition you know, one from a movie, or say 'Pass.'" Keep the talk on everyday, playful superstitions (mirrors, umbrellas, black cats, salt). If someone mentions a serious religious practice, thank them and move on.
ICQs before the card sort
How many sentences do you make? (Sixteen.) · After matching, how many groups? (Three: always true, superstition, one real future.) · In the discussion, do you have to say what you believe? (No. You can say "Pass.") · What does a token do? (One token = one turn.) · What do you write on the sticky notes? (Two real life hacks, with "If… / When…".)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give 8 of the 16 sentences only (the ones with picture flashcards: umbrella, mirror, salt, water, bread, phone, bus, coffee), and pair the learner with a partner who reads aloud. Focus on one pattern each: zero = "If…, it…" (present, present) and first = "If…, I'll…". Target: 3 spoken sentences with If…, I'll… about this week, using the picture cards as prompts, and one life hack said aloud (a partner writes the sticky note).

Stretch

Use as soon as and before / after with the same rule ("As soon as I get home, I'll call you."). In the discussion, give a reason for belief or doubt ("I don't believe it, because…"). Write a life hack with unless ("Your bread goes hard unless you keep it in a bag."). Early finishers write three warnings for a new learner at the school ("If you're late, the door will be locked. Unless you bring your book, you won't…").

L1 watch-outs
  • "If it will rain" / "When I will get home": Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, French (quand je serai), Portuguese (quando eu chegar is subjunctive, but learners may translate as "will"), Tagalog (kung uulan, future aspect) and Korean speakers use a future form in the condition. This is the #1 error. Use the red ❌ gesture every time and praise self-correction.
  • "If it rains, I stay home" (present in the result for one future): Spanish, German, Dutch and Chinese speakers (the present can mean the future in the L1). It is understood; recast with I'll.
  • "If it rain tomorrow, I go" (no tense marking at all): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Haitian Creole speakers. Focus on the -s in the if-clause and the 'll in the result. Vietnamese speakers may also drop final /l/ in I'll, so "I'll go" sounds like "I go": exaggerate the l.
  • "unless you don't…" (double negative): many learners, especially Spanish (a menos que), French and Arabic speakers who translate "if not" word for word. Teach: unless already has the "not" in it.
  • when vs if: German wenn means both "if" and "when," so German and Dutch speakers may say "When I win the lottery…". Also Chinese 如果 / 要是 and Arabic إذا cover both. CCQ: "Are you sure?"
  • No comma / comma everywhere: learners from Arabic, Chinese and Japanese writing traditions punctuate differently. Teach the one rule, and don't mark it hard in speaking tasks.

2.2 · C5

Second Conditional — Dreams, Dilemmas & Advice

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the second conditional: If + past simple, would / 'd / wouldn't + base verb: If I won the lottery, I'd buy a little house by the sea.
  • understand that the past verb means unreal, not past time (If I had a car… = I don't have a car now)
  • choose first (real, likely) or second (imaginary, unlikely) conditional for the same idea
  • use were for all persons, and give friendly advice with If I were you, I'd… / I wouldn't…
  • use could and might in the result: If I had a car, I could drive you home.
  • discuss everyday dilemmas: say what they would do, give a reason, and agree or disagree politely

If you run long

This class has a lot of talk, which is good. Cut the advice role-play to 3 cards each (the workbook Ex 4 covers the rest at home) and do only 4 sentences of Script 2.2-B. Never cut the dilemma discussion: it is the real communicative task of the lesson and where I'd… becomes automatic.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: imaginary, unlikely or impossible — now or in the future. The speaker steps out of reality.

"If I won the lottery, I'd quit my job." Did I win the lottery? (No.) Is it likely? (No, very unlikely.) Is won about the past? (No! It's about an imagined now / future.) Am I going to quit my job? (No, it's a dream.)
"If I had a car, I'd drive you home." Do I have a car? (No.) Can I drive you home? (No.) · "If animals could talk, life would be strange." Can animals talk? (No, impossible.)

Meaning: first vs second = how likely do I think it is?

"If I have time, I'll call you." / "If I had time, I'd call you." Which one: maybe I'll have time? (The first.) Which one: I don't have time? (The second.) · "If it rains, I'll stay home." Is rain realistic? (Yes.) "If it rained gold, we'd all be rich." Realistic? (No, impossible.)

Meaning: advice.

"If I were you, I'd take the job." Am I you? (No, impossible.) Is it advice? (Yes.) Is it strong like an order? (No, soft and friendly.) Is it softer than "You should take the job"? (Yes.) · "If I were you, I wouldn't worry." Should you worry? (No.)

Form.

PositiveIf + past simple, would ('d) + base verbIf I had more time, I'd learn to paint. · She'd be happier if she lived near the sea.
Negativedidn't + verb · wouldn't + base verbIf they didn't work so much, they wouldn't be so tired.

Pronunciation. In speech would is almost always 'd: I'd /aɪd/, you'd /jud/, she'd, we'd, they'd. If learners drop the /d/ ("I buy a house"), the sentence sounds real, not imagined, so this matters: exaggerate the /d/ at first. Before a /t/ or /d/ sound it almost disappears ("I'd take it") — accept that. wouldn't is /ˈwʊdənt/: the l is silent, and the middle is a small /ə/. were is weak in "If I were you" /ɪf aɪ wər ju/, stressed only in short answers. The question What would you do if…? links: "what-wud-ya-DO". The if-clause rises ↗, the result falls ↘, as in C4.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "by the sea" → "a house by the sea" → "I'd buy a house by the sea" → "If I won the lottery, I'd buy a house by the sea." Choral ×3, halves of the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Make it a dream (first → second): T: "If I have time, I'll call you." → S: "If I had time, I'd call you." T: "If she knows, she'll tell us." → S: "If she knew, she'd tell us." T: "If it's warm, we'll swim." → S: "If it were warm, we'd swim."
  3. Question and answer chain: S1 to S2: "What would you do if you had a free day tomorrow?" S2: "I'd sleep late! What would you do if you could fly?" S2 → S3 … (open pairs, then closed pairs for 1 minute).
  4. were drill: T: "I / rich" → S: "If I were rich…" T: "it / warmer" → S: "If it were warmer…" T: "he / here" → S: "If he were here…" Then finish each with I'd / we'd / he'd.
  5. Advice: T mimes a problem (yawning, shaking a slow phone, looking at a watch). Learners: "If I were you, I'd go to bed earlier." / "…I'd buy a new phone." / "…I'd leave earlier."
Dilemma discussion — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4. Dilemma cards face down, one reaction-phrase strip each, 3 talking tokens each. Roles: asker (turns and reads the card), timekeeper (about 1 minute per person), reporter. Change the asker with every card.
  • Round-robin first: everyone answers the card in turn with a full sentence and a reason: "If I found a wallet, I'd take it to the police station, because the owner might go there." The options A / B / C on the card help; "your own idea" is always allowed.
  • Then open discussion (tokens: one per turn): "I'd do the same." · "Really? I wouldn't. I'd…" · "That depends. If there was a phone number, I'd call." · "Why would you…?"
  • All dilemmas are light and everyday on purpose (a found wallet, too much change, a lottery win, a superpower, an ugly gift, a free vacation). Some touch on honesty; there is no one right answer, and nobody is judged. Remind the class: "These are imaginary. Answer as yourself, as your 'imagine a person' card, or invent an answer. You can say 'Pass' and take the next card."
  • Walk around and note sentences for feedback. Don't correct during the discussion (fluency first). If one learner is doing all the talking, stop by and say, "Let's hear from someone who still has tokens."
  • Fast groups: use the two blank cards to write their own light dilemma for another group.
ICQs before the advice role-play and the dilemma discussion
Advice cards: Who has the problem, A or B? (A.) What are B's first words? ("If I were you, I'd…") · "If I was you" or "If I were you"? (Were.)
Dilemmas: Who reads the card? (The asker.) · First, does everyone answer, or only one person? (Everyone, in turn.) · What do you add after your answer? (A reason, with "because".) · What does a token do? (One token, one turn.) · Don't like a card? (Say "Pass" and take the next one.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the picture dilemma cards (the emoji + a partner reading aloud) and the phrase strip "If I…, I'd… because…". Give a ready answer frame on a card: "If I found a wallet, I'd _____." Target: the chunk I'd… + one verb, for 4 dilemmas, and one "If I were you, I'd…" piece of advice. Don't insist on were vs was at this stage.

Stretch

Use could and might in answers ("I might keep a little for myself — no, I'm joking!"). Answer with a condition inside a condition ("If there was an address, I'd take it back myself, but if not, I'd…"). In the report stage, compare the group ("Most of us would…, but…"). Early finishers write their own light dilemma card for another group.

L1 watch-outs
  • "If I would have money…" (would in both halves): German (wenn ich … hätte / würde), Dutch, Swedish and Norwegian speakers, and many others by analogy. Teach: would lives in the result only. Use the same red ❌ gesture as for "if … will" in C4.
  • "If I have money, I would…" (present in the if-clause): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian speakers (no past form to signal "unreal"), and Japanese / Korean speakers whose conditional is one suffix. Point at the board: "Dream → past."
  • "If I had money, I bought a car" (past in both halves): Russian and Ukrainian (если бы…, я бы купил), Polish. Their L1 uses the past in both halves; the English result needs would.
  • Dropped 'd: "If I won, I buy a house." Spanish, Portuguese, Vietnamese and Chinese speakers often drop final /d/. Use the ✋ / 💭 listening game and exaggerate the /d/.
  • "If I am you" / "If I was you": Spanish (si yo fuera tú) and Portuguese speakers usually get were easily (subjunctive); Arabic, Chinese and Tagalog speakers may say "If I am you." Teach "If I were you, I'd…" as one fixed chunk.
  • Strong "should" advice: learners from cultures with direct advice (German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew) may sound bossy with "You must…". Learners from indirect cultures (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Filipino) may find any advice uncomfortable. "If I were you…" is the polite English middle.

2.3 · C6

Wish & If Only — Small Wishes, Real Fixes

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use wish / if only + past simple for a present wish: I wish I had more free time. If only it were Friday!
  • understand the "flip": a wish is the opposite of reality (I wish I lived near work = I don't live near work)
  • use were for all persons after wish (I wish it were sunny), and wish + could for ability (I wish I could sing)
  • use wish + would to complain kindly about someone or something else (I wish the bus would come on time), never about themselves
  • choose hope (possible) or wish (not real now)
  • say 6+ small, everyday wishes (weather, skills, commute) and suggest a real fix with a conditional

  • Workbook 2.3 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "My six small wishes")
  • Slides 14–18 · Flashcards (small wishes: weather, skills, commute) · Wish Mingle sheet (1 per learner) · Small Wishes, Real Fixes cards (1 set per pair) · "imagine a person" cards
  • Listening scripts 2.3-A (Maya and Leo on a gray day) and 2.3-B (dictation)

Keep wishes small — say it out loud

Before the mingle and before the Small Wishes task, say: "Today we make small, everyday wishes: the weather, a skill, the bus, your phone, a noisy neighbor. You don't need to share anything private. Choose a card, invent a wish, or say 'Pass'." Never give or invite examples about family far away, home countries, migration, health or money problems, and don't ask "What do you wish about your life?" The online lesson's "Mindset" box and its "I wish I hadn't eaten so much last night" sentence (a past wish) are not used in class. If a learner offers a deep regret anyway, say "Thank you for sharing that," and gently move to the next card.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: wish + past = I want the present to be different. The past form is the same "unreal" signal as in the second conditional.

"I wish I lived closer to work." Do I live close to work? (No.) Do I want to? (Yes.) Is it about the past? (No, now.) · "I wish I didn't have so much homework." Do I have a lot of homework? (Yes!)
"I wish it were sunny." Is it sunny now? (No.) "If only it were Friday!" Is it Friday? (No.) Which is stronger, I wish or If only? (If only.)

Meaning: wish + could = ability.

"I wish I could swim." Can I swim? (No.) Would I like to? (Yes.) · "I wish I can swim" — OK? (No. can → could.)

Meaning: wish + would = someone / something else, and I'm annoyed.

"I wish the bus would come on time." Does the bus come on time? (No.) Am I happy about it? (No, annoyed.) Is it about me or about the bus? (The bus.) · "I wish I would be taller" — OK? (No. About me → "I wish I were taller.")

Meaning: hope vs wish.

"I hope it's sunny tomorrow." Is it possible? (Yes, maybe.) "I wish it were sunny." Is it sunny now? (No.) Your friend has a job interview tomorrow. Hope or wish? ("I hope you get it!")

Form.

Present wishwish / If only + past simpleI wish I had more time. · She wishes she spoke Italian. · If only I knew the answer!
Negativewish + didn't + verb · wish + weren'tI wish I didn't have so much work. · I wish my phone weren't so slow.
bewish + were (all persons) · was = informalI wish I were taller. · I wish it were the weekend.
Abilitywish + could + base verbI wish I could sing. · He wishes he could drive.
Annoyancewish + [someone / something else] + would / wouldn't + verbI wish it would stop raining. · I wish he wouldn't interrupt me.
hopehope + present / will (real, possible)I hope it's sunny tomorrow. · I hope you can come.

Pronunciation. wish /wɪʃ/: a short /ɪ/ and a soft /ʃ/ ("sh"), not /wiʃ/ or /wis/. Stress falls on the wished-for word, not on wish: "I wish it were SUNny." · "I wish I could SING." were is weak /wər/. If only has strong stress and emotion: "If ONly it were FRIday!" (a sigh helps). could /kʊd/: silent l. wish + would often has a slightly annoyed, falling tone: "I wish the bus would COME." ↘

Drill script
  1. The flip (point at REAL NOW on the board): T: "It's raining." → S: "I wish it were sunny." T: "I can't swim." → S: "I wish I could swim." T: "I don't have a car." → S: "I wish I had a car." T: "I have a lot of work." → S: "I wish I didn't have so much work."
  2. Stress drill: "I wish it were SUNny." / "I wish I could SING." / "I wish I lived CLOSer." Choral, then individuals. Then If only with feeling: "If ONly it were FRIday!"
  3. Annoyance chain: T: "The bus is late again." → S1: "I wish the bus would come on time!" S1: "My neighbor's music is loud." → S2: "I wish my neighbor would turn it down!" … around the room.
  4. hope or wish? T: "Tomorrow's forecast: sunny." → S: "I hope it's sunny tomorrow." T: "Today: rain." → S: "I wish it were sunny today." T: "Your friend has a test tomorrow." → S: "I hope you pass!"
  5. Wish → fix (preview of the freer task): S1: "I wish I could cook rice better." S2: "If I were you, I'd watch a video." / "If you use less water, it'll be perfect."
Small Wishes, Real Fixes — how to run it
  • Pairs, one set of 16 cards face down: ☀️ weather, 🎸 skills, 🚌 commute, 🏠 home & phone. Each card has a small situation and a pattern hint (past / were / could / would).
  • A takes a card, reads the situation and makes a wish with feeling: "It's so cold. I wish it were warmer!" B answers in one of two ways: 💭 Dream: a second-conditional question: "If it were warmer, what would you do?" or 🔧 Fix: "If I were you, I'd…" / a first conditional: "If you wear gloves, your hands won't hurt." A answers or reacts ("Good idea!" / "I might try that."). Swap after every card.
  • Round 2: each learner writes 3 small wishes of their own (weather, a skill, the commute) in the workbook table, or copies wishes from a card or an "imagine a person" card. Share with a new partner, who suggests a fix.
  • Keep it light, practical and a little funny. There are no deep regrets in this task — see the amber box above.
ICQs before the mingle and Small Wishes
Mingle: What is the question? ("Do you wish you could…?" / "Do you wish it were…?") · "Yes, I do" → what do you do? (Write the name and ask the extra question.) · How many names per box? (One.)
Small Wishes: A makes a wish. What does B do? (Asks a dream question or gives a fix.) · Can B say "If I were you, I'd…"? (Yes.) · Big wishes or small wishes? (Small, everyday.) · Do you have to share your real wishes? (No. Card, invented, or "Pass.")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use two patterns only: I wish I could… (with the skill flashcards: sing, swim, dance, cook, play the guitar) and I wish it were… (with the weather flashcards: sunny, hot, cold). The partner reads the Small Wishes cards aloud. In the mingle, give a sheet with the questions fully written, and learners mark ✓ and the first letter of the name. Target: 4 spoken wishes and 1 "If I were you, I'd…".

Stretch

Add a second-conditional result to each wish: "I wish I lived closer to work. If I did, I'd walk every day." Use I wish there were… for the neighborhood ("I wish there were more buses at night."). In the fix, give two options with unless or might. Early finishers write a short, friendly complaint message to a roommate with one I wish you would… and one If you…, I'll….

L1 watch-outs
  • hope / wish mix-up: Arabic atamanna (أتمنى), Tagalog sana, Chinese xīwàng (希望), Vietnamese ước / hy vọng overlap, and French souhaiter can mean both. Expect "I wish you come to my party" and "I wish I will pass the test." Use the hope / wish drill: possible → hope.
  • "I wish I have / I wish I can" (present after wish): Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese and Arabic speakers. Point at the flip on the board: "Wish → one step back."
  • "I wish that you would come" for a polite invitation: Spanish speakers (ojalá vengas = I hope you come). Teach: invitation → "I hope you can come!"
  • "I wish I would…" about oneself: German and Dutch speakers (ich wünschte, ich würde) and many others by analogy. would is for other people and things.
  • /ɪ/ vs /i/ and /ʃ/: Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic and Tagalog speakers may say "weesh" or "wis" (Spanish has no /ʃ/ in many accents; Tagalog has /ʃ/ only in loans). Model "wish, fish, dish."
