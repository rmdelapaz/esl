Module 8 — Fluency & the Final Project

The last three classes of B1 are about sounding natural and keeping talk alive. In C23 learners use question tags (with the right intonation) and interested responses, follow-up questions and backchanneling to keep a conversation going. In C24 they build a practical set of phrasal verbs for work, home and relationships, and master the separable / inseparable rule. In C25 there is no new grammar: each learner gives a 3–4 minute presentation and takes part in a panel discussion. You observe with a checklist that feeds the C26 final speaking assessment.

3 lessons Lessons 8.1–8.3 Classes C23–C25 Weeks 12–13 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C25 is a task-based class: pre-task → planning → rehearsal → task → report.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-8-final-project.html. Print materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html before C23. It holds the flashcards, the intonation arrow cards, the small-talk mingle cards, the "Sort It Out" A/B cards, the Split or Stick? sorting game, the phrasal-verb talk cards, the presentation topic cards, the panel role cards, the presentation rubric, the listening scripts and the teacher observation checklist.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. Learners know auxiliaries from every question they have ever asked, Let's… from A2, and a few phrasal verbs (get up, turn on, look for) since A1. The new B1 work is the tag system and its intonation, the conversation "tools" as a set, the separable / inseparable rule with pronouns, and a long, structured speaking turn.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use some British forms: memorise, practise, organise, neighbour, rubbish, holiday, film, kettle, move house, "Did you not sleep well?" and get on (well) with. Teach memorize, practice, organize, neighbor, trash / garbage, vacation, movie, move, "Didn't you sleep well?" and get along with next to get on with. Let's…, shall we? is the normal tag in American English too. Accept British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Keep it going → say it naturally → stand up and speak. In C23 learners learn the small words that make a conversation two-way: a tag that invites a reply, an "Oh really?", a follow-up question, an "mm-hm." In C24 they learn the everyday verbs that real people use instead of textbook ones (find out, not discover; sort it out, not resolve it). In C25 they use everything from B1 in one long turn and one real discussion. The end of every class is the same freer skill in a bigger form: talking with people, not at them.

Fluency before accuracy — and the dominant speaker

This module is about fluency. During freer stages, do not interrupt to correct. Walk around with a notepad and write what you hear (good and not so good, no names). Correct after the task, on the board, as a class puzzle. Correct on the spot only when the error blocks meaning or when it is the target of a drill.

Dominant speakers are the biggest risk in C23 and C25: one confident learner can fill a whole conversation or panel. Use structure, not criticism: (1) talking tokens, 3 paper slips each, one slip per turn, the turn ends when your slips are gone; (2) give the confident learner a listening job: chair, timekeeper, or "follow-up question master" (they may only ask questions); (3) a visible timer on the screen for each speaker; (4) a quiet word in private: "You're a great speaker. Today can you help Mei and Tomás speak more? Ask them a question each time." Quiet learners get thinking time (10 seconds to note ideas before they speak) and a phrase strip.

Trauma-informed: small talk and presentations

Small talk can touch painful areas fast: home country, family, why someone moved, work status. The mingle cards use safe topics (the weather, food, the weekend, movies, the neighborhood, this class). Never make "Tell us about your country" a required topic. For the C25 presentation, every learner chooses: a real topic they care about (a hobby, a skill, a place, a change they want in the neighborhood), or a topic card with ready ideas. Nobody explains their choice. In the tag game, "You're from…, aren't you?" is not a model sentence: use "You like coffee, don't you?"

8.1 · C23

Question Tags & Keeping a Conversation Going

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form question tags with the mirror rule: positive statement → negative tag (You're coming, aren't you?), negative statement → positive tag (She doesn't smoke, does she?)
  • copy the auxiliary, be or modal into the tag, or use do / does / did when there is none (You live here, don't you? · They arrived late, didn't they?)
  • use the special cases: I'm late, aren't I? · Close the door, will you? · Let's start, shall we?
  • choose the intonation on purpose: falling ↘ = "I expect you to agree" · rising ↗ = "I'm really checking"
  • keep a conversation going with interest phrases (Oh really? · You're kidding!), echo questions (Did you? · Has she?), follow-up questions (What was it like?) and backchanneling (mm-hm, right, uh-huh, no way!)
Materials
  • Workbook 8.1 (New phrases, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, small-talk mingle)
  • Slides 3–9 · Tag flashcards + ↗ / ↘ arrow cards (1 pair per learner) · Small-talk mingle cards (1 per learner) + "Keep it going" strips · talking tokens (3 paper slips per learner)
  • Listening scripts 8.1-A (two conversations: flat and warm), 8.1-B (↗ or ↘?) and 8.1-C (dictation)

Left — the mirror: ➕ You're tired, aren't you? · ➖ He isn't ready, is he? · no helper? → do / does / did: She works late, doesn't she? · They went home, didn't they?

Center — special cases: I'm late, aren't I? · Pass the salt, will you? · Come in, won't you? · Let's go, shall we?

Right — two voices: ↘ "Nice day, isn't it?" = I'm sure. Agree with me! · ↗ "You haven't seen my keys, have you?" = I don't know. Tell me!

Bottom — keep it going: REACT (Oh really? / Did you?) → ASK (What was it like?) → SHARE (I… too!) · 👂 mm-hm · right · uh-huh · no way!

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A tag turns a statement into an invitation to reply. It checks something, asks for agreement, or simply keeps the other person in the conversation.

"It's cold today, isn't it?" Am I giving information only, or inviting you to answer? (Inviting.) What answer do I expect? ("Yes, it is!") Is it a real question? (Not really. I'm making conversation.)
"She doesn't eat meat, does she?" Is the first part positive or negative? (Negative.) So the tag is…? (Positive: does she.) Why does? (No helper verb in "eat," and it's present, she → does.)
"You haven't seen my keys, have you? ↗" Do I know the answer? (No.) Am I really asking? (Yes.) "Lovely weather, isn't it? ↘" Do I know the answer? (Yes, I think so.) Am I really asking? (No. I want you to agree.)
How do you answer "You don't drive, do you?" if you don't drive? ("No, I don't.") Answer the tag or the fact? (The fact.)

Form. Two questions every time: (1) Is my statement positive or negative? → flip it. (2) Which verb do I copy? → the helper (be, have, will, can, should…), or do / does / did if there is none. The subject becomes a pronoun (Tom → he, the kids → they).

Pronunciation. Tags are short and unstressed at the start, with the movement on the tag itself: "It's a LOVEly day, ↘ ISN'T it?" A falling tag starts high on the auxiliary and drops. A rising tag starts low and goes up, like a real yes/no question. Teach the hand signal: hand slides down = sure; hand goes up = checking. Contractions link: isn't it /ˈɪzənɪt/, didn't you /ˈdɪdənʧu/ ("didn-choo"), won't you /ˈwoʊnʧu/. Echo questions are usually rising and warm ("Did you? ↗"). Backchannel sounds are short and low ("mm-hm"); a surprised "No way!" jumps up high.

Drill script
  1. Tag ping-pong (you say the statement, learners give only the tag): "You're tired…" → "aren't you?" · "She works late…" → "doesn't she?" · "They went home…" → "didn't they?" · "He can't swim…" → "can he?" · "I'm next…" → "aren't I?" · "Let's go…" → "shall we?" Choral, then individuals, then faster.
  2. Back-chain a falling tag: "isn't it?" → "a beautiful day, isn't it?" → "It's a beautiful day, isn't it? ↘" Choral ×3, then half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Then the same with a rising tag: "You haven't seen my keys, have you? ↗"
  3. Same words, two meanings: "You're coming to the party, aren't you?" First ↘ (you're sure), then ↗ (you're checking). Learners copy both and hold up the right arrow.
  4. Situation drill: T: "You're sure. The coffee here is great." → S: "The coffee's great here, isn't it? ↘" · T: "You're not sure. Is the store open on Sunday?" → S: "The store's open on Sunday, isn't it? ↗" (or "…isn't open…, is it? ↗")
  5. Echo chain: T: "I went to Chicago." → S1: "Did you? How was it?" → S1 to S2: "I've started a new job." → S2: "Have you? Do you like it?" → around the room.
Small-talk mingle — how to run it
  • Model first with a strong learner. Read your card: "At the bus stop. It's raining." → "Terrible weather, isn't it? ↘" Your partner answers ("It really is!"), and you keep going: React → Ask → Share. Show one token going on the table every time you take a turn.
  • Rules: 2 minutes per conversation. Open with a falling tag. Use at least one echo question and one follow-up question each. Listeners backchannel (mm-hm, right, really?). When your tokens are gone, you can only ask questions, not tell.
  • Bell → "Nice talking to you!" → swap cards with your partner → find a new partner. Aim for 5–6 conversations.
  • Round 4 twist, guess & check: before you start, guess one small, safe thing about your new partner (likes coffee, can cook, has seen a certain movie, walked to class today). Check it with a rising tag: "You walked here today, didn't you? ↗" Your partner answers the fact.
  • Anyone can say "Let's talk about something else, shall we?" if a topic feels too personal. Teach this sentence and say it is a good, polite way to change the topic.
  • You walk around and listen for tags, intonation and follow-up questions (write examples for feedback, no names). Don't correct during the mingle.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on the six most common tags: isn't it? · aren't you? · don't you? · didn't you? · can you? · shall we?. Give learners the tag flashcards to hold up instead of writing. In the mingle, use the "Keep it going" strip with pictures (😮 react · ❓ ask · 🙋 share) and 4 ready phrases: Oh really? · Did you? · What was it like? · Me too! Target: open with one tag and use one echo + one follow-up question.

Stretch

Use tags with there and indefinite pronouns (There's a problem, isn't there? · Somebody called, didn't they?). In the mingle, keep each chat going for 3 minutes and use a negative statement + positive tag at least once (You don't work on Sundays, do you?). Early finishers write a 10-line conversation (the online project) with 6 tags, marking ↗ or ↘ on each.

L1 watch-outs
  • One tag for everything: "…, isn't it?" / "…, no?" / "…, right?" (Spanish ¿no?, ¿verdad?; French n'est-ce pas?; German nicht wahr?; Portuguese não é?; Russian да?; Chinese 吧 / 对吧; Hindi/Urdu na?; Tagalog 'di ba?). "You like pizza, isn't it?" is the classic error (also frequent in South Asian and West African Englishes). Tell learners that "right?" is a correct, informal American alternative, but practice the full tag system today.
  • Same polarity: "She has left, has she?" In English this is not a checking tag; it can sound surprised or even sarcastic. Point to the mirror on the board.
  • Answering yes / no to a negative question: Japanese, Korean, and many Chinese, Arabic and Vietnamese speakers answer the question ("You don't drive, do you?" — "Yes." = "Yes, you're right, I don't"). English answers the fact: "No, I don't." Practice this; the mix-up causes real misunderstandings.
  • Flat intonation: learners from tonal or syllable-timed languages (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Spanish, French) may say every tag flat, which can sound bored or cold. Exaggerate the fall and the rise with your hand; let learners exaggerate too.
  • Backchanneling differs by culture: Japanese aizuchi (hai, ee, sō desu ne) are very frequent; English speakers may think a Japanese listener agrees when they only mean "I'm listening." Some cultures listen silently to show respect; in English, silence can seem like no interest. Say both are fine at home; in English, a few "mm-hm"s help.

Teach about 8 per group in class; the online lesson adds the travel set. Don't give a long alphabetical list. Every verb goes with a sentence the learner can picture: "I look after my neighbor's cat on weekends."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The particle changes the meaning of the verb. Some are literal (the meaning is in the words); many are idiomatic (learn them like new words).

"He gave up smoking." Is it about giving something to someone? (No.) Does he smoke now? (No.) So give up means…? (Stop.) · "I ran into my teacher at the supermarket." Did I plan to meet her? (No.) Did I run? (No! By chance.)
"Can you look after my cat?" Is it about looking with your eyes? (No.) What do I need? (Someone to take care of it: food, water.) · "We put off the meeting." Is the meeting today? (No, later.) Is it canceled? (No, only later.)
"I get along with my coworkers." Do we fight? (No.) Do we like each other? (Yes.) · "I'm looking forward to the weekend." Am I happy or worried? (Happy.) Is it past or future? (Future.)

Form. Learn each verb with its type. With a noun object, separable verbs work both ways. With a pronoun, they must split.

✂️ Separableverb + particle + noun or verb + noun + particleTurn on the TV. = Turn the TV on. → Turn it on. (never "turn on it")
🔗 Inseparableverb + particle + object (always together)I look after the kids. → I look after them. (never "look them after")
🚫 No objectverb + particle (nothing after it)I get up at 7. · Let's get together on Friday. · Carry on!
Three-partverb + 2 particles + object (inseparable)I get along with her. · I'm looking forward to it. · Let's catch up with them.
+ -inglook forward to + noun / -ing (to is a preposition here)I'm looking forward to seeing you. (not "to see")

Pronunciation. In a phrasal verb the particle usually carries the stress: "I get UP at seven." "Turn it OFF." "She looks AFter her brother." (with look after the stress is on af-). With a pronoun in the middle, the pronoun is weak and linked: "turn it off" → /tɜrnɪˈdɔf/ ("tur-ni-DOFF", with the American flap t), "pick them up" → "pick-em-UP". Linking makes the pronoun almost disappear; that is why learners don't hear it and leave it at the end.

Drill script
  1. Mime and say: you mime (yawn and stretch; press a switch; hand in a paper; hug), learners say the verb: "wake up / turn on / hand in / get along with."
  2. Noun → pronoun substitution (the key drill): T: "Turn off the TV." → S: "Turn it off." · "Pick up the kids." → "Pick them up." · "Fill out the form." → "Fill it out." · "Look after the baby." → "Look after her." (no change in order!) · "Call back the customer." → "Call him / her back."
  3. Formal → everyday: T: "discover" → S: "find out" · "postpone" → "put off" · "continue" → "carry on" · "raise children" → "bring up" · "meet by chance" → "run into" · "solve" → "sort out".
  4. I'll do it! (recycles A2 I'll): T: "The music's too loud." → S: "I'll turn it down." · "There are clothes on the floor." → "I'll put them away." · "The customer called." → "I'll call her back."
  5. look forward to: T: "the weekend" → S: "I'm looking forward to the weekend." T: "see my friends" → S: "I'm looking forward to seeing my friends."
Split or Stick? — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4. Each group gets the three header cards (✂️ SPLIT · 🔗 STICK · 🚫 NO OBJECT) and the 24 verb cards face down. Each verb card has a small example object on it (turn off + the TV).
  • Players take turns: turn over a card, say it with a pronoun ("Turn it off!"), and put it under a header. The group must agree. If they disagree, they test it: "Does 'look them after' sound right?" A wrong card goes back to the bottom of the pile.
  • When all cards are placed, show the answer (slide 13 / the key on the materials sheet). One point per correct card. Two-way cards: wake up is NO OBJECT (I woke up) and SPLIT (wake him up); accept either with a correct sentence.
  • Round 2 — pronoun race (5 min): you call a verb + noun ("Throw away the old milk!"). Teams race to say it with a pronoun ("Throw it away!") and hold up the card. First correct team gets a point. Include 2–3 inseparable ones as traps ("Look after the kids!" → "Look after them!").
ICQs before the game and the talk cards
Split or Stick: What do you say before you place a card? (The verb with a pronoun: "it" or "them.") Three places: what are they? (Split, stick, no object.) If the group disagrees? (Test it: does it sound right?) · Talk cards: How long do you talk? (One minute.) How many phrasal verbs? (At least two.) What does the group do? (React, echo, and ask a follow-up question.) Too personal? (Take a new card.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with 10 verbs only: wake up, get up, turn on / off, put away, look for, fill out, call back, look after, get along with, get together, using the picture flashcards and mime. In the sorting game, play in a mixed group and be the "tester" (say it out loud) while a partner places the card. Target: 6 phrasal verbs in spoken sentences about your day and 2 correct pronoun sentences (turn it on, put them away).

Stretch

Learn a second meaning: take off (a plane / your coat), pick up (lift / collect someone / learn a language easily), bring up (raise children / mention a topic). Use three-part verbs (run out of, come up with, get along with). Early finishers write the online project: "A day in my life" with 10+ phrasal verbs from 3 groups, marked S or I.

L1 watch-outs
  • Avoiding phrasal verbs: speakers of Romance languages (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian) often choose the Latin one-word verb (discover, continue, postpone, extinguish). It is correct, but sounds formal. Praise it, then offer the everyday form.
  • Pronoun at the end ("turn on it," "pick up them"): all learners, especially Spanish, French, Arabic, Chinese and Russian speakers, whose languages don't split verbs. German and Dutch speakers are used to split verbs (Mach das Licht an) and often find this easier.
  • Splitting an inseparable verb ("look the baby after") after learning the split rule: overgeneralization. Keep the three columns on the board.
  • "look forward to see you" (all learners: to looks like an infinitive). Board: look forward to + noun / -ing.
  • Missing or wrong particle ("get the bus," "get up the bus," "wait me"): prepositions don't map across languages. Teach each verb as a chunk with its particle.
  • Hearing the particle: Vietnamese, Chinese and Thai speakers may drop final consonants, so pick it up becomes "pi-i-u." Drill the linking slowly, then at speed.

Review Project — Presentation & Panel Discussion

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • understand the main points of a short talk and a panel discussion, and notice the signposting
  • plan a presentation in three parts (introduction → 2–3 points → conclusion) with a planning frame of key words
  • give a 3–4 minute presentation from notes, with signposting and at least four different B1 structures
  • take part in a panel discussion as chair, speaker or audience: open and close, give and defend a view, agree and disagree politely, ask and answer questions
  • give a classmate kind, specific feedback with the rubric (one star, one wish)

If you run long

Protect planning, rehearsal, the presentations and the panel. Cut in this order: (1) shorten the warmer to 3 minutes; (2) do Ex 4 and Ex 5 as homework (keep the slide language); (3) make the panel 5 minutes per group. With a large class, run TASK A in groups of 3. Never cut Looking Ahead. Learners must leave knowing exactly what C26 looks like.

Why 3–4 minutes in class, but 1–2 minutes online?

The online lesson (8.3) asks self-study learners for a 1–2 minute talk, because they work alone. In class you have listeners, a rehearsal partner and a planning frame, so the target is a 3–4 minute talk: two or three points, each with an example. If a learner can only do 2 minutes with confidence, that is a success; note it on the checklist and don't push. The panel adds the interaction the online lesson can only imagine.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review)

No new grammar. The job today is structure (signposting) and interaction (panel language), while reaching for the B1 structures learners already know. Keep CCQs fast.

"To sum up…" Beginning, middle or end? (End.) · "Another point is…" Is it my first point? (No, the second or third.) · "For example…" Am I giving a new point or making a point clearer? (Clearer.)
"I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree." Do I agree? (No, not completely.) Am I rude? (No: I say something positive first.) · "No, you're wrong." Is this OK in a panel? (No, too direct.)
(Chair) "Ana, what's your view on that?" Why does the chair say this? (To bring Ana in: maybe she hasn't spoken.) · "We have time for one more question." Is the panel starting or ending? (Ending soon.)

Pronunciation for presenting. Pause after each signpost ("First of all… [pause] it's healthy."). Stress the key word in each point. Slow down: a calm pace sounds more confident, and speed hides good English. Look up from the notes at the end of each sentence. In the panel, the polite "I see your POINT, but…" has a fall-rise on point; a flat, fast "No" sounds rude even with polite words.

Drill script (quick, choral → individual)
  1. Signpost chain: T shows a topic ("cooking at home"). S1: "Today I'd like to talk about cooking at home." S2: "First of all, it's cheaper." S3: "Another point is that it's healthier." S4: "For example, …" S5: "To sum up, …" New topic, new chain.
  2. Soften it: T (blunt): "No, that's wrong." → S: "I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree." · T: "Bad idea." → S: "That's an interesting idea, but I think it might be difficult."
  3. Chair in action: T: "(bring in Omar)" → S: "Omar, what's your view on that?" · T: "(time is almost up)" → S: "We have time for one more question." · T: "(end)" → S: "To wrap up, let's thank our speakers."
The task — presentations and the panel

Topic choices (say them out loud): (1) a topic you really care about: a hobby, a skill, a place you love, a change you'd like to see in the neighborhood or at work, a useful habit, a piece of technology; (2) a topic card with three ready points; (3) the topic of your online capstone. Nobody has to talk about their home country, family or personal history, and nobody explains their choice.

TASK A (groups of 4): speaker stands or sits, glances at key words (reading full sentences is not allowed), speaks 3–4 minutes. The timekeeper (the next speaker) shows 1 finger at 3 minutes. Listeners write one audience question for the panel. Each learner fills the peer rubric for the speaker on their left (star + wish only, no numbers for peers).

TASK B (fishbowl panel): two groups join. Panel group sits in a row facing the other group. Roles: Chair (1), Speakers (the other 3; each gives a 1-minute position linked to their talk), Audience (the other group: each person asks one question to a named speaker). The chair's card has the order: welcome → question card → each speaker 1 min → audience questions → "one more question" → wrap up. 7 minutes, then swap. Choose as chair a learner who talks a lot (they must now invite others) or a confident learner who presented well.

ICQs: How long is your talk? (Three to four minutes.) Can you read a full script? (No, only key words.) What do listeners write? (One question and a star and a wish.) In the panel, who opens and closes? (The chair.) How long does each speaker talk in the panel? (About one minute.) Audience: how many questions each? (One.) Disagree with a person or an idea? (An idea, politely: "I see your point, but…")

Small or uneven classes: with 6–7 learners, run one group of presentations (in two rounds) and one panel with the rest of the class as audience. With 5 or fewer, you are the chair and everyone is a speaker. A learner who cannot stay for the whole class presents first.

The rubric and the observation checklist → C26

The presentation rubric (classroom materials, set H, and the self-check in Workbook 8.3) has five criteria: 1 structure & signposting · 2 range of B1 language · 3 fluency & delivery · 4 pronunciation & clarity · 5 interaction in the panel. Three levels: ★★★ strong · ★★ getting there · ★ not yet. Peers give only a star and a wish. Learners self-assess at home. You can use the full rubric for your notes, but don't grade C25: it's practice. Tell learners so.

The observation checklist (set I) has one row per learner and 10 can-do items. Three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help, or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You won't see every learner do everything; listen to each learner for at least one full talk and part of the panel. After class, use it to:

  • Plan each C26 speaking assessment. The B1 final speaking assessment is an individual interview (about 8 minutes): warm-up, a short story, a 1-minute talk from a topic card, a discussion where you gently push back, and a complaint or job-interview role-play. Learners may reuse their C25 topic for the talk. For learners with ○ on items 1–3, start with the part they did best.
  • Choose the discussion prompt. Note which Module 7 discussion topic (or which C25 panel question) suits each pair.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become the 10-minute review before the assessment starts.
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who stopped early gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is friendly. It's like today, but shorter. I'll help you."
Differentiation
Literacy support

The planning frame can be three boxes of pictures or symbols (one per part) with 3–4 key words each. Offer a topic card with ready points and pictures. Target: a 2-minute talk with Today I'd like to talk about… · First… · Another point… · To sum up…, and one question or one polite agree / disagree in the panel. Give the audience role with a question already written on a card. Rehearse with a supportive partner.

Stretch

A full 4 minutes with three points, each with an example, and 6+ B1 structures (a second conditional, reported speech, the passive, a past perfect, a question tag, a phrasal verb). Take the chair role and bring every speaker in by name. In the panel, answer a hard question with "That's a great question. I think…" and a reason. Write the talk up as a 200-word article at home.

L1 watch-outs
  • Reading a script word for word: learners from education systems that value memorized, written speeches (many East Asian, Middle Eastern and Eastern European schools) may write the whole talk. Allow it at home for confidence, but in class the frame has key words only. Collect scripts at the door if needed, kindly.
  • Very direct disagreement ("No, this is not true," German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew) or no disagreement at all (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Filipino speakers may agree to be polite). Teach both groups the English middle: acknowledge, then "but…".
  • Missing articles in topics and points (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean): "I want to talk about importance of sport." Accept in fluency; board it in feedback (the importance of…).
  • Rising intonation on every sentence in a presentation sounds unsure. Model falling intonation at the end of each point: confident and finished.
  • "I will talk about…" as the opener (Spanish, French, Portuguese): correct, but Today I'd like to talk about… sounds more natural. Praise, then offer.
  • "Discuss about" ("Today we discuss about…"): Spanish discutir sobre, Arabic, Hindi and others. English: discuss something / talk about something.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C25: Module 8 and B1 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18: isn't it? ↘ / Oh really? What was it like? → sort it out / look after them → my talk, our panel. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook and look back at the "I can…" lists from Module 1. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you, for the C26 warm-up.

The C26 final assessment covers the whole B1 course. From this module it checks: question tags (form and intonation), interested responses and follow-up questions, phrasal verbs with pronoun objects, signposting a short talk, and polite agreeing and disagreeing in a discussion.

Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, a friendly final speaking assessment: a one-to-one interview of about 8 minutes — warm-up questions, a short story, a 1-minute talk on a topic card (the C25 topic is fine), a discussion where you gently disagree so the learner defends a view (just like the panel), and a short complaint or job-interview role-play. With more than 8 learners, start interviews in C25 (see the kit's final assessment). There are also short listening, reading and writing tasks in the kit's B1 final assessment. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next step, the B2 course, Confident English.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You talk with a partner and with me, like today, but shorter. You can use the same topic. No surprises: it's everything we practiced. Then we celebrate!"

Reduce anxiety: show B1 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you talk for three minutes and then run a discussion?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that the recording of their talk is the best practice for the long turn.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite 2–3 volunteers to give a 1-minute "graduation speech" with the C25 signposts ("Today I'd like to say thank you…").

Teacher reflection (after C25)

Look at your checklist and answer three questions: Which two can-dos need a C26 warm-up? Which learners need a quiet word of encouragement? Which topic will each learner use for the 1-minute talk, and which role-play (complaint or job interview) suits each learner best? Write your answers on the back of the checklist.
