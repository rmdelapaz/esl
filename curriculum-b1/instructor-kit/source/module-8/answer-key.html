Module 8 — Fluency & the Final Project: Answers

Answers to every Module 8 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (intonation script, Split or Stick?, Sort It Out cards), guidance for the open presentation tasks and the rubric, and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 8.1–8.3Classes C23–C259 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B1, mark communication first. In this module most tasks are spoken, and the goal is fluency. A tag with the right form but flat intonation is a success with a note; a phrasal verb with a pronoun in the wrong place is worth correcting, because it is a clear rule. Accept right? as an informal American tag in conversation, but not in the tag exercises. American English is the model; British forms (get on with, fill in, rubbish, holiday, practise, neighbour) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

8.1 · Question Tags & Keeping a Conversation Going (C23)

Exercise 1 — Add the question tag
  1. aren't you (positive are → negative)
  2. does she (negative → positive; present, she → does)
  3. didn't they (past simple, no helper → did)
  4. can't he
  5. have we
  6. shall we (Let's → shall we)
  7. doesn't he (your brother → he; present → does)
  8. aren't I (I am → aren't I)

Wrong: "don't he" (item 7: the subject is he → does) · "isn't it" for everything · "amn't I" · "am I?" (item 8: same polarity). "Am I not?" in 8 is grammatically correct but very formal; accept it with a note.

Exercise 2 — ↗ or ↘? (Script 8.1-B, items 1–6)

1 ↘ (sure: it's a beautiful day) · 2 ↗ (checking: is the bank open?) · 3 ↘ (we both saw it; expecting agreement) · 4 ↗ (really asking: I lost my phone) · 5 ↘ (sure: it's delicious) · 6 ↗ (checking the time).

The script gives a short context before each tag ("Oh, I'm not sure…" / "Mmm!") so learners can hear and reason. Items 7–10 of the script are for the class drill only (7 ↗ · 8 ↘ · 9 ↗ · 10 ↘). If a learner argues for the other arrow and can explain it ("Maybe she's really not sure about the movie"), praise the thinking: both are possible grammatically; the voice decides.

Exercise 3 — Echo questions

1 Is she? · 2 Have they? · 3 Can he? · 4 Don't you? · 5 Did you? · 6 Are you?

Echo questions keep the same polarity as the statement (unlike tags): "I don't like coffee." → "Don't you?" Also correct: "Oh really?" in any item (but the exercise asks for the echo). Wrong: "Does she?" in 1 (she's living = is) · "Did they?" in 2 (they've bought = present perfect → have) · "Do you?" in 4.

Exercise 4 — Fix the tag
  1. You like pizza, don't you? (the pronoun is you, not it)
  2. I'm right, aren't I?
  3. She has left, hasn't she? (positive → negative)
  4. Open the window, will you? (also: would you? / can you? / won't you?)
  5. Let's dance, shall we?
Exercise 5 — Keep it going (open)

Look for a reaction (echo or interest phrase) + a follow-up question that fits. Models:

  1. Have you? That sounds fun! What are you learning to cook?
  2. Did she? That's wonderful! What was the prize for?
  3. Oh no! That's terrible. Have you talked to your neighbor about it?
  4. Are you? How nice! Which beach are you going to?

Accept any correct echo: 1 "Have you?" · 2 "Did she?" · 3 "Can't you?" (or "Oh no!") · 4 "Are you?". Common slips: "Do you?" for 1 (I've started → have) · "What it was like?" (word order: What was it like?) · a closed follow-up that ends the chat ("Is it good?"); encourage open questions (What / Where / How…?).

Dialogue — Priya and Tom

Tags (4): didn't you · is it · won't you · shall we. Reactions (2): Oh really? · Is it? (an echo question). Follow-up (1): What was it like? Note: "Is it?" is both an echo and a reaction. The workbook version is slightly shorter than the online dialogue (the online line "You should try it, shouldn't you?" is left out because the tag there is unusual: we rarely use a tag to tell someone what they should do).

Speaking — small-talk mingle (open)

Success = the learner opens with a correct falling tag, uses at least one echo and one follow-up question per conversation, backchannels while listening, and uses a rising tag to check a guess in round 4. Answers to guess & check: answer the fact ("You don't eat meat, do you?" — "No, I don't." if true).

Self-check
Q1
"She doesn't work on Fridays, ___?"
A) doesn't she
B) does she
C) isn't she
Why: a negative statement takes a positive tag, and present simple borrows does. A keeps the same polarity; C uses the wrong verb.
Q2
You are really not sure and want to check. How do you say the tag?
A) With a falling voice ↘
B) With a rising voice ↗
C) It makes no difference.
Why: a rising tag is a real question. A falling tag expects agreement. The words are the same; the voice changes the meaning, so C is wrong.
Q3 · new
"I'm late again, ___?"
A) amn't I
B) aren't I
C) isn't I
Why: the tag for I am is the fixed form aren't I?. "Amn't I" is not standard English (you may hear it in Scotland and Ireland); "isn't I" is never correct.
Errors you will hear most (8.1)

"…, isn't it?" / "…, no?" after every statement · "She has left, has she?" (same polarity) · "You live here, live you?" / "…, aren't you?" for a present-simple verb · "You don't drive, do you?" — "Yes." (meaning I don't) · flat intonation on every tag · silence while listening (no backchannel) · "How was it like?" (What was it like? / How was it?). Fix the form in controlled practice; in the mingle, note errors for delayed correction and praise any warm reaction.

8.2 · Phrasal Verbs — A Practical Toolkit (C24)

Exercise 1 — Leo and Nadia (Script 8.2-A): 12 phrasal verbs

wake up (woke up) · get back (to sleep) · set off · check in · turn on · sort out (sort it out) · look after (looking after) · get along with (UK: get on with) · look forward to (looking forward to) · come up (comes up) · deal with · find out.

Accept the forms as heard (woke up, comes up). Accept get on with (the online version). Stretch: which three are separable here? (turn on, sort out, find out; sort it out shows the pronoun rule.)

Exercise 2 — Complete the phrasal verb

1 up · 2 off · 3 after · 4 up · 5 out · 6 forward (look forward to) · 7 with · 8 into.

Note: up is used twice. Also possible in 1: I wake up (but the verb given is get). Wrong: "look for my cat" in 3 changes the meaning (search for a lost cat); if the learner explains that meaning, accept it with a smile.

Exercise 3 — Say it the everyday way

1 take off · 2 turn off (also: switch off, put out) · 3 sort out (also: work out, figure out) · 4 give up (also: quit is already everyday) · 5 put off (They put off the meeting / put the meeting off) · 6 carry on (also: go on, keep going).

take off is not in the class toolkit; many learners know it from airports. If nobody does, teach it here (and its second meaning: take off your coat).

Exercise 4 — Fix the pronoun
  1. The radio's too loud — turn it off.
  2. I found your keys. I'll pick them up later.
  3. It's a hard puzzle, but I'll work it out.
  4. Here's the trash — please throw it away.
  5. Don't give it up now!

Also correct: turn it down in 1 (a better meaning for "too loud"). British rubbish is fine.

Exercise 5 — Split or stick?
1fill out the form✂️fill it out
2look for my glasses🔗look for them
3put away the dishes✂️put them away
4call back Mr. Diaz✂️call him back
5get along with my coworkers🔗get along with them
6hand in the report✂️hand it in

With a noun, "call Mr. Diaz back" and "call back Mr. Diaz" are both fine; with a person's name, Americans usually split it ("call Mr. Diaz back").

Classroom materials — Split or Stick? (set D) answers
✂️ SPLIT (11)turn on / off (the TV) · turn up / down (the music) · put away (the clothes) · throw away (the old milk) · clean up (the kitchen) · fill out (the form) · call back (the customer) · sort out (the problem) · put off (the meeting) · hand in (the report) · bring up (children)
🔗 STICK (8)look for (my keys) · deal with (the complaint) · get along with (my coworkers) · look after (the baby) · catch up with (an old friend) · run into (my neighbor) · count on (my friends) · look forward to (the weekend)
🚫 NO OBJECT (4)get up · carry on · get together · set off
Two-waywake up: 🚫 (I woke up at five) or ✂️ (Wake him up!). Accept either with a correct sentence.

The set has 24 cards: 11 SPLIT (turn on / off and turn up / down are one card each), 8 STICK, 4 NO OBJECT and 1 two-way card (wake up). Watch: carry on can take with (carry on with your work), which is inseparable; accept STICK if the learner says "carry on with it."

Classroom materials — Sort It Out cards (set E) model answers

A1 The TV is too loud. → I'll turn it down. · A2 There are clothes all over the floor. → I'll put them away. · A3 A customer called while you were out. → I'll call her / him back. · A4 This milk smells bad. → I'll throw it away. · A5 I can't find my glasses. → I'll help you look for them. · A6 The kitchen is a mess after the party. → Let's clean it up together.

B1 I have to fill out this long form. → I'll help you fill it out. · B2 The report is ready. → Great, I'll hand it in. · B3 The printer isn't working. → Don't worry, I'll sort it out. · B4 I don't know the price of the tickets. → I'll find it out. / I'll find out. · B5 I'm too tired for the meeting today. → Let's put it off until Monday. · B6 My sister's baby is sick, and she has to work. → I can look after him / her. (🔗: no split!)

find out with it is less common ("I'll find out" alone is most natural). Accept both.

Speaking — talk cards (open)

Look for 2+ phrasal verbs per answer, a pronoun in the middle when the verb is separable, and look forward to + -ing. The group should react with an echo and ask a follow-up question (C23 recycling).

Self-check
Q1
The light is on. Which sentence is correct?
A) Turn off it, please.
B) Turn it off, please.
C) It off turn, please.
Why: turn off is separable, so a pronoun object must go in the middle. A leaves the pronoun at the end; C has the wrong word order.
Q2
Which phrasal verb is 🔗 stick (it can't split)?
A) sort out
B) look after
C) turn on
Why: look after is inseparable: "look after the kids," never "look the kids after." Sort out and turn on can split.
Q3 · new
"I'm really looking forward to ___ you next week."
A) see
B) seeing
C) saw
Why: in look forward to, to is a preposition, so it takes a noun or an -ing form. A is the most common error at B1.
Errors you will hear most (8.2)

"Turn off it." / "I'll pick up them." · "Can you look my cat after?" · "I look forward to see you." · "I get up the bus." (get on) · "I'm waiting you." (waiting for) · avoiding phrasal verbs entirely (I will discover the price) · "I get along my boss." (missing with). Correct pronoun position every time in controlled practice; in freer speaking, praise any natural phrasal verb first.

8.3 · Review Project — Presentation & Panel Discussion (C25)

Exercise 1 — Maya's talk and the panel (Script 8.3-A)
  1. It's healthy, it saves money, and (if more people cycled) streets would be cleaner and quieter.
  2. Her expensive bus pass.
  3. That cycling is not always safe.
  4. If cities built more bike lanes.
  5. Because some people live far away (from work).

Signposts heard (6): Today I'd like to talk about · First of all · Another point is · Finally · To sum up · Thank you for listening. Not heard: For example · In conclusion. Stretch: find the B1 structures (past perfect had never felt; phrasal verb gave up; second conditional if more people cycled… would be; reported speech told me she had stopped; tags is it? · isn't it? · shall we?).

Exercise 2 — Mixed B1 review

1 rains (If + present, will) · 2 was (backshift from am) · 3 given (had + past participle) · 4 hearing (look forward to + -ing) · 5 shouldn't we · 6 out.

Also correct: 2 felt (She said she felt tired). Wrong: "will rain" in 1 · "gave" in 3 · "hear" in 4.

Exercise 3 — Correct the mistake
  1. I gave a presentation about the environment.
  2. If I have time tomorrow, I'll practice again.
  3. He told me that the room was ready. (or: He said that…)
  4. You're coming to the panel, aren't you?
  5. I have finished my slides.

British practise (verb) is fine in 2.

Exercise 4 — Put the talk in order

b 1 · g 2 · d 3 · f 4 · a 5 · c 6 · e 7

So: a 5 · b 1 · c 6 · d 3 · e 7 · f 4 · g 2. Introduction (b + g), point 1 + example (d + f), point 2 (a), conclusion (c), close (e). Accept f after a (4 ↔ 5 swap: the example could illustrate either point) if the learner explains it.

Exercise 5 — Say it politely (open)
  1. That's an interesting idea, but I'm not sure it would work. / I see what you mean, but I think it might be difficult.
  2. That's a good point. However, I think buses might be better for some people. / I see your point, but I prefer the bus.
  3. (Chair) What's your view on that, [name]? / [Name], what do you think?
  4. (Chair) We have time for one more question. / To wrap up, let's thank our speakers.

Accept any version that acknowledges first, then gives the view, or (chair) invites or closes politely. Softening words to praise: I think · might · maybe · a little · to be honest.

Planning frame, presentation and panel (open)

What a strong B1 talk looks like (3–4 minutes, about 350–450 words spoken): a clear introduction with a reason ("I chose this topic because…"); 2–3 points, each signposted and with an example; at least four different B1 structures; a conclusion that repeats the main idea; one audience question answered. The teacher's model (Script 8.3-B) is a good example to show.

Frames: key words only. If a learner has written full sentences, help them underline 3–4 key words per box and practice from those.

3 · Fluency & delivery3–4 min from notes; steady pace; keeps going after a slipAbout 2–3 min; some long pauses or some readingUnder 2 min, or reads a script word for word

C25 is practice: use the rubric for feedback and your C26 planning, not for a grade. Peers give only a star (★) and a wish (🎯). The C26 final assessment has its own rubric in the kit's B1 assessment key.

Self-check
Q1
Which phrase best ends a presentation?
A) Today I'd like to talk about…
B) To sum up…
C) Another point is…
Why: To sum up… signals the conclusion. A opens a talk; C adds a point in the middle.
Q2
In a discussion, how do you disagree politely?
A) No, you're wrong.
B) I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree.
C) (Say nothing and wait.)
Why: acknowledge the other view first, then add yours. A sounds harsh in English; C means you don't take part.
Q3 · new
You are the chair. Ana hasn't spoken yet. What do you say?
A) You, talk now.
B) Ana, what's your view on that?
C) Ana is quiet, isn't she?
Why: a chair brings people in by name with an open, polite question. A is an order; C talks about Ana and could embarrass her (even though the tag is correct!).
Errors you will hear most (8.3)

Reading a script word for word · "Today I will talk you about…" (talk to you about / tell you about) · "Today we discuss about…" · missing articles ("importance of sport") · "In conclusion" used at the start · "I am agree" / "I agree with you, but…" said so fast it sounds rude · "If more people will cycle…" · rising intonation at the end of every point. Give feedback on communication first (Was it clear? Did people follow?), then one or two forms per learner.
