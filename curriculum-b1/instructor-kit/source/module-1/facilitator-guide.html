Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 1 — Time, Experience & Past Habits

Three classes that open the B1 level. Learners already know the present perfect, the past simple and used to from A2. The B1 skill is choosing: present perfect or past simple? Present perfect simple or continuous? used to or would? Class 1 also welcomes a new group and gives you a quick A2 diagnostic. By the end of the module learners can open a topic with an experience, give the details, talk about what they have been doing lately, and discuss how life has changed.

3 lessons Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, discussion) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-1-time-experience.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the flashcards, the welcome question cards and the A2 diagnostic grid, the new identity cards, the timeline interview cards, the "Have you ever…?" Find Someone Who, the "What have you been doing?" clue game, the "How life has changed" discussion cards and roles, and all the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • This is extension, not a first meeting. A2 taught each form on its own (present perfect for experiences in A2 Module 4, used to in A2 Module 5). B1 puts the forms side by side and asks learners to choose. Spend less time on form and more on the choice, the reason, and fluent use.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use some British forms (film, flat, cinema, football, biscuits, neighbourhood, kilometres, practising, mobile phones, I've just got home). Teach movie, apartment, movie theater, soccer, cookies, neighborhood, kilometers / miles, practicing, cell phones, I've just gotten home, and accept the British forms when learners use them. Americans also often use the past simple with just / already / yet ("Did you eat yet?"). Teach the present perfect version (it is correct everywhere) and accept both.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

What I've done → what I've been doing → how things used to be. Every lesson in this module links the past to now. In C1 learners open a topic with an experience or some news (I've started a new job) and move into the finished details (I started two weeks ago). In C2 they talk about activities that are still going on, or that explain what we can see now (I've been painting. Look at my hands!). In C3 they compare then and now (People used to write letters. Every Sunday we would…) and give an opinion: was it better before? Each class ends with a longer speaking task than the last: a pair interview, then a group game and a catch-up chat, then a group discussion with roles.

Trauma-informed: the past is personal

This whole module is about the past, and for some adults the past holds hard things: a home they had to leave, a job they lost, people far away. Never ask "How was life in your country?", "Why did you come here?", "How long have you lived here?" or "What did you use to do before you came?" in a whole-class task. Keep personal questions to small, safe topics: food, hobbies, phones, work routines, free time. For every personal task offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card (in the classroom materials), or people in general / a place you know ("People used to write letters"). In C3 the discussion is about technology, work and daily life, not about any one country. Anyone can say "Pass." Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

B1 speaking: fluency first, then accuracy

B1 learners need long turns: more than one sentence, with a reason or an example. In every freer task, do not interrupt to correct. Walk around, listen, and write down 4–6 sentences you hear (good ones and ones with errors, no names). Give feedback in two steps: content first ("Lina's story about her first job was great: who else heard a surprising story?"), language second (the sentences on the board; learners fix them together). Correct on the spot only when the meaning breaks down or during controlled practice and drills.

Dominant speakers. Mixed-level groups often have one or two confident learners who fill every silence. Use talking tokens (3 per person; put one down when you take a long turn; when yours are gone, wait until everyone has used theirs), roles (the confident learner is the chair whose job is to make others talk, or the reporter who must repeat what others said), and think time (1 minute of silent notes before any discussion). Say it kindly in private: "You have great ideas. Today your job is to help Maria and Kofi talk more."

If not, the C1 welcome mingle doubles as a quick A2 diagnostic (see Lesson 1.1). It will not replace a placement test, but it will show you who needs extra support and who is close to B2.
  • Print the welcome question cards (Set B, one set per 12 learners) and one copy of the A2 diagnostic grid for you, on a clipboard. Print name tents (folded paper is fine).
  • Print the picture flashcards (Set A, one set for the board), the time-word sort cards (one set per pair for C1) and the new identity cards (Set C). Keep the identity cards on a side table where anyone can take one without asking.
  • For C1: one timeline interview card per learner (Set E). For C2: one Find Someone Who sheet per learner (Set H) and one clue game deck per group of 3–4 (Set F). For C3: one set of discussion topic cards and role cards per group of 4 (Set G) and talking tokens (3 per learner: paper clips, beans or small squares of paper).

If you run long

The welcome mingle is the most important part of C1. Do not cut it: the group needs it, and you need the diagnostic. Cut instead: Ex 4 goes to homework, and the dictation (Script 1.1-B) moves to the C2 warmer. Keep at least 10 minutes for the timeline interview, because it is the first real B1 speaking task.

Running the diagnostic — what to listen for

The mingle is a friendly first conversation, not a test. Learners must never feel watched. Hold the grid on a clipboard, stand near a pair for 30–60 seconds, and join in sometimes ("Oh, have you really cooked for fifty people? I haven't!"). Mark each learner ✓ (easy and accurate) · ~ (understands, some errors) · ✗ (can't produce it yet) in four columns:

  • PAST SIMPLE: irregular verbs (went, bought, saw), Did you…? and didn't + base verb. Listen for "I goed," "Did you went?" and "I no went."
  • PRESENT PERFECT: Have you ever…? + past participle, I've never…, short answers. Listen for "Have you ever went?", "I have see," and present perfect with a finished time.
  • FUTURE FORMS: going to for plans, will / might for guesses, present continuous for arrangements. Listen for "I going to," "I will to," and the present simple for a plan ("Tomorrow I visit…").
  • FLUENCY: Does the learner give more than one sentence? Ask a question back? Keep going when they don't know a word? This column matters most at B1.

What to do with the results: Mostly ✓ and ~ = ready for B1. Many ~ in the PRESENT PERFECT column is normal: that is exactly what C1–C2 teach. ✗ in two or three columns = check the placement privately (the toolkit's oral placement interview takes 5 minutes); the learner may do better in the A2 course, or may need extra support from Day 1. Lots of ✓ and strong fluency = give stretch tasks and consider the B2 course later. Never share the grid with learners. Keep it; compare it with Progress Test 1 in C13.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The choice is not about how long ago. It is about whether the time is finished (past simple) or the action is connected to now (present perfect: an experience in my life up to now, news with a result now, or a situation that is still true).

"I've lost my keys." Do I have my keys now? (No.) Do we know when I lost them? (No.) Is that important? (No, the result is important: I can't open the door!)
"I lost my keys yesterday." When? (Yesterday.) Is yesterday finished? (Yes.) Can I say "I've lost my keys yesterday"? (No! A finished time word needs the past simple.)
"She has lived here for ten years." Does she live here now? (Yes.) · "She lived there for ten years." Does she live there now? (No.)
"I've been to Japan." Am I in Japan now? (No, I went and came back.) · "She's gone to Japan." Is she here now? (No, she's in Japan.)
"I've just finished." A long time ago or a short time ago? (Very short.) · "I still haven't finished." Did I finish? (No.) Am I surprised or a bit unhappy? (Yes.)

Form. Learners know both forms. Put them side by side and focus on the time words:

Past simplesubject + past form (+ finished time)I saw that movie last Friday. · Did you see it? — Yes, I did.
Present perfectsubject + have / has + past participleI've seen it three times. · Have you ever been to Canada?
for / sincefor + a period · since + a start pointfor ten years, for a long time · since 2014, since Monday, since I was a child
just / alreadybetween have and the participleI've just finished lunch. · She's already left.
yet / stillyet at the end (? and −) · still before haven'tHave you eaten yet? · I haven't eaten yet. · I still haven't packed.
Open → detailspresent perfect (no time) → past simple (time, place, how)I've run a marathon. I ran it last year in Boston. It was really hard.

Pronunciation. Use the contractions: I've /aɪv/, she's /ʃiz/, haven't /ˈhævənt/. The 've is small but it carries the meaning: "I've seen it" vs "I seen it" (non-standard). Have learners say /v/ with teeth on the lip. for is weak: /fər/ ("fer ten years"). been is usually weak: /bɪn/. Have you ever…? links: /həv jə ˈɛvər/. Questions with yet rise at the end ("Have you eaten yet? ↗").

Drill script
  1. Time-word switch: T: "I've seen that movie." + "last Friday" → S: "I saw that movie last Friday." T: "She's called me." + "yesterday" → S: "She called me yesterday." T: "We visited Chicago." + "three times" → S: "We've visited Chicago three times." Choral, then individuals.
  2. for or since? T: "2021" → S: "since 2021." "six months" → "for six months." "last summer" → "since last summer." "ages" → "for ages." Then full sentences: "I've known her… (2014)" → "I've known her since 2014."
  3. Open → details chain: T: "I've been to a big concert." → S1: "When did you go?" → T: "I went in May." → S2: "Who did you go with?"… Then S1 opens with a new experience and the class asks.
  4. just / already / yet / still with mimes: T snaps fingers + "finish" → S: "I've just finished." T shakes head impatiently + "not / call me" → S: "She still hasn't called me." T: "you / eat?" + rising hand → S: "Have you eaten yet?"
Timeline interview — how to run it
  • Model first with your timeline on the board: 4 small pictures or words (🍞 bread class, 🚲 bike, 🎸 guitar, ✈️ Mexico) and one arrow to NOW (📚 "teach English"). Invite learners to interview you.
  • Learners draw pictures or one word only, no sentences. Years are optional. Events can be small and happy: a first cake, a new phone, a concert, a good book, a new skill. Nobody has to put their real life on paper. A new identity card has a ready-made timeline.
  • The interviewer opens with the present perfect: "I see a guitar. Have you ever played in a band?" / "How long have you had your bike?" Then asks for details in the past simple: "When did you start? Who taught you? How was it?"
  • The speaker answers in full, long turns: 2–3 sentences for every question. Stronger pairs add just / already / yet / still.
  • Report: "Kofi has played the guitar since 2020. He learned from videos, and he played at a wedding last year!"
ICQs before the timeline interview
Do you write sentences on your timeline? (No, pictures or one word.) · Must it be your real life? (No. A new identity card or an imagined life is fine.) · What tense is the first question? (Present perfect: "Have you ever…?") · And the next questions? (Past simple: "When did you…?") · How long are your answers? (Two or three sentences.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Draw the timeline for the learner if needed, from what they say. Give a phrase strip: "Have you ever…?" / "Yes, I have. / No, I've never…" / "When did you…?" / "I… in / last / ago." In the sort, use the picture side of the time cards (📅 🔚 for finished, ➡️● for connected to now). Target: two experiences, each with one past-simple detail. Workbook: Ex 1 (listening) and Ex 2, items 1–5 only.

Stretch

Use still and so far ("I've visited four states so far"). Explain why each tense fits, in their own words (the online journal task). In the interview, ask a surprise follow-up the speaker didn't plan. Early finishers write 3 pairs of sentences about the same thing: one present perfect, one past simple ("I've broken my phone!" / "I broke it this morning.").

L1 watch-outs
  • "I have seen him yesterday." French (passé composé), German (Perfekt), Italian (passato prossimo), Dutch and Spanish (in Spain) use a "have" tense for finished past time. Point at the FINISHED box on the board every time.
  • "I live here since five years" / "I am here since Monday." Spanish desde hace, French depuis, German seit, Portuguese há, Russian and Polish all use the present. It's understood; recast: "Oh, you've lived here for five years."
  • "since three years" / "for 2019." The same languages use one word for both (depuis, desde, seit). Drill: length → for; start → since.
  • Past simple everywhere ("Did you ever eat sushi?", "I already ate"): Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic, Russian, Tagalog and Korean have no present perfect. Also normal in American English, so don't mark it wrong in speech; practice the present perfect as the safe choice.
  • Portuguese tenho feito means "I've been doing (again and again)," so Brazilian learners may avoid "I've done." Give lots of "I've + participle" for single experiences.
  • Dropped endings: Vietnamese, Cantonese and some Spanish speakers drop the final /v/ in I've and the /t/ or /d/ in worked, lived. Say it slowly: "I've — lived — here."

Present Perfect Continuous — "What Have You Been Doing?"

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • form the present perfect continuous: I've / she's been + -ing, haven't been, Have you been…?, How long have you been…?
  • use it for an activity that started in the past and is still going on, with for / since: I've been studying English for three years.
  • use it for a recent activity with a result we can see now: His hands are dirty. He's been fixing the car.

If you run long

Shorten the Find Someone Who to 5 minutes (no report) and set Ex 4 as homework. Keep both freer tasks, but you can make "catching up" a single round of 5 minutes. Don't cut the stative-verb box: it is the most common B1 error with this tense.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning 1: duration up to now. An activity started in the past and is still going on. We want the listener to feel the time.

"I've been studying English for three years." When did I start? (Three years ago.) Am I still studying? (Yes.) Is it finished? (No.) Which question does it answer: how long or how many? (How long.)
"It's been raining all day." Is it raining now? (Probably yes.) When did it start? (This morning.)

Meaning 2: a recent activity, a result now. The activity maybe stopped a moment ago, but we can see or feel the result.

"I'm tired. I've been running." Am I running now? (No, I just stopped.) How do you know I was running? (You're tired, red, out of breath.) · "Your eyes are red. Have you been swimming?" Is this a question about the eyes or the activity? (The activity.)

Meaning 3: simple or continuous?

"I've painted the kitchen." Is the job finished? (Yes.) · "I've been painting the kitchen." Is it finished? (Maybe not.) Why is the kitchen messy? (Because I've been painting.) · "I've written three emails." How many? (Three.) Simple or continuous? (Simple: we count it.)
"I've known her for ten years." Can I say "I've been knowing her"? (No.) Why? (Know is a state, not an activity.) · "I've been having problems with my laptop." Is this OK? (Yes: having problems = experiencing, an activity.)

Form. Three pieces that never change order: have / has + been + verb-ing. Only have / has changes.

Positivesubject + have / has been + -ingI've been working. · She's been studying.
Negativehaven't / hasn't been + -ingThey haven't been sleeping well lately.
QuestionHave / Has + subject + been + -ing?Have you been waiting long? — Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.
How long?How long have / has + subject + been + -ing?How long have you been learning to drive?
Simple vs continuoushow many / result → simple · how long / activity → continuousI've read 50 pages. · I've been reading for two hours.
Stative verbsknow, like, love, hate, want, need, believe, understand, be, have (= own), belongsimple only: I've had this car since 2019.

Pronunciation. been is weak and short, like "bin" /bɪn/: "I've been waiting" = /aɪv bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/. Say 've been as one quick piece. The stress goes on the main verb and the time: "I've been WORKing here since JUNE." In How long have you been…? only long and the main verb are strong: "How LONG have you been WAITing?" Some learners over-pronounce the -ing as /ɪŋɡ/ (Spanish, Arabic, Cantonese): keep the /ŋ/ soft, no hard /g/.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "for three years" → "studying English for three years" → "been studying English for three years" → "I've been studying English for three years." Choral ×3, then individuals.
  2. Person substitution: T: "She… work here… since June" → S: "She's been working here since June." T: "They… wait… for an hour" → S: "They've been waiting for an hour." T: "It… rain… all day" → S: "It's been raining all day."
  3. How long? question, open pairs: S1: "How long have you been learning English?" S2: "For two years / Since 2023." S2 → S3 with a new verb (living in this apartment, working at…, driving, cooking…). Remind: a new identity answer is fine.
  4. Evidence prompts (flashcards or mime): wet hair → "You've been swimming!" · black hands → "He's been fixing the car." · yawning → "You haven't been sleeping well." · flour on hands → "She's been baking."
  5. How many or how long? T: "emails, three" → S: "I've written three emails." T: "emails, all morning" → S: "I've been writing emails all morning." T: "know her, ten years" → S: "I've known her for ten years." (the stative trap)
Clue game — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one deck face down. Player A takes a card, looks at the picture and the clue, and acts it out or reads the clue in the first person: "Oh, my back hurts, and I have mud on my knees!" A must not say the activity.
  • The others guess with a full question: "Have you been gardening?" A answers "Yes, I have!" or "No, I haven't. Try again!" The small gray hint on the card is for A only.
  • The first correct guesser wins the card and asks one "how many / how much" question: "How many flowers have you planted?" A answers in the simple: "I've planted twenty!" This is the simple vs continuous switch.
  • Stronger groups: A answers "No, I haven't" to the first correct guess and gives a second clue, to make more questions. Most cards wins.
  • Some cards have more than one good answer ("Your eyes are red": swimming, chopping onions, reading all night…). Any logical guess with the correct form wins.
ICQs before the clue game
Do you say the activity on your card? (No! You act it or read the clue.) · What's the first word of the guess? ("Have.") · Is "Have you painting?" correct? (No. "Have you been painting?") · After a correct guess, what question does the winner ask? (How many / how much.) · And the answer is simple or continuous? (Simple: "I've painted two rooms.")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on I've been / you've been + -ing and one use (evidence). In the clue game, the learner only guesses from the picture side; a partner reads the clue aloud. Phrase strip: "Have you been …ing?" / "Yes, I have. / No, I haven't." / "I've been …ing for … ." Target: 3 correct guesses and 2 true sentences about "lately." Workbook: Ex 1, Ex 2 and Ex 4 only.

Stretch

Use lately / recently / all week / for ages naturally. In "catching up," give a reason and a feeling ("I've been getting up at five, so I've been really tired"). Explain the difference between "I've painted the kitchen" and "I've been painting the kitchen" to a partner. Early finishers write 3 clue cards for the game.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am working here since 2020" / "I work here for two years." Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, German, Russian and Polish use the present (simple or continuous) for duration up to now. Point at the wavy line on the board: it started in the past → have been.
  • Dropping been: "I have working all day" (Arabic, Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog, where there is no helper verb chain). Clap three beats: have — been — working.
  • Stative verbs in -ing: "I've been knowing him," "I'm having a car." Common for speakers of Hindi, Urdu, Bengali and other South Asian languages, and heard in Indian and Nigerian English. Accept that it is normal in those varieties, but teach the standard form for tests and work.
  • "He have been": learners who drop the he/she -s (Chinese, Vietnamese, Haitian Creole speakers) also drop the has. Color has on the board.
  • Counted results with -ing: "I've been reading three books." Common for everybody who has just learned the new form. Ask: "How many or how long?"

1.3 · C3

Used to & Would — How Life Has Changed

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use used to + base verb for past habits and past states that are not true now: People used to write letters. There used to be a movie theater here.
  • make negatives and questions with did and the correct spelling: didn't use to, Did you use to…?
  • use would + base verb for repeated past actions once the past setting is clear: Every Sunday we would…
  • know that would does not work for states (live, like, have, be, know)
  • discuss how technology, work and daily life have changed, and say whether life was better before, with a reason

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: used to. A habit or a state in the past that is not true now. Everyone hears the unspoken "…but not anymore."

"I used to smoke." Did I smoke in the past? (Yes.) Once or many times? (Many times, a habit.) Do I smoke now? (No.) · "There used to be a movie theater here." Is it here now? (No, it's gone.)
"Did you use to have a bike?" Is this about now or the past? (The past.) What's the short answer? ("Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.")

Meaning: would. Repeated past actions in a story or a memory, after the past setting is clear.

"When I was a child, every Sunday my father would cook a big breakfast." Is this about the future? (No.) An imagined situation? (No.) One time or many times? (Many times, every Sunday.) · "I would live in Rome." Is this OK for a past state? (No. Live is a state: "I used to live in Rome.")
"I lived in Rome for two years." / "I used to live in Rome." Do I live in Rome now? (No, in both.) Which one feels like a general memory? (used to.) Which one gives the exact length? (The past simple.)

Form.

Positivesubject + used to + base verbI used to walk to school. · There used to be a bakery here.
Negativesubject + didn't use to + base verbI didn't use to like coffee.
QuestionDid + subject + use to + base verb?Did you use to play an instrument? — Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.
wouldsubject + would (+ always / often) + base verbEvery summer we would visit my grandparents. · He would always tell jokes.
The ruleactions: used to or would · states: used to onlyWe would go to the beach. ✅ · We would have a dog. ❌ → We used to have a dog. ✅
Not the same!be used to + -ing = be comfortable with (a present feeling)I'm used to getting up early. (Not today's lesson: just recognize it.)

Pronunciation. used to is one quick word: /ˈjustə/ ("YOO-stuh"), with an s sound, not a z, and a weak to. use to (after did) sounds the same, so the spelling rule only matters in writing. would is usually 'd after a pronoun: "we'd go," "she'd always sing." Stress the contrast words: "We USED to write LETTERS. NOW we send MESSAGES."

Drill script
  1. Then → now (flashcards): T shows the post office + phone: "letters / messages" → S: "People used to write letters. Now they send messages." Next: "cash / cards," "paper maps / GPS," "video store / streaming."
  2. Negative and question: T: "I liked vegetables." (shake head) → S: "I didn't use to like vegetables." T: "you / have a nickname?" → S: "Did you use to have a nickname?" → another S: "Yes, I did. / No, I didn't."
  3. /ˈjustə/ chorally: "used to — used to be — there used to be — there used to be a bakery here." Check nobody says "use-ed to."
  4. One finger or two? T says: "live near the sea" (1: only used to) · "play soccer every Saturday" (2: both) · "have long hair" (1) · "visit my aunt every summer" (2) · "know everyone" (1) · "tell stories at night" (2). Then S says the sentence with would for every "2."
  5. Memory chain: T: "When I was younger, every Saturday I would…" → S1 continues with would + an action → S2 adds another… (real or imagined).
How life has changed — how to run it
  • Topics, not biographies. Every card is about the world: phones, shopping, work, getting around, free time, food, money, learning. Learners can talk about people in general ("People used to…"), a place they know (any place: this city, a TV show, their workplace), their own life, or a new identity card. Never ask a learner to say which country they are talking about.
  • Roles (on the role cards): the chair asks each person in turn and invites quiet people ("Kofi, what do you think?"); the timekeeper says "One minute left!"; the questioner asks one follow-up per person ("Did people use to…?"); the reporter takes notes and gives the group's answer at the end. Give the chair role to a confident speaker, and the reporter role to someone who talks a lot but listens little.
  • Talking tokens: 3 per person. Put one in the middle when you take a long turn. When your tokens are gone, you can ask questions but not give opinions, until everyone has used theirs.
  • Discussion shape on each card: How did people use to…? → What would people do every day / week? → What's different now? → Was it better before, or is it better now? Why? Model an opinion phrase: "I think it was better before, because…" / "In my opinion, it's better now, because…"
  • Listen and note 6 sentences for feedback. Don't correct during the discussion unless the meaning breaks down.
ICQs before the discussion
How many topics does your group talk about? (Two.) · What do you do before you talk? (Two minutes of silent notes.) · Chair, what's your job? (Ask everyone. Help quiet people talk.) · What do you do with a token? (Put it down when I take a long turn.) · Do you have to talk about your own life? (No. People in general, a place, or a new identity card.) · Can we use would with "live" or "have"? (No! Only used to.)

Literacy support

Stretch

In the discussion, disagree politely with a reason ("I see your point, but…"). Early finishers: write the paragraph for Ex 5 with one negative and one question, and explain the difference between "I used to get up early" and "I'm used to getting up early."

L1 watch-outs
  • "I use to go to the gym" for a present habit. Spanish suelo, Portuguese costumo and Italian di solito work in the present, so many learners say "I use to drink coffee every morning" (meaning usually). Very common! Teach: present habit → usually; past habit → used to.
  • "Before, I live in a village" / "Before I am shy." Arabic, Chinese, Tagalog (dati), Vietnamese and Korean use a time word with no past form. It is understood; recast: "Oh, you used to live in a village."
  • "I used to went" / "I didn't used to" / "Did you used to…?" Everyone. Point at the red circle on the board.
  • "I would live in Rome" for a past state: learners who have just met would for past habits over-use it. Ask: "Action or state?"
  • "I was used to walk to school" (Russian, Polish and French speakers mixing used to with be used to). Keep be used to for recognition only today.
  • "use-ed to" with three syllables or a /z/ sound (Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese, Korean speakers read the spelling). Drill /ˈjustə/.

Closing the Module

End of C3: Module 1 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, the "past to now" line: I've been to… / I went… (experience → details) → I've been …ing for… (still going) → I used to… / We would… (not true now). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C4 warmer and again in the C13 progress-test catch-up.

Watch for in Module 2: conditionals bring back would, but with a different meaning (If I had more time, I would…). Learners who just learned would for past habits may be confused. Say it clearly in C5: "Same word, different job. Last week: past habits. Now: imagined situations." Keep a 2-minute "then and now" or "lately" warmer in C4–C6.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it tests: present perfect vs past simple with time words, for / since, just / already / yet / still, been vs gone, the form of the present perfect continuous, simple vs continuous (how many / how long), stative verbs, used to / didn't use to / Did … use to…?, and used to vs would for states and actions. The speaking task may ask learners to describe how something has changed.
