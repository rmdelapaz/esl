Module 5 — Narrating Past & Future

Three classes that turn learners into storytellers and planners. First the past perfect lets them rewind to "the past before the past" (When I got to the station, the train had already left). Then the three narrative tenses work as one team to tell a real story with a scene, a problem and an ending. Last, learners sort out will / going to / the present continuous and add the future continuous and time clauses (This time tomorrow I'll be flying. I'll call you when I land.). The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

3 lessons Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, discussion) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-5-narrating.html. Print materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html before C14. It holds the flashcards, the story & future identity cards, the listening scripts, the What Had Happened? picture-clue game, the Worst Day Ever story-chain kit and the Life in 2040 discussion cards.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. Learners met the past continuous + past simple ("I was cooking when the phone rang") and going to / will / might / present continuous for arrangements at A2. Only the past perfect, the future continuous and time clauses are really new. Check what they know in each lead-in and move fast where they are strong. Spend the saved time on speaking.
  • Fluency first, accuracy after. In the freer stages, don't stop a story to fix a tense. Listen, write down 4–6 sentences you hear (good ones too), and correct them together at the end, with no names. During the controlled stages, correct on the spot and briefly.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use some British forms (film, cinema, holiday, lift = a free ride, cancelled, labelled, organised, practise, round the table). Teach movie, movie theater, vacation, ride, canceled, labeled, organized, practice, around the table. Accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Rewind → tell → fast-forward. In C14 learners learn to rewind: to explain what had already happened before a past moment (the cause behind a surprise). In C15 they put the whole camera together: background (was raining), main events (the bus stopped) and flashback (I'd forgotten my umbrella), with connectors, to tell a real story. In C16 they fast-forward: they choose the best future form, picture themselves in the middle of a future moment (This time next week I'll be…), and discuss how life might look in 2040. Every class ends with a longer speaking turn than the class before: a 6-sentence story → a 2-minute group story → a 15-minute discussion.

Trauma-informed: stories and futures are personal

Past stories. "Tell me about your worst day" can open painful memories (war, migration, loss, accidents, illness). This module keeps stories light and fictional by default: the picture-clue game and the story chain use invented characters. When learners tell their own story, the choice is always theirs: a small, light real story (a missed bus, lost keys, a cooking disaster, a funny mistake), a story identity card, or an invented story. Say the "light" rule out loud: "Choose a small problem with a happy or funny ending." Never ask a follow-up about why someone chose a card.

Futures. For some adults the future is uncertain: a visa or asylum decision, a job search, family far away. Never ask "Where will you be living next year?", "Will you stay here?" or "When will you see your family?" In C16 the main discussion is about society (transport, work, shopping, schools in 2040). The personal part is optional and short: this time next week (safe and small), a future identity card, or an imagined person in 2040. Anyone can say "Pass." Offer these choices every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Before the Module

  • Mark Progress Test 1 before C14. Write a short, positive comment on every paper: one thing they did well + one thing to practice. Collect 4–5 common errors from different papers for the C14 board (no names). Note which learners scored below about 60% so you can offer a quiet 1:1 chat at the break.
  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board). Card stock helps.
  • For C14: one What Had Happened? picture-clue deck per group of 3–4 (16 cards). Fold or cover the answer strip at the bottom of each card before class, or cut it off and keep the answers yourself.
  • For C15: one Worst Day Ever kit per group of 4: the story map (A4), 1 starter card, 12 event cards, 1 reaction strip. Cut the cards.
  • For C16: one set of Life in 2040 topic cards and one set of role cards per group of 4, plus 3 talking chips per learner (paper clips, coins or the printed chips).
  • Print the story & future identity cards (6 people). Keep them on a side table for all three classes: anyone can take one without asking.
  • Optional: a timer on screen (phone or free online timer) for the story retells in C15 and the discussion in C16.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the past before the past. Two past actions; the past perfect marks the one that happened first. It is about order, not about "a long time ago."

"When I arrived, the train had already left." Two actions: what are they? (I arrived; the train left.) Which happened first? (The train left.) Did I see the train? (No.) Which verb is in the past perfect? (had left: the earlier one.)
"When I arrived, the train left." Did I see the train? (Yes, it was leaving as I arrived.) So is it the same meaning? (No!)
"I was hungry because I hadn't eaten all day." Which came first: not eating, or being hungry? (Not eating.) Is "hadn't eaten" the cause or the result? (The cause.)

Meaning: when you don't need it. A plain sequence in order: I got home, cooked dinner and watched TV. The order is already clear, so use past simples. Save the past perfect for when you jump back.

"I got up, had breakfast and left." Do I need "had had breakfast"? (No. The order is clear: one, two, three.) · "After I'd finished, I rested." Is "after I finished" also OK? (Yes. "After" already shows the order.)

Contrast with the present perfect (stretch, 1 minute): I've eaten, so I'm not hungry now. / I'd eaten, so I wasn't hungry then.

Form.

Positivesubject + had ('d) + past participleShe had gone. · They'd left. · I'd finished my work.
Negativesubject + hadn't + past participleWe hadn't met before that day.
QuestionHad + subject + past participle…?Had you seen the email? — Yes, I had. / No, I hadn't.
Wh- questionWh- + had + subject + pp…?Why had he already gone? · What had they done?
Positionhad + already / just / never + ppThe bus had already gone. · I had just sat down. · I'd never flown before.
Signal wordsby the time · before · after · because · whenBy the time we arrived, it had closed.

Pronunciation. In speech had is almost always 'd, and it is very small: I'd /aɪd/, she'd /ʃid/, they'd /ðeɪd/, it'd /ˈɪtəd/. Learners must learn to hear it: "I'd finished" and "I finished" sound almost the same, so the participle and the context carry the meaning. Stress falls on the participle and the time word: "the train had alREADy LEFT." hadn't /ˈhædənt/ is stressed and clear (two syllables). In short answers use the full, stressed form: "Yes, I HAD." (never "Yes, I'd.") Remind learners that 'd + participle = had; 'd + base verb = would (I'd gone / I'd go).

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "left" → "already left" → "the train had already left" → "When I arrived, the train had already left." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Then a personal version with 'd: "When I got there, I realized I'd forgotten my phone."
  2. Two actions → one sentence (T gives two past actions in the real order; learners join them): T: "The movie started. Then we arrived." → S: "When we arrived, the movie had started." T: "The shop closed. Then I got there." → S: "By the time I got there, the shop had closed."
  3. 'd substitution: T: "I had forgotten my keys." → S: "I'd forgotten my keys." T: "She… lose her phone" → S: "She'd lost her phone." T: "They… never see snow" → S: "They'd never seen snow."
  4. Question and short answer, open pairs: S1: "Had you eaten when you came to class?" S2: "Yes, I had. / No, I hadn't." S2 → S3: "Had you used English at work before this course?" S3 answers… (Keep questions small and everyday.)
  5. Because chain: T: "Omar was tired." → S1: "…because he hadn't slept well." → T: "Ana was happy." → S2: "…because she'd passed her test."
What Had Happened? — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one deck face down. Player 1 is the reader: takes a card, reads the scene aloud ("When Ana got to the platform, she was very upset.") and shows the picture clues. The answer strip is hidden (folded under).
  • The others are detectives. They ask yes/no questions with Had…? ("Had the train left?" "Had she lost her ticket?") and the reader answers "Yes, she had." / "No, she hadn't." They can also guess with a statement: "I think she'd missed the train."
  • The first detective to find the answer on the strip keeps the card. After 8 questions with no answer, the reader reads the strip aloud ("The train had already left.") and keeps the card. Then the next player is the reader. The most cards wins.
  • Stretch rule: before a detective can guess, they must say the earlier and the later action ("First the train left, then she arrived").
  • Round 2 (if time): groups use the two blank cards to invent a scene for another group.
  • Walk around and listen. Note sentences for feedback; don't stop the game to correct.
ICQs before the game
Can the detectives see the answer? (No!) · What's the first word of your question? ("Had.") · "Had she lost her ticket?" What does the reader say? ("Yes, she had." or "No, she hadn't.") · How many questions before the reader tells the answer? (Eight.) · Who keeps the card? (The person who finds the answer.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with a small bank of 8 participles (left, gone, lost, forgotten, eaten, closed, started, finished) on a strip, and the frame "When she arrived, ___ had ___." In the game, the teacher or a partner reads the scene aloud; the learner uses the picture clues and one question frame: "Had he/she ___?" Target: 4 spoken past perfect sentences and 3 questions. In Ex 1, number only 4 events (the first four).

Stretch

Use all six signal words in the "My story" plan. Add a because … had … cause and a never … before first-time experience. Contrast the present perfect ("I've lost my keys" = now I can't get in) with the past perfect ("I'd lost my keys" = back then). In the game, give the reason in two ways: "When she arrived, the train had left." / "The train had left by the time she arrived."

Handling dominant speakers (all module)

In group games one confident learner often answers every card. Use turn rules, not personality: the reader changes every card; each detective may ask only one question per turn (clockwise); the person who just won a card asks last next time. Say it as a game rule for everyone, so no one feels singled out. If one learner still leads, give them a job: "You're the judge. Check that every question starts with Had." Quiet learners can read the card aloud first, which gives them a safe, prepared turn.

L1 watch-outs
  • "When I arrived, the train already left." (past simple + already): Chinese (已经), Vietnamese (đã), Thai, Indonesian, Tagalog and Russian (uzhe) show order with a time word, not a tense. It is understood. Recast and point at the time line: "Which came first? → had left."
  • "She had forget / had went": Arabic, Spanish and many others. Same participle list as the present perfect: go → gone, forget → forgotten, leave → left.
  • "Had you did it?" / "Did you had seen…?": learners who have just drilled did questions. "One helper only: had."
  • Overusing the past perfect for any "long ago" past: "I had been born in 1985." "When I was a child, I had lived in a village." Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian speakers sometimes use their pluperfect this way. Ask "Earlier than what?" If there is no later past moment, use the past simple.
  • Not hearing 'd: Vietnamese, Chinese and Korean speakers (final consonants) may drop the /d/ in I'd and the /t/ in hadn't. Use the 1-finger / 2-finger listening game and the dictation.
  • 'd = had or would? Learners from any L1 mix these in reading. Teach the test: look at the next verb (participle → had; base verb → would).

Narrative Tenses — Telling a Story

90 min

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the camera. The past simple is the action shots that move the story forward. The past continuous is the wide background shot (weather, place, what was going on). The past perfect is a quick flashback.

"It was raining when Dev left the house." Which started first: the rain, or Dev leaving? (The rain.) Did the rain stop when he left? (No, it continued.) Which is the background? (It was raining.)
"While Dev was drinking his coffee, the bus stopped." Which is long? (Drinking coffee.) Which is short and sudden? (The bus stopped.) Can I say "the bus was stopping" here? (Not for a sudden event: "stopped.")
"He'd left his umbrella at home." Did this happen in the middle of the story or before it? (Before.) Why does the listener need it? (To understand why he got wet.)

Balance. The past simple is the most common tense in a story. The past perfect appears only a few times, for the moments you step back. Tell learners: "Most of the story: past simple. A little background. One or two flashbacks."

Form.

Past simpleverb + -ed / irregularmain events, in orderShe opened the door and walked in.
Past continuouswas / were + -ingbackground · action in progressIt was raining and the wind was blowing.
Past perfecthad + past participlewhat had happened earlierShe had left her umbrella at home.
while / whenwhile + was -ing · when + past simplelong action + short interruptionWhile we were eating, the lights went out. · I was walking home when I saw an old friend.
Connectorsfirst · then · after that · while · suddenly · a few minutes later · by the time (+ had) · in the end

Pronunciation. Storytelling is performance. was and were are weak in the middle of a sentence: /wəz/, /wər/ ("it wəz RAINing"). Pause before suddenly and stress it: "…and SUDdenly…". In the end falls to finish the story. Past simple -ed: remind learners of /t/ /d/ /ɪd/ (walked, arrived, started); it carries the whole story. Listener reactions need big intonation: "No WAY! ↗↘" A flat "no way" sounds bored.

Drill script
  1. Background + event (T gives two prompts; learners join them with when): T: "I / walk home · it / start to rain" → S: "I was walking home when it started to rain." T: "we / have dinner · the lights / go out" → S: "We were having dinner when the lights went out."
  2. Now with while: same prompts → "While I was walking home, it started to rain." Choral, then individuals.
  3. Add a flashback: T: "I got wet." → S: "I got wet because I'd forgotten my umbrella." T: "She was nervous." → S: "She was nervous because she'd never given a speech before."
  4. Connector chain: T holds up a connector card; the next learner adds a sentence to a class story: "First…" → "Then…" → "While…" → "Suddenly…" → "By the time…" → "In the end…"
  5. Reaction ping-pong: T says a story line ("…and the bus broke down!"); learners react with the right phrase and intonation ("Oh no!" / "No way!" / "Then what happened?").
Worst Day Ever story chain — how to run it
  • Groups of 4 with the story map (A4) in the middle, a starter card (a fictional character and a big day: a wedding photographer, a first day at a new job, an anniversary dinner…) and 12 event cards face down.
  • Player 1 reads the starter card and says step ① (the scene) with the past continuous: "It was a hot Saturday morning, and the birds were singing." Player 2 takes an event card and uses it in step ② or ③, and so on round the table. The map shows which tense each step needs. Go round the table twice (steps 1–6, then a better second version).
  • Anyone can change an event card for a new one. The story should end well or funny: "In the end, … and everyone laughed."
  • Retell: every learner can tell the group story alone, so everyone practices the whole thing. Partners from other groups listen, react ("No way!") and ask one question ("How did it end?").
  • Real stories are optional. In the second retell, learners may tell their own light story (a small problem with a happy or funny ending) or a story identity card story. Never ask anyone to tell a real "worst day."
  • Third and mixed conditionals. Stories invite "If I had left earlier, I would have…". This is B2. Accept it if a learner says it, and don't teach it. Offer the B1 way: "I should leave earlier next time!" or "Next time I'll check the time."
ICQs before the story chain
Is this a true story or an invented story? (Invented.) · Step 1: which tense? (was / were + -ing: the background.) · Step 5 starts with…? ("By the time" + had.) · How many times do you go round the table? (Twice.) · Must the story end badly? (No, happy or funny.) · When your partner tells a story, what do you do? (React and ask one question.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the story map with picture icons only (🎥 scene, ⏪ flashback, ⚡ suddenly, ➡️ then, ⏱️ by the time, 🏁 in the end) and a sentence strip for each step ("It was ___ and ___ was ___-ing." / "Suddenly, ___." / "In the end, ___."). In the chain, the learner takes steps 1, 3 or 6 (the easiest frames). Target: tell 3 steps of the story with 3 connectors. In Ex 1, answer questions 1, 3 and 6 only.

Stretch

Add a second flashback with just or never … before. Use a few minutes later and it turned out that…. In the retell, change the point of view (tell it as a different character) or add direct speech ("She said, 'Don't worry!'"). Early finishers write the group story (8–10 sentences) and label every verb S / C / P.

Fluency before accuracy — the retell

In the retell rounds, your job is to listen, not to correct. Walk around with a notebook and write sentences down exactly as you hear them. Correct only if the listener can't understand. The time limit is part of the task: telling the same story again, faster, to a new person builds fluency. Afterwards, do the delayed correction with anonymous sentences, and always include one or two excellent sentences to praise.

L1 watch-outs
  • Continuous for sudden events: "Suddenly the phone was ringing." Russian and Polish (aspect), Hindi/Urdu and Turkish speakers may choose by "how it feels," not by "short or long." Ask: "Long background or one short moment?"
  • Continuous with state verbs: "I was knowing him." "The house was having a garden." Spanish, Portuguese and French speakers map their imperfect (which covers states) onto was -ing. Teach: know, have (own), like, want, believe stay simple.
  • Dropping was / were: "I walking home when…" Arabic, Chinese, Vietnamese, Haitian Creole. Point at the board: was + -ing.
  • he / she in a story about a character: Tagalog, Chinese, Finnish, Turkish, Persian, Yoruba and Hungarian speakers (one word for he/she). Stories with named characters make this very visible. Recast gently: "Dev? He got wet."
  • "At the end" / "At the final" / "Finally" for "in the end": Spanish al final, Portuguese no final, French à la fin. In the end = the result of the story; at the end of + noun (at the end of the day).
  • "Actually" for "now / at the moment": Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, Portuguese atualmente. In English actually = "in fact" (a surprise), which is great in stories: "It was actually my old teacher!"

5.3 · C16

Future Forms & Future Continuous

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • choose the best future form: will (decision now, prediction, offer, promise), going to (plan / intention, evidence), present continuous (fixed arrangement)
  • form and use the future continuous (will be + -ing) for an action in progress at a future time: This time tomorrow I'll be flying to Chicago.
  • ask polite, no-pressure questions: Will you be using the car tonight?
  • use the present simple (not will) after when, as soon as, before, after, until, by the time: I'll call you when I arrive.
  • discuss life in 2040 with reasons and at least three future forms, and agree / disagree politely

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: which future? (review, fast)

(Phone rings.) "I'll get it!" Did I plan this? (No.) When did I decide? (Now.) · "I'm going to call her tonight." Plan or decision now? (A plan I already had.) · "I'm meeting Sara at six." Is it in my diary? (Yes: a fixed arrangement.) · "Look at those clouds: it's going to rain." Can I see something? (Yes: evidence.)

Meaning: future continuous. An action in progress at a point in the future: already started, not finished. It is the future version of the past continuous.

"This time tomorrow, I'll be flying to Chicago." At this time tomorrow, is the flight starting or already happening? (Already happening: I'm in the middle of it.) · "At 8 p.m. I'll watch the game." / "At 8 p.m. I'll be watching the game." Which one starts at 8? (The first.) In which one am I already watching at 8? (The second.)
"Will you be using the car tonight?" Am I asking you to do something? (No.) Am I asking about your plans? (Yes, politely.) Why is it polite? (It doesn't push. Maybe I want the car, but I don't say it directly.)

Meaning: time clauses. After when, as soon as, before, after, until, by the time, the meaning is future but the verb is present.

"I'll call you when I arrive." Have I arrived? (No: future.) Which verb is future? ("I'll call.") Why not "when I will arrive"? (After when, we use the present.) · "as soon as" = slow or fast? (Immediately.) · "We'll wait until she comes." Do we wait after she comes? (No, we stop waiting.)

Form.

willwill ('ll) + base verbdecision now · prediction · offer · promiseIt's cold. I'll close the window.
going toam / is / are going to + base verbplan / intention · evidenceWe're going to paint the kitchen.
Present continuousam / is / are + -ing + timefixed arrangementWe're flying to Madrid on Saturday.
Future continuouswill be + -ing · won't be + -ing · Will you be + -ing?in progress at a future time · polite questionAt 8 p.m. I'll be watching the game. · Will you be joining us?
Time clausemain clause (will) + when / as soon as / before / after / until + present simplefuture meaningI'll text you as soon as I land.

Pronunciation. I'll be links into one beat: /aɪl bi/ ("ail-bee"); stress the main verb: "I'll be FLYing to ChiCAgo." won't be /woʊnt bi/: keep the t (it carries the negative). In time clauses the stress is on both verbs: "I'll CALL you when I arRIVE." Polite questions rise gently at the end: "Will you be using the CAR tonight? ↗". In the discussion, softeners make predictions sound natural and open: "I think… · Probably… · I don't think… will…".

Drill script
  1. This time tomorrow (T shows a clock time and an activity): T: "9 a.m. · work" → S: "At 9 a.m. tomorrow, I'll be working." T: "8 p.m. · cook dinner" → S: "At 8 p.m., I'll be cooking dinner." Change the person: "she / they."
  2. Negative + reason: T: "Don't call me at 7." → S: "I won't be answering. I'll be driving."
  3. Make it polite: T: "Will you use the printer later?" → S: "Will you be using the printer later?" T: "Will you come to the meeting?" → S: "Will you be coming to the meeting?"
  4. Time-clause chain: S1: "I'll relax when I get home." → S2: "When I get home, I'll…" → S3: "As soon as I…, I'll…" Around the room.
  5. Which future? — fast: T gives a situation; learners answer at once: "Someone knocks at the door." (I'll get it.) · "Your flight is booked for Friday." (I'm flying on Friday.) · "You decided to learn to cook." (I'm going to take a class.) · "Your friend is about to drop a glass." (You're going to drop that!)
Life in 2040 discussion — how to run it
  • Warm-up (pairs, optional personal): "This time next week, I'll be…" Learners choose: their real next week (small and safe), a future identity card, or an imagined person in 2040 ("In 2040, Maya will be working on a farm in the city."). Nobody talks about visas, family plans or where they will live.
  • Groups of 4 with roles (role cards): the Chair reads the topic and makes sure everyone speaks; the Question-asker asks "Why do you think so?" and "What about…?"; the Timekeeper moves on after 3 minutes; the Reporter notes the most surprising prediction. Everyone also gives opinions.
  • Topics are about society, not about the learners' own lives: getting around, work, shopping, homes, schools, food, money, phones, our city, free time, doctors and hospitals, learning languages. Each card has two questions and a sentence starter.
  • Talking chips: each learner has 3 chips. Put one in the middle every time you speak for more than a sentence. When your chips are gone, you listen and ask questions until everyone has used theirs. Then everyone takes their chips back.
  • Language targets on the card: 1 prediction with will / might / won't / will probably, 1 will be + -ing ("In 2040 most people will be working from home."), 1 time clause ("When cars drive themselves, people won't need a license."), and one polite disagreement ("I'm not sure. I think…").
ICQs before the discussion
Are we talking about your own life in 2040? (No, about society: work, transport, shopping…) · Must you talk about your own next week? (No. You can choose a card or an imagined person.) · What does the Chair do? (Makes sure everyone speaks.) · When do you put a chip in the middle? (When I speak.) · No chips left: what do you do? (Listen and ask questions.) · What do you say if you disagree? ("I'm not sure. I think…")
Handling dominant speakers

B1 discussions often turn into one learner's speech. The talking chips and roles fix most of it without comment. Give a strong, talkative learner the Question-asker or Timekeeper role: they stay active but spend their energy getting others to talk. Give a quiet learner the Chair role with the card in hand: reading the question aloud is a safe first turn. If someone still takes over, walk past and say to the whole group: "Chair, has everyone answered this one?" Never correct a quiet learner in front of the group during the discussion.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the picture topic cards (🚗 💼 🛒 🏠 🏫) and three frames only: "In 2040, people will ___." / "I think ___ might ___." / "I don't think ___ will ___." For the future continuous, use the clock frame "At ___ tomorrow, I'll be ___-ing" with the Priya pictures from Ex 4. Target: 3 predictions and 1 "I'll be -ing" sentence. Pair the learner with a supportive Reporter.

Stretch

Use will probably / probably won't in the right position, by 2040 with the future continuous ("By 2040, we'll be using…" is fine), and two time clauses in one idea ("As soon as batteries get cheaper, most people will drive electric cars, and when that happens, cities will be quieter."). Early finishers write a 6-sentence "A day in 2040" paragraph with four future forms.

L1 watch-outs
  • "When I will arrive": French (quand j'arriverai), Russian (kogda ya priedu, future form), Greek and others use a future form after "when." Spanish and Portuguese use a subjunctive, so learners guess. Link it to the first conditional: "like if: present after when."
  • Present simple for the future: "I call you tomorrow." "Next week I go to Chicago." (German, Russian, Spanish, Chinese, Japanese). Understood; recast with the best form. Timetables are the exception: "The train leaves at 6."
  • "I'll flying" / "I will be fly": missing be (Arabic, Vietnamese, Chinese) or missing -ing. Board chant: "will + be + -ing."
  • "After ten years, cars will fly." for "in ten years": Spanish después de, Chinese 十年后, Korean 10년 후. In + amount of time = from now; after + amount = after something else.
  • German / Dutch "will" = want: "I will a new job" = I want. Back again from A2: "I want (a wish) / I'll (future)."
  • Very direct disagreement: "No. You are wrong." Some learners (German, Dutch, Russian, Israeli) may sound blunt; others (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Filipino) may never disagree. Teach the middle: "I'm not sure. I think…" and "Maybe, but…".

Closing the Module

End of C16: Module 5 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "rewind, tell, fast-forward": had + pp (the earlier past) → was -ing / past simple / had + pp together (a whole story) → will / going to / -ing / will be -ing (the future). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C17 warmer.

Check the C14 goals. At the end of C16, invite learners to read their Progress Test goal again (the "Look back" box in Workbook 5.3): "Did you practice it? What will you do next?" This is private: no one reports to the class.

Watch for in Module 6: articles (a / the / zero) show up in every story. Learners who just told stories will say "I went to the work" or "The life is hard." Keep a 2-minute story warmer in C17–C19: one learner gives a 3-sentence story, the class listens for articles as well as tenses.

Final assessment (C26) covers all eight modules. From this module it tests: past perfect (+ / − / ?), earlier vs later action, by the time / already / just / never … before, narrative tenses in a story, while / when, story connectors, the choice of future form, the future continuous, and time clauses with the present simple.
