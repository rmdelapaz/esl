Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 6 — Precision & Detail

Three classes on the small words that make B1 English sound exact. First the articles (a / an, the and no article), organized around one question: Does my listener already know which one I mean? Then verb patterns: which verbs take -ing and which take to, plus the verbs that change meaning (stop, remember, forget, try). Last, degree and quantity: too / enough, so / such … that, (a) few / (a) little and all / most / some / none of, used for real in complaints and compliments.

3 lessons Lessons 6.1–6.3 Classes C17–C19 Weeks 9–10 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle, role-play) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-6-precision.html. Print materials/module-6/classroom-materials.html before C17. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Editor texts, the Describe & Draw place cards, the verb-sort and meaning-change sentence cards, the Find Someone Who game, the complaints & compliments role-play cards, the Community Center problem-solving task and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • This is a precision module, not a new-tense module. Learners already communicate without perfect articles or verb patterns. The goal is accuracy that grows over time, not overnight perfection. Say this out loud in C17: "Article mistakes almost never stop people understanding you. We're polishing, not fixing something broken." Learners from article-free languages need to hear it.
  • Extension, not a first meeting. A2 covered like / want / would like + -ing / to and too / enough with adjectives. Check what learners know in each lead-in and move fast where they are strong. The new B1 work is the choice: the article decision flow and the fixed cases, verb + object + to, -ing after prepositions, the meaning-change verbs, so / such … that and few vs a few.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons often use British forms: film, holiday, favourite, practise, travelling, post a letter, queue, play football, at the weekend, and (6.1) go to hospital. Teach movie, vacation, favorite, practice, traveling, mail a letter, line, play soccer, on the weekend and go to the hospital (American English keeps the here). Accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Which one? → Which form? → How much? In C17 learners decide which thing they mean (a new one, the one we both know, or things in general), and they act as editors who fix a real text. In C18 they decide which verb form follows another verb, and they learn that sometimes the form changes the meaning (I stopped buying coffee ≠ I stopped to buy coffee). In C19 they say how much (too cold, not warm enough, so loud that…, a few towels) and use it for a real-life job: complaining politely and giving a compliment. Every class ends with a speaking task where precision makes a difference to the listener.

Fluency first, then accuracy — and dominant speakers

Precision is easy to over-correct. In the freer stages, do not stop speakers to fix an article or an -ing form. Walk around with a notepad and write down what you hear (good and not yet right). Correct after the task, on the board, without names. Only interrupt if a partner truly cannot understand. In controlled stages (workbook, drills), correct on the spot, quickly and kindly.

Dominant speakers. B1 classes often have one or two fluent, confident talkers. Give every pair and group task a visible structure: a timer for each speaker, a talking token (a pen that passes), or a role (speaker / listener / checker in C18). In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice, please" and wait 5 seconds before you accept an answer. Give strong speakers a stretch job (the checker, the Editor B text, the manager role in C19) so they are busy, not bored.

Trauma-informed: places, memories and complaints

Three tasks in this module can touch painful things. C17 asks learners to describe a place they know well: for some, "home" is a camp, a shelter, or a place they lost. C18 uses remember doing: childhood memories are not easy for everyone. C19 practices complaints: some learners have had real, frightening conflicts with landlords, employers or officials. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: a real place or memory, a new identity card (in the classroom materials), or a place card / an invented one. In C19 the role-plays are clearly roles on cards; nobody has to use a real complaint. Anyone can say "Pass." Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the one question. Does my listener already know which one I mean? If yes → the. If no, and it's one countable thing → a / an. If I mean things in general (a plural or an uncountable) → no article.

"I saw a dog in the park." Do you know this dog? (No, it's new.) · "The dog was huge." Which dog? (The one I just told you about.) · "Can you close the door?" Which door? (That one. We can both see it.) · "The sun is hot." How many suns? (One. It's unique.)
"I love music." All music, or one song? (All music, in general.) · "I love the music in this café." In general, or this music? (This music, here.) · "She's a doctor." Is this her job? (Yes.) Can I say "She's doctor"? (No. Jobs need a / an.)
"an hour" — the first letter is h. Why an? (We don't say the h. The first sound is a vowel.) · "a university" — the first letter is u. Why a? (It sounds like "you." That's a consonant sound.)
Fixed cases: "I go to bed at eleven." Am I going to the bed to fix it or to sleep? (To sleep.) · "My mom is in the hospital." American or British? (American. British says "in hospital.") · "She plays the piano. He plays soccer." Which one has the? (Instruments. Sports don't.)

Form.

a / ansingular countable noun: new, one of many, a jobI bought a phone. · Pass me a pen. · He's an engineer.
thesingular, plural or uncountable: the listener knows whichThe phone stopped working. · the sun · the best day · the woman who called
— (zero)plural or uncountable noun, in generalI like cats. · Water is essential. · Life is hard.
zero (fixed)most countries, languages, meals, days, cities, streets, sportsin Japan · speak Spanish · have lunch · on Monday · play chess
the (fixed)some countries, instruments, unique thingsthe USA · the UK · play the guitar · the internet
purpose vs placego to bed / school / work / church (for its purpose)go to the school (the building) · US: go to the hospital

Pronunciation. Articles are almost always weak and unstressed: a /ə/, an /ən/, the /ðə/ before a consonant sound. Before a vowel sound many speakers say the /ði/: "thee apple," "thee hour." An links to the next word: "an‿apple" sounds like "a-napple." Learners who stress every article ("I saw A dog") sound slow and robotic; drill the weak form in chunks: "a‿dog, the‿dog, an‿hour." The th sound /ð/: Spanish, French, Russian, Tagalog, Arabic and Vietnamese speakers may say "de" or "ze"; show the tongue between the teeth, but don't make it the focus today.

Drill script
  1. First → second mention: T: "I bought… jacket." → S: "I bought a jacket. The jacket was expensive." T: "I saw… man." → S: "I saw a man. The man was singing." Choral ×2, then 5–6 individuals with new nouns (a cake, an umbrella, a letter).
  2. a or an? T says a noun; learners say it with the article, fast: "hour" → "an hour" · "university" → "a university" · "honest man" → "an honest man" · "European city" → "a European city" · "umbrella" → "an umbrella" · "uniform" → "a uniform".
  3. General or specific? T: "music, general" → S: "I love music." T: "music, this café" → S: "I love the music in this café." T: "dogs, general" → S: "Dogs are loyal." T: "dogs, next door" → S: "The dogs next door are loud."
  4. Jobs chain: S1: "I'm a cook. What do you do?" → S2: "I'm an office assistant. What do you do?" (real job, a new identity card, or a dream job: "I want to be a…").
  5. Fixed chunks, choral: "go to bed · go to work · have breakfast · by bus · on the bus · play the guitar · play soccer." Then T gives a picture card; learners make a sentence about their day.
The Editor — how to run it
  • Set the scene: "You're the editors of the community newsletter. A new volunteer wrote this. Her first language has no articles. Her ideas are great; your job is to make it perfect before it's printed." This makes the mistakes someone else's and treats the writer with respect.
  • Tell them the number: 12 mistakes: 6 missing, 3 extra, 3 wrong. Knowing the number keeps pairs searching and stops them "fixing" correct articles. There are also correct articles that look suspicious (The children come every Friday; Most beds belong to families). Those are traps.
  • Editor's code: ^ add an article · / delete it · circle it and write the new one above. Pairs must agree on a reason for each change, using the board question.
  • Finished early? Editor B (a text message about a new job, 10 mistakes: 4 missing, 5 extra, 1 wrong). Answers are in the answer key.
  • L1 note to share: "If your language has no articles, like Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese or Polish, missing articles are your typical mistake. If your language has articles, like Spanish, French, Portuguese or Arabic, extra the in general sentences is your typical mistake. Which editor are you?" Learners enjoy spotting their own pattern.
Describe & Draw — how to run it
  • A sits with the notes; B has a blank paper and a pencil. B must not see A's notes. A describes: "There's a big window on the left. The window has green curtains. Under the window there's a sofa…" B draws and checks: "Is the sofa under the window?"
  • Each description needs 8+ sentences: new things with a / an, then the same things with the, plus 2 general sentences with no article ("It sells coffee and cakes." "I love quiet mornings there.").
  • Place cards (café, bus station, library, laundromat, playground, workplace kitchen) give ready ideas. A learner with a new identity card describes that person's place. Nobody explains why they chose a card.
  • While they talk, you write down sentences for the feedback stage. Don't correct during the task.
ICQs before the Editor and Describe & Draw
Editor: How many mistakes? (Twelve.) · How many are missing articles? (Six.) · What does ^ mean? (Add an article.) · Do you change every article? (No! Some are correct.) · Do you need a reason? (Yes, for every change.)
Describe & Draw: Can B see A's notes? (No.) · First time you say "window": a or the? (A.) · Second time? (The.) · Must you describe your real home? (No. A place card or a new identity card is fine.) · How many sentences? (Eight or more.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on two jobs of articles: a for a new thing and the for the same thing again, plus a / an with jobs. In the Editor, give a large-print copy of the first four lines only, with each mistake already underlined: the learner only decides "add, delete or change." In Describe & Draw, the learner uses the place card's picture list and says 4 pairs of sentences ("There's a table. The table is big."). Pair them with a patient partner who draws.

Stretch

Add the + defined by what follows (the café on the corner, the man who works there) and superlatives (the best seat, the only window) to the description. In the Editor, explain each change in writing using the grammar words (first mention, general, unique). Early finishers do Editor B and then write a "trap" sentence with a correct but surprising article for the class to judge.

L1 watch-outs
  • No articles at all ("I bought phone. Phone is broken."): Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, Vietnamese, Thai, Hindi/Urdu, Turkish (bir ≈ one only), Swahili and many more. These learners often cannot hear the weak /ə/ at first. Be patient: noticing comes first, then accuracy. Tagalog markers (ang, ng, si) are not articles, so Filipino learners also drop or add them unpredictably.
  • Extra "the" for general ideas ("The life is hard." "I like the animals." "She studies the medicine."): Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, German and Arabic (al- works for general meaning, al-ḥayāt = "the life"). Teach "In English, general = no article."
  • No "a" with jobs ("She's doctor." "I want to be teacher."): Spanish soy doctora, Portuguese, French je suis médecin, German Ich bin Lehrer, Arabic (no indefinite article), Russian. Very frequent. Drill it.
  • "the France / the Brazil": French la France, Portuguese o Brasil, Italian l'Italia. Most countries = no article; only the USA, the UK, the Netherlands, the Philippines (plural or "united / republic / kingdom" names).
  • Meals and instruments: "have the breakfast" (French le petit-déjeuner, Spanish el desayuno), "play guitar" (fine in casual US English, so accept it; teach play the guitar as the standard).
  • a / an by spelling: "a hour, an university." Everyone, especially learners who read more than they listen. Always say it; don't look at it.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: two forms after a verb. The first verb decides. Many -ing verbs are about experiences and reactions (enjoy, avoid, mind); many to-verbs look forward (want, hope, plan, promise). It's a helpful idea, not a rule. Learn them in groups.

"I enjoy cooking." Is cooking an activity I like now, in general? (Yes.) · "I want to learn to bake." Now or in the future? (The future.) · "She told him to wait outside." Who waits? (He does.) Who gives the instruction? (She does.) · "He asked to help." Who helps? (He does.) "He asked me to help." Who helps now? (I do.)
"I'm looking forward to seeing you." Can I say "looking forward to the weekend"? (Yes.) So is this to part of a verb, or a preposition? (A preposition.) So…? (-ing.) · "Swimming is good for you." What's the subject of the sentence? (Swimming.)

Meaning: the four special verbs.

stop: "He stopped working last year." Does he work now? (No.) · "On the way home, he stopped to buy bread." Did he stop walking or driving? (Yes.) Why? (To buy bread.) Does to mean "in order to"? (Yes.)
remember / forget: "I remember locking the door." Did I lock it? (Yes.) Is it a memory? (Yes, the past.) · "Remember to lock the door." Did you lock it? (Not yet.) Is it a task for later? (Yes.) · "I forgot to call my mom." Did I call her? (No.)
try: "My laptop froze. Try restarting it." Is it difficult to restart a laptop? (No.) Is it a test, an idea to fix the problem? (Yes.) · "I tried to open the jar, but I couldn't." Easy or difficult? (Difficult.) Did I make an effort? (Yes.)

Form.

verb + -ingenjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, keep, mind, can't stand, feel like, give up, miss, practice, imagineDo you mind waiting? · He suggested going out.
verb + towant, decide, hope, promise, learn, offer, refuse, manage, agree, plan, need, can't afford, would likeWe managed to finish on time.
verb + person + towant, tell, ask, advise, allow, remind, invite, warn, encourage, teachI want you to listen. · She warned him not to touch it.
preposition + -inggood at, interested in, tired of, before, after, without, instead of, look forward to, be used toWe walked instead of taking the bus.
-ing as subject-ing + verb…Getting up early is hard in winter.
like / love / hate+ -ing or to (almost the same)I like swimming. = I like to swim.

Pronunciation. -ing is /ɪŋ/: one nasal sound at the back, no hard /g/ after it ("sing-g" is a common over-correction by Spanish and Polish speakers) and not /ɪn/ in careful speech. to is weak /tə/ and joins the verb: "want to" /ˈwɑntə/, "decided to" /dɪˈsaɪdɪd tə/. In "stopped to buy," the /t/ of stopped and to run together, so learners may hear "stop to buy." Stress carries the meaning: "I stopped to BUY coffee" / "I stopped BUYing coffee." Vietnamese and Cantonese speakers may drop the -ed and final consonants; clap the two beats: "stopped · to."

Drill script
  1. -ing or to, quick-fire: T: "enjoy / swim" → S: "I enjoy swimming." T: "decide / stay home" → S: "I decided to stay home." T: "mind / wait" → "Do you mind waiting?" T: "manage / finish" → "We managed to finish." Choral, then individuals, faster.
  2. Report the instruction: T: "Teacher to Ana: 'Open the window.'" → S: "The teacher told Ana to open the window." T: "Doctor to Omar: 'You should rest.'" → S: "The doctor advised Omar to rest." T: "Mom to me: 'Don't be late.'" → S: "My mom told me not to be late."
  3. Preposition + -ing chain: S1: "I'm good at cooking. What are you good at?" → S2: "I'm good at fixing bikes. What are you interested in?" → S3: "I'm interested in learning Korean. What are you looking forward to?" → S4: "I'm looking forward to seeing…"
  4. Two meanings, two gestures: T says a sentence; learners point back over the shoulder (memory) or forward (task): "I remember meeting her." (back) · "Remember to call her." (forward) · "I forgot to pay the bill." (forward: a task I didn't do) · "I'll never forget seeing the ocean." (back).
  5. Stop, act it: learners walk; T says "Stop to tie your shoe!" (they stop and tie) / "Stop walking!" (they freeze). Fun and fast; 1 minute.
Meaning-change sentence cards — how to run them
  • Each pair gets 12 sentence cards (6 pairs, e.g. "She stopped buying coffee." / "She stopped to buy a coffee.") and 12 meaning cards ("She doesn't buy coffee anymore." / "She paused on her way so she could buy one."). Spread them face up.
  • Pairs match each sentence to its meaning (5 min). The two sentences in a pair look almost the same, so learners must read the form carefully: -ing or to.
  • Check: one pair reads a sentence, another pair reads the meaning. Ask "Why?" and point at the board ("memory or task?", "test or effort?").
  • Round 2 (stretch or fast pairs): turn the meaning cards face down. A reads a sentence; B says the meaning without looking. Or A reads a meaning; B says the sentence.
  • Keep the cards for the C19 warmer (turn them over and play memory / pairs).
Find Someone Who & the two-minute talk — how to run them
  • Before the mingle, learners turn two prompts into questions with you: "enjoys cooking for other people" → "Do you enjoy cooking for other people?" · "remembers learning to ride a bike" → "Do you remember learning to ride a bike?" The -ing / to stays the same in the question. That's the point.
  • One name per line; ask each person 1–2 questions, then move on. A "No" is fine: "OK, thanks!" and ask someone else. Anyone can answer as their new identity card person, or say "Pass."
  • The two-minute talk: the speaker talks; the checker listens and ticks patterns on the card (enjoy + -ing, decide + to, told me + to, good at + -ing, a meaning-change verb, an -ing subject…). The checker does not correct. They only count. This keeps confident speakers honest and gives quiet learners a clear job.
  • Dominant speaker in a pair? The timer rules: at 2:00 the speaker stops, even mid-sentence. Then swap.
ICQs before the mingle and the talk
Mingle: Do you read the line, or make a question? (Make a question.) · "enjoys cooking": what's the question? ("Do you enjoy cooking…?") · "Yes": what do you do? (Write the name, ask the extra question.) · How many names for one person? (One.)
Talk: How long does A speak? (Two minutes.) · What does the checker do? (Listens and ticks the patterns.) · Does the checker correct? (No, only count.) · Must you talk about your real life? (No. A new identity card is fine.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Work with 6 verbs only: enjoy, like, can't stand (+ -ing) and want, need, would like (+ to), plus stop. In the verb sort, give 6 cards with a small picture next to the verb (😊 enjoy, 😖 can't stand, 🎯 want). In the mingle, give a sheet with the questions fully written; the learner reads the question and writes the first letter of the name. Talk target: 1 minute with 5 patterns, using the plan's sentence starters.

Stretch

Add regret (I regret not studying more) and need + -ing (My car needs washing) as "extra collection" verbs. In the two-minute talk, aim for 15 patterns, all four meaning-change verbs and two -ing subjects. Early finishers write 4 "true for me" pairs: "I stopped eating meat / I stopped to eat lunch at a gas station."

L1 watch-outs
  • Infinitive after a preposition and as a subject ("before to leave," "interested in learn," "To swim is good," "Swim is good for you"): Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and French use the infinitive here (antes de salir, interesado en aprender). Very common. Keep saying: "After a preposition: -ing. Always."
  • "I want that you listen" / "She said me to wait": Spanish quiero que escuches, Portuguese, French, Russian, Arabic. Drill "want you to, told me to."
  • "He suggested to go" / "I suggest you to go": German vorschlagen zu, Spanish sugerir, French. Teach suggest + -ing (or suggest that we go for stretch).
  • No -ing form at all ("I enjoy cook," "I finish read"): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai (no verb endings). The -ing is a small sound: say it clearly and write it in color.
  • "I enjoy the swimming": Arabic uses a verbal noun with al- (the). Link to C17: "general activity = no article."
  • "Looking forward to see you": everyone, including very fluent speakers and many native writers. Use the noun test every time.

6.3 · C19

Degree & Quantity — Complaints & Compliments

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use too + adjective (a problem) and adjective + enough / enough + noun, with a to-infinitive result (It's too cold to swim. She's old enough to drive.)
  • choose too many (countable) or too much (uncountable)
  • add emphasis with so + adjective / adverb and such (a) + (adjective +) noun, plus … that + result
  • feel the difference between a few / a little (some, positive 🙂) and few / little (not many / not much, negative 🙁)
  • use all / most / some / none, with of before the / my / them
  • make a polite complaint and give a compliment, saying exactly what is wrong and what is good

If you run long

Four small grammar points in one class is a lot. all / most / some / none is the easiest to shorten: teach only the of rule with two examples and leave the rest to Ex 3 Part B at home. Keep the 🙂 / 🙁 listening: it's fast and memorable. Don't cut the role-play below 12 minutes; do two rounds instead of three.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: too / enough / very. too = more than you want: there's a problem or a result you don't like. enough = the right amount. very = strong, but no problem.

"The coffee is too hot." Can I drink it now? (No.) Is it a problem? (Yes.) · "The coffee is very hot." Can I drink it? (Maybe, carefully.) · "She's old enough to drive." Can she drive? (Yes.) · "We don't have enough chairs." Can everyone sit down? (No.)
"There are too many cars." Can we count cars? (Yes: one car, two cars.) So…? (too many.) · "There's too much noise." Can we count noise? (No.) So…? (too much.) · "You worry too much." Is there a noun? (No, it's after a verb: too much.)

Meaning: so / such. Same meaning: "really, very." The difference is grammar: what comes after.

"It was such a good movie that I watched it twice." Was the movie good? (Very good.) What happened because of that? (I watched it twice.) Is there a noun after "such a good"? (Yes, "movie.") · "It was so good that I watched it twice." Is there a noun after "so good"? (No.)

Meaning: a few / few, a little / little.

"I have a few friends here." Some friends or no friends? (Some.) Am I OK? (Yes 🙂.) · "I have few friends here." Many or not many? (Not many, almost none.) How do I feel? (A bit lonely 🙁.) · "We have a little time." Hurry, or relax? (Relax. There's some time.) · "We have little time." (Hurry!) · Friends: countable or not? (Countable: few.) Time? (Uncountable: little.)
"Most people like music." All people in the world, generally? (Yes, in general.) Is there of? (No.) · "Most of the people in this room like music." General, or a specific group? (Specific: this room.) So…? (Add of.) · "None of them came." How many came? (Zero.)

Form.

tootoo + adjective / adverb (+ to…)It's too cold to swim. · He drives too fast.
enoughadjective + enough · enough + noun (+ to…)She's old enough to drive. · We don't have enough chairs.
too many / muchtoo many + plural noun · too much + uncountable noun · verb + too muchtoo many meetings · too much sugar · You worry too much.
soso + adjective / adverb (+ that…)I was so tired that I fell asleep. · She spoke so quietly that…
such (a)such a / an (+ adj.) + singular noun · such (+ adj.) + plural / uncountablesuch a long show · such kind people · such nice weather
(a) few / (a) little🙂 a few + plural · a little + uncountable · 🙁 few / little (= not many / not much)a few minutes · a little money · few people came · little time
all / most / some / none+ noun (general) · + of + the / my / this / them (specific)Most people agree. · Some of my friends came. · None of them.

Pronunciation. too is always stressed and long /tuː/: "It's TOO hot." (Compare weak to /tə/.) enough /ɪˈnʌf/: gh = /f/, stress on the second syllable. so and such carry the emphasis, so they are stressed and can be stretched: "It was sooo good!" such /sʌtʃ/ ends in "ch" (Spanish and Portuguese speakers may say "such" like "sush"). a few /ə ˈfjuː/: the /ə/ is tiny but it changes the meaning; say "a‿few" as one word and let learners hear it several times. For complaints, intonation matters more than grammar: a soft, rising start ("Excuse me… I'm sorry to bother you, but…") sounds polite; a flat, loud "The room is too cold." can sound angry even with perfect grammar.

Drill script
  1. too or enough? T shows a card and says the adjective: "hot tea, hot" → S: "It's too hot to drink." · "15 years old, old, drive" → S: "She isn't old enough to drive." · "small apartment, big" → S: "It isn't big enough." · "10 people, 6 chairs" → S: "We don't have enough chairs."
  2. many or much? T: "cars" → S: "too many cars." T: "sugar" → "too much sugar." T: "people" → "too many people." T: "homework" → "too much homework." T: "noise" → "too much noise." Fast.
  3. so or such? T: "tired" → S: "I was so tired!" T: "a long day" → S: "It was such a long day!" T: "kind people" → "They're such kind people!" T: "quickly" → "The time went so quickly!"
  4. Complaint → compliment: T: "the room / cold" → S: "Excuse me, the room is too cold." T: "the staff / helpful" → S: "The staff were so helpful. Thank you!" Practice the polite intonation, choral then individual.
  5. of or no of? T: "most / people" → "Most people…" T: "most / the people here" → "Most of the people here…" T: "none / them" → "None of them…"
Complaints & Compliments — how to run it
  • Six situation cards: a hotel room, a restaurant, a store return, a phone and internet company, a delivery order, and a community class. A (customer) has 3 problems as prompts (🥶 room / warm → "The room isn't warm enough."). B (staff) has what they can and can't offer ("You have a few rooms free on the 5th floor. None of them have a view.").
  • The polite complaint shape (on slide 18): 1 Excuse me / I'm sorry to bother you, but… 2 the exact problem (too / not … enough / too many) 3 a request: Could you…? / Would it be possible to…? B: 4 I'm so sorry about that. I'll… / Let me see what I can do. A: 5 a compliment when it's fixed (so / such … that).
  • Precision is the point. "The room is bad" is not enough; "The room is too cold, and there aren't enough towels for three people" gets a solution. Tell learners: "In real life, a precise complaint gets a faster answer."
  • The compliment is not optional. Many learners have learned complaining is rude or dangerous; the round ends with thanks and praise, so it feels safe and positive. It also practices so / such in a friendly way.
  • These are roles. Nobody needs to share a real complaint. If a situation card is uncomfortable (for example, the apartment or the phone company), the pair takes a different one. Some learners prefer to solve the problem as a group: give them the Community Center sheet (below).
Community Center problem-solving — the alternative task
  • Groups of 3–4. The sheet lists 8 complaints from members (too few chairs, not enough parking, the room is too cold in winter, too many people at the Saturday class…) and a budget of $1,500 with 9 possible fixes and their prices.
  • The group chooses the fixes to buy (the total must be $1,500 or less), then reports: "Most of the complaints are about the big room. It's too cold, so we'll buy a heater. There aren't enough chairs, so we're buying 20. We have a little money left for…"
  • Use it for early finishers, as a 12-minute replacement for round 3 of the role-play, or as the main freer task in a class that prefers group discussion to role-play. It is also a good "discussion" preview for Module 7.
ICQs before the role-play
Is A the customer or the staff? (The customer.) · Do you write the whole dialogue first? (No, just plan for a minute.) · Is "The room is bad" enough? (No, say exactly what: too cold, not enough towels.) · What does A say at the end? (A compliment: "You've been so helpful.") · Must you use a real complaint? (No. They're roles.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on too + adjective, not … enough and so + adjective. Use the picture flashcards (hot, cold, small, expensive) as prompts. In the role-play, give the phrase strip (on the role-play page) and the hotel card only; the partner or teacher reads the situation aloud. Target: 2 complaints ("It's too cold. It isn't big enough.") + 1 request ("Could you help me, please?") + 1 compliment ("You're so kind. Thank you!").

Stretch

Play the staff role with a difficult customer: B can't fix everything and must offer an alternative ("None of the rooms with a view are free, but I can give you a free breakfast."). Use so … that and such … that in every compliment, and a few / little pair. Early finishers write the online review of the place in 8 sentences, including most of / none of.

L1 watch-outs
  • "too" as praise ("The food was too delicious!" "You're too kind" meaning very kind): Chinese tài hǎo le 太好了, Tagalog sobra, Spanish demasiado (in some countries), Arabic, Hindi/Urdu bahut. It can sound like a complaint. Teach "so / really / very" for praise. (Note: "You're too kind" is a fixed polite phrase in English and is fine.)
  • "enough big" / "enough old": Spanish suficientemente grande, Portuguese grande o suficiente (OK) but bastante grande, French assez grand, Russian, Chinese gòu dà 够大 (enough before the adjective). Very common. Chant: "big enough, old enough, warm enough."
  • "so a nice day" / "such nice" (no noun): Spanish tan and French si / tellement work before adjectives and nouns. Use the noun test.
  • "much people" / "too much cars": Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Arabic (no countable/uncountable grammar). Link to C17: "Can you put a number in front?"
  • "the most of people" / "most of people": Spanish la mayoría de la gente, Portuguese a maioria das pessoas, French la plupart des gens. Teach: general → no of; specific → of the.
  • Direct complaints: in some cultures a complaint is very indirect (learners may only say "It's a little cold, maybe…"), in others very direct ("This is bad. Change it."). Teach the English middle: soft start + exact problem + polite request.

Closing the Module

End of C19: Module 6 wrap-up

Recap on slide 20, the three precision questions: Which one? (a / the / no article) → Which form? (-ing / to, and the meaning-change verbs) → How much? (too / enough, so / such, a few / few). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in the C20 warmer.

Keep it alive. Precision grows slowly. For the rest of the course, use a 2-minute "Editor" warmer once a week: write one sentence from learners' homework with an article or verb-pattern slip and let the class fix it. Keep the C17 board question ("Does my listener know which one?") on a poster on the wall.

Watch for in Module 7: discussion language (I agree / I'm not sure I agree / That's a good point, but…) is a natural place to recycle this module: too vs very when giving opinions ("That's too expensive for most people"), most of / some of in summaries ("Most of us think…"), and verb patterns (I suggest waiting, I refuse to believe…). The Community Center task is a good bridge.
