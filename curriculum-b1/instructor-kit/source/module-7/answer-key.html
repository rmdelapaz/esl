Module 7 — Real-World Communication: Answers

Answers to every Module 7 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, success criteria for the speaking tasks (structured discussion, complaint carousel, mock interview panels), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 7.1–7.3Classes C20–C229 self-check items16 exercises
Marking principle

This is a functional module, so mark appropriacy and meaning first, then form. Many polite phrases are correct in a gap or a rewrite. Accept any phrase that is polite, clear and fits the situation, even if it's not the one in the key. Mark wrong: rude or blaming language, a missing request (a complaint that only describes the problem), I'm agree, So am I after a do-verb, and the infinitive after a preposition (good at to cook). American English is the model; British forms (apologise, favourite, parcel, organise, practise, CV) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

7.1 · Agreeing, Disagreeing & Discussion (C20)

Exercise 1 — Phones at school (Script 7.1-A)
  1. David.
  2. Grace: "I couldn't agree more."
  3. Parents sometimes need to contact their children, for example when the bus is late.
  4. age ("I suppose it depends on the age.")
  5. No phones in class (during lessons).
  6. Any two of: maybe, to be honest, I suppose.

Also correct in 5: "Phones should stay in bags during class." In 3, accept "for safety / emergencies" if the learner connects it to contacting children.

Exercise 2 — Agree or disagree?

1 A · 2 D · 3 A · 4 D · 5 A · 6 D · 7 A · 8 D.

Discuss 1: it has couldn't but means complete agreement. Discuss 5: "That's a good point" is agreement with respect, though it can also come just before a "but" ("That's a good point, but…"). If a learner says so, praise the thinking. Alone, it's agreement.

Exercise 3 — Complete the discussion

1 opinion · 2 point · 3 sure · 4 honest · 5 think · 6 agree · 7 concerned · 8 seems.

Wrong: "It seem to me" (no -s) · "As far as I concern" · "I couldn't agree most."

Exercise 4 — So… I / Neither… I
  1. So am I.
  2. Neither do I.
  3. Neither can I.
  4. So do I.
  5. Neither have I.
  6. Neither did I.

Also correct (informal): Me too (1, 4) and Me neither (2, 3, 5, 6). Nor do I is also correct. Wrong: "So do I" for 1 (the verb is be) · "Me too" after a negative · "Neither I do."

Exercise 5 — Make it polite (model answers)
  1. I'm not so sure. I think working from home can be a bit lonely.
  2. That may be true, but I think children need phones for safety.
  3. I see your point, but to be honest, I find the city too noisy.
  4. I'm afraid I disagree. Actually, I think online shopping is faster.

Accept any version that (1) acknowledges or cushions, (2) uses but or another soft opener, (3) gives the view without attacking the person. A softener (a bit, to be honest, maybe) is a bonus. Wrong: keeping "You're wrong" or "That's stupid" anywhere in the answer.

Dialogue — underline task

Opinion: Personally, I feel… · As far as I'm concerned… · Agreeing: That's a good point. · Exactly! · I couldn't agree more. · Polite disagreement: I'm not so sure. · I see your point about…, but… · But don't you think…? · Softeners: to be honest · a bit · Maybe · I suppose · Actually.

Speaking — structured discussion (success criteria)

Answers are personal. A successful speaker: gives one opinion with a reason, agrees once, disagrees politely once (acknowledge → but → view), asks someone for their opinion by name, uses a softener, and waits for their turn. A successful chair asks every person at least once. A successful summarizer reports both sides: "Most of us think…, but Ana doesn't agree because…" Observers' bingo lines show which phrases were used. Use them in feedback.

Self-check
Q1
Your colleague says, "We should cancel the trip." You disagree politely.
A) No, you're wrong.
B) I see your point, but I think we should wait a little longer.
C) Absolutely, let's cancel it.
Why: B acknowledges first, then gives a different view. A attacks the person; C agrees.
Q2
"I love long walks." You love them too.
A) So am I.
B) So do I.
C) Neither do I.
Why: love is a present simple verb, so the auxiliary is do. Neither is for negative statements.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) I'm agree with you.
B) I agree with you.
C) I am agreeing you.
Why: agree is a verb, so no am. It's a state verb, so no -ing, and it takes with + a person.
Errors you will hear most (7.1)

"I'm agree." · "So am I." (after "I like it") · "Me too." (after a negative) · "In my opinion, I think…" · "According to me…" · "We discussed about it." · "Actually" meaning "now" · a flat "No." or "You're wrong." · interrupting mid-sentence. Correct I'm agree every time (it fossilizes fast). For bluntness, don't mark it as a "mistake"; model the cushioned version and ask "Which one would you rather hear?"

Exercise 2 — Soften the rude complaint

1 possible · 2 afraid · 3 bother · 4 appreciate · 5 apologize · 6 mind.

Also correct in 5: apologise (British). Tell learners both are right. Wrong: "Would you mind to move us" (6 needs the -ing already given: mind moving).

Exercise 3 — Complete the complaint

1 problem · 2 work · 3 charged · 4 refund · 5 arrived · 6 sort · 7 since · 8 declined.

Also correct in 2: "doesn't work / isn't working" (if the learner changes the verb). In 6, American English also says "I'll take care of it right away." Wrong: "leaking for Monday" (7) · "My card was decline" (8, missing -d).

Exercise 4 — Match the problem to the request

1 → b · 2 → a · 3 → d · 4 → c · 5 → e · 6 → f.

Exercise 5 — Complaint email (model, option a)
Subject: Wrong item in order #58213
Dear Customer Service Team,
I'm writing to complain about my recent order. On May 3, I ordered a blue sweater in size M from your website. Unfortunately, the package arrived yesterday with a red sweater inside. I have kept the receipt and the original packaging.
I would appreciate it if you could send me the blue sweater as soon as possible, or give me a full refund. Could you please also send me a return label for the red one?
Thank you for your help. I look forward to hearing from you.
Sincerely,
Maria Lopez

Check for: a clear subject with a number · I'm writing to complain about… · details (date, item, what's wrong) · a specific request (I would appreciate it if you could… / Could you please…?) · a polite ending and name · no blaming ("You sent me the wrong thing" is OK but less polite than "the package arrived with…"). Option b: "…I was charged $30 for a service I did not order… I would appreciate it if you could remove the charge and refund the $30." Don't mark down short sentences; B1 emails can be simple.

Dialogue — find task

Soft opener: Excuse me, I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid there's a problem with this jacket. · Problem: the zipper is broken. · Request: Would it be possible to get a refund? · Escalation: I understand, but I'd really prefer a refund. Could I speak to a manager, please?

Speaking — complaint carousel (success criteria)

The customer: uses a soft opener, describes the problem clearly (with a detail: date, amount, how long), makes a specific polite request, stays calm, and, when the staff card refuses, escalates with "I understand, but…" + a request for a manager or supervisor. On the phone, they spell a name, give a number, ask for a reference number or name, and confirm the arrangement. The staff member: apologizes and offers an action. At the desk: the email has the parts of the Ex 5 frame, and the reply has an apology + action.

Self-check
Q1
You want a replacement for a broken kettle.
A) Replace this now. It's broken.
B) I'm afraid this kettle is faulty. Would it be possible to replace it?
C) This kettle doesn't work at all.
Why: B has a soft opener, the problem and a polite request. A is an order; C describes the problem but doesn't ask for anything.
Q2
A customer says their order is wrong. You work in the store.
A) That's not our fault, sorry.
B) Oh dear, that's a shame.
C) I'm terribly sorry about that. I'll sort it out right away.
Why: apology + action. A blames; B is sympathy with no action.
Q3 · new
The first person on the phone can't help you.
A) You're useless. Get me your boss.
B) I understand, but I'd like to speak to a manager, please.
C) OK, never mind. Goodbye.
Why: polite escalation: acknowledge, then a clear, calm request. A attacks the person; C gives up, and the problem isn't fixed.
Errors you will hear most (7.2)

"I want that you give me a refund." · "Give me my money back." · "The sink is leaking since Monday." · "I wait since two hours." · "Would you mind to check?" · "I want to do a complaint." · "You charged me two times!" · "My card was decline." · "complain" for "complaint" (and vice versa). Correct the verb patterns (mind + -ing, has been + -ing since) every time. For too-direct requests, don't just correct: show the ladder and ask "Which one gets the refund faster?"

7.3 · Work & Interviews — Describing Your Experience (C22)

Exercise 1 — Samuel's interview (Script 7.3-A)
  1. He's a delivery driver, and he's been doing it for about two years.
  2. In a supermarket (stocking shelves and dealing with customers).
  3. Any two: he likes being active · he's good at organizing things · the company offers forklift training (and he'd love to learn).
  4. S: Last winter a snowstorm closed a road on his route. · T: He had 15 deliveries to finish before 6 (p.m.). · A: He called his supervisor, planned a new route with a map app, and phoned customers to say he'd be a little late. · R: He delivered every package that day, and two customers wrote nice reviews.
  5. He used to get nervous speaking in meetings. He's been taking an English class and practicing speaking up.
  6. "What does the training look like for new staff?"
Exercise 2 — Complete the interview answers

1 working · 2 responsible · 3 experience · 4 strengths · 5 good · 6 applied · 7 colleagues · 8 deadlines.

Wrong: "I've been work as" (1) · "a lot of experiences in sales" (3: in this meaning experience is uncountable) · "One of my main strength" (4: one of + plural).

Exercise 3 — The right little word or form

1 for · 2 in · 3 at · 4 dealing · 5 training · 6 for · 7 since · 8 for.

Also acceptable in 2: experience with marketing (common in American English). Wrong: "responsible of" · "good in languages" · "involves to deal" · "since three years."

Exercise 4 — Match the question to a good answer

1 → b · 2 → d · 3 → a · 4 → e · 5 → f · 6 → c.

Exercise 5 — Put the STAR story in order

In the order printed: A · S · R · T. The story reads: S (volunteering in a community kitchen) → T (the cook was sick; lunch for 80 people by noon) → A (organized volunteers, gave jobs, cooked) → R (served on time; asked to lead the Saturday team).

Point out: the Action uses I and past simple verbs (organized, gave, cooked), and volunteering is real experience.

Exercise 6 — My interview answers (model)

Answers are personal. Model (candidate profile: Amina, nursing assistant): I'm currently finishing a nursing assistant course at the community college. I've been caring for my elderly neighbor for two years, so I have experience in helping people with daily tasks. Before that, I worked in a hotel as a housekeeper. Now I'm looking for a position in a care home, which is why I applied for this job. STAR: Last month, my neighbor fell in her kitchen (S). I had to help her and get medical help quickly (T). I checked that she could move, called 911, and stayed with her until the ambulance came (A). She was OK, and the paramedic said I did the right things (R).

Check for: present perfect with for / since · preposition + -ing · a clear Present → Past → Future order · a STAR story with all four parts and past verbs · a question for the interviewer.

Speaking — mock interview panels (success criteria)

Use the observer checklist (materials Set G). A successful candidate: greets and thanks · answers "Tell me about yourself" in Present → Past → Future (30–60 seconds) · uses the present perfect correctly at least once · gives a strength with an example · tells a STAR story with a Result · turns a weakness into growth · asks one question at the end · uses a filler instead of a long silence. A successful observer gives one specific strength and one specific tip.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) I'm responsible for to manage the team.
B) I'm responsible for managing the team.
C) I'm responsible managing the team.
Why: for is a preposition, so it takes -ing. C is missing for.
Q2
The strongest answer about strengths.
A) I'm just a really good worker.
B) I don't really have any weaknesses.
C) I'm good at working under pressure. For example, I regularly met tight deadlines in my last job.
Why: a specific strength + an example (proof). A is vague; B answers a different question and sounds arrogant.
Q3 · new
"I've been working as a cook ___ 2019."
A) for
B) since
C) from
Why: 2019 is a starting point, so since. For is for a length of time (for five years). "From 2019" needs an end ("from 2019 to 2022").
Errors you will hear most (7.3)

"I have 5 years working here." · "I work here since 2019." · "since five years" · "I been working…" (no 've) · "responsible of" · "good in" · "good at to cook" · "I work as nurse." · "my actual job" (= current) · "apply to a job" · "a lot of experiences" · STAR stories in the present tense or with no Result · "We did…" all through the Action. Also listen for modesty that hides skills ("I'm not so good…"). Treat that as a genre issue, not an error: "Give yourself credit, then add proof."
