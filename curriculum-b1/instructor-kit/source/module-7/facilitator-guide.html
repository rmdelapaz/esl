Module 7 — Real-World Communication

Three practical speaking classes about using English in the situations adults care about. First, a structured discussion: giving opinions, agreeing, disagreeing politely and taking turns on everyday community topics. Then complaints and getting things fixed with stores, landlords and utility companies, in person, on the phone and by email, with polite escalation to a manager. Last, a job-interview lab: describing experience with the present perfect, STAR-style answers, and mock interview panels with observer checklists.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 90 min per class CEFR B1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (groups, role-plays, panels) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes. In this module the freer stage is the biggest stage of every class (20–23 minutes). Protect it: if you run late, cut controlled practice and give the rest for homework.
  • Present from slides/module-7-real-world.html. Print materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html before C20. It holds the flashcards, discussion topic cards, discussion role cards and talking tokens, Discussion Phrase Bingo, the complaint role-play cards (in person, phone, email), the candidate profile cards, interviewer question cards, the observer checklist and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • This is a functional module, not a new tense. The language is mostly fixed chunks (I see your point, but…; Would it be possible to…?; I'm responsible for…). Teach them as whole phrases and drill them for rhythm and polite intonation. The grammar inside them is recycled: the present perfect and present perfect continuous (1.1–1.2) for experience, the passive (4.1) for describing a problem without blame, and -ing after prepositions (6.2) for good at / responsible for / mind. Treat these as extension and contrast, not re-teaching.
  • Building on A2. A2 Module 7 practiced simple returns ("There's a problem with this jacket. Can I have a refund?"). The new B1 work is register (how polite, how formal), three channels (in person, phone, email), and polite escalation.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use some British forms: favourite, colour, apologise, organisation, organising, practise (verb), parcel, mobile, CV. Teach favorite, color, apologize, organization, organizing, practice, package, cell phone, résumé. Accept British forms when learners use them. Note: in online lesson 7.2 Exercise 1, the gap checker accepts only apologise. Tell learners that apologize is also correct.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Speak up → stand up for yourself → present yourself. In C20 learners take part in a fair group discussion: they give an opinion, agree, disagree without offending anyone, and invite quieter people in. In C21 they use the same softening language to get something fixed: a broken sink, a double charge, a faulty jacket. They open politely, describe the problem, ask for action and, if needed, ask for a manager. In C22 they present their own experience to an employer with confidence and proof (STAR stories). The same idea runs through all three classes: calm, polite and clear gets results. Say this at the start of every class.

B1 speaking classes: fluency first, then accuracy

During the freer tasks, do not stop learners to correct. Walk around with a notepad split into two columns: Great language I heard and Fix it together. Correct on the spot only if a mistake blocks meaning (the listener can't understand). After the task, give feedback in this order: (1) content ("Which group changed their mind? What was the best argument?"), (2) great language (read 2–3 good phrases aloud, no names, and praise them), (3) fix it together: 3–4 sentences on the board, no names, learners correct in pairs. This keeps confidence high and makes feedback useful.

Dominant and quiet speakers: make turn-taking fair

Every B1 class has one or two people who talk a lot and a few who never start. This module gives you tools. (1) Talking tokens (materials Set C): each person has three. They put one in the middle each time they speak. When your tokens are gone, you wait until everyone has used theirs. (2) Roles: give the most talkative learner the chair or observer role. Their job is to bring others in, not to speak. (3) Think time: give 1 minute of silent planning before every discussion, so slower speakers are ready. (4) Round first: each person says one sentence before open discussion starts. (5) Praise listening out loud: "Great, you asked Mai what she thinks." Never shame a strong speaker. Say "Hold that thought. Let's hear from someone new first."

Trauma-informed and safe: topics, problems and work histories

C20 topics are everyday community topics (public transport, working from home, phones in schools, city vs country, online shopping). Do not use politics, religion, immigration, or any topic about a learner's country or identity. If a learner brings one up, redirect kindly: "That's a big topic. Let's keep to our card today." C21: problems with a landlord or a utility company can be very real and very stressful (housing, shut-off notices, fear of trouble). The role-plays use fictional situation cards. Nobody has to share a real problem. This class teaches communication, not law. If a learner has a real housing, billing or safety problem, do not give legal advice. Connect them with your program's case manager or navigator, or local legal aid or a tenant helpline, after class. C22: some learners have gaps in their work history, qualifications that are not recognized here, or jobs they don't want to talk about. Say it every time: "Use your real experience or a candidate profile card. Both are good." Caregiving, volunteering, farming, a family business and studying all count as experience.

Before the Module

  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board): topics (7.1), problems (7.2) and jobs (7.3), plus the word-only phrase cards. Card stock helps.
  • For C20: one set of discussion topic cards and one set of role cards (chair, timekeeper, observer, summarizer) per group of 4–5. Cut talking tokens (3 per learner), or use coins, paper clips or dried beans. One Discussion Phrase Bingo card per observer (cards A and B are different).
  • For C21: one set of complaint role-play cards per pair (in person and phone), the email task cards, and the escalation cards (the "I'm afraid I can't do that" staff cards). Set up the room as three stations: a "counter" (a desk), "phone" chairs back to back, and a writing table.
  • For C22: one candidate profile card set on a side table, interviewer question cards per trio, and three observer checklists per learner (one per round). Ask learners in C21 to bring a real job posting to C22 if they want (from a job board, a store window or your program's job developer). Bring 3–4 printed postings for people who don't have one.
  • If your program has a career services team, job developer or WIOA workforce partner, invite them to be a "guest interviewer" for 20 minutes in C22. Learners love a real interviewer, and it builds a link to services.
  • A toy or real phone for the C21 phone demo helps. Back-to-back chairs make phone role-plays realistic (no lip-reading, no gestures).

7.1 · C20

Agreeing, Disagreeing & Discussion

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • give an opinion with a reason, using a variety of openers: In my opinion, … · It seems to me that… · As far as I'm concerned, … · If you ask me, … · Personally, I feel…
  • agree at different strengths: Exactly. · I couldn't agree more. · That's a good point. · I know what you mean. and agree about themselves with So do I / So am I / Neither do I
  • disagree politely with the pattern acknowledge → but → my view: I see your point, but… · I'm not so sure. · Actually, I think… · That may be true, but…
  • ask for opinions (What do you think? How do you feel about…?) and soften (maybe, I suppose, to be honest, a bit)
  • take part in a fair 10-minute group discussion with a chair, and take turns without interrupting
Materials
  • Workbook 7.1 (Useful phrases, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Discussion planner)
  • Slides 3–8 · Topic flashcards + word-only phrase cards · Discussion topic cards + role cards (1 set per group) · talking tokens (3 per learner) · Discussion Phrase Bingo (for observers)
  • Listening script 7.1-A (Phones at school: a parents' meeting) and 7.1-B (dictation)

Four columns: 💬 OPINION In my opinion, … / It seems to me that… / As far as I'm concerned, … · 👍 AGREE Exactly. / I couldn't agree more. / That's a good point. / So do I. · 🤝 DISAGREE POLITELY I see your point, but… / I'm not so sure. / Actually, I think… · ❓ ASK What do you think, …? / How do you feel about…?

Under the columns: 1 acknowledge → 2 but → 3 my view. "I see your point, but I think the cost is too high."

Right corner: softeners: maybe · I suppose · to be honest · a bit · kind of. Golden rule: attack the idea, not the person. ❌ I'm agree → ✅ I agree.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: opinion openers. They all mean "this is my view, not a fact." They differ in register: If you ask me is casual; In my opinion is neutral to formal; It seems to me that is careful and thoughtful; As far as I'm concerned is a strong personal view.

"In my opinion, cities are too crowded." Is this a fact? (No, an opinion.) Can another person think differently? (Yes.) · "If you ask me, the country is more relaxing." Would you say this in a job interview or to a friend? (To a friend. It's casual.)

Meaning: I couldn't agree more. This is the strongest agreement, even though it has couldn't.

"I couldn't agree more." Is it negative or positive? (Positive.) Do I agree a little or completely? (Completely.) Is it the same as "I couldn't care less"? (No! That means "I'm not interested at all.")

Meaning: polite disagreement. First show that you heard the other person, then give your view.

"I see your point, but I think the cost is too high." Do I agree? (No, not really.) Did I listen to the other person? (Yes: "I see your point.") Is it rude? (No.) · "Actually, I find the city quite relaxing." Does "actually" mean "now"? (No. It means "in fact," and it softens a different opinion.)

Form.

Opener + commaIn my opinion, / As far as I'm concerned, / If you ask me, / Personally, + sentenceIn my opinion, the buses are too slow.
Opener + (that) clauseI think (that) / It seems to me that / I feel (that) + subject + verbIt seems to me that both sides have a point.
agree = a verbI agree (with you / with that). · I don't agree. (never "I'm agree")I agree with Priya.
So / Neither+ statement → So + auxiliary + I · − statement → Neither + auxiliary + II love it. → So do I. · I'm tired. → So am I. · I can't cook. → Neither can I. · I don't drive. → Neither do I.
Disagreeacknowledge + but + your viewThat may be true, but it's very expensive.

Pronunciation. In English, tone carries politeness. A flat, low voice sounds rude even with polite words. Model a wide, warm pitch. I see your POINT ↘↗, but… has a fall-rise on point: it says "there's more coming." Stress: I couldn't agree MORE; oPINion; ACtually /ˈæk.tʃu.ə.li/ (3–4 syllables, not "ak-TWAL-ly"); NEIther /ˈniː.ðɚ/ in American English (/ˈnaɪ.ðɚ/ is also fine). So do I stresses I: "So do I."

Drill script
  1. Phrase rhythm (choral): "In my o-PIN-ion" ×3 · "I couldn't agree MORE" ×3 (big smile) · "I see your POINT, but…" ×3 (fall-rise) · "I'm not SO sure" ×3. Half the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Same opinion, new opener: T: "The buses are too slow. Casual!" → S: "If you ask me, the buses are too slow." T: "Careful!" → S: "It seems to me that the buses are too slow." T: "Strong!" → S: "As far as I'm concerned, the buses are too slow."
  3. So / Neither quick-fire (point at learners): T: "I love coffee." → S: "So do I." · "I'm hungry." → "So am I." · "I don't like the rain." → "Neither do I." · "I can't swim." → "Neither can I." · "I've been to the new library." → "So have I."
  4. Blunt → polite transformation: T (in a flat voice): "No. That's wrong." → S: "I'm not so sure about that." · T: "You're wrong. It's expensive." → S: "I see your point, but it's quite expensive." · T: "Bad idea." → S: "Maybe, but don't you think…?"
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1: "In my opinion, everyone should learn to cook. What do you think, Ali?" → S2 agrees or disagrees politely, then gives a new opinion and asks S3. Then all pairs, 1 minute.
Structured discussion — how to run it
  • Groups of 4–5. Mix first languages and mix strong and quieter speakers. Each group gets one topic card (two sides + idea prompts) and a role card for each person.
  • Roles: Chair starts, asks every person, and stops interruptions ("Let's hear from Mai first."). Timekeeper says "Two minutes left." Observer listens, ticks Phrase Bingo and does not speak (give this role to a dominant speaker in round 1). Summarizer reports the result. In a group of 5, the fifth person is a speaker only. Everyone except the observer gives opinions.
  • Talking tokens: 3 each. Put one in the middle every time you speak. When yours are gone, you listen until everyone has used theirs, then everyone takes their 3 back.
  • Each speaker must: give one opinion with a reason, agree once, disagree politely once, ask someone "What do you think?", and use one softener. It's on their Discussion planner.
  • No "winner." The goal is to hear everyone and try to find a shared idea, or "agree to disagree" politely.
  • Round 2: rotate roles clockwise and pass the topic card to the next group.
ICQs before the discussion
Who asks everyone for their opinion? (The chair.) · Does the observer talk? (No, the observer listens and ticks bingo.) · What do you do with a token? (Put it in the middle when I speak.) · No tokens left: can you speak? (No, I wait.) · How do you disagree? (Say "I see your point, but…" first.) · Is there a winner? (No.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a phrase strip with four phrases only, one per column and with an icon: 💬 "I think… because…" · 👍 "That's a good point." · 🤝 "I see your point, but…" · ❓ "What do you think?" Learners point to the phrase as they say it. Use the topic cards with a picture (bus, phone, desk) and read the card aloud to the group. Target: one opinion with because, one agreement and one polite disagreement. The timekeeper role is a good first role (it needs little reading).

Stretch

Use the more careful openers (It seems to me that…, As far as I'm concerned…) and concede then counter: "That may be true, however, …" Add a second conditional (2.2) to argue: "If buses were free, more people would leave their cars at home." As summarizer, report with reported speech (3.1): "Tom said that he missed his colleagues." Early finishers write a 4-sentence opinion paragraph for their topic.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I'm agree" / "I am agree with you": Spanish estoy de acuerdo, Portuguese estou de acordo, French je suis d'accord, Russian я согласен (an adjective form). Teach: agree is a verb. "I agree. · I don't agree." Board it, and use a hand signal (cross out "am").
  • "Actually" = now: Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, Portuguese atualmente, Italian attualmente, German aktuell all mean "currently." "Actually I work in a hotel" may mean "Currently…". Recast: "Oh, you mean currently / right now."
  • "According to me" / "In my opinion, I think…": French selon moi, South Asian English. Use one opener, not two.
  • "Me too" after a negative: "I don't like rain." — "Me too." Teach "Me neither" (informal) and "Neither do I."
  • "We discussed about it": Spanish discutir sobre, French discuter de, Arabic. Discuss takes a direct object: "We discussed the topic." Also, Spanish discutir can mean "argue." In English, a discussion is calm.
  • Directness differs. Some learners (e.g. German, Dutch, Russian, Hebrew speakers) may sound blunt in English with a flat "No, this is wrong." Others (e.g. many Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Vietnamese and Filipino learners) may avoid disagreeing at all and say "maybe" to mean "no." Neither is wrong. Teach the English middle: disagree, but cushion it. Say clearly that polite disagreement is welcome in this class.

7.2 · C21

Complaints & Getting Things Fixed

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • describe a problem clearly: It doesn't work. · It's faulty. · It's the wrong size. · I was charged twice. · It hasn't arrived yet. · The sink has been leaking since Monday.
  • use the passive to describe a problem without blaming a person: I was overcharged (not "You overcharged me")
  • make a complaint in three parts: soft opener → problem → polite request (Would it be possible to…? · Could you…? · Would you mind + -ing…? · I'd appreciate it if you could…)
  • respond to a complaint as staff with apology + action, and escalate politely as a customer: I understand, but I'd like to speak to a manager, please.
  • complain on the phone (spell names, give numbers, ask for a reference number) and in a short email (I'm writing to complain about…)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: why polite works. A polite complaint separates the problem from the person. The staff member is not the enemy. They are the person who can fix it.

"I'm afraid there's been a mistake with my bill." Am I scared? (No!) What does "I'm afraid" do? (It softens bad news.) Is it a complaint? (Yes, a polite one.) · "I was charged twice." Who charged me? (We don't say. The company, the machine.) Why don't we say "You charged me"? (It blames the person. It makes them defensive.)
"Would it be possible to get a refund?" Is it a question or an order? (A question, so it's polite.) Is it very polite or a little polite? (Very polite.) · "Would you mind checking my order?" If the answer is "Not at all," is it yes or no? (Yes! "Not at all" = "I don't mind. I'll do it.")

Meaning: escalation. If the first person can't help, you ask for someone with more power. You stay calm, and you don't blame the first person.

"I understand, but I'd like to speak to a manager, please." Am I angry? (No, calm.) Is the first person bad? (No, they just can't help.) What do I say first? ("I understand" = I heard you.)

Word stress: REfund (noun) vs reFUND (verb); comPLAINT vs comPLAIN: the final /t/ matters. Phone English: spell letters in groups and use a check word: "S for Sam." Say numbers one by one: "four-four-oh-nine."

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the opener: "a problem with my bill" → "there's a problem with my bill" → "I'm afraid there's a problem with my bill" → "I'm sorry to bother you, but I'm afraid there's a problem with my bill." Choral ×3, halves, then individuals.
  2. Active → passive (no blame): T: "You charged me twice." → S: "I was charged twice." · "You didn't clean the room." → "The room wasn't cleaned." · "You sent the wrong size." → "I was sent the wrong size." / "The wrong size was sent."
  3. Climb the ladder: T holds up a flashcard (jacket) and says "Refund!" → S1: "Can I get a refund?" → S2: "Could I get a refund, please?" → S3: "Would it be possible to get a refund?" → S4: "Would you mind giving me a refund?"
  4. Staff reply chain: T (as customer): "My food is cold." → S: "I'm terribly sorry about that. I'll heat it up right away." Everyone must say an apology and an action.
  5. Phone spelling: learners spell their first name (or a card name) and a 6-digit "account number" to a partner, who writes it and reads it back: "So that's O-S-E-I, four-four-oh-nine-one-eight?"
Complaint carousel — how to run it
  • Three stations, labeled on the wall: 🏪 COUNTER · 📞 PHONE · ✉️ DESK. Pairs start at different stations and move clockwise every 6–7 minutes when you ring a bell or clap.
  • Counter and Phone: A takes a customer card, B the matching staff card. B's card tells B how helpful to be. Some staff cards say "You can't give a refund. Say: I'm afraid I can't do that." A must stay polite and escalate: "I understand, but could I speak to a manager, please?" B then turns over the card and becomes the manager, who fixes the problem. Swap roles for the next card.
  • Phone rules: chairs back to back, no gestures. The caller must give a name (spell it) and a number, and must end by asking for a reference number or the staff member's name, and by confirming: "So someone is coming on Saturday at ten."
  • Desk: the pair writes one complaint email together (5–6 lines) from an email task card, using the Workbook Ex 5 frame. Then they swap emails with another pair at the desk and write a 2-line reply as the company: apology + action.
  • Keep it realistic but light: fictional names, fictional numbers. Nobody needs to use their real address or account.
ICQs before the carousel
What are the three parts of a complaint? (Soft opener, the problem, a polite request.) · At the phone station, can you see your partner? (No, we sit back to back.) · Staff card says "I can't." What does the customer say? ("I understand, but I'd like to speak to a manager, please.") · Do you shout? (No!) · What do you ask for at the end of a phone call? (A reference number or a name.) · When do you move? (When the teacher claps.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the picture cards (remote, jacket, sink, bill) as the "situation" instead of the text cards. Give a 3-line complaint strip: 🧸 "Excuse me, I'm sorry to bother you." · 🔎 "This ___ doesn't work." · 🙏 "Could you help me, please?" plus one escalation line: "Could I speak to a manager, please?" Pair with a supportive partner who plays staff. At the desk station, the learner dictates and the partner writes, or the learner copies the frame and fills 2 gaps. Target: one complete 3-part complaint, in person and on the phone.

Stretch

Use the top of the ladder (Would it be possible…? · I'd appreciate it if…) and give details: dates, amounts, what you've already done ("I've already called twice"). Negotiate: when staff offer a weak solution (a coupon), politely ask for a better one ("That's kind, but I'd really prefer a refund."). Write the email in full formal style, including a clear deadline: "I would appreciate a reply by Friday, May 9."

L1 watch-outs
  • "The sink is leaking since Monday" / "I wait since two hours": Spanish desde hace, French depuis, German seit + present. Recast with the present perfect (continuous): "It's been leaking since Monday." · "I've been waiting for two hours." (1.2 recycle).
  • "I want that you give me a refund": Spanish quiero que me dé, French je veux que, Portuguese. Teach I'd like you to… or, better, the polite ladder. "I want…" in English sounds like a demand. In some languages (e.g. Russian, Arabic, German) a direct request is neutral, so learners don't hear the problem. Explain it without judgment.
  • "Would you mind to check?" (many L1s): mind + -ing. Link to 6.2 verb patterns.
  • "Do a complaint" / "complain for": Spanish hacer una queja / quejarse de, Arabic. It's make a complaint; complain about something, complain to a person.
  • Letters on the phone: Spanish b/v, Arabic p/b, Japanese and Korean l/r, and the vowels A / E / I (Spanish and Italian speakers say A as /a/, E as /e/, I as /i/). Teach the English letter names A /eɪ/, E /iː/, I /aɪ/, G /dʒiː/, J /dʒeɪ/, and a check word: "B as in Boy."
  • Final consonants (Vietnamese, Cantonese, Thai, some Spanish speakers): "complain" vs "complaint," "charge" vs "charged." In a complaint, "I was charged" without /d/ sounds like the present. Drill the endings.

Work & Interviews — Describing Your Experience

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use work vocabulary: apply for, position / role, colleague, deadline, responsibilities, skills, strengths, weaknesses, experience
  • describe their work with the present perfect: I've been working as a… for three years / since 2021. · I've worked in two warehouses.
  • use I'm responsible for / My job involves / I have experience in / I'm good at + noun or -ing
  • answer "Tell me about yourself" with Present → Past → Future, and a "Tell me about a time…" question with a STAR story (Situation, Task, Action, Result)
  • take part in a mock interview panel as candidate, interviewer and observer, and give kind, useful feedback with a checklist

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: present perfect for experience. It links your past work to now. The interviewer wants to know what you bring today.

"I've been working as a cook for three years." Did I start in the past? (Yes, three years ago.) Am I still a cook? (Yes.) · "I worked as a cook in 2019." Am I a cook now? (We don't know. Maybe not.) · "I've been working here since 2021." Is 2021 a length of time or a start point? (A start point, so since.)
"A colleague" — is it my boss? (Not usually. A person I work with.) · "The deadline is Friday." Can I finish on Saturday? (No! Friday is the last day.) · "I applied for the position." Is a position a place? (No, a job.)

Meaning: STAR. Interviewers often say "Tell me about a time when…" They want a short true story that proves a skill.

In STAR, what is A? (Action: what I did.) Do we say "we" or "I" in the Action? (Mostly "I." The interviewer is hiring you!) What tense is the story? (The past simple: I called, I planned, I finished.) Does the story need a Result? (Yes, a good end, and maybe what I learned.)

Pronunciation. Word stress: exPErience · reSPONsible · responsiBILity · COLleague · poSItion · INterview · DEADline. Contractions sound confident: "I've been" /aɪv bɪn/ (don't drop the /v/: "I been working" ❌). Past endings in STAR stories: called /d/, fixed /t/, planned /d/, delivered /d/, started /ɪd/. Speak slowly, and pause after each STAR part. A pause sounds confident, not nervous.

Drill script
  1. for / since quick-fire: T: "three years" → S: "for three years." · "2021" → "since 2021." · "last March" → "since last March." · "six months" → "for six months." · "I was 18" → "since I was 18."
  2. Build the sentence (back-chain): "for three years" → "as a cook for three years" → "I've been working as a cook for three years." Choral, then each learner with their own job (or card job).
  3. Preposition + -ing: T: "responsible for… train" → S: "I'm responsible for training new staff." · "good at… organize" → "I'm good at organizing." · "experience in… sales" → "I have experience in sales." · "My job involves… deal with customers" → "My job involves dealing with customers."
  4. STAR relay (groups of 4): T gives a skill ("working under pressure"). S1 says the Situation, S2 the Task, S3 the Action, S4 the Result. Then the group says the whole story together. Do it twice with different skills.
  5. Filler + answer: T fires a hard question ("What's your biggest weakness?"). S must start with a filler ("That's a good question. Let me think…") and then give one sentence. Fast, fun, 6–8 learners.
Mock interview panels — how to run it
  • Trios (a group of four works: two observers). Each trio gets one set of interviewer question cards. Every learner has 3 observer checklists.
  • Interviewer: welcomes the candidate ("Thanks for coming in."), asks 5–6 questions from the cards, including one "Tell me about a time…" card, and closes ("Thank you. We'll be in touch."). Encourage a real follow-up question: "Can you tell me more about that?"
  • Candidate: greets and thanks, answers from their notes (not reading full sentences), uses a filler if they need time, tells one STAR story, and asks one question at the end. Candidates using a profile card answer as that person. Nobody has to explain why.
  • Observer: ticks the checklist and writes one great thing + one tip, then gives this feedback kindly in 1–2 minutes. Tips must be specific ("Add a Result to your story."), not general ("Speak better.").
  • Ground rules for interviewers: use only the question cards. No questions about age, family, religion, home country or immigration status. Real interviewers should not ask most of these either. If a learner wants to practice answering one, see the stretch box.
  • Rotate roles every 7 minutes (5 interview + 2 feedback). If time is short, run two rounds and let the third role be homework with a family member or friend.
ICQs before the panels
How many roles are there? (Three: interviewer, candidate, observer.) · Does the candidate read full sentences? (No, just look at notes.) · What does the observer write? (One great thing and one tip.) · Which question needs a STAR story? ("Tell me about a time…") · What does the candidate do at the end? (Ask a question.) · Can you use a profile card? (Yes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a fill-in answer card: "I'm a ___. I've been working as a ___ for ___ years. I'm good at ___ing. Now I'm looking for a job as a ___." Use the job flashcards as prompts (nurse, cook, cleaner, driver, mechanic). The interviewer reads only 3 question cards (Tell me about yourself / What are your strengths? / Do you have any questions?). The STAR story can be spoken with 4 pictures or icons the learner draws (🌨️ → 📦 → 📞 → ✅). Target: Present → Past → Future in 3 sentences + one question at the end.

Stretch

Answer the harder cards: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague" (use 7.1 language in the story!) and "Why did you leave your last job?" (stay positive, no blame). Practice a polite redirect for an inappropriate question: "I'm not sure how that relates to the job, but I can tell you that I'm available for all the shifts." Use would like to and future forms (5.3) for "Where do you see yourself in five years?" Write a 4-line thank-you email after the interview.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I have 5 years working here" / "I work here since 2019" / "since five years": Spanish tengo / llevo cinco años, French depuis, German seit, Portuguese há cinco anos. Recast: "I've been working here for five years / since 2019."
  • "responsible of" / "experience on" / "good in": Spanish and French responsable de, Russian опыт в (sometimes "on"). Drill the chunks: responsible for, experience in, good at.
  • "I'm good at to cook" / "responsible for to train" (most L1s): after a preposition, -ing. Link to 6.2.
  • "I work as nurse" (Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Korean, Japanese: no articles; Spanish and French: no article with jobs, soy enfermera). English needs a: "as a nurse." Link to 6.1.
  • "My actual job" = current job (Spanish actual, French actuel). Recast: "my current job." It's the actually false friend from C20 again.
  • Talking about yourself: in many cultures (e.g. Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Filipino, many African cultures) praising yourself feels rude, so learners say "I'm not so good." In US interviews, modesty can sound like a lack of skill. Teach it as a genre rule, not a personality change: "In an interview you're informing, not bragging. Give proof with an example." Eye contact and handshakes vary too. They're not required in class, and a warm "Nice to meet you" works everywhere.
Workforce link (WIOA and career-pathway programs)

This class is a natural bridge to employment services. Keep the observer checklists: they're good evidence of workplace-readiness skills for a learner portfolio. Invite learners to repeat the mock interview with your program's career services staff or job developer. Learners' Interview planners can become the base for a résumé "Summary" line. Remind learners that transferable skills count: a parent who manages a family budget "has experience in budgeting." A person who helped at a mosque, church or community kitchen "has experience in food preparation and working in a team."
