Module 1 — Time, Experience & Past Habits: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives (including American and British variation), the classroom-materials answers (time-word sort, Find Someone Who, clue game, discussion), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 1.1–1.3Classes C1–C39 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B1, mark the choice as well as the form. The key question for every item is: does the learner's form say what the sentence means? Be generous where real English is flexible. American English often uses the past simple with just / already / yet ("Did you eat yet?", "I just ate"): accept it in speech and in open writing, but in Exercises 2 and 4 of Lesson 1.1 the target is the present perfect. Simple and continuous overlap with some verbs (I've lived / I've been living here for five years are both correct). British forms (got, film, flat, football, practise, garden) are acceptable when a learner uses them. A small spelling slip ("writting") is a success with a note.

1.1 · Present Perfect vs Past Simple (C1)

Exercise 1 — Sam's news (Script 1.1-A)
  1. He's started a new job (at a hotel downtown, as a receptionist).
  2. Two weeks ago.
  3. No, never. (No, he hasn't.)
  4. At a bank, for three years.
  5. Yes, he has. He met her on his first day.
  6. He still hasn't learned everyone's name.
  7. She's just finished her exams.

Discussion question: Sam opens with the present perfect ("I've started a new job") and gives the details in the past simple ("I started two weeks ago," "I worked at a bank," "I met her on my first day"). Accept short answers ("two weeks ago," "a bank, three years").

Exercise 2 — Present perfect or past simple?
  1. have seen ('ve seen): an experience, no finished time.
  2. saw: last Friday is a finished time.
  3. has lived ('s lived): since 2020 and still true. (Also correct: has been living.)
  4. Have … been: ever = in your life up to now.
  5. haven't finished: yet = up to now.
  6. started: two weeks ago is a finished time.
  7. 've lost (have lost): news with a result now (I can't find them). "I lost my keys" is understood and common in American English; accept it with a note.
  8. did … get: When…? asks for a finished time, so it always takes the past simple.

Wrong: "I have seen it last Friday" · "When have you got your first phone?" · "She lives in Paris since 2020" · "Have you ever went…?"

Exercise 3 — for or since?

1 for (five years = a length) · 2 since (college = a start point) · 3 since (last week) · 4 for (a long time) · 5 for (two years) · 6 since (Monday).

Item 2: since university / since school are also fine. If a learner writes "since we were in college," praise it: since + a past-simple clause is a stretch form.

Exercise 4 — just, already, yet, still

1 yet · 2 still · 3 just · 4 already · 5 yet · 6 just.

Item 1: "Have you already finished your homework?" is possible English (it shows surprise that it happened so early), but already goes before the participle, not at the end, so in this position only yet is correct. Item 4 is the same idea: surprise → already.

Exercise 5 — Finished or connected to now? Then "My story"
🔚 Finished time (past simple)➡️● Connected to now (present perfect)
yesterday · last summer · in 2019 · two hours ago · when I was a child · on Fridayever · this week · never · today · so far · since Monday

today and this week go with the present perfect while the day or week is still going on ("I've had three coffees today"). A learner who says "I had a big breakfast today" (the morning is finished) is also right: praise the thinking.

My story (open). Look for: an opener in the present perfect with no time word, then 3–4 past-simple details with times and places. Model: I've cooked for fifty people. I did it at my cousin's wedding last summer. We started at six in the morning and made rice, chicken and three salads. Everyone loved it, but I was so tired!

Speaking — Timeline interview (classroom materials, Set E)

Answers are personal. Listen for: openers with Have you ever…? / How long have you…? + past participle; follow-ups with When / Where / Who … did you…?; answers of 2–3 sentences; for / since for the "still true now" event. In the report: "He's played… He learned…" (not "He has learned it last year").

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) I have called him yesterday.
B) I called him yesterday.
C) I did called him yesterday.
Why: yesterday is a finished time, so the past simple. A breaks the golden rule; C is a double past (did + past form).
Q2
"I've studied English ___ three years."
A) for
B) since
C) ago
Why: three years is a length → for. since needs a start point (since 2023); ago goes with the past simple ("I started three years ago").
Q3 · new
"Where's Maria?" — "She's ___ to Japan. She's there for a month."
A) been
B) gone
C) went
Why: gone = she went and she is still there. been = went and came back (an experience). C is the past simple, and "She's went" is not a form.
Errors you will hear most (1.1)

"I have seen him yesterday." · "I live here since five years." · "since three years" / "for 2019" · "When have you arrived?" · "Have you ever went…?" · "I've been to Japan in 2019." · "She's been to the store. She's there now." (for gone) · "I've already eat." Correct the present perfect + finished time and the for / since mix every time. Let "I already ate / Did you eat yet?" go: it is normal American English.

Accept notes ("driving class · January · 8 lessons"). The result column is the simple vs continuous contrast: point it out when you check.

Exercise 2 — Make present perfect continuous sentences
  1. She's been working here since June.
  2. They haven't been sleeping well lately.
  3. How long have you been waiting?
  4. It's been raining all day.
  5. He's been learning to drive for six months.

Also correct: full forms (She has been working…) and They've not been sleeping (less common). Wrong: "She has working" · "She's been work" · "How long you have been waiting?" · "It's raining all day" (for duration up to now).

Exercise 3 — Simple or continuous?
  1. have read ('ve read): how many → result.
  2. have been reading: how long → activity.
  3. has painted: counted and finished (two rooms already).
  4. have been waiting: duration. (have waited is possible but less natural.)
  5. have known: know is a stative verb, so no continuous.
  6. has been driving: how long (for six hours), and it explains why she needs a break.
  7. have drunk ('ve drunk): how many (three cups). "I've drank" is common in speech but non-standard; correct it gently.
Exercise 4 — What have they been doing?

1 have been working ('ve been working) · 2 has been baking ('s been baking) · 3 Have … been running · 4 have been planting · 5 has been fixing · 6 Have … been swimming.

In 1, "I've worked all day" is also acceptable (the day is finished, a result). The continuous is better because it explains the tiredness.

Exercise 5 — The stative-verb trap
  1. ✗ → I've known Maria since school.
  2. ✓ (an activity: duration)
  3. ✗ → She's had this car since 2019. (have = own)
  4. ✗ → They've wanted a dog for ages.
  5. ✓ (having problems = experiencing, an activity)
  6. ✗ → He's liked his new job since he started. (Also correct: He likes his new job.)

Item 6: "I'm loving it" is heard in advertising and casual speech. Explain that it is informal, and not the standard form for tests or work.

Speaking — Catching up and the "lately" table (open)

Check: have / has been + -ing, for / since for how long, and one result in the simple. Model: I've been learning the guitar for six months. I've learned about ten songs. I've been working a lot lately, so I haven't been sleeping well. I've been cooking more at home since January. I've tried twelve new recipes!

Clue game & Find Someone Who (classroom materials)

Clue game (Set F): the target answer is printed small on each card, but any logical guess with Have you been + -ing? is correct. Find Someone Who (Set H): answers are personal; check the question form Have you ever + past participle? and a past-simple follow-up (When did you…?). The common participles: been, eaten, met, seen, run, cooked, won, flown, sung.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) She has working here since May.
B) She has been working here since May.
C) She has been work here since May.
Why: the form is has + been + verb-ing. A drops been; C drops the -ing.
Q2
"I've ___ five emails this morning."
A) written
B) been writing
C) wrote
Why: five emails is a counted result (how many?), so the simple. B focuses on duration; C is the past simple (and "I've wrote" is not a form).
Q3 · new
"I ___ her since we were at school."
A) 've been knowing
B) 've known
C) know
Why: know is a stative verb, so the present perfect simple even for duration. C is the present simple, which can't go with since.
Errors you will hear most (1.2)

"I have working all day." · "I am working here since 2020." · "I've been knowing him for years." · "She's been having a car since 2019." · "I've been reading three books." · "He have been running." · "How long you have been waiting?" Correct the missing been, the present tense with since, and the stative verbs every time. A choice of simple where the continuous is more natural ("I've waited for an hour") is not an error.

1.3 · Used to & Would — Past Habits (C3)

Exercise 1 — Then and now (Script 1.3-A)

1 T · 2 F (people didn't use to shop online, so there weren't many packages) · 3 T · 4 F (she works on the phone and online now) · 5 T · 6 F (he liked it better before).

Discussion question (open): most learners say Joe (letters → packages) or Grace (counter → app). Any answer with a reason is correct. Listen for used to in the answers.

Exercise 2 — used to (+ / − / ?)
  1. used to play
  2. didn't use to like
  3. Did … use to have
  4. used to be
  5. didn't use to have

Also correct: never used to for 2 and 5 ("She never used to like vegetables"). Wrong: "didn't used to" · "Did you used to" · "used to played." Note: many native writers write "didn't used to"; it is common but not standard. Mark it as a spelling point, not a grammar error.

Exercise 3 — used to or would?
  1. both: used to tell / would tell (a repeated action)
  2. only used to: live is a state
  3. both: used to go / would go (a repeated action)
  4. only used to: have (= own) is a state
  5. only used to: know is a state
  6. both: used to sing / would sing (a repeated action; the past time frame is clear)
Exercise 4 — Rewrite with used to
  1. When I was a teenager, I used to listen to rock music.
  2. My town used to have a train station. (Also: There used to be a train station in my town.)
  3. We used to eat dinner together every evening. (Also: We would eat…)
  4. She used to be very shy at school.
  5. People didn't use to shop online.
  6. Did you use to walk to school when you were a child?

Wrong: "She would be very shy" (a state) · "People didn't used to shop" · "Did you used to walk…?"

Exercise 5 — Then and now (open)

Look for: two used to sentences (at least one state is ideal), one would for a repeated action, one "now" sentence in the present simple, and an opinion with because. Model (getting around): People used to use paper maps. There didn't use to be GPS. On long trips, my father would stop and ask for directions. Now our phones tell us where to go. I think it's better now, because we don't get lost, but people used to talk to each other more.

Speaking — How life has changed (classroom materials, Set G)

Open discussion. Success = everyone speaks (the tokens are used), each person gives at least one used to and one opinion with a reason, and the reporter summarizes the group. Listen for would with states and the used to / use to spelling in any notes. Do not mark the content: "better before" and "better now" are both good answers.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is spelled correctly?
A) Did you used to live here?
B) Did you use to live here?
C) Did you used to living here?
Why: after did, the verb goes back to its base form: use to, just like "Did you want?" (not "Did you wanted?").
Q2
"When I was young, I ___ have a dog."
A) used to
B) would
C) was used to
Why: have (a dog) is a state, so only used to. would works only for repeated actions; be used to means "comfortable with" and needs -ing.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) There would be a movie theater on Main Street.
B) There used to be a movie theater on Main Street.
C) There use to be a movie theater on Main Street.
Why: be is a state, so used to, not would. C has the wrong spelling: no did, so it's used with a d.
Errors you will hear most (1.3)

"I would live in a small town." · "Did you used to…?" / "I didn't used to…" · "I used to went." · "I use to drink coffee every morning." (for a present habit: teach usually) · "Before, I live in…" · "I was used to walk to school." · "use-ed to" (pronunciation). Correct would + state and the present-habit "use to" every time: they change the meaning. The spelling after did matters only in writing.

Module 2 — If & Imagining (Conditionals): Answers

Answers to every Module 2 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (card sort, dilemmas, Small Wishes, Wish Mingle), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 2.1–2.3Classes C4–C69 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At B1, mark the verb pair first: present + present (zero), present + will (first), past + would (second), wish + past / were / could / would. A wrong pair changes the meaning ("If I had time, I'll call") and is an error. Be generous with the choice between zero and first where both make sense (If you heat water, it boils / it'll boil), and accept was for were in speaking (informal but common), while noting were as the standard for writing. Contractions and full forms are both correct. American English is the model; British forms (flat, holiday, neighbour, football, apologise) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

  2. rains … won't go

Exercise 4 — if / when / unless + commas

1 When (sure) · 2 If (maybe) · 3 unless · 4 Unless · 5 when · 6 unless.

Commas: a) If I see Anna, I'll tell her. · b) no comma · c) When you're ready, call me.

Exercise 5 — One sentence + "My six predictions"
  1. If I have time, I'll help you.
  2. If you see Anna, give her this book.
  3. If we leave early, we won't hit traffic.
  4. I'll be late unless I leave now.
  5. You won't succeed unless you try.

Also correct: the clauses in the other order (no comma): "I'll help you if I have time." · 2 "Please give her this book if you see her." · 4 "Unless I leave now, I'll be late."

My six predictions (open). Look for: present after if / when / unless, will / won't (or can / might) in the result, one unless, one when, commas. Model: If I finish work early on Friday, I'll cook something nice. If it's sunny on Saturday, we'll go to the park. When I get my paycheck, I'll buy new shoes. I won't be tired on Monday unless I stay up late. If the bus is late, I'll walk. If I practice every day, my English will get better.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is correct?
A) If it will rain, I'll stay home.
B) If it rains, I'll stay home.
C) If it will rain, I will stay home.
Why: present simple after if; will goes in the result only.

B) when

B) get
C) got
Why: when follows the same rule as if: present simple for a future meaning. got is past.

2.2 · Second Conditional (C5)

Exercise 1 — Sam and Rosa (Script 2.2-A)

1 F — Rosa would travel for a whole year; Sam would buy a little house by the sea. · 2 T · 3 T · 4 F — "If I were you, I'd take it." · 5 T · 6 T ("I'm going to go on Saturday").

"I'd" sentences: I'd quit my job · I'd travel for a whole year · I'd buy a little house by the sea · I'd take it · I'd say yes right away. Also "I might visit the city first" (might in the result).

Exercise 2 — Second conditional
  1. had … would learn ('d learn)
  2. knew … would call ('d call)
  3. would travel ('d travel) … didn't have
  4. were … wouldn't say
  5. would … do … met

Also correct: 4 "was" (informal; note were as standard) · 1 "could learn". Wrong: "If I would have" · "we would travel more if we wouldn't have" · "What you would do".

Exercise 3 — First or second?
  1. rains … will take ('ll take) — first
  2. were … would change ('d change) — second
  3. go … will feel ('ll feel) — first
  4. could … wouldn't need — second
  5. pass … will celebrate ('ll celebrate) — first
  6. were … would sleep ('d sleep) — second

Also correct: 1 "might take" · 3 "might feel". Wrong: mixed pairs ("If I were the president, I'll change…").

Exercise 4 — Advice (sample answers)
  1. If I were you, I'd go to bed earlier. / …I wouldn't drink coffee at night.
  2. If I were you, I'd buy a new one. / …I'd delete some old apps.
  3. If I were you, I'd watch movies in English every day.
  4. If I were you, I'd call her / him / them and apologize. / I wouldn't wait too long, if I were you.
  5. If I were you, I'd put a small bowl by the door for your keys.
  6. If I were you, I'd buy a travel mug. / …I'd make coffee at work.

Any sensible advice is correct. Check: If I were you + I'd / I wouldn't + base verb. "If I was you" = informal (note it). Wrong: "If I were you, I will…" · "If I am you…" · "I'd to go".

Exercise 5 — What would you do if…? (open)

Check: If I + past, I'd + base verb in every answer, one If I were…, one could / might. Model: If I had a free day tomorrow, I'd sleep late and cook a big breakfast. If I found $50 on the bus, I'd give it to the driver. If I could have any job for one day, I'd be a pilot. If I could speak every language, I'd work as a tour guide. If I met my favorite singer, I might ask for a photo. If I were on a free vacation, I'd go to the mountains.

Dilemma cards & advice role-play (classroom materials)

Open answers: there is no right answer to a dilemma. Check the form (If I…, I'd… because…) and the reactions (I'd do the same. / Really? I wouldn't.). Advice role-play: any If I were you, I'd… that fits.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence uses the second conditional correctly?
A) If I will have money, I would travel.
B) If I had money, I would travel.
C) If I would have money, I travel.
Why: If + past simple (had), would + base verb. No will or would after if.

"If I would have money, I'd…" · "If I have a million dollars, I'd…" · "If I had time, I'll call you." · "I buy a house" (dropped 'd) · "If I am you…" · "What you would do if…?" Accept "If I was you" in speech.

Speaking — structured discussion (success criteria)

Answers are personal. A successful speaker: gives one opinion with a reason, agrees once, disagrees politely once (acknowledge → but → view), asks someone for their opinion by name, uses a softener, and waits for their turn.   1. You like pizza, don't you? (the pronoun is you, not it)
  2. I'm right, aren't I?
  3. She has left, hasn't she? (positive → negative)
  4. Open the window, will you? (also: would you? / can you? / won't you?)
  5. Let's dance, shall we?
Exercise 5 — Keep it going (open)

Look for a reaction (echo or interest phrase) + a follow-up question that fits. Models:

  1. Have you? That sounds fun! What are you learning to cook?
  2. Did she? That's wonderful! What was the prize for?
  3. Oh no! That's terrible. Have you talked to your neighbor about it?
  4. Are you? How nice! Which beach are you going to?

Accept any correct echo: 1 "Have you?" · 2 "Did she?" · 3 "Can't you?" (or "Oh no!") · 4 "Are you?". Common slips: "Do you?" for 1 (I've started → have) · "What it was like?" (word order: What was it like?) · a closed follow-up that ends the chat ("Is it good?"); encourage open questions (What / Where / How…?).

Dialogue — Priya and Tom

Tags (4): didn't you · is it · won't you · shall we. Reactions (2): Oh really? · Is it? (an echo question). Follow-up (1): What was it like? Note: "Is it?" is both an echo and a reaction. The workbook version is slightly shorter than the online dialogue (the online line "You should try it, shouldn't you?" is left out because the tag there is unusual: we rarely use a tag to tell someone what they should do).

Speaking — small-talk mingle (open)

Success = the learner opens with a correct falling tag, uses at least one echo and one follow-up question per conversation, backchannels while listening, and uses a rising tag to check a guess in round 4. Answers to guess & check: answer the fact ("You don't eat meat, do you?" — "No, I don't." if true).

Self-check
Q1
"She doesn't work on Fridays, ___?"
A) doesn't she
B) does she
C) isn't she
Why: a negative statement takes a positive tag, and present simple borrows does. A keeps the same polarity; C uses the wrong verb.
Q2
You are really not sure and want to check. How do you say the tag?
A) With a falling voice ↘
B) With a rising voice ↗
C) It makes no difference.
Why: a rising tag is a real question. A falling tag expects agreement. The words are the same; the voice changes the meaning, so C is wrong.
Q3 · new
"I'm late again, ___?"
A) amn't I
B) aren't I
C) isn't I
Why: the tag for I am is the fixed form aren't I?. "Amn't I" is not standard English (you may hear it in Scotland and Ireland); "isn't I" is never correct.
Errors you will hear most (8.1)

"…, isn't it?" / "…, no?" after every statement · "She has left, has she?" (same polarity) · "You live here, live you?" / "…, aren't you?" for a present-simple verb · "You don't drive, do you?" — "Yes." (meaning I don't) · flat intonation on every tag · silence while listening (no backchannel) · "How was it like?" (What was it like? / How was it?). Fix the form in controlled practice; in the mingle, note errors for delayed correction and praise any warm reaction.

8.2 · Phrasal Verbs — A Practical Toolkit (C24)

Exercise 1 — Leo and Nadia (Script 8.2-A): 12 phrasal verbs

wake up (woke up) · get back (to sleep) · set off · check in · turn on · sort out (sort it out) · look after (looking after) · get along with (UK: get on with) · look forward to (looking forward to) · come up (comes up) · deal with · find out.

Accept the forms as heard (woke up, comes up). Accept get on with (the online version). Stretch: which three are separable here? (turn on, sort out, find out; sort it out shows the pronoun rule.)

Exercise 2 — Complete the phrasal verb

1 up · 2 off · 3 after · 4 up · 5 out · 6 forward (look forward to) · 7 with · 8 into.

Note: up is used twice. Also possible in 1: I wake up (but the verb given is get). Wrong: "look for my cat" in 3 changes the meaning (search for a lost cat); if the learner explains that meaning, accept it with a smile.

Exercise 3 — Say it the everyday way

1 take off · 2 turn off (also: switch off, put out) · 3 sort out (also: work out, figure out) · 4 give up (also: quit is already everyday) · 5 put off (They put off the meeting / put the meeting off) · 6 carry on (also: go on, keep going).

take off is not in the class toolkit; many learners know it from airports. If nobody does, teach it here (and its second meaning: take off your coat).

Exercise 4 — Fix the pronoun
  1. The radio's too loud — turn it off.
  2. I found your keys. I'll pick them up later.
  3. It's a hard puzzle, but I'll work it out.
  4. Here's the trash — please throw it away.
  5. Don't give it up now!

Also correct: turn it down in 1 (a better meaning for "too loud"). British rubbish is fine.

Exercise 5 — Split or stick?
1fill out the form✂️fill it out
2look for my glasses🔗look for them
3put away the dishes✂️put them away
4call back Mr. Diaz✂️call him back
5get along with my coworkers🔗get along with them
6hand in the report✂️hand it in

With a noun, "call Mr. Diaz back" and "call back Mr. Diaz" are both fine; with a person's name, Americans usually split it ("call Mr. Diaz back").

Speaking — talk cards (open)

Look for 2+ phrasal verbs per answer, a pronoun in the middle when the verb is separable, and look forward to + -ing. The group should react with an echo and ask a follow-up question (C23 recycling).

Self-check
Q1
The light is on. Which sentence is correct?
A) Turn off it, please.
B) Turn it off, please.
C) It off turn, please.
Why: turn off is separable, so a pronoun object must go in the middle. A leaves the pronoun at the end; C has the wrong word order.
Q2
Which phrasal verb is 🔗 stick (it can't split)?
A) sort out
B) look after
C) turn on
Why: look after is inseparable: "look after the kids," never "look the kids after." Sort out and turn on can split.
Q3 · new
"I'm really looking forward to ___ you next week."
A) see
B) seeing
C) saw
Why: in look forward to, to is a preposition, so it takes a noun or an -ing form. A is the most common error at B1.
Errors you will hear most (8.2)

"Turn off it." / "I'll pick up them." · "Can you look my cat after?" · "I look forward to see you." · "I get up the bus." (get on) · "I'm waiting you." (waiting for) · avoiding phrasal verbs entirely (I will discover the price) · "I get along my boss." (missing with). Correct pronoun position every time in controlled practice; in freer speaking, praise any natural phrasal verb first.

8.3 · Review Project — Presentation & Panel Discussion (C25)

Exercise 1 — Maya's talk and the panel (Script 8.3-A)
  1. It's healthy, it saves money, and (if more people cycled) streets would be cleaner and quieter.
  2. Her expensive bus pass.
  3. That cycling is not always safe.
  4. If cities built more bike lanes.
  5. Because some people live far away (from work).

Signposts heard (6): Today I'd like to talk about · First of all · Another point is · Finally · To sum up · Thank you for listening. Not heard: For example · In conclusion. Stretch: find the B1 structures (past perfect had never felt; phrasal verb gave up; second conditional if more people cycled… would be; reported speech told me she had stopped; tags is it? · isn't it? · shall we?).

Exercise 2 — Mixed B1 review

1 rains (If + present, will) · 2 was (backshift from am) · 3 given (had + past participle) · 4 hearing (look forward to + -ing) · 5 shouldn't we · 6 out.

Also correct: 2 felt (She said she felt tired). Wrong: "will rain" in 1 · "gave" in 3 · "hear" in 4.

Exercise 3 — Correct the mistake
  1. I gave a presentation about the environment.
  2. If I have time tomorrow, I'll practice again.
  3. He told me that the room was ready. (or: He said that…)
  4. You're coming to the panel, aren't you?
  5. I have finished my slides.

British practise (verb) is fine in 2.

Exercise 4 — Put the talk in order

b 1 · g 2 · d 3 · f 4 · a 5 · c 6 · e 7

So: a 5 · b 1 · c 6 · d 3 · e 7 · f 4 · g 2. Introduction (b + g), point 1 + example (d + f), point 2 (a), conclusion (c), close (e). Accept f after a (4 ↔ 5 swap: the example could illustrate either point) if the learner explains it.

Exercise 5 — Say it politely (open)
  1. That's an interesting idea, but I'm not sure it would work. / I see what you mean, but I think it might be difficult.
  2. That's a good point. However, I think buses might be better for some people. / I see your point, but I prefer the bus.
  3. (Chair) What's your view on that, [name]? / [Name], what do you think?
  4. (Chair) We have time for one more question. / To wrap up, let's thank our speakers.

Accept any version that acknowledges first, then gives the view, or (chair) invites or closes politely. Softening words to praise: I think · might · maybe · a little · to be honest.

Planning frame, presentation and panel (open)

What a strong B1 talk looks like (3–4 minutes, about 350–450 words spoken): a clear introduction with a reason ("I chose this topic because…"); 2–3 points, each signposted and with an example; at least four different B1 structures; a conclusion that repeats the main idea; one audience question answered. The teacher's model (Script 8.3-B) is a good example to show.

Frames: key words only. If a learner has written full sentences, help them underline 3–4 key words per box and practice from those.

Self-check
Q1
Which phrase best ends a presentation?
A) Today I'd like to talk about…
B) To sum up…
C) Another point is…
Why: To sum up… signals the conclusion. A opens a talk; C adds a point in the middle.
Q2
In a discussion, how do you disagree politely?
A) No, you're wrong.
B) I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree.
C) (Say nothing and wait.)
Why: acknowledge the other view first, then add yours. A sounds harsh in English; C means you don't take part.
Q3 · new
You are the chair. Ana hasn't spoken yet. What do you say?
A) You, talk now.
B) Ana, what's your view on that?
C) Ana is quiet, isn't she?
Why: a chair brings people in by name with an open, polite question. A is an order; C talks about Ana and could embarrass her (even though the tag is correct!).
Errors you will hear most (8.3)

Reading a script word for word · "Today I will talk you about…" (talk to you about / tell you about) · "Today we discuss about…" · missing articles ("importance of sport") · "In conclusion" used at the start · "I am agree" / "I agree with you, but…" said so fast it sounds rude · "If more people will cycle…" · rising intonation at the end of every point. Give feedback on communication first (Was it clear? Did people follow?), then one or two forms per learner.
