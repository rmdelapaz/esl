🎤 Lesson 4.3: Review Project — Opinions & Storytelling

This is it — the final lesson of B1. You've built a whole toolkit of grammar; now you'll learn the language of opinions and discussion, and then bring everything together to tell a story and argue a point. This is the moment where all the pieces click into one confident, independent speaker. Let's finish strong. 🎉

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Give, support, and respond to opinions using natural phrases
  • Agree and disagree politely in a real discussion
  • Combine B1 structures — perfect tenses, conditionals, reported speech, relative clauses, the passive — in extended speaking

The Finish Line

Think back to where you started. At A1 you introduced yourself. At A2 you told simple stories. Now, at the end of B1, you can hold a real discussion — you can say what you think, why you think it, and respond thoughtfully when someone disagrees. That is a huge jump, and this lesson celebrates it by putting it all to work.

We'll do two things. First, we'll learn the functional language of opinions — the phrases that let you enter a conversation gracefully and keep it polite. Then we'll take a guided tour back through every B1 structure you've studied and see how much meaning you can now express. By the end, you won't just know the grammar — you'll be able to use it live, in a story and a debate.

🧠 Mindset

You may still feel you "don't know enough." Almost every learner feels that — even fluent speakers. But look at what you're about to do: narrate, hypothesise, report, describe, and argue, all in a second language. That isn't a beginner's ability; it's independence. Confidence doesn't arrive before you speak — it grows because you speak. So today, speak. 🌱

Giving & Supporting Opinions

A good opinion has two parts: the claim (what you think) and the support (why). English gives you neat phrases for both. Start by learning a handful of openers so you never have to begin with a flat "I think."

Ways to give an opinion

In my opinion, … I think / believe / feel (that) … If you ask me, … As far as I'm concerned, … It seems to me that … Personally, I'd say …

📖 Register note

In my opinion and It seems to me that sound a little more formal (good for essays, presentations, meetings). If you ask me and Personally, I'd say are more informal and conversational. All are polite — choose the level that fits the situation.

Ways to support and structure your point

An opinion without a reason is just noise. Use these to add reasons, examples, and balance:

The main reason is … For example / For instance, … On the one hand … on the other hand … Firstly … secondly … finally … That's because … What's more, …

Claim + support in action: "In my opinion, cities are better for young people. The main reason is that there are more jobs. For example, my cousin found work within a month of moving. On the one hand, the countryside is calmer — on the other hand, it can feel isolated."

Notice how each phrase does a job: open the opinion → give the reason → illustrate it → weigh both sides.

Agreeing & Disagreeing Politely

A discussion is a two-way street. Half the skill is responding — showing you agree, or pushing back without sounding rude. In English, disagreement is almost always softened: you acknowledge the other person first, then add your view.

Agreeing

I completely agree. That's a good point. Exactly! So do I. / Neither do I. You're absolutely right. I couldn't agree more.

Disagreeing — politely

I see what you mean, but … I'm not sure I agree. I take your point, however … I'm afraid I disagree. That may be true, but … I'd look at it differently.

⚠️ Stay polite

A flat "You're wrong" or "No, that's not true" sounds harsh in English and can end a conversation. The polite pattern is acknowledge, then disagree: "I see what you mean, but I think…" This shows respect and keeps the discussion friendly — a key mark of an independent, socially fluent speaker.

💡 So do I / Neither do I

To agree quickly, echo the verb form. After a positive statement: "I love travelling." → "So do I." After a negative statement: "I don't like crowds." → "Neither do I." Match the auxiliary: "I've been there." → "So have I."

A Discussion in Action

Here's how the pieces sound together. Two friends discuss whether social media is good for us. Watch how they give opinions, support them, and disagree politely.

A: If you ask me, social media has done more harm than good.

B: Really? I'm not sure I agree. What's the main reason you say that?

A: Well, the main reason is that people compare their lives to everyone else's. For example, my sister feels worse every time she scrolls.

B: I see what you mean, but on the other hand, it keeps families in touch. If I didn't have it, I'd never speak to my cousins abroad.

A: That's a good point. Maybe it depends on how you use it.

B: Exactly. It seems to me that it's a tool — it's neither good nor bad on its own.

A: I take your point. I'd still say we spend too long on it, though!

✅ What made that work

  • Each opinion came with a reason or an example.
  • Disagreement was softened: "I'm not sure I agree", "I see what you mean, but…".
  • They acknowledged each other: "That's a good point", "Exactly".
  • They stayed friendly even while disagreeing — the goal is discussion, not winning.

Here's the fun part. Look how much you can now express! Every structure below came from a B1 lesson — and each one adds a shade of meaning to an opinion or a story. Read the table slowly and notice: you can do all of these.

Practice & Capstone Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match the function

Objective: What is each phrase doing — giving an opinion, agreeing, or disagreeing politely?

  1. "As far as I'm concerned, exams are outdated."
  2. "I take your point, however, they do measure effort."
  3. "That's a good point — I hadn't thought of that."
  4. "It seems to me that we need both."
  5. "I'm afraid I disagree."
✅ Answer

1. giving an opinion · 2. disagreeing politely · 3. agreeing · 4. giving an opinion · 5. disagreeing politely

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the B1 structure

Objective: Complete each sentence with the natural form.

  1. If I (have) more time, I would learn another language. (imaginary)
  2. It is often (say) that travel broadens the mind. (passive)
  3. My teacher told me that I (make) real progress. (reported speech)
  4. Prices have risen a lot, is why people are worried. (relative clause)
  5. He's not answering — he be asleep. (deduction, fairly sure)
✅ Answer

1. had · 2. said · 3. was making (or: had made) · 4. which · 5. must

🏋️ Exercise 3: Turn a fact into an opinion

Objective: Rewrite each plain statement as an opinion with support. (Answers are models — many versions are correct.)

  1. Cars cause pollution.
  2. Learning a language is hard.
  3. Social media is popular.
✅ Answer

1. "In my opinion, cars cause too much pollution. The main reason is that most people drive alone." · 2. "If you ask me, learning a language is hard at first, but it gets easier — for example, I couldn't speak a word two years ago." · 3. "It seems to me that social media is popular because it keeps us connected, which is why so many people use it daily."

🎯 Your Capstone Project: Story & Opinion

This is the big one — the task that pulls your whole B1 year together. Do it solo (record yourself on your phone) or with a partner / group (they respond in Part B). Take a few minutes to plan, then speak.

Part A — Tell a connected story (2–3 min)

  1. (3 min – plan) Choose a true story from your life: a trip, a challenge, a lesson learned, a memorable day.
  2. (2–3 min – speak) Tell it as a connected story, using at least:
    • a perfect tense ("I've always wanted…" / "I'd never done that before")
    • past simple for the events ("So I went…", "It happened when…")
    • a relative clause ("the friend who came with me…", "…, which surprised everyone")
    • reported speech ("She told me that…", "Everyone said it would be fine")

Part B — Give and justify an opinion (2–3 min)

  1. (2 min – plan) Pick a familiar topic: technology, city vs country life, learning languages, or social media.
  2. (2–3 min – speak) Give your opinion with reasons, using at least:
    • an opinion phrase ("As far as I'm concerned…")
    • a conditional ("If we did X, things would improve")
    • a deduction or passive where natural ("It is often said that…" / "That can't be the whole story")
  3. (2 min – respond) If you have a partner, they now respond — agreeing or disagreeing politely ("I see what you mean, but…") and adding one reason of their own. Then swap roles.
  4. (2 min – review) Solo? Listen back. Did you use every required structure? Did your opinion have reasons, not just claims?

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My story used a perfect tense AND the past simple
  • ☐ My story included a relative clause and reported speech
  • ☐ My opinion opened with an opinion phrase and gave at least one reason
  • ☐ I used a conditional in my opinion
  • ☐ I used a deduction OR a passive where it felt natural
  • ☐ (With a partner) I agreed or disagreed politely, acknowledging first
  • ☐ I spoke for the full time without stopping to translate every word

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Always pair a claim with a reason — "because…", "the main reason is…". An opinion without support feels weak.
  • Acknowledge before you disagree: "I take your point, however…". It keeps the conversation warm.
  • Use the second conditional for hypothetical opinions ("If schools taught cooking, kids would eat better").
  • Vary your openers — don't start every sentence with "I think." You have six phrases now; use them.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Blunt disagreement: "You are wrong" ❌ → "I'm not sure I agree — I see it differently" ✅.
  • Mixing conditionals: "If I will have time…" ❌ → "If I have time, I will…" (real) or "If I had time, I would…" (imaginary) ✅.
  • Opinion with no reason: "It's bad." ❌ → "It's bad, because…" ✅.
  • "So do I" mismatch: after "I don't agree", reply "Neither do I", not "So do I" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Give opinions with phrases like In my opinion, As far as I'm concerned, It seems to me that — and always add a reason.
  • Structure your point: the main reason, for example, on the one hand… on the other hand, firstly… secondly.
  • Agree ("That's a good point", "So do I") and disagree politely ("I see what you mean, but…") — acknowledge first, always.
  • You can now combine every B1 structure — perfect tenses, conditionals, reported speech, relative clauses, passive, modals — in a real story and a real discussion.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's next after B1?

The next step is B2 — "Confident English", where you handle abstract topics, express nuance, and build real fluency. It's available now on the course roadmap — and you're more than ready for it, because B1 is exactly the foundation B2 builds on. Beyond it, C1 and C2 are ready too.

How do I keep improving from here?

Three habits: immerse yourself in real English (shows, podcasts, articles, conversations); speak regularly, even imperfectly — output is where growth happens; and get feedback from a teacher, a partner, or a language app. Revisit these lessons whenever a structure feels shaky. Little and often beats rare and intense.

Am I fluent now?

You've reached real independence — you can communicate about most things you need to, which is a serious achievement. "Fluent" isn't a finish line, though; it's a scale that keeps growing your whole life, even in your first language. So celebrate B1 for what it truly is: the level where English stops being a subject you study and starts being a language you live in.

🔭 Looking Ahead

Beyond B1 lies B2 — Confident English: discussing abstract and unfamiliar topics, expressing shades of meaning and nuance, following faster natural speech, and speaking with growing fluency and spontaneity. Everything you built this year is the launchpad. For now, though, the road ahead can wait — you've earned a moment to look back at how far you've climbed.
