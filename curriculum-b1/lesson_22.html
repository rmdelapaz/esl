❓ Lesson 8.1: Question Tags & Keeping a Conversation Going

Every day, people end their sentences with little questions: "It's a lovely day, isn't it?" "You've been here before, haven't you?" Those short add-ons are question tags, and they're one of the friendliest sounds in spoken English. Together with a handful of interested responses, they turn a flat exchange into a real, warm conversation — and that's exactly what this lesson is about.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form question tags — positive statement → negative tag, and the other way round
  • Choose the right auxiliary (or do/does/did) so the tag matches the statement
  • Handle the special cases — aren't I?, imperatives, and Let's … shall we?
  • Use interested responses and follow-up questions to keep a conversation going

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Have (or record) a short conversation that uses six or more question tags and three or more interested responses naturally.

What Is a Question Tag?

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement. Its job is to invite a response — to check something, to ask for agreement, or simply to keep the other person involved.

Look at the difference:

Plain statementStatement + tag
It's cold today.It's cold today, isn't it?
You know Maria.You know Maria, don't you?
He can't drive.He can't drive, can he?

The plain statement just gives information. The version with a tag reaches out — it turns your comment into a small invitation for the other person to say something back. That's why tags are everywhere in friendly, everyday talk.

🧠 Mindset

Question tags can look fiddly — so many little endings to remember! But they follow one mirror pattern, and once it clicks you'll build them automatically. Don't try to memorise a giant table of tags. Learn the logic (mirror the verb, flip the polarity), then let your ear do the rest by listening to real conversations. You already do this in your own language without thinking — you're just learning where English puts the pieces. 🌱

The Basic Rule

There is one idea at the heart of every question tag: the tag is a mirror of the statement. If the statement is positive, the tag is negative. If the statement is negative, the tag is positive.

📖 The pattern

Positive statement → negative tag: You're coming, aren't you?

Negative statement → positive tag: She doesn't smoke, does she?

The tag also copies the subject as a pronoun (you, she, it, they…) and uses a contracted verb in the negative (isn't, don't, hasn't).

Here's the flip in one picture — positive leans to negative, and negative leans back to positive:

    C["negative statement"] --> D["positive tag"]

✅ Quick self-check

Two things to ask yourself, every time: (1) Is my statement positive or negative? — then flip it. (2) Which verb am I mirroring? — copy that verb into the tag. Get those two right and the tag builds itself.

Choosing the Right Auxiliary

The tag copies the auxiliary verb or be from the statement. If the statement already contains one — is, are, was, will, have, can, should — you simply reuse it (and flip the polarity).

Verb in the statementExample statementTag
beShe is a doctor,isn't she?
willYou will help,won't you?
have (perfect)They have left,haven't they?
canHe can swim,can't he?
shouldWe should go,shouldn't we?

⚠️ When there's no auxiliary: use do / does / did

In the present simple and past simple, ordinary verbs have no auxiliary to copy. So the tag borrows do, does, or did — exactly as questions do.

  • Present simple: You live here, don't you? · She works late, doesn't she?
  • Past simple: They arrived late, didn't they? · He saw it, didn't he?

Match the tense and the person: present → do/does, past → did.

Here's the whole decision in one line: Is there a helper verb (be / a modal / have)? Copy it. No helper? Reach for do, does, or did.

It works, doesn't it? You went there, didn't you? She'll be fine, won't she? They haven't paid, have they? We should leave, shouldn't we?

Special Cases

Most tags follow the mirror rule perfectly. A few common ones don't — and native speakers use them constantly, so they're worth learning as fixed phrases.

📖 Three tags to memorise

  • I am → aren't I?  We say "I'm late, aren't I?" — never "amn't I". It looks odd on paper, but it's the natural form.
  • Imperatives → will you? / won't you?  "Close the door, will you?" "Sit down, won't you?" (softer, more polite)
  • Let's → shall we?  "Let's go for a walk, shall we?" This tag is used only after Let's.
Statement typeExampleTag
I am …I'm next,aren't I?
Imperative (request)Pass the salt,will you?
Imperative (invitation)Come in,won't you?
Let's …Let's start,shall we?

✅ A couple more you'll hear

Two small ones worth recognising: after words like nobody / everybody / someone, the tag uses they — "Somebody called, didn't they?" And "There's a problem, isn't there?" keeps there in the tag. You don't need to master these yet — just notice them when they come up.

Two Ways to Say a Tag

Here's something the written form can't show you: the same tag can mean two different things depending on your voice. This is where question tags become powerful.

📖 Falling vs rising intonation

Falling voice (↘) = "I expect you to agree." You already think it's true and just want a friendly yes. "Lovely weather, isn't it?" ↘

Rising voice (↗) = "Is this really true?" You're genuinely unsure and really asking. "You haven't seen my keys, have you?" ↗

Say both versions of "You're coming to the party, aren't you?" out loud. Drop your voice at the end and it sounds like you're sure and just being friendly. Lift your voice and it sounds like you're actually checking. Same words — different message. Most social small talk uses the falling tone.

💡 Tip: When you're making small talk and just want to connect — "Nice place, isn't it?" — let your voice fall. Rising tags can sound like a real question or even a little anxious, so save them for when you truly don't know the answer.

Keeping a Conversation Going

Question tags are one tool for keeping talk alive. There are two more that fluent speakers lean on constantly: interested responses (short reactions that say "I'm listening!") and follow-up questions.

Short "echo" and interest responses

When someone tells you something, you don't just stay silent. You react — with a short sound or phrase that shows you're engaged. Two kinds are especially useful:

📖 React and show interest

Interest phrases: Oh really? · That's interesting! · How lovely! · Oh no! · You're joking! · That sounds great.

Echo questions repeat the auxiliary to show surprise or attention: someone says "I went to Japan," and you reply "Did you?" Someone says "She's moved house," and you say "Has she?"

They say…You react with an echo…
I love cooking.Do you? / Oh really?
He can play the violin.Can he? / Wow!
We went to Rome.Did you? / How lovely!
She 's just had a baby.Has she? / That's wonderful!

Follow-up questions

After you react, keep the door open with a follow-up question — a short question that asks for more: "Oh really? Where did you go?" "Did you? What was it like?" "That sounds great — how long for?" This is the single easiest way to sound like a good conversationalist: react, then ask one more thing.

Watch all three tools working together in one exchange:

Priya: You went to the new café on Bridge Street, didn't you?

Tom: I did, yeah. We had breakfast there on Sunday.

Priya: Oh really? What was it like?

Tom: Honestly, it was fantastic. The coffee's amazing.

Priya: Is it? I love a good coffee. It's not too expensive, is it?

Tom: Not really. You should try it, shouldn't you?

Priya: I should! You'll come with me, won't you?

Tom: Of course. Let's go on Saturday, shall we?

Notice how the tags (didn't you, is it, won't you, shall we) keep pulling the other person in, while the responses (Oh really?, Is it?) and the follow-up (What was it like?) show real interest. That combination is what makes a conversation feel warm and alive.

Oh really? Did you? What was it like? That's interesting! You're joking! How lovely! Tell me more.

🎯 Your Project: A Real, Flowing Conversation

Put it all together in a short conversation. Speak out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder — or write it first and then read it aloud.

  1. (3 min) Choose a friendly, everyday topic: the weekend, the weather, a film, food, a trip.
  2. (8 min) Build a conversation of at least ten lines. Include six or more question tags — mix falling (expecting agreement) and rising (real question).
  3. (4 min) Add three or more interested responses (Oh really?, Did you?, That's interesting!) and at least two follow-up questions.
  4. (3 min) Include at least one special case: aren't I?, an imperative tag (will you? / won't you?), or Let's … shall we?
  5. (2 min) Read or perform it aloud, paying attention to your intonation. If you recorded it, listen back and check that your tags mirror the statements correctly.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Build the tag in two steps: flip the polarity, then copy the verb (or use do/does/did).
  • Match the subject pronoun in the tag (Tom → he, the girls → they).
  • Let your voice fall for friendly small talk, rise for a genuine question.
  • React before you reply — an echo or interest phrase, then a follow-up question, keeps talk flowing.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Same polarity: "She has left, has she?" ❌ → "She has left, hasn't she?" ✅ (positive → negative).
  • Wrong pronoun: "You like pizza, don't it?" ❌ → "…don't you?" ✅.
  • "amn't I": "I'm late, amn't I?" ❌ → "I'm late, aren't I?" ✅.
  • Wrong imperative tag: "Sit down, do you?" ❌ → "Sit down, won't you?" ✅.
  • Forgetting do/does/did: "You live here, live you?" ❌ → "You live here, don't you?" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A question tag mirrors the statement: positive → negative tag, negative → positive tag.
  • The tag copies the auxiliary or be; if there's none, it borrows do / does / did to match the tense.
  • Special cases: I am → aren't I?, imperatives → will you? / won't you?, Let's → shall we?
  • Intonation matters: falling voice expects agreement; rising voice is a genuine question.
  • Keep conversations alive with interested responses (Oh really?, Did you?) and follow-up questions.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've picked up one of the warmest, most human features of spoken English. Question tags and interested responses are exactly what turn a series of statements into a real conversation — and they signal to other people that you're engaged and easy to talk to. That's a genuine leap in sounding natural. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I really need tags, or can I just ask a full question?

You can always ask a full question, and it's correct — but tags do something a full question can't: they invite agreement and keep the tone friendly and light. In everyday chat, tags make you sound relaxed and connected. Learn to recognise them first; using them will come naturally as you listen more.

Why is "aren't I?" correct when "I am" isn't negative with "aren't" anywhere else?

It's simply an accident of history — English never settled on "amn't", which sounds awkward, so "aren't I?" became the standard tag for "I am". Treat it as a fixed phrase: whenever you say "I'm …," the tag is "aren't I?".

What if I answer someone's tag question — do I say yes or no?

Answer the fact, not the tag. If someone says "You're coming, aren't you?" and you are, say "Yes, I am." If someone says "You don't drive, do you?" and you don't, say "No, I don't." Focus on whether the statement is true, and your yes/no follows naturally.
