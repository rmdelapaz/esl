💬 Lesson 7.1: Agreeing, Disagreeing & Discussion

Real conversations aren't just about swapping facts — they're about sharing opinions and responding to other people's. In this listening and speaking lesson you'll learn how to give your view, agree warmly, disagree politely, ask what other people think, and soften your words so you sound thoughtful rather than blunt. These are the phrases that let you join any discussion — at work, in class, or over coffee — and hold your own.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Give an opinion clearly (In my opinion…, It seems to me that…)
  • Agree naturally and disagree politely without sounding rude
  • Ask other people for their opinions and keep a discussion going
  • Use softening words (maybe, I suppose, to be honest) to sound diplomatic

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Hold a short discussion or friendly debate on a given topic, using opinion, agreeing, and polite-disagreeing phrases.

The Language of Discussion

When people talk about a topic — whether working from home is better, whether city life beats country life, whether a film was any good — they do four things again and again: they state an opinion, they agree, they disagree, and they ask others what they think. Each of these has its own set of ready-made phrases in English, and once you have them on the tip of your tongue, you never have to search for words in a discussion again.

You may have used a few opinion phrases before — "I think…" is one everyone learns early. This lesson takes you further: it gives you a whole toolkit, and, just as importantly, it shows you how to disagree without upsetting anyone — a skill that matters as much in English as in your own language.

📖 Four moves in every discussion

  • Give an opinion — say what you think.
  • Agree — show you're on the same side.
  • Disagree politely — offer a different view, kindly.
  • Ask & respond — invite others in and keep it flowing.

🧠 Mindset

Many learners stay quiet in discussions — not because they have nothing to say, but because they're afraid of sounding rude or wrong. Here's the freeing truth: in English, how you disagree matters more than whether you disagree. A soft opener like "I see your point, but…" turns a clash into a conversation. Learn a handful of these phrases and you'll speak up far more often. Your opinion is welcome — you just need the words to frame it. 🌱

Giving Your Opinion

Instead of always starting with "I think…", spread your wings. English has a rich range of opinion openers, from casual to careful. They all do the same job — they signal "this is my view" — but they add variety and make you sound more fluent.

PhraseFeel / registerExample
I think / I believe…everyday, neutralI think working from home saves time.
In my opinion…a little more formalIn my opinion, cities are too crowded.
It seems to me that…thoughtful, carefulIt seems to me that both have advantages.
As far as I'm concerned…strong personal viewAs far as I'm concerned, health comes first.
If you ask me…casual, friendlyIf you ask me, the country is more relaxing.
Personally, I feel…warm, personalPersonally, I feel happier near nature.

📖 A tiny grammar note

After In my opinion and As far as I'm concerned, we use a comma and then a normal sentence: "In my opinion, the plan is too expensive." After It seems to me that and I think that, we add a full clause: "It seems to me that people work harder at home." The word that is optional in speech, as always.

I think… In my opinion… It seems to me that… As far as I'm concerned… If you ask me… Personally, I feel…

Agreeing

Agreeing does more than say "yes" — it shows the other person you're listening and that you value what they said. Strong, warm agreement keeps a conversation friendly and encourages the speaker to say more.

PhraseHow strong?
Exactly. / Absolutely.strong, enthusiastic
I couldn't agree more.very strong (a favourite!)
That's a good point.agreeing with respect
You're right.clear, direct
I know what you mean.warm, understanding
So do I. / So am I. / Me too.agreeing about yourself

⚠️ Careful: "I couldn't agree more" means you agree completely!

It sounds negative because of couldn't, but it's actually the strongest way to agree — "there's no way I could agree any more than I already do." Don't confuse it with "I couldn't care less" (which is dismissive). Say "I couldn't agree more" with a smile.

✅ Agreeing with "So do I" and "Neither do I"

To agree with a positive statement about yourself, use So + auxiliary + I: "I love the countryside." → "So do I." To agree with a negative one, use Neither + auxiliary + I: "I don't like long commutes." → "Neither do I." Match the auxiliary to the verb: "I'm tired." → "So am I."

Disagreeing Politely

This is the most important part of the lesson. In English, disagreeing bluntly — "No." / "You're wrong." / "That's not true." — can sound aggressive, even if you don't mean it to. Fluent speakers almost always soften a disagreement: they acknowledge the other view first, then gently offer their own.

📖 The polite-disagreement pattern

1. Acknowledge → 2. Signal a "but" → 3. Give your view.

"I see your point, but I think the cost is too high."

That little bit of acknowledgement at the start makes all the difference.

Polite openerWhy it works
I see your point, but…respects their view first
I'm not so sure (about that).gentle, leaves room to talk
I'm afraid I disagree.I'm afraid = a polite cushion
Actually, I think…softly introduces a different view
Yes, but don't you think…?turns it into a question
That may be true, however…concedes, then counters

⚠️ "Actually" is your friend

Actually softens a correction or a different opinion beautifully: "Actually, I find the city quite relaxing." It signals "here comes a small surprise" in a gentle way — much kinder than a flat "No." Note it does not mean "now / currently" (that's a false friend in several languages).

Golden rule: Attack the idea, never the person. Say "I'm not sure that plan will work," not "You're wrong." Keeping it about the topic keeps the conversation warm — and keeps people listening to you.

Asking for Opinions & Softening

A good discussion is a two-way street. Fluent speakers don't just talk — they invite others in. And they soften what they say so nothing sounds too final or too forceful.

Asking what others think

What do you think? How do you feel about…? What's your view on…? Do you agree? Wouldn't you say…?

Softening / hedging words

These little words make you sound thoughtful and open, not bossy. They admit that you might be wrong — which, oddly, makes people trust your opinion more.

HedgeExample
maybe / perhapsMaybe it depends on the person.
I supposeI suppose you could be right.
to be honestTo be honest, I'm not a fan of it.
kind of / sort ofIt's kind of stressful, isn't it?
a bitI find it a bit boring, personally.

Listen: A Real Discussion

Here three friends discuss a topic many people argue about: working from home versus working in an office. Read it through once, then use it as a listening text. Watch how they give opinions, agree, disagree politely, and pull each other into the conversation.

Nadia: So, now that our company lets us choose — do you two prefer working from home or going into the office? Personally, I feel much happier at home.

Tom: Really? I'm not so sure. I mean, I see your point about saving time on the commute, but I miss talking to people. To be honest, I get a bit lonely at home.

Nadia: That's a good point. But don't you think you concentrate better without all the noise?

Tom: Maybe. It depends on the day, I suppose.

Priya: As far as I'm concerned, the best answer is a mix — a few days at home, a few in the office. That way you get the quiet and the company.

Nadia: Exactly! I couldn't agree more. A mix keeps everyone happy.

Tom: Actually, that does sound sensible. I'd be happy with two or three days at home.

Priya: So would I. What do you think the boss will say, though?

Nadia: Well, if we all agree, I think she'll listen. It seems to me that a happy team works harder anyway.

2. Why isn't Tom keen on working from home?

Strong agreement: "I couldn't agree more" (also "Exactly!"). Polite disagreement: "I'm not so sure" and "I see your point, but…".

🎯 Your Project: Hold a Discussion or Debate

Time to use the language for real. Do this with a partner or small group if you can; on your own, play both sides out loud or record yourself.

  1. (3 min) Choose a topic with two clear sides — for example: "Is it better to live in the city or the countryside?" or "Is working from home a good thing?"
  2. (5 min) Plan two or three opinions for each side, each with a reason.
  3. (8 min) Hold the discussion. Every person must: give at least one opinion, agree with someone at least once, and disagree politely at least once.
  4. (2 min) Use at least one softening word (maybe, I suppose, to be honest) and one question that asks for an opinion.
  5. (2 min) Try to reach a shared conclusion — or agree to disagree, politely!

Role-play starter: Person A: "In my opinion, city life is more exciting. What do you think?" Person B: "I see your point, but to be honest, I find the city a bit stressful…"

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I gave at least one clear opinion with a reason
  • ☐ I agreed with someone using a natural phrase
  • ☐ I disagreed politely (acknowledged first, then countered)
  • ☐ I asked at least one person for their opinion
  • ☐ I used a softening word to sound diplomatic
  • ☐ I kept it about the ideas, not the person

🎯 Quick Quiz

Question 1: Your colleague says, "We should cancel the trip." You disagree, but want to be polite. Which is best?

Question 2: Your friend says, "I love long walks." You love them too. What's the natural reply?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Always acknowledge first before you disagree: "I see your point, but…".
  • Vary your opinion openers — don't start every sentence with "I think".
  • Use softening words (maybe, I suppose, to be honest) to sound open-minded.
  • Ask other people what they think — it keeps the discussion alive and warm.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Bluntness: "No, that's wrong" ❌ → "I'm not so sure about that" ✅ — the meaning is similar, the tone is friendlier.
  • "I'm agree": "I'm agree" ❌ → "I agree" ✅ (agree is a verb, not an adjective — no am/is/are).
  • Confusing "actually": it means "in fact / surprisingly," not "now/currently."
  • Wrong auxiliary with So/Neither: "I like it." → "So do I" ✅ (not "So am I").
  • Attacking the person: "You always say that" ❌ → keep it about the idea, not the speaker.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Give opinions with variety: In my opinion…, It seems to me that…, As far as I'm concerned…, If you ask me….
  • Agree warmly: Exactly, That's a good point, I couldn't agree more, So do I / Neither do I.
  • Disagree politely with the pattern acknowledge → but → your view: I see your point, but…, I'm not so sure, I'm afraid I disagree, Actually….
  • Ask & soften: What do you think? / How do you feel about…? plus maybe, I suppose, to be honest.
  • Attack the idea, never the person — and remember agree is a verb (no "I'm agree").

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now step into any discussion and hold your own — sharing what you think, backing a friend, pushing back kindly, and pulling other people into the conversation. This is exactly the language that makes group talk feel comfortable instead of scary, and it opens the whole listening-and-speaking strand of this module. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Isn't it easier to just say "yes" and "no" instead of all these phrases?

You can, but plain "yes/no" answers sound flat and can end a conversation quickly. Phrases like "That's a good point" or "I'm not so sure" invite more talk and make you sound engaged and fluent. Learn just three or four to start and add more over time.

Why do I have to be so polite when I disagree? In my language we're more direct.

Directness varies a lot between cultures, and that's fine. But in English — especially at work or with people you don't know well — softening a disagreement is expected, and it makes people more willing to listen to you. It's a small habit that pays off hugely.

What's the difference between "I couldn't agree more" and "I couldn't care less"?

They look similar but mean opposite things. "I couldn't agree more" = I completely agree (warm, positive). "I couldn't care less" = I'm not interested at all (dismissive). Use the first freely; save the second for when you really mean it.
