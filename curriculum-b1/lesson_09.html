🔗 Lesson 3.3: Relative Clauses

Relative clauses are how you join two ideas into one smooth sentence — they let you describe which person or thing you mean, or add an extra detail without starting a new sentence. Mastering them is a huge step: your English stops sounding like a list of short sentences and starts to flow.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Choose the right relative pronoun — who, which, that, where, when, whose
  • Build defining relative clauses that identify exactly which person or thing you mean
  • Build non-defining relative clauses that add extra information
  • Punctuate non-defining clauses correctly with commas, and know when the pronoun can be dropped

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe six or more people, places, and things in your life using relative clauses — "My best friend is someone who…", "The town where I grew up…".

Joining Ideas Together

Imagine you say two short sentences: "I have a friend. She lives in Canada." A beginner leaves them apart. An independent speaker joins them: "I have a friend who lives in Canada." That little group of words — who lives in Canada — is a relative clause. It gives extra information about a noun (here, friend).

Every relative clause starts with a relative pronoun (who, which, that, where, when, whose) and describes the noun that comes just before it. There are two types, and knowing the difference between them is the heart of this lesson:

  • Defining clauses tell you which one — essential information, no commas.
  • Non-defining clauses add an extra detail — non-essential, wrapped in commas.

🧠 Mindset

If long, connected sentences feel out of reach right now, that's completely normal — you're building a new gear. Every time you turn two choppy sentences into one flowing one, you're literally rewiring how you think in English. Don't aim for perfect punctuation on day one; aim to join ideas. The commas will settle into place with practice. 🌱

The Relative Pronouns

Each relative pronoun matches a certain kind of noun. Choose it based on what you are describing.

PronounUsed forExample
whopeopleThe teacher who helped me was very kind.
whichthings / animalsThe phone which I bought is broken.
thatpeople or things (defining only)The book that won the prize is here.
whereplacesThis is the café where we met.
whentimesI remember the day when we moved house.
whosepossession (of a person/thing)That's the man whose dog barks all night.

📖 Quick note on that

that is the flexible one: in defining clauses it can replace who (for people) or which (for things). "The man who called" = "The man that called." But — as you'll see — that is not allowed in non-defining clauses.

Defining Clauses — which one?

A defining relative clause gives essential information. It tells you exactly which person or thing you mean. If you removed it, the sentence would lose its meaning — you wouldn't know who or what was being talked about.

"The man who lives next door is a doctor."

Which man? The one who lives next door. Remove the clause and you're left with "The man… is a doctor" — which man? The information is essential, so there are no commas.

SentencePronounWhy
The woman who / that called you is my aunt.who (people)identifies which woman
The keys which / that open the garage are lost.which (things)identifies which keys
She's the only person who / that understands me.who (people)identifies which person
This is the restaurant that / which I recommended.which (things)identifies which restaurant

✅ Key features of defining clauses

  • The information is essential — it identifies the noun.
  • No commas.
  • that can replace who or which.

Non-Defining Clauses — extra information

A non-defining relative clause adds extra, non-essential information. We already know which person or thing we mean; the clause just tells us something more. Because it's extra, we set it off with commas — and if you removed it, the sentence would still make complete sense.

"My brother, who lives in Canada, is a teacher."

We already know which person — my brother. "…who lives in Canada" is a bonus fact. Remove it and "My brother is a teacher" still works perfectly. The extra fact sits between two commas.

SentenceExtra information about…
Paris, which is the capital of France, is beautiful.Paris (we know which city already)
My mother, who is a nurse, works nights.my mother
Our new house, which we bought last year, has a garden.our new house
Mr Silva, whose son is my classmate, teaches maths.Mr Silva

⚠️ Two rules you must not break

  • In non-defining clauses you cannot use that — use who for people, which for things. ❌ "My car, that is red, is old." → ✅ "My car, which is red, is old."
  • You cannot omit the pronoun in a non-defining clause — the pronoun always stays.

The Two Side by Side

This is the distinction to burn into your memory. The same words can be a defining or a non-defining clause — and the commas change the meaning.

💡 The core contrast

DefiningNon-defining
Jobidentifies which one (essential)adds extra info (non-essential)
Commas?No commasYes — commas around it
Can use that?Yes (who/which → that)No — only who / which
Can drop pronoun?Sometimes (see next section)Never
Remove the clause?Meaning is lostSentence still works

Look how the commas change the message:

Defining: "My sister who lives in Rome is a lawyer."
→ I have more than one sister. This tells you which one — the Rome one.

Non-defining: "My sister, who lives in Rome, is a lawyer."
→ I have only one sister. The Rome fact is just extra.

Dropping the Pronoun

Here's a piece of natural English that surprises many learners: in defining clauses, you can often leave out the relative pronoun. The trick is knowing when.

💡 Subject vs object — the rule

Ask: after the pronoun, is there already a subject (a new person/thing doing the verb)?

  • Object pronoun → can drop it. "The film (that) I watched was great." (After that comes a new subject, I — so that is optional.)
  • Subject pronoun → must keep it. "The man who called was rude." (Who is the subject — the man did the calling — so you cannot drop it.) ❌ "The man called was rude."
SentencePronoun roleCan you drop it?
The book (that) I read…object (I read it)✅ Yes
The people (who) we met…object (we met them)✅ Yes
The woman who lives here…subject (she lives)❌ No
The bus that goes to town…subject (it goes)❌ No
⚠️ Remember: you can only drop the pronoun in defining clauses, and only when it's the object. In non-defining clauses the pronoun always stays.

Where, When & Whose

Three more pronouns unlock places, times, and possession.

whose — possession

whose replaces his / her / its / their. It shows that something belongs to the noun.

"That's the woman whose car was stolen." (= her car was stolen)

"I know a boy whose father is a pilot." (= his father is a pilot)

where vs "which… in"

where describes a place. It often means the same as which… in / at / to, but it's shorter and more natural.

With whereLonger version
The house where I live is old.The house which I live in is old.
This is the town where I was born.This is the town which I was born in.

Sentence starters & phrase bank

Keep these ready — they're the scaffolding for the project.

…someone who… …a person who always… …the place where… …a thing which… …the day when… …the friend whose… …which is why…

Here's a short descriptive model that stitches several clauses together:

"My grandmother, who is eighty-two, still lives in the village where she was born. The house, which has a big garden, is the place where I spent every summer. She's the person whose stories I remember best — and the one who first taught me to cook."

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose the right relative pronoun

Objective: Fill each gap with who, which, where, when, or whose.

  1. That's the teacher gave us so much homework.
  2. This is the restaurant we had our first date.
  3. I met a woman brother is a famous actor.
  4. The laptop I bought last week is already broken.
  5. I'll never forget the summer we drove across the country.
✅ Answer

1. who (person, subject) · 2. where (place) · 3. whose (possession) · 4. which (thing; or "that", or dropped) · 5. when (time)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Combine into one sentence

Objective: Join each pair using a relative clause.

  1. I have a friend. She speaks five languages.
  2. This is the book. It changed my life.
  3. We visited a village. My mother grew up there.
  4. Tom is a colleague. His wife is a doctor. (use commas)
✅ Answer

1. I have a friend who speaks five languages.
2. This is the book which/that changed my life.
3. We visited a village where my mother grew up.
4. Tom, whose wife is a doctor, is a colleague. (non-defining → commas, no "that")

🏋️ Exercise 3: Commas or not? Can the pronoun be omitted?

Objective: Decide if each sentence is defining or non-defining. Add commas where needed, and mark whether the pronoun could be dropped (✂️) or must stay.

  1. My car which is very old broke down again.
  2. The people who we met on holiday were lovely.
  3. The girl who won the race trains every day.
  4. Venice which is full of canals is sinking slowly.
✅ Answer

1. Non-defining (I have one car): "My car, which is very old, broke down again." Pronoun must stay.
2. Defining (which people? the ones we met): no commas. who is the object → ✂️ can be dropped: "The people we met…"
3. Defining: no commas. who is the subject (she won) → must stay.
4. Non-defining (we know which city): "Venice, which is full of canals, is sinking slowly." Pronoun must stay.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Ask "Is this information essential?" first. Essential → defining (no commas). Extra → non-defining (commas).
  • Use where for places, when for times, whose for possession — they make you sound natural.
  • In everyday speech, drop the object pronoun in defining clauses: "the film I saw", "the guy I met" — it's fluent and correct.
  • Pause slightly at the commas of a non-defining clause when you speak — it signals "extra info coming".

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Using that in non-defining clauses: "London, that is huge…" ❌ → "London, which is huge…" ✅.
  • Dropping a subject pronoun: "The man called me was angry" ❌ → "The man who called me was angry" ✅.
  • Forgetting the second comma: "My sister, who is a nurse works nights" ❌ → "My sister, who is a nurse, works nights" ✅.
  • whose vs who's: whose = possession; who's = "who is". "The man whose dog…" ✅ (not "who's").
  • Double subject: "The book which it changed my life" ❌ → "The book which changed my life" ✅ (the pronoun is the subject).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Relative pronouns: who (people), which (things), that (either, defining only), where (places), when (times), whose (possession).
  • Defining clauses give essential info (which one) — no commas, and that is allowed.
  • Non-defining clauses add extra info — commas around them, no that, and the pronoun can't be dropped.
  • In defining clauses you can drop the pronoun only when it's the object ("the film I saw"), never when it's the subject.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now join ideas the way fluent speakers do — identifying exactly which person or thing you mean, or slipping in an extra fact without breaking your flow. Those long, natural sentences are a real marker of an independent B1 speaker, and now they're in your toolkit. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When should I use which and when that?

In a defining clause about a thing, both work: "the book which/that I read." In a non-defining clause you must use which: "This book, which I loved, is old." Many writers prefer that for defining and reserve which for non-defining — a tidy habit to copy.

How do I know if information is "essential" or "extra"?

Try removing the clause. If the sentence still makes sense and you still know who/what is meant, it's extra → non-defining (commas). If removing it leaves you asking "which one?", it's essential → defining (no commas). Proper names (Paris, my mother) usually take extra info, because we already know which one.

Is dropping the pronoun informal? Can I write it too?

It's completely standard in both speech and writing — "the house we bought", "the man she married" are perfectly correct English. Just remember it only works in defining clauses when the pronoun is the object.
