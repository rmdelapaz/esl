📖 Lesson 5.2: Narrative Tenses — Telling a Story

A good story isn't just a list of things that happened. It has main events that move it forward, a background that sets the scene, and moments where we step back to explain what had happened earlier. In English, three past tenses work together to do exactly that — and once you can weave them, your stories will sound natural, vivid, and easy to follow.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the past simple for the main events of a story, in order
  • Use the past continuous for the background and actions in progress
  • Use the past perfect for what had happened before the story
  • Link everything with storytelling connectors like first, then, suddenly, while, and in the end

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–60 minutes

🎯 Project: Tell a true story from your own life (an adventure, a mishap, a memorable day) using all three narrative tenses and clear connectors.

Three Tenses, One Story

You've already met two of these tenses at A2, and one in your last lesson. Now the goal is to make them cooperate. Real stories aren't told in a single tense — they layer three of them together, each doing a different job:

📖 The narrative team

  • Past simple — the main events, one after another (I woke up, I left, I saw…)
  • Past continuous — the background, scenes in progress (the sun was shining, people were talking…)
  • Past perfect — what had happened before the story began (I had forgotten my keys…)

You met the past continuous and past simple together back at A2 ("I was cooking when the phone rang"). This lesson brings them together with the past perfect you learned last time — so that you can tell a full, rich story, not just a single moment.

🧠 Mindset

Don't think of this as "three separate tenses to juggle." Think of it as a camera. The past simple is the action shots that push the story on. The past continuous is the wide background shot behind them. The past perfect is a quick flashback. You already know how to tell a story in your own language — you're just learning which tense the camera reaches for. 🌱

What Each Tense Does

Let's look at each tense's job, its form, and the exact moment you'd use it. Keep this table close — it's the heart of the whole lesson.

TenseFormIts job in a storyExample
Past simpleverb + -ed / irregularthe main events, in orderShe opened the door and walked in.
Past continuouswas / were + -ingbackground & actions in progressIt was raining and the wind was blowing.
Past perfecthad + past participlewhat had happened earlierShe had left her umbrella at home.

💡 The classic combination: while and when

A long background action (past continuous) is often interrupted by a sudden main event (past simple):

  • I was walking home when I saw an old friend. (walking = background, saw = sudden event)
  • While we were eating, the lights went out.

Use while before the long action (was -ing) and when before the short one (past simple).

Here's how the three fit onto one timeline — the past perfect deepest in the past, the continuous stretching across the background, the simple marking the main beats:

graph LR
    A["past perfect / had happened earlier"] --> B["past continuous / was happening in background"] --> C["past simple / main events in order"] --> D["now"]

✅ A simple test for each tense

As you build a story, ask:

  • "Is this a main step forward?" → past simple
  • "Was this already going on around it?" → past continuous
  • "Did this happen before the story started?" → past perfect

A Model Narrative

Here's a short story with the three tenses labelled so you can see the pattern. Read it slowly and notice how they take turns.

It was a cold winter evening, and snow was falling gently outside (past continuous — background). Maria sat by the window with a cup of tea (past simple — main event). She had had a long, difficult day at work (past perfect — earlier), and all she wanted was to relax. While she was reading her book (past continuous — in progress), the doorbell suddenly rang (past simple — interruption). She opened the door and found a small parcel on the step (past simple — main events). Someone had left it there while she wasn't looking (past perfect + past continuous). In the end, it turned out to be a gift from an old friend.

📖 See the layers

  • Background (continuous): was falling, was reading, wasn't looking — the scene around the action.
  • Main events (simple): sat, rang, opened, found, turned out — the story's steps.
  • Earlier (perfect): had had, had left — things finished before that moment.
Notice the balance: the past simple carries most of the story forward, the past continuous paints the scene, and the past perfect appears only twice — for the two things that had happened before. That's a natural mix: don't overuse the past perfect; use it only when you truly step back.

Storytelling Connectors

Tenses show time; connectors show sequence and drama. Together they make a story flow. Sprinkle these words through your narrative and it will instantly sound more organised and alive.

ConnectorWhat it signalsExample
firstthe opening eventFirst, I packed my bags.
then / after thatthe next stepThen we drove to the airport.
whiletwo things at onceWhile I was waiting, I read a book.
suddenlya surprise eventSuddenly, the alarm went off.
by the timepoints to an earlier actionBy the time we arrived, it had closed.
in the endthe final resultIn the end, everything was fine.
First, … Then, … After that, … While I was …, suddenly … By the time we got there, … A few minutes later, … In the end, it all worked out.

💡 Match the connector to the tense

Certain connectors naturally partner with certain tenses:

  • while → past continuous (while I was cooking)
  • suddenly / then / first → past simple (suddenly the phone rang)
  • by the time → past perfect (by the time we arrived, it had ended)

A Story in Action

Now hear someone tell a real story out loud. Notice how the three tenses and the connectors work together — and how natural it sounds when they do.

A: You'll never believe what happened to me yesterday.

B: Go on — tell me!

A: So, I was walking to the station, and it was raining really hard. I'd forgotten my umbrella at home, of course.

B: Typical! What did you do?

A: Well, while I was standing under a tree, waiting for the rain to stop, a car suddenly pulled up next to me. It was my old teacher! I hadn't seen her for years.

B: No way! What a coincidence.

A: Exactly. She offered me a lift, so I got in. We talked the whole way, and in the end she drove me right to my door.

B: That's such a lovely story.

The three tenses at work: background — was walking, was raining, was standing (past continuous); main events — pulled up, offered, got in, talked, drove (past simple); earlier — had forgotten, hadn't seen (past perfect). And the connectors — so, while, suddenly, in the end — carry us smoothly from one moment to the next.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Order and build a short story

Objective: Below are five events out of order. Put them in a logical order (1–5), then write them as a short story using the three narrative tenses and at least two connectors.

  • we finally found the cinema
  • we decided to see a film
  • the film had already started

✅ Answer

(Your wording will differ — check you used past simple for main events, past continuous for the background, and past perfect for the earlier action.)

🎯 Your Project: Tell Your Own True Story

Turn a real memory into a well-told story. Speak it out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder — or write it, then read it aloud.

  1. (4 min) Pick a memorable true story from your life: a small adventure, a funny mishap, a surprise, an important day.
  2. (6 min) Set the scene with the past continuous (weather, place, what was going on around you), then tell the main events in the past simple.
  3. (4 min) Add at least two past perfect sentences for things that had happened before the story ("I had never…", "I had just…").
  4. (3 min) Weave in at least four connectors: first, then, while, suddenly, by the time, in the end.
  5. (3 min) Tell it aloud from start to finish. If you recorded it, listen back and check each tense is doing its proper job.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I set the scene with the past continuous (background)
  • ☐ I told the main events in the past simple, in order
  • ☐ I used two or more past perfect sentences for earlier events
  • ☐ I used at least four storytelling connectors
  • ☐ I used while + past continuous for two things at once
  • ☐ I ended with in the end and a clear result

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Let the past simple carry the story forward — it should be your most common tense in a narrative.
  • Use the past continuous to paint the background and for a long action that gets interrupted.
  • Save the past perfect for the few moments you step back to something earlier — don't overuse it.
  • Add connectors (first, then, while, suddenly, in the end) so your listener can follow the sequence.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Continuous for a quick event: "Suddenly the phone was ringing" ❌ → a sudden event is past simple: "the phone rang" ✅.
  • Past perfect for everything: telling the whole story in had + participle ❌ → use it only for what happened before the main events.
  • while + past simple: "While I walked…" ❌ → while loves the continuous: "While I was walking…" ✅.
  • No connectors: a flat list of past-simple sentences sounds robotic → add then, suddenly, in the end to give it shape.
  • Mixing up which came first: if two things clearly happened one after the other, two past simples are enough — you don't need the past perfect.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Past simple tells the main events of a story, in order.
  • Past continuous (was/were + -ing) sets the background and shows actions in progress — often interrupted by a past-simple event.
  • Past perfect (had + participle) reaches back to what had happened earlier — use it sparingly.
  • while pairs with the continuous; when / suddenly / then pair with the simple; by the time pairs with the perfect.
  • Connectors (first, then, after that, suddenly, in the end) give your story shape and flow.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now tell a proper story in English — not a flat list of events, but a living narrative with a scene, a sequence of actions, and moments that step back to explain what came before. This is one of the most satisfying and social skills in a language, and you've just unlocked it. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know when to use the past continuous instead of the past simple?

Ask whether the action was in progress around another event, or was the event itself. Long, background, "was happening" actions take the continuous (I was cooking); short, completed steps that move the story on take the simple (the phone rang). They very often appear together: a continuous background interrupted by a simple event.

Do I have to use the past perfect in every story?

Not at all. Many good stories use only the past simple and past continuous. Add the past perfect only when you genuinely need to jump back to something that had happened earlier — a cause, a first-time experience, a reason. Used too often, it makes a story heavy; used well, it adds depth.

What's the difference between "when" and "while"?

While introduces a longer action in progress and takes the past continuous: "While I was reading…". When often introduces a shorter, completed event in the past simple: "…when the doorbell rang." A classic story sentence puts them together: "While I was reading, the doorbell rang."
