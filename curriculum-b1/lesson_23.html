🧰 Lesson 8.2: Phrasal Verbs — A Practical Toolkit

If you've ever wondered why native speakers say "get up," "set off," "sort it out," and "look after the kids" instead of simpler-looking words, you've met phrasal verbs. They are everywhere in real English — in conversation, texts, and TV. In this lesson you'll stop being surprised by them and start using them yourself, with a clear system and a friendly starter set.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what a phrasal verb is and why they matter in everyday English
  • Tell the difference between literal and idiomatic meanings
  • Use the separable vs inseparable rule — and place pronoun objects correctly
  • Use a topic-grouped set of common phrasal verbs in real situations

What Is a Phrasal Verb?

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a little word called a particle (a preposition or adverb like up, off, on, out, after). Together, they act as a single verb with its own meaning.

📖 The shape

verb + particle = one meaning

get + up = get up (leave your bed) · turn + on = turn on (start a machine) · look + after = look after (take care of)

Why do they matter? Because English speakers use phrasal verbs far more than their formal one-word cousins in everyday speech. We say "I'll find out" more often than "I'll discover," and "the plane took off" rather than "the plane departed." If you want to sound natural — and understand real conversation, films and songs — you need them.

🧠 Mindset

Phrasal verbs have a bad reputation: learners hear there are "thousands" of them and feel overwhelmed. Please don't. Nobody learns them from a giant list. You'll meet the common ones again and again in real life, and each time you notice one, it settles a little deeper. Aim to recognise plenty and use a solid few well. Little and often, always in context — that's the whole secret. 🌱

Literal vs Idiomatic

Some phrasal verbs mean exactly what the two words suggest. Others have a meaning you can't guess from the parts. Knowing which is which helps you relax about the tricky ones.

Phrasal verbTypeMeaning
sit downliteralmove your body downwards — easy to guess
pick up the boxliterallift it up
give upidiomaticstop trying — nothing to do with "give"
look afteridiomatictake care of — not about "looking"
find outidiomaticdiscover — not about "finding" outside

✅ Good news about literal ones

Literal phrasal verbs (sit down, stand up, come in, go out) barely need "learning" — the meaning is right there. Save your energy for the idiomatic ones, and learn those the way you'd learn any new word: in a whole sentence you can picture and reuse.

Separable vs Inseparable

This is the one grammar point that really matters with phrasal verbs. Some can be split by their object; some can't. Getting this right is what makes your phrasal verbs sound correct.

📖 The two types

Separable: the object can go after the whole verb or between the two parts. Both are fine:

Turn on the light. = Turn the light on.

Inseparable: the two parts must stay together — the object always comes after:

I'll look after the baby. ✅   I'll look the baby after. ❌

TypeExampleCan you split it?
Separableturn on the light / turn the light onYes
Separablesort out the problem / sort the problem outYes
Inseparablelook after the kidsNo
Inseparableget on the busNo
💡 How do I know which is which? There's no visible rule you can see from the words — it's a property of each verb, so learn it with the verb. Good news: with a full noun object, separable verbs work both ways, so you can't go far wrong. The one place it really counts is with pronouns — that's next.

Where the Pronoun Goes

Here's the single most useful rule in the whole lesson. When the object is a pronoun (it, him, her, them, us), a separable phrasal verb must split — the pronoun goes in the middle.

⚠️ Pronoun objects go in the middle

With a separable verb and a pronoun:

✅ Turn it on.   ❌ Turn on it.

✅ Sort it out.   ❌ Sort out it.

✅ Give it up.   ❌ Give up it.

The pronoun is short and "light," so English tucks it inside the verb. Never leave a pronoun stranded at the end of a separable phrasal verb.

Picture the choice for a separable verb like turn on:

graph TD
    A["turn on + object"] --> B["noun object: either order works"]
    A --> C["pronoun object: must go in the middle"]
    B --> D["turn on the light / turn the light on"]
    C --> E["turn it on / not turn on it"]

✅ The safe habit

Whenever you're about to say it, them, him, or her with a separable phrasal verb, slot the pronoun between the verb and the particle: pick it up, throw them away, switch it off, work it out. Say those aloud a few times and the rhythm will feel right forever.

Your Topic Toolkit

Instead of a scary alphabetical list, here's a small, useful set grouped by topic. Learning them in themed clusters helps them stick — and these are ones you'll genuinely use.

🌅 Daily life

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
wake upstop sleepingI wake up at seven.
get upleave your bedI get up ten minutes later.
turn on / offstart / stop a machineTurn the kettle on.

✈️ Travel

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
set offbegin a journeyWe set off at dawn.
check inregister at a hotel / airportWe checked in at noon.
get on / offboard / leave a bus, train, planeGet on the train at platform two.

💬 Social life

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
get on withhave a good relationship withI get on well with my neighbours.
look forward tofeel happy about a future eventI look forward to the weekend.
look aftertake care ofShe looks after her little brother.

🛠️ Dealing with problems

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
sort outsolve / organiseI'll sort it out today.
deal withhandle / manageShe dealt with the complaint calmly.
give upstop trying / quit a habitDon't give up! He gave up smoking.
find outdiscover / learnI need to find out the price.

📖 Notice the patterns

  • look after, get on with, look forward to, deal with are inseparable — keep them whole.
  • turn on/off, sort out, give up, find out are separable — split them with pronouns (sort it out, give it up).
  • look forward to is followed by an -ing verb: "I look forward to seeing you."
I usually get up at seven. What time do we set off? I get on well with my colleagues. Don't worry, I'll sort it out. I'm looking forward to the trip.

Learn Them in Context

The best way to make phrasal verbs stick is to meet them inside real sentences — not on flashcards on their own. Read this everyday conversation and count how many phrasal verbs slip by naturally:

Nadia: Morning! You look tired. Did you not sleep well?

Leo: I woke up at five and couldn't get back to sleep. Then I had to set off early for the airport.

Nadia: Oh no. Did you check in okay?

Leo: Eventually. The machine wouldn't turn on, so a staff member had to sort it out.

Nadia: Typical! And who's looking after the dog while you're away?

Leo: My neighbour. I get on really well with her, luckily. I'm looking forward to a proper rest, though.

Nadia: You deserve it. If anything comes up, just call and I'll deal with it.

Leo: Thanks. I'll find out the hotel's number and send it to you.

Ten phrasal verbs in one short chat — woke up, get back, set off, check in, turn on, sort out, look after, get on with, look forward to, come up, deal with, find out — and none of them felt forced. That's how common they are, and that's the level of comfort you're aiming for.

✅ A simple habit that works

When you meet a new phrasal verb, write down the whole sentence, not just the verb. Add a note: is it separable? What preposition or -ing form follows it? One sentence you can picture beats ten isolated words you'll forget.

🎯 Your Project: A Day in My Life

Turn your own routine into a short, natural account rich in phrasal verbs. Speak out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder — or write it first and then read it aloud.

  1. (4 min) Jot down the shape of a real or typical day: waking, travelling, work or study, people, an evening plan.
  2. (8 min) Write it as a short paragraph (about eight to twelve sentences), using ten or more phrasal verbs from at least three topic groups (daily life, travel, social, problems).
  3. (3 min) Include at least two separable verbs with a pronoun in the middle (turn it off, sort it out) and at least two inseparable verbs (look after, get on with).
  4. (2 min) Underline each phrasal verb and mark it S (separable) or I (inseparable).
  5. (2 min) Read it aloud. If you recorded it, listen back and check that no pronoun is stranded at the end of a separable verb.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Learn each phrasal verb in a full sentence you can picture and reuse.
  • Note whether it's separable or inseparable as you learn it — that's the detail that matters.
  • With separable verbs, always put a pronoun in the middle (pick it up).
  • Group new verbs by topic — travel, daily life, problems — so they support each other.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Pronoun at the end: "turn on it" ❌ → "turn it on" ✅.
  • Splitting an inseparable verb: "look the baby after" ❌ → "look after the baby" ✅.
  • Wrong particle: "get up the bus" ❌ → "get on the bus" ✅.
  • Forgetting the -ing: "I look forward to see you" ❌ → "I look forward to seeing you" ✅.
  • Trying to memorise a huge list: learn a handful well, in context, rather than a hundred badly.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A phrasal verb = verb + particle, acting as one verb; they're extremely common in everyday English.
  • Meanings can be literal (sit down) or idiomatic (give up, find out) — learn the idiomatic ones like new words.
  • Separable verbs can split (turn the light on); inseparable ones can't (look after the kids).
  • A pronoun object always goes in the middle of a separable verb: turn it on, never turn on it.
  • Learn them in context and by topic, not as a giant list — a solid few, used well, beats a hundred half-known.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How can I tell if a phrasal verb is separable or inseparable?

You usually can't tell just by looking — it's a fixed property of each verb, so learn it together with the verb. A helpful clue: many verbs with after, for, with, to (like look after, wait for, deal with, look forward to) are inseparable. When in doubt, keep the verb whole with a noun, and remember the pronoun-in-the-middle rule only applies to the separable ones.

There seem to be so many. How many should I actually learn?

Focus on the common ones you keep meeting — the set in this lesson is a great start. You'll naturally pick up more from conversation, films, and reading. Recognising phrasal verbs comes first; confident use follows with exposure. Quality and context beat quantity every time.

Can the same phrasal verb have more than one meaning?

Yes, and that's normal — take off can mean a plane departing or removing clothes ("take off your coat"). Don't panic: the context makes the meaning clear, and you'll learn the extra meanings as you meet them. Learning in whole sentences helps you keep the meanings apart.
