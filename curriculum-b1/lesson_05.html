💭 Lesson 2.2: Second Conditional — Imagining

What would you do if you won the lottery? If you could live anywhere in the world? These "what if" questions are the heart of the second conditional — the structure English uses to step out of reality and into the imaginary. In the last lesson you handled real conditions; now you'll learn to talk about the situations that aren't true, but are wonderful to imagine.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the second conditional: If + past simple, would + base verb
  • Use it for imaginary, unlikely, or impossible situations in the present or future
  • Contrast the first conditional (real/possible) with the second (unreal/imaginary)
  • Give natural advice with the fixed phrase "If I were you, I'd…"

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Project: Answer a set of imaginative "What would you do if…?" questions with six or more second-conditional sentences — win the lottery, meet a hero, live anywhere.

Stepping Into the Imaginary

In Lesson 2.1 you practised the zero and first conditionals — the ones for facts and for real, likely situations ("If it rains, I'll take an umbrella"). But so much of what we love to talk about isn't real at all: daydreams, wishes, impossible worlds, and advice that begins "If I were you…". For all of that, English has a special structure — the second conditional.

The clever, slightly surprising part is this: to talk about the unreal present or future, we use a past-tense verb. When you say "If I won the lottery…", you're not talking about the past — you're signalling "this isn't real." That small trick is the whole secret of the lesson.

🧠 Mindset

Learning the second conditional isn't just grammar — it's learning to play in English. Imagining out loud, joking, dreaming, giving warm advice: these are the things that make a language feel like yours. If the "past tense for the present" idea feels strange at first, that's completely normal. Say the sentences out loud a few times and your ear will start to accept the pattern long before your brain fully explains it. Trust the practice. 🌱

The Form

The second conditional has two clauses, just like the first — but the verbs change. The if-clause uses the past simple, and the result clause uses would (or wouldn't) + the base verb.

📖 The pattern

If + past simple, ... would / wouldn't + base verb

"If I had more time, I would travel more."

"If I won the lottery, I'd buy a house."

If-clause (condition: past simple)Result clause (would + base verb)
If I had more money,I would travel around the world.
If she knew the answer,she would tell you.
If we lived by the sea,we would swim every day.
If they didn't work so much,they wouldn't be so tired.
If animals could talk,what would they say?
💡 Key point: The past-simple verb (had, won, lived, knew) does not mean past time here. It signals that the situation is unreal or imaginary. "If I had a dog" = I don't have a dog now, but I'm imagining it.

You can also put the result first — the meaning is identical. When the if-clause comes second, you don't need a comma:

If I had more time, I would learn the guitar.

I would learn the guitar if I had more time. (same meaning, no comma)

✅ Contractions

In speech and informal writing, would almost always shrinks to 'd: I'd, you'd, he'd, she'd, we'd, they'd. So "I would buy a boat" becomes "I'd buy a boat." The negative is wouldn't. Using contractions will make your speaking sound far more natural.

When We Use It

Use the second conditional to talk about the present or future when the situation is one of these:

Type of situationExampleWhy second conditional?
ImaginaryIf I were a bird, I would fly south for the winter.I'm not a bird — pure imagination.
UnlikelyIf I won the lottery, I'd quit my job.Possible, but very improbable.
ImpossibleIf animals could talk, life would be strange.It simply can't happen.
Contrary to fact (now)If I were rich, I wouldn't worry about money.I'm not rich — the opposite of reality.

Notice the feeling behind it: the speaker is stepping away from what's true and imagining a different world. That's why we can talk about winning the lottery, flying, or living on the moon — the second conditional is built for exactly these "unreal" ideas.

📖 could & might in the result

Instead of would, you can use could (= would be able to) or might (= would perhaps) in the result clause to soften or change the meaning:

  • If I had a car, I could drive you home. (would be able to)
  • If she asked me, I might say yes. (would perhaps)

First vs Second Conditional — The Key Skill

This is the heart of the lesson. Both conditionals talk about the future, but they feel completely different. The choice tells your listener how likely you think it is.

💡 The core contrast

First conditional = real and possible. You think it might genuinely happen.
"If I have time, I'll call you." → Maybe I will have time. It's realistic.

Second conditional = unreal, imaginary, or unlikely. You don't really expect it.
"If I had time, I'd call you." → But I don't have time. I'm just imagining.

First Conditional (real / likely)Second Conditional (imaginary / unlikely)
If I have money, I'll buy a coffee. (realistic — I might)If I had a million, I'd buy an island. (dream — I won't)
If it rains, we'll stay home. (it really might rain)If it rained gold, we'd all be rich. (impossible)
If she studies, she'll pass. (she can study)If she studied harder, she'd pass. (but she isn't studying)
If you ask him, he'll help. (you probably will ask)If you asked a celebrity, would they reply? (unlikely)

    B -->|"No, it's imaginary or unlikely"| SC["Second Conditional: If + past, ... would"]
⚠️ Don't mix them: Keep the pair matched. "If I had time, I will call you" ❌ mixes past + will. Use either "If I have time, I'll call" (first) or "If I had time, I'd call" (second) ✅.

"If I Were You…"

Here's a special feature of the second conditional: with the verb be, we traditionally use were for every person — I, he, she, it — not was.

PersonSecond conditional (standard)
IIf I were rich, …
He / She / ItIf he were here, … / If it were warmer, …
You / We / TheyIf you were me, … / If they were honest, …

⚠️ "was" vs "were"

You will hear people say "If I was you…" in casual, informal speech — it's very common and not "wrong" in conversation. But were is the standard, correct form, and it's the one to use in writing, exams, and careful speech. The safest habit: always say "If I were…" Then you're right in every situation.

The most useful place for were is the fixed phrase for giving advice:

💡 Giving advice: "If I were you, I'd…"

This is the most natural, friendly way to advise someone in English. You imagine yourself in their situation, then say what you would do:

  • "If I were you, I'd talk to your boss about it."
  • "If I were you, I wouldn't worry so much."
  • "If I were in your position, I'd take the job."

It's softer and warmer than "You should…" — perfect for helping a friend.

Phrase Bank & Dialogue

Keep these ready-made openers on the tip of your tongue for daydreaming and giving advice:

What would you do if…? If I were you, I'd… I'd probably… If I could, I'd… If I had the chance, I'd… Imagine if… I wouldn't… if I were you. If it were up to me, I'd…

Here's a natural conversation — two friends daydreaming, then giving advice. Notice how the second conditional carries both.

A: What would you do if you won the lottery tomorrow?

B: Oh, easy! I'd quit my job and I'd travel for a whole year. What about you?

A: Honestly? If I had that much money, I'd buy a little house by the sea. I wouldn't tell anyone, either!

B: Ha! Speaking of jobs — I actually got offered a new one, but it's in another city.

A: Really? If I were you, I'd take it. You've always wanted a change.

B: Maybe. If it weren't so far from my family, I'd say yes right away.

A: True. If I were in your position, I might visit the city first before deciding.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Before choosing, ask yourself: "Do I really think this could happen?" Yes → first conditional. No / just imagining → second.
  • Use contractions in speech — I'd, she'd, they'd — to sound natural.
  • Reach for "If I were you, I'd…" whenever you want to give gentle advice.
  • Remember the past-simple verb after if means unreal, not past time.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "would" in the if-clause: "If I would have time…" ❌ → "If I had time…" ✅. Would belongs in the result, not the condition.
  • Mixing first and second: "If I had a car, I will drive you" ❌ → "…I would drive you" ✅.
  • "was" in careful English: "If I was you…" (informal) → "If I were you…" ✅ (standard).
  • Present tense after if: "If I have a million dollars, I'd…" ❌ → "If I had a million dollars, I'd…" ✅ (it's imaginary).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Form: If + past simple, ... would / wouldn't + base verb.
  • The past-simple verb signals unreal / imaginary, not past time.
  • Use it for imaginary, unlikely, or impossible present/future situations.
  • First = real/likely ("If I have time, I'll call"); second = imaginary/unlikely ("If I had time, I'd call").
  • Use were for all persons; "If I were you, I'd…" is the natural way to give advice.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Why do we use the past tense to talk about the present or future?

Think of the past-tense verb as a "distance marker." Just as the past is far away in time, English uses the same form to show something is far away from reality — imaginary rather than real. It's a signal, not a time reference. Many languages do something similar.

Is "If I was you" really wrong?

Not exactly — it's extremely common in casual, spoken English and no one will misunderstand you. But were is the standard, "correct" form for writing, exams, and formal speech. Make "If I were you" your default and you're always safe.

How is the second conditional different from "wish"?

They're close cousins! The second conditional imagines a result ("If I had a car, I'd drive"), while wish expresses a regret or desire about the situation itself ("I wish I had a car"). You'll explore wish and if only in the very next lesson.
