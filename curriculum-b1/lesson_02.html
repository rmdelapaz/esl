🔄 Lesson 1.2: Present Perfect Continuous

In the last lesson you learned to choose between the present perfect and the past simple. Now we add a partner tense: the present perfect continuous. It answers a different question — not "how many?" but "how long?" Use it and your English gains a natural way to talk about the ongoing, still-happening activities that fill a real life.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the present perfect continuous: have/has been + verb-ing
  • Use it to emphasise the duration of an activity that is still going on now
  • Use it for a recent activity with a present result ("I'm tired because I've been running")
  • Choose correctly between the present perfect simple and continuous

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Project: Talk (or record yourself) for two minutes about ongoing things in your life — learning, work, a project, a hobby — using "I've been -ing" and how long.

A Tense for "How Long?"

Imagine someone asks, "What have you been doing lately?" You could answer with the simple present perfect — "I've learned some Spanish" — but that sounds like a finished result. What you often want to say is: "I've been learning Spanish" — an activity that started a while ago and is still going on right now. That "still going, and I want you to feel the length of it" meaning is exactly what the present perfect continuous does.

Think of the two tenses as a pair. The simple looks at the result (Is it done? How many?). The continuous looks at the activity (How long? Is it still happening?). Learning to feel that difference is the heart of this lesson.

🧠 Mindset

Two very similar tenses that overlap in meaning can feel frustrating — like the language is being difficult on purpose. It isn't. English gives you two tools here because native speakers genuinely want to say two slightly different things. Every time you pause and ask "result or activity?", you're training the instinct that eventually makes the choice automatic. Confusion now is the sound of a real skill being built. 🌱

The Form

The present perfect continuous is built from three pieces that never change order:

subject + have / has + been + verb-ing

The have/has agrees with the subject; been is always been; the main verb always ends in -ing.

I've been working. She's been studying. They haven't been sleeping. Have you been waiting long? How long have you been -ing?
💡 Pronunciation tip: In natural speech been is usually weak and short — it sounds like "bin". "I've been waiting" comes out as /aɪv bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/. Say it quickly and let 've been blur together.

Use 1: Duration Up to Now

This is the main job of the tense: an action that started in the past and is still continuing now, with the focus on how long it has been going on. We very often add for or since, and we answer the question "How long…?"

SentenceWhat it means
I've been studying English for three years.Started three years ago, still studying now.
She's been working here since 2020.Started in 2020, still works here.
It's been raining all day.Started this morning, still raining now.
How long have you been waiting?Asking about the length of an activity still happening.

✅ The "for / since" reminder

Just like the simple present perfect (Lesson 1.1): for + a period (for two hours, for years) and since + a start point (since Monday, since I arrived). The continuous adds the feeling of an activity in progress across that whole time.

Use 2: Recent Activity, Present Result

The second use looks at a continuous activity that has just stopped or just been happening, where you can still see or feel the result now. The activity itself explains the present situation — often the evidence is visible.

Present result / evidenceExplained by the activity
I'm tiredbecause I've been running.
Your eyes are red —have you been crying?
His hands are dirty.He's been fixing the car.
Sorry I'm late —I've been waiting for the bus for ages.

Notice the pattern: here the activity may be finished a moment ago, but its effect is present. You use the continuous because you're pointing at the activity that produced what we can see now.

A: Hi! Sorry, have you been waiting long?

B: A little — about ten minutes. So, what have you been doing lately?

A: Oh, I've been decorating my flat. That's why I look exhausted!

B: Nice! I've been learning to cook, actually. I've made about six new dishes this month.

A: Six? That's impressive. How long have you been doing that?

B: Since January. I've been trying to eat out less.

💡 Did you spot it? B says "I've been learning to cook" (the ongoing activity) but "I've made six new dishes" (the countable result). Same conversation, two tenses, two focuses. That switch is what the next section is all about.

Simple vs Continuous — the Heart of the Lesson

This is the choice B1 speakers need to master. Both tenses connect the past to now, so the difference is one of focus:

📖 The key idea

Present perfect SIMPLE → focus on the result, the completion, or the quantity. Answers how much / how many. "I've written three emails." (Done. Counted.)

Present perfect CONTINUOUS → focus on the activity and its duration, often unfinished. Answers how long. "I've been writing emails all morning." (The ongoing activity. Maybe still going.)

Present Perfect Simple (result / quantity)Present Perfect Continuous (activity / duration)
I've written three emails. (how many — finished)I've been writing emails all morning. (how long — the activity)
She's read two books this week. (completed, counted)She's been reading that book for days. (still going)
We've painted the kitchen. (job done — result)We've been painting the kitchen. (the activity — that's why it's messy)
I've run five kilometres. (a measured amount)I've been running, so I'm tired. (the activity, a present result)

💡 The quick test

Ask which question the sentence answers:

  • "How much / how many?" → present perfect simple ("I've read 50 pages.")
  • "How long?" → present perfect continuous ("I've been reading for two hours.")

If you can count a finished amount, go simple. If you want to feel the time spent on the activity, go continuous.

The Stative-Verb Warning

There's one important limit. Some verbs describe a state, not an activity — know, like, love, hate, want, need, believe, understand, be, have (meaning possess), seem, own, belong. These stative verbs are not normally used in any continuous form, including this one.

⚠️ Watch Out: no "-ing" with stative verbs

  • I've known her for years. ✅   /   I've been knowing her for years. ❌
  • I've had this car since 2019. ✅   /   I've been having this car… ❌ (have = possess)
  • We've wanted a dog for ages. ✅   /   We've been wanting a dog… ❌
  • She's been ill all week. ✅   /   She's been being ill… ❌

With stative verbs, use the present perfect simple even when the meaning is "duration up to now": "I've known him for ten years."

💡 A useful exception: Some verbs change meaning and can take the continuous. Have as an activity works fine — "I've been having problems with my laptop" (having = experiencing, not possessing). The rule is about the stative meaning, not the word itself.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Ask "how long or how many?" — how long pushes you to the continuous, how many to the simple.
  • Use the continuous for visible results of a recent activity ("You're wet — have you been swimming?").
  • Reach for the continuous when you want the listener to feel the time spent, not just the finished outcome.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Dropping "been": "I have working all day" ❌ → "I have been working all day" ✅.
  • Continuous with stative verbs: "I've been knowing her for years" ❌ → "I've known her for years" ✅.
  • Continuous for a counted result: "I've been reading three books" ❌ → "I've read three books" ✅ (or "I've been reading all week" for duration).
  • Wrong auxiliary: "He have been running" ❌ → "He has been running" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Form: subject + have/has + been + verb-ing (I've been working, she's been working).
  • Use 1 — duration: an activity that started in the past and is still going on, often with for/since and "How long…?"
  • Use 2 — present result: a recent activity whose effect we see now ("I'm tired — I've been running").
  • Simple vs continuous: simple = result / quantity (how many); continuous = activity / duration (how long).
  • Stative verbs (know, like, want, be, have=possess) don't take the continuous — use the simple.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've added a genuinely new tense to your repertoire and — more importantly — learned to weigh it against the simple present perfect you already knew. Being able to switch between "how long?" and "how many?" in the same breath is a real mark of intermediate control. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is the continuous just "more emphatic" than the simple?

Not quite — it changes the focus. The simple points at a finished result or a number; the continuous points at the activity itself and how long it's lasted. They can describe the same event ("I've painted the room" / "I've been painting the room") but they highlight different things.

Can I always use for and since with the continuous?

Very often, yes, because they measure duration ("for two hours", "since Monday"). Just remember that with stative verbs you keep for/since but switch to the simple: "I've known her for years."

When exactly is the action "finished" in the continuous?

It can be either. In "I've been studying for three years" it's still going. In "I'm tired — I've been running" it may have just stopped, but the result is present. The tense doesn't force finished or unfinished; it just keeps your focus on the activity.
