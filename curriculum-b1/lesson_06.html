🌠 Lesson 2.3: Wish & If Only — Regrets & Wishes

You've just mastered the second conditional's unreal past — a past form that describes an imaginary present. Now you'll put that same idea to work in one of the most human things we do with language: wishing that things were different. By the end, "I wish I…" and "If only…" will roll off your tongue naturally.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use wish / if only + past simple to express wishes and regrets about the present
  • Use wish + were for all persons (I wish I were…)
  • Use wish + could to talk about ability and possibility
  • Understand that wish + would expresses annoyance about someone else's behaviour

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Write and say six or more true wishes about your present life and the world — using "I wish I…" and "If only…".

From Conditionals to Wishes

In the last lesson you learned the second conditional: "If I had a car, I'd drive to work." The trick was that a past form (had) described an unreal present — you don't actually have a car right now. Wishes work in exactly the same way, and that's why this lesson will feel like a natural next step.

When you say "I wish I had a car," you're using a past form (had) to describe something that isn't true now. The past tense doesn't mean the past here — it signals unreal, imaginary, not-the-way-things-are. That single idea unlocks the whole lesson.

🧠 Mindset

A wish can feel like a small sadness — "I wish I could sing," "If only I had more time." But reframe it: every wish is a quiet map of what you value. Wishes are the raw fuel of goals. The thing you keep wishing for is often the thing worth planning toward. So as you practise these sentences, notice what you wish for — you might be looking at your next goal. 🌱

Wish + Past Simple: The Core Pattern

Here is the main structure of the whole lesson. Learn this and the rest is detail:

📖 The key idea

wish + past simple = a wish or regret about a present situation you want to be different.

The past form tells the listener: this is NOT true now, and I wish it were.

Wish sentenceThe real situation now
I wish I had more money.I don't have much money. 😕
I wish I lived by the sea.I don't live by the sea.
I wish I knew her name.I don't know her name.
She wishes she spoke Italian.She doesn't speak Italian.
We wish we didn't have so much work.We have a lot of work.

Notice the "flip": the wish and reality are always opposite. A positive wish (I wish I had…) means a negative reality (I don't have…), and a negative wish (I wish I didn't have…) means a positive reality (I do have…).

💬 "If only" — the emotional big brother

If only means the same as wish, but it's stronger and more emotional. The grammar is identical — if only + past simple:

  • I wish I knew the answer. → If only I knew the answer!
  • I wish she was here. → If only she were here!

Use if only when the feeling is strong — it often ends with an exclamation mark and a sigh.

⚠️ Present wishes only (for now): Everything in this lesson is about wishes for the present (wish + past). Wishes about the past — regrets about things that already happened — use wish + had done ("I wish I had studied harder"). That's a higher-level structure; we'll meet it later. For now, keep to the present.

Were for All Persons

Here's a special rule that surprises many learners. After wish and if only, we use were — not was — with I, he, she, and it. This is the same "unreal" were you met in the second conditional ("If I were you…").

SubjectStandard (recommended)Informal (spoken)
II wish I were taller.I wish I was taller.
he / sheShe wishes she were here.She wishes she was here.
itI wish it were sunny.I wish it was sunny.
you / we / theyI wish you were here. (already "were" — no change)

⚠️/💡 Was or were?

You will hear native speakers say "I wish I was taller" in casual conversation — it's very common and not "wrong" in speech. But were is the standard, correct form, especially in writing and exams. When in doubt, choose were: it's always safe. 💡 Tip: the fixed phrase "I wish I were you" is a good one to memorise — it locks the pattern in.

Wish + Could: Ability & Possibility

When your wish is about being able to do something — a skill you don't have, or something that isn't possible for you right now — use wish + could (+ the base verb).

Wish + couldThe real situation now
I wish I could sing.I can't sing.
I wish I could come to your party.I can't come (I'm busy).
He wishes he could drive.He can't drive.
I wish we could stay longer.We can't stay — we have to go.

Think of could here as simply the "unreal" version of can. I can't sing (reality) becomes I wish I could sing (wish). It's the go-to pattern whenever the word can would fit the real situation.

Wish + Would: Annoyance About Others

This last pattern is different — and very useful for real life. We use wish + would to complain about someone else's (or something's) annoying behaviour that we want to change. There's a feeling of irritation behind it.

Wish + wouldWhat's annoying you
I wish you would stop shouting.You keep shouting — please stop!
I wish it would stop raining.It won't stop raining. 🌧️
I wish my neighbours would be quieter.They're noisy every night.
I wish he wouldn't interrupt me.He keeps interrupting.

💡 Don't use wish + would about yourself

wish + would is for other people's behaviour you want to change — not your own. To wish about yourself, use the base patterns:

  • ❌ I wish I would be more organised.
  • ✅ I wish I were more organised. (wish + were)
  • ✅ I wish I could be more organised. (wish + could)

Why? Because would here carries the idea of "if only they were willing to change" — and you can't complain about your own unwillingness in the same way.

💡 Wish vs the second conditional — same logic, different job

These two structures use the same unreal past, but they do different things:

  • Wish states the desire: "I wish I had a car." (I don't have one, and I want one.)
  • Second conditional states the result: "If I had a car, I'd drive to work." (imagining the consequence.)

Same unreal had; the wish expresses the longing, the conditional plays out what would follow. Once you see that, the second conditional and the wish reinforce each other. 🔗

Phrase Bank & Dialogue

Keep these openers ready. They fit almost any topic — your life, your town, the weather, the world:

I wish I had… I wish I lived… I wish I were… If only I knew… If only it were… I wish I could… I wish you would… I wish it would stop…

Listen to two friends sharing their wishes and small regrets over coffee:

Maya: Ugh, it's so grey today. I wish it were sunny — I'd go for a walk.

Leo: I know. And I wish it would stop raining so my football match wasn't cancelled again!

Maya: Ha. Honestly, some days I wish I lived somewhere warm. If only I had the money to move to Spain.

Leo: Same. Though I wish I could speak Spanish first — I only know about five words.

Maya: You could learn! I wish I were as good at languages as you are, though.

Leo: You're not bad! Anyway — I wish my flatmate would do the dishes. That's my real problem right now.

Maya: Now that's a wish I understand completely.

Notice the mix: I wish it were sunny (were), I wish it would stop raining (would — annoyance), I wish I lived… (past simple), If only I had… (if only), I wish I could speak… (could), I wish my flatmate would… (would — someone else's behaviour). All the patterns in one natural chat.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Complete the wish (correct past form)

Objective: Put the verb into the correct form — remember were for I/he/she/it.

  1. I wish I (have) a bigger flat.
  2. I wish it (be) the weekend.
  3. She wishes she (know) how to swim.
  4. If only I (be) taller!
  5. I wish we (not live) so far from the city.
✅ Answer

1. had · 2. were (was is OK in casual speech, but were is standard) · 3. knew · 4. were · 5. didn't live

🏋️ Exercise 2: wish + past / wish + could / wish + would?

Objective: Choose the right pattern for each situation. Complete the wish.

  1. You can't play the guitar, but you'd love to. → I wish I play the guitar.
  2. Your brother never answers your messages, and it annoys you. → I wish my brother answer my messages.
  3. You don't have a garden, and you want one. → I wish I a garden.
  4. The dog keeps barking all night. → I wish the dog stop barking.
  5. You're not very patient, and you'd like to be. → I wish I more patient.
✅ Answer

1. could (ability) · 2. would (annoying behaviour of someone else) · 3. had (past simple, present wish) · 4. would (annoying behaviour) · 5. were (about yourself → were, not would)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Rewrite the complaint as a wish

Objective: Turn each present complaint into a wish. Use wish + past, wish + could, or wish + would as appropriate.

  1. My job is boring. → I wish…
  2. I can't drive. → I wish…
  3. My phone is so slow. → I wish…
  4. You always leave the door open. (it annoys me) → I wish…
  5. It's cold and I want it to be warm. → If only…
✅ Answer

Sample answers (yours may vary):

1. I wish my job weren't boring / I wish I had a more interesting job. · 2. I wish I could drive. · 3. I wish my phone weren't so slow / I wish I had a faster phone. · 4. I wish you would stop leaving the door open / I wish you wouldn't leave the door open. · 5. If only it were warm!

🎯 Your Project: Six True Wishes

Speak out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder — and write them down too.

  1. (3 min) Write six or more true wishes about your present life and the world. Start each with "I wish I…" or "If only…".
  2. (2 min) Include at least one of each pattern: a wish + past (I wish I lived…), a wish + were (I wish I were…), a wish + could (I wish I could…), and a wish + would (I wish someone would…).
  3. (2 min) Add one wish about the wider world: "I wish there were…," "If only people would…".
  4. (2 min) Say all six aloud with feeling — use if only for the ones you feel most strongly.
  5. (1 min) If you recorded it, listen back: did you use were (not was) and keep would only for other people?

  • ☐ I used wish + could for at least one ability
  • ☐ I used wish + would for someone else's behaviour (not my own)
  • ☐ I used If only… for my strongest wish

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Remember the flip: a wish is always the opposite of reality. I wish I had… = I don't have.
  • Default to were (not was) after wish and if only — it's always safe.
  • Use could for ability, would only for other people's annoying behaviour.
  • Reach for if only when the feeling is strong — it adds emotion.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Present tense after wish: "I wish I have a car" ❌ → "I wish I had a car" ✅ (present wish = past form).
  • would about yourself: "I wish I would be taller" ❌ → "I wish I were taller" ✅.
  • can instead of could: "I wish I can swim" ❌ → "I wish I could swim" ✅.
  • will after wish: "I wish it will stop raining" ❌ → "I wish it would stop raining" ✅.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • wish / if only + past simple = a wish or regret about the present; the past form signals "unreal / not true now".
  • if only = a stronger, more emotional wish — same grammar.
  • Use were for all persons after wish (I wish I were…); was is informal.
  • wish + could = ability/possibility; wish + would = annoyance about someone else's behaviour (never about yourself).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've taken the unreal past from the second conditional and turned it into one of the most expressive tools in English — the language of longing, regret, and hope. Being able to say exactly what you wish were different is a big step in sounding like a real, feeling speaker rather than a textbook. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is "I wish I was" actually wrong?

Not in everyday speech — millions of native speakers say it and no one blinks. But were is the standard, "correct" form, especially in writing and exams. Learn were; recognise was when you hear it.

What's the difference between "I wish" and "I hope"?

Hope is for real, possible futures: "I hope it's sunny tomorrow" (it might be!). Wish is for things that are unreal now: "I wish it were sunny" (but it isn't). If it's genuinely possible, use hope; if you're imagining a different reality, use wish.

How do I wish about the past — things I regret already happened?

For past regrets, English uses wish + had done: "I wish I had studied harder." That's the past-perfect version, which we'll cover at a higher level. This lesson stays with present wishes (wish + past simple) — master these first.
