Placement Test, Pre-A1 to C2

One test for placing adult learners into the right course, from English Foundations (A1) to Mastery English (C2). Learners do a written part (grammar and vocabulary, reading, writing) and have a short one-to-one speaking interview with you. You combine the two results into one recommended course.

Part A 50 multiple choice Part B 2 reading texts Part C writing Part D 5-min interview About 75 min + interviews
Teacher pages
Before you start: this is a placement, not an exam

Many adult learners have bad memories of tests, and some have had little or no schooling. Say it plainly, with gestures or a shared language if you have one: "This test is to find the right class for you. You can't pass or fail. Stop when it's too difficult. That's OK." Nobody should sit for an hour in front of questions they can't read.

Administering the test

PartTimeWhoWhat happens
Welcome5 minT–SExplain the purpose (see above). Give out the learner pages. Show how to circle an answer, using the example on the board. Learners write their name, or a first name only.

You need
  • One copy of the learner pages (Parts A–C) per learner
  • One copy of the interview script (Part D), with the picture card, for you
  • The answer key and band descriptors (placement-test-key.html)
  • Pencils, and a clock the learners can see
Ground rules
  • No dictionaries or phones during Parts A–C.
  • Explain instructions in any language that helps; don't explain the questions.
  • Let learners stop at any point. Collect their pages with a smile.
  • Personal questions are always optional. A learner may give a "new identity": a made-up name, town or job.

📖 Text 2. Only read this text if Text 1 was easy for you. Read the article and circle the best answer: a, b, c or d.

Could a shorter week make us better workers?

When a group of companies agreed to try a four-day working week for six months, few observers expected the results to be so striking. Employees kept the same salary but worked roughly 20 percent fewer hours, on the condition that they maintained their usual output. By the end of the trial, most of the participating firms reported that productivity had either stayed the same or improved, and the vast majority chose to continue with the new schedule.

Supporters argue that the benefits go beyond the office. Staff in the trial reported sleeping better, feeling less stressed and having more time for their families, and many companies found it easier to recruit and keep good people. Critics, however, point out that the firms taking part were volunteers — organizations that already believed the idea would work — and that a four-day week is far harder to arrange in hospitals, schools or factories, where someone has to be present at all times.

What seems clear is that the traditional five-day week is not a law of nature. It was itself a reform, introduced about a century ago. Whether the next change comes quickly or slowly, the debate is no longer about whether working time can change, but how.

  1. During the trial, employees…
    a) earned less money for fewer hours.b) worked fewer hours for the same pay.c) worked longer days but only four days a week.d) chose their own hours.
  2. At the end of the trial, most companies…
    a) returned to the five-day week.b) reduced salaries.c) decided to keep the four-day week.d) asked the government for help.
  3. What is one criticism mentioned in the text?
    a) Workers felt more stressed.b) Productivity fell sharply.c) The companies already believed the idea would work.d) Hospitals refused to take part.
  4. The word striking (paragraph 1) is closest in meaning to…
    a) violent.b) surprising and impressive.c) disappointing.d) expected.
  5. The writer's main point in the last paragraph is that…
    a) the five-day week will end very soon.b) working time has changed before and can change again.c) the four-day week is a failed experiment.d) governments should make a new law.

Part D · Speaking interview (5 minutes)

Interview one learner at a time. Start at the A1 band and move up. Spend about a minute on each band the learner handles comfortably; you won't use every band with every learner. Rephrase once if a question isn't understood. Stop rule: when the learner can't manage two prompts in a band, even after rephrasing, stop there, finish with a friendly closing question they can answer, and thank them. Take notes as you go; rate afterwards with the speaking descriptors in the key.

Keep it safe and respectful

Don't ask why someone left their country, about family members who aren't with them, or about immigration status. If a learner seems uncomfortable with a personal question, move on straight away ("No problem! Next question…"). Every personal prompt below has a neutral alternative in brackets.

A2Past, future, everyday descriptions~1 min
What did you do last weekend?
What are you going to do next weekend?
Tell me about your town [or: this town]. Is it big or small? What do you like about it?

Listen for: past forms (went, visited) · going to · linked sentences with and, but, because · simple comparisons.
B1Experiences, opinions, hypotheticals~1 min
Tell me about a time when something went wrong, like a trip or a day at work. What happened, and what did you do?
If you could live anywhere in the world, where would you live? Why?
What advice would you give someone who has just moved to this area?
Listen for: a connected story with a beginning, middle and end · reasons and simple opinions · would · keeping going without long pauses, even with errors.
B2Arguing a view, pros and cons~1 min
Some people say that smartphones make our lives more stressful. What do you think? Give me some reasons and examples.
What are the advantages and disadvantages of working from home?
How has your town or country [or: the place you know best] changed in recent years? Why do you think that is?
Listen for: a clear position developed with reasons · balancing two sides (on the other hand, although) · few errors that cause misunderstanding · a range of vocabulary beyond everyday words.
C1Abstract topics, explanation, challenge~1 min
Explain something from your work or studies, a process or an idea, as if I knew nothing about it.
What do you think are the main reasons housing has become so expensive in many cities, and what could realistically be done about it?
(Push back politely) I'm not sure I agree. Couldn't you argue the opposite?
Listen for: fluent, well-organized explanation · hedging and precise vocabulary · handling a challenge without losing the thread · errors rare and usually self-corrected.
C2Nuance, register, persuasion~1 min
Some would argue that tradition holds societies back. How far would you go along with that?
What, for you, is the difference between persuading someone and manipulating them?
Now explain your answer again, but as you would to a twelve-year-old.
Listen for: subtle distinctions and exact word choice · idiomatic, natural phrasing · shifting register at will · effortless flow, with a fully convincing argument.

Step 1 · The written result

The written band is the highest Part A section the learner secured, with every easier section secured too. A lucky score in Section 5 doesn't count if Section 3 wasn't secured. Use Part B to confirm: a secured A2 or B2 band should come with Text 1 or Text 2 at 4/5 or better. If the reading disagrees, trust the lower result and look closely at the interview. Step 2 · Combine with the speaking band

The speaking band is the highest band the learner handled comfortably in the interview. Pre-A1 counts as "nothing secured." As with the written result, the recommended course is the next level up: a learner comfortable at A2 goes into Independent English (B1).

If…Then…
Written and speaking bands matchPlace in the next course up. Done.
They differ by one bandPlace by the lower band, note the stronger skill on the learner profile, and do a move-up check after two weeks. At B2 and above you may place by the higher band if the learner's goals are mostly in their stronger skill.
Speaking is two or more bands higher than writingUsually a literacy or schooling gap, not a language gap. Don't put a fluent speaker in a beginner class. Do the literacy check below, then place one course below the speaking result, with literacy support, or alongside a literacy class.
Writing is two or more bands higher than speakingOften a learner who studied grammar at school but rarely speaks. Place one course above the speaking result, and flag speaking fluency as their focus.
Specialist courses: IELTS Academic Preparation and Business English

Both are 12-lesson skills courses for learners at about B1 and above. Recommend them when a learner's result points to Confident English (B2) or higher and their goal matches: a university or registration band score (IELTS), or work communication (Business). Learners can take them alongside a general course. Below B1, raise the general level first.

Placing learners with limited literacy

Don't rely on the written test

A written test measures reading and writing in English and familiarity with tests. For a learner who had little schooling, reads slowly in their own language, or uses a non-Latin script (Arabic, Amharic, Chinese, Tigrinya and others), the written score says very little about their English. Some of these learners speak quite a lot of English.

  1. Watch the first minute of Part A. If a learner can't write their name, holds the pencil uncertainly, or looks at the page without starting, quietly collect the paper ("Thank you, let's talk instead") and go straight to the interview. No one should be left to struggle.
  2. Use the oral interview and the picture card. Rate speaking as usual. Many learners reach A1 or A2 in speaking with no reading at all.
  3. Do a two-minute literacy check. Can they: write their first name? copy a short sentence (I like tea.)? read a few common signs (EXIT · OPEN · STOP · BUS · PUSH)? read numbers and a clock? recognize upper- and lower-case letters? Note what they can and can't do. Don't mark it as a score.
  4. Place by speaking, and plan literacy support. Pre-literate and low-literacy learners at Pre-A1 or A1 speaking go into English Foundations (A1), using the literacy-support strand in each lesson (picture cues, tracing and copying, fewer items). A learner who speaks at A2 or above but reads at a very low level should not start again at A1 for speaking. See Step 2, and if your program has one, combine the class with a literacy class.
  5. Record it kindly. On the learner profile, write what the learner can do ("reads numbers, copies words, speaks in short sentences"), not a list of gaps.
