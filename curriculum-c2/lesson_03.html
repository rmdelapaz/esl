🔗 Lesson 1.3: Fixed Expressions, Binomials & Advanced Collocation

Fluent English is not built word by word. Much of it is assembled — from ready-made chunks that a master reaches for whole, exactly as they are, in exactly the right order. This lesson trains that prefabricated layer: the binomials, restricted collocations, and institutionalised expressions that, got precisely right, sound unmistakably native — and, got slightly wrong, betray you in an instant.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use binomials and trinomials in their fixed, irreversible order
  • Deploy restricted (strong) collocations — the precise partner-words that mark native-like fluency
  • Wield institutionalised expressions, fixed similes, and lexical bundles with an ear for register
  • Understand fixedness — why the words and their order cannot be changed at will

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Project: Write and deliver a short piece — a toast, a review, or an anecdote — densely and naturally woven with binomials, fixed expressions, and strong collocations, without a whiff of overstuffing.

The Prefabricated Language

There is a persistent illusion that we speak by selecting words one at a time and threading them onto a grammatical string. In truth, a vast proportion of fluent speech and writing is pre-assembled: we store — and retrieve whole — thousands of ready-made chunks. Native speakers don't construct "by and large" or "a foregone conclusion"; they recall them, intact, order and all.

This is why a learner can be flawless in grammar and rich in vocabulary yet still sound faintly translated. The words are right; the chunks are subtly homemade. At C2, the frontier is precisely this prefabricated layer — knowing not only the words but the exact, conventional shape they come in.

🧠 Mindset

You cannot deduce fixed expressions from rules — you can only collect them. Treat your reading and listening as a mine: whenever a phrase sounds pleasingly whole, whenever two words seem welded together, note it down verbatim, order intact. This is not memorisation as chore but connoisseurship as habit. The master's fluency is, in large part, a very large and very accurate archive of chunks. 🌱

⚠️ Fixedness: you can't reorder or substitute freely

The defining trait of these chunks is fixedness. We say salt and pepper, never "pepper and salt"; bread and butter, never "butter and bread"; knife and fork, never "fork and knife." No rule of grammar forbids the reversal — convention does, and convention is absolute here. To swap the order or the partner-word is not wrong in the schoolroom sense; it is foreign in the far more revealing sense.

Binomials & Trinomials

A binomial is a fixed pair of words — typically joined by and or or — that always appears in the same order. A trinomial is the same trick with three. Their order is irreversible: reverse it and the phrase collapses into oddity.

BinomialMeaningNatural use
by and largegenerally, on the wholeBy and large, the plan worked.
part and parcelan inseparable part ofLate nights are part and parcel of the job.
give and takemutual compromiseAny marriage needs give and take.
ups and downsalternating good and bad timesWe've had our ups and downs.
here and therein various scattered placesA few errors here and there.
sooner or latereventually, inevitablySooner or later he'll find out.
touch and gouncertain, precariousIt was touch and go for a while.
null and voidlegally invalid (formal/legal)The contract is null and void.
rank and fileordinary members (not leaders)The rank and file were restless.
wear and teardamage from ordinary useNormal wear and tear is expected.
peace and quietcalm and stillnessI just want some peace and quiet.
sick and tiredthoroughly fed up (informal)I'm sick and tired of waiting.
back and forthto and fro; repeatedlyEmails went back and forth for days.
law and ordersocial/civic controlA breakdown in law and order.
pros and consadvantages and disadvantagesWeigh the pros and cons.

Trinomials are rarer and more emphatic — three beats that land like a gavel:

TrinomialMeaningNatural use
lock, stock and barrelcompletely, the whole thingThey bought the firm lock, stock and barrel.
hook, line and sinkercompletely (of being deceived)He fell for it hook, line and sinker.
signed, sealed and deliveredfinalised, done, guaranteedThe deal is signed, sealed and delivered.

💡 Why the order is irreversible

The fixed sequence is rarely arbitrary. Often the shorter or older word comes first (bread and butter, salt and pepper); often phonology decides it, with the higher, lighter vowel leading the lower, heavier one (flip and flop, tick and tock, this and that). You needn't recite the reasons — but knowing they exist reminds you that the order is load-bearing, not incidental. Reverse it and a native ear flinches.

by and large part and parcel give and take ups and downs sooner or later touch and go null and void wear and tear peace and quiet back and forth pros and cons lock, stock and barrel hook, line and sinker

Restricted (Strong) Collocation

In Lesson 2.x (C1 collocation) you learned that words keep habitual company: you make a decision, you do the dishes, you take a risk. Much of that is free collocation — a word that pairs happily with many partners. Here we cross to the far, more rigid end of the same continuum: restricted or strong collocation, where a word admits essentially one partner, and no near-synonym will do.

You can be bitterly disappointed but not "sourly" or "harshly" disappointed. An outcome can be a foregone conclusion but not a "predetermined" or "obvious" one — the phrase is welded. Getting these exact is a C2 signature; approximating them is the tell of a very good non-native speaker.

Strong collocationMeaningThe trap
a foregone conclusionan outcome certain in advancenot "a predetermined result"
a moot pointa debatable / now-irrelevant issuenot "a discussable point"
a vested interesta personal stake in an outcomenot "an invested interest"
drive a hard bargainnegotiate toughlynot "make a hard bargain"
a resounding successa great, unambiguous successnot "a loud success"
bitterly disappointeddeeply, painfully let downnot "harshly / sourly disappointed"
a burning ambitionan intense, driving desirenot "a hot / flaming ambition"
a vexed questiona difficult, much-disputed issuenot "an annoyed question"
run-of-the-millordinary, unremarkablenot "run-of-the-factory"
few and far betweenscarce, rarenot "few and rare"
⚠️ Free vs restricted: the difference is one of latitude. In free collocation you have choices — strong, compelling, and powerful argument all work. In restricted collocation you have none — the partner is fixed, and a synonym, however logical, sounds wrong. When in doubt about which end you're at, assume rigidity and verify: it is safer to learn the fixed phrase whole than to reason your way to a plausible-but-foreign substitute.

  • moot point

  • resounding success
  • bitterly disappointed

Institutionalised Expressions

Institutionalised expressions — also called lexical bundles — are conventional multi-word frames we use to organise, hedge, or frame what we say. They are the connective tissue of natural discourse: prefabricated, high-frequency, and doing a specific interpersonal job.

ExpressionFunctionRegister note
to cut a long story shortsignals a compressed summaryspoken / informal
if truth be toldintroduces a candid admissionslightly literary, warm
at the end of the day"what ultimately matters is…"very common — bordering on cliché
when all is said and doneafter everything is consideredneutral, mildly formal
that saidconcedes, then qualifiesneutral — safe in writing
for what it's worthoffers a view modestlyconversational, self-deprecating
needless to sayflags the obvious / expectedneutral
believe it or notflags something surprisinginformal, engaging

⚠️ Cliché risk

Several of these — above all at the end of the day — are so overused that they have thinned into clichés. That does not forbid them; it obliges awareness. Dropped once, deliberately, they sound easy and idiomatic. Sprinkled through every paragraph, they signal a speaker running on autopilot. The master knows which bundles are still crisp (that said, for what it's worth) and which are worn smooth (at the end of the day), and rations accordingly.

to cut a long story short if truth be told when all is said and done that said for what it's worth needless to say believe it or not

The chunks at work (a review, spoken register):

"By and large, it's a triumph. The pacing has its ups and downs — a saggy middle, a few scenes that go back and forth without advancing — but the ending is a resounding success. If truth be told, I went in expecting something run-of-the-mill and came out flabbergasted. When all is said and done, films this assured are few and far between."

Notice the density is high yet nothing is forced: each chunk earns its place, the registers agree (all conversational-reviewerly), and no bundle repeats. That balance — rich but natural — is the target.

Fixed Similes

English hoards ready-made comparisons — fixed similes of the pattern as ADJECTIVE as NOUN or like NOUN. Their comparison is conventional, not invented: you don't reason your way to them, you recall them whole. They are vivid and idiomatic — and almost all decidedly informal.

Fixed simileMeaningNatural use
as good as goldvery well-behavedThe children were as good as gold.
as dry as a bonecompletely dryThe riverbed was as dry as a bone.
as easy as pievery easyThe exam was as easy as pie.
get on like a house on firebond quickly and warmlyThey got on like a house on fire.
as clear as daycompletely obviousHer meaning was as clear as day.

💡 Informal by nature

Fixed similes belong to warm, spoken, and colloquial registers — a toast, a chat, a lively review. Drop as easy as pie into a formal report and it clangs. This is not a flaw in the simile but a fact about it: every chunk carries a register stamp, and using it well means using it where its stamp belongs. In a solemn context, reach instead for the plain word (straightforward, self-evident).

Getting Them Exactly Right

Two dangers attend this whole layer, and the master avoids both.

The first is overuse. Fixed expressions, clichés, and similes are seasoning, not the meal. A sentence stuffed with them reads as a phrasebook emptied onto the page — the opposite of the effortless fluency they're meant to signal. Accuracy and restraint: that is the rule.

The second, and more damning, is garbling. Because these chunks are fixed, an approximation is instantly audible. The reworded or mis-heard version — the "eggcorn" — is one of the surest giveaways of a non-native (or inattentive native) speaker.

⚠️ Garbled fixed expressions — the dead giveaway

Garbled ❌Correct ✅
a doggy-dog worlda dog-eat-dog world
for all intensive purposesfor all intents and purposes
a mute pointa moot point
nip it in the buttnip it in the bud
should of / could ofshould have / could have
peak my interestpique my interest
escape goatscapegoat
on tender hookson tenterhooks

Every one of these is grammatically fluent and phonetically close — which is exactly why it's so revealing. The chunk must be exact. Better to use a phrase you're sure of than to gamble on one you half-remember.

✅ The master's rule of thumb

Use fixed expressions accurately, sparingly, and with register in mind. Know the exact wording, know where each belongs, and let them fall naturally — one well-placed binomial does more than five crowded ones. Precision here is not pedantry; it is the difference between sounding at home in the language and sounding like a visitor with an excellent map.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Complete the chunk

Objective: Supply the missing half (in its correct, fixed order).

  1. Long hours are part and of the job.
  2. By and , the reforms have worked.
  3. Sooner or , the truth comes out.
  4. He fell for the scam hook, line and .
  5. The old contract is now null and .
  6. I just want a little peace and .
✅ Answer

1. parcel · 2. large · 3. later · 4. sinker · 5. void · 6. quiet. Note the order is fixed throughout — "large and by," "later or sooner," etc. are impossible.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the strong collocate

Objective: Only one partner is idiomatic. Choose it.

  1. The result was a foregone / a predetermined conclusion.
  2. She was bitterly / harshly disappointed.
  3. He drives / makes a hard bargain.
  4. Whether he knew is now a moot / a mute point.
  5. The launch was a resounding / a loud success.
  6. Real bargains are few and far between / few and rare.
✅ Answer

1. a foregone conclusion · 2. bitterly disappointed · 3. drives a hard bargain · 4. a moot point ("mute" is a classic garble) · 5. a resounding success · 6. few and far between. In each, the alternative is logical but not idiomatic — the collocation is restricted.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the garble

Objective: Each contains a misheard or misordered fixed expression. Correct it.

  1. "For all intensive purposes, the project is finished."
  2. "It's a doggy-dog world out there."
  3. "We need to nip this problem in the butt."
  4. "That documentary really peaked my interest."
  5. "After the storm, the fields were dry as a stone."
  6. "We waited on tender hooks for the verdict."
✅ Answer

1. for all intents and purposes · 2. a dog-eat-dog world · 3. nip it in the bud · 4. piqued my interest · 5. dry as a bone (the fixed simile) · 6. on tenterhooks. Each corrected version is the only fixed form; the garbles are grammatically fine but instantly foreign.

A: So — how did the interview go? Don't keep me on tenterhooks.

B: To cut a long story short, I got it. Signed, sealed and delivered.

A: That's a resounding success! I knew it was a foregone conclusion.

B: Hardly. It was touch and go for a while — they drive a hard bargain on salary. Give and take on both sides in the end.

A: Well, by and large you played it beautifully. And, for what it's worth, they'd have been fools to pass you up.

B: Needless to say, I'm relieved. Now — some peace and quiet at last.

Six turns, a dozen chunks — yet it reads as ordinary, warm conversation. That naturalness, not the raw count, is the mark of control.

🎯 Your Project: A Chunk-Woven Piece

Write and deliver a short piece — pick one: a wedding/farewell toast, a film or restaurant review, or a personal anecdote (2–3 minutes spoken, or ~200–250 words). Weave in the prefabricated language densely but naturally.

  1. (4 min) Choose your genre and its register. A toast and a review keep different company — pick your chunks to match.
  2. (10 min) Draft. Aim to land at least six binomials/fixed expressions and three strong collocations, but let them arise where the sense invites them — never force a slot.
  3. (4 min) Ruthless edit: read aloud and cut any chunk that sounds crammed in, any cliché that's gone stale, any register clash. If two sit in one sentence, remove one.
  4. (3 min) Verify exactness: check every fixed phrase against its true wording and order. Hunt your own eggcorns.
  5. (Solo) Record yourself delivering it; listen back for naturalness. (Partner/group) Perform it live and have listeners flag any chunk that sounded forced or wrong — then defend or cut it.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ At least 6 binomials / fixed expressions, used in exact order
  • ☐ At least 3 restricted (strong) collocations, correct partner-words
  • ☐ Register consistent throughout — no formal report wearing an as-easy-as-pie
  • ☐ Every chunk verified for exact wording (no garbles, no misorderings)
  • ☐ I cut at least one chunk for crowding — it reads natural, not stuffed

Question 1: Which is the correct, fixed order?

Question 2: Which completes the restricted collocation "a ______ conclusion" (an outcome certain in advance)?

Tips & Common Pitfalls

✅ Do's

  • Collect chunks whole from your reading and listening — verbatim, order intact. This layer is gathered, not deduced.
  • Learn the register of each alongside its wording — a fixed simile is not for a formal report; that said is fine anywhere.
  • Verify before you deploy a half-remembered phrase — exactness is the whole point, and a corpus or search check settles it.

❌ Common Pitfalls

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Garbling: "for all intensive purposes," "a mute point," "should of" — grammatically fluent, phonetically close, and instantly damning. Get the chunk exact.
  • Reordering: binomials are irreversible. "Butter and bread," "quiet and peace," "cons and pros" all read as foreign, however sensible they seem.
  • Overstuffing: a paragraph crammed with fixed expressions sounds like a phrasebook, not a person. Ration them; let each land clean.
  • Register blindness: dropping as easy as pie or at the end of the day into solemn prose — right chunk, wrong room.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Fluent English is largely prefabricated — retrieved as fixed chunks, not built word by word; getting the chunk exactly right marks native-likeness.
  • Binomials and trinomials have an irreversible order (salt and pepper, lock, stock and barrel) — reverse it and it reads as foreign.
  • Restricted (strong) collocations admit essentially one partner (a foregone conclusion, bitterly disappointed); a logical synonym still sounds wrong.
  • Institutionalised expressions and fixed similes carry a register stamp — many are informal or worn to cliché; the master uses them accurately, sparingly, and in the right room.
  • A garbled chunk ("for all intensive purposes") is a dead giveaway — exactness is everything.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've begun to hear the language as native speakers store it — not as a lexicon of independent words but as a vast, ordered archive of ready-made chunks. You can now use binomials in their fixed order, reach for the one right collocate, and place a lexical bundle or fixed simile where its register belongs — all without the tell-tale crowding or garbling that betrays the learner. This is fluency at its most granular. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

If these can't be deduced, how do I ever learn them all?

You don't learn them all — no one does — and you don't need to. You build the archive steadily by collecting: verbatim notes from your reading and listening, a few chunks a week, each with its register. Frequency does the sorting for you — the most useful chunks are also the most common, so wide reading surfaces them naturally.

How dense is "too dense"? Where's the line?

Read your draft aloud. If a chunk sounds placed rather than arisen — if you can hear the seams — it's too dense. A rough gauge: rarely more than one fixed expression per sentence, and never two clichés in a paragraph. The aim is invisibility; the chunks should feel like the natural way to say the thing, not decorations bolted on.

Are clichés simply to be avoided?

No — avoided only when overused or misplaced. A cliché is just a fixed expression worn smooth by popularity; dropped once, deliberately, and in the right register, it reads as easy and idiomatic. The lapse is the reflex, not the phrase — reaching for it automatically, every time, without noticing it's tired.
