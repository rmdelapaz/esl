Progress Test 1 — Modules 1–4

A halfway check after Lesson 4.3. It covers the whole first half of C2: the mot juste (connotation, semantic prosody, force, and specificity), register agility and tone, binomials and restricted collocation, implicature and presupposition, irony and understatement, figurative language, discourse and stance markers, cohesion and coherence, summary, paraphrase and synthesis, rhetoric and persuasion, and style and voice. It tells you what to refine in Weeks 7–13. It does not rank anyone, and it is not a mock proficiency exam.

~62 min written + 9 min pair speaking Written /60 · Speaking /15 · Total /75 CEFR C2
Why a progress test at C2, and how to frame it

C2 learners are often accomplished professionals (translators, teachers, academics, managers) who have not been "tested" in years, and some are preparing for a proficiency exam and will read every mark as a forecast. Say it plainly: "This is a check, not an exam. At this level almost nothing here is about right and wrong grammar. It is about precision, appropriacy, and effect. It tells me which of those are already reliable for you and which we should polish in the second half." Point out that many items have more than one good answer (the rewrites, the gist, the writing): you are scoring whether the choice works, not whether it matches a key word for word. Read the listening at a natural, unhurried pace, with the intonation marked in the script. At C2, hearing irony and hesitation in the voice is part of what is being tested, so don't flatten it and don't exaggerate it into pantomime.

Literacy-adapted version (learners whose reading and writing lag behind their speaking)

Rare at C2, but it happens: a learner whose English was built almost entirely through work and conversation, or who has dyslexia. Use the same paper and change how it is done, not what it tests. Read every instruction and item aloud, enlarge the paper, and accept spoken answers in Parts 2–4 (for the register rewrites, the learner says the new version and you score what they say). Read the column aloud twice for Part 4. In Part 5 the learner may dictate the talk while you scribe, or simply deliver it aloud; score content, rhetoric, and register, not spelling. Give extra time or split the paper over two sittings. Write "Adapted" at the top and note what changed. For these learners, listening and speaking are the main evidence; the key explains how to act on the result.

Opinions are optional; sides can be assigned

Part 5 and the speaking discussion ask learners to argue a position. They may argue either side, and in the speaking task you can assign sides. Say before the test starts: "Your side or the other side, it makes no difference. I'm scoring the craft, not your views." Nothing in the speaking task asks about a learner's own life; if someone reaches for a personal example, real or imagined is equally fine. Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status.

ReadSkim the questions first. Then read this column from a city magazine, once for sense and once for its craft.
The Room Where Nothing Happens

¶1For forty years, the second floor of the Eastgate Public Library has held a room in which, officially, nothing happens. There are no computers, no meetings, no talking; there are long oak tables, green lamps, and a sign that says only Quiet, please. This spring the library board announced that the room would become a so-called "flexible collaboration hub," complete with movable pods and a coffee bar. The change, we are told, will supposedly "bring the library into the twenty-first century."

¶2The board insists that the reading room was under-used. Perhaps. It is certainly true that on a weekday morning you will rarely find more than a dozen readers there. But the public consultation received 412 responses; the board's published summary ran to a single paragraph. Tellingly, it did not mention that nine in ten respondents asked for the room to stay.

¶3Admittedly, libraries must change, and I have no wish to preserve them in amber. A building that refuses every new idea soon becomes a museum of itself. But the reading room was never a relic. For the student with five siblings at home, the night-shift nurse between jobs, the retired teacher learning Italian, it was a harbor: a still stretch of water in a city that never stops churning. Replace it with pods and a coffee bar, and we will have built one more busy dock and lost the only calm mooring in town.

¶4Who, exactly, was the reading room failing? Not its readers. The board may yet be persuaded to keep one quiet room while it modernizes the rest of the building; that would be a compromise worth having. What it should not do is mistake silence for emptiness.

Writing

___ / 12
WriteWrite the opening of a short persuasive talk. (160–200 words)

Your town is holding a public meeting on this motion: "Main Street should be closed to cars every Sunday and become a pedestrian zone." Write the first minute of your talk. You may argue either side. It does not have to be your personal view.

  • Open with a hook, not a throat-clearing generality, and make your line clear.
  • Appeal through ethos, pathos, and logos (at least one of each), and use at least two named figures (tricolon, antithesis, anaphora, rhetorical question, hypophora, chiasmus). Label your two figures in the margin.
  • Concede the other side's strongest fair point, then refute it.
  • Keep one register throughout, choose your words for connotation, and avoid mixed metaphors and tired clichés.
As someone who has … Picture, for a moment, … The figures are stark: … I'll grant that … ; that said, … There is force in the objection that … It is not that … ; rather, … What does this mean for us? Simply this: …

Finished. Now reread, and cut one word that isn't earning its place.

Part 1 · Listening Scripts

Give 30 seconds to read the questions for each section. Read each script twice at a natural pace, with a pause of about 10 seconds between readings, and say the item number first ("Number one"). Don't read the answer choices aloud. In Part A, change your voice or position for the two speakers, or read with a colleague. The delivery notes in brackets are part of the test: the pause before the faint praise, the flat tone of the irony, the drawn-out "Friday… hmm." Deliver them as a real speaker would, then stop; don't add a knowing look.

A · What does the second speaker really mean?
Example, number zero. A: Is Tom a good cook? B: (a short pause) He makes excellent toast. (Show the circle on a.)
Number 1. A: So, what did you think of the new restaurant? B: Well… (pause) the napkins were beautifully folded.
Number 2. A: How did the presentation go? B: (flat, dry) Oh, swimmingly. The projector died, and then so did I.
Number 3. A: Could you review my report before Friday? B: Friday… hmm. (pause) That might be a little tricky, to be honest.
Number 4. Manager: (to an employee, crisply) Have you finally stopped leaving the meeting room in a mess?
Number 5. A: How's the new booking system? B: (calmly, after a long day) Well, it crashed on Monday, we've had about two hundred complaints, and the phones haven't stopped. So we may have a slight issue with it.
B · Dana Price, Town Talk (read the whole commentary twice, then give 1 minute)
Good morning. On this week's Town Talk I want to say a few words about the city's so-called "smart" parking app: the one that, we were promised, would make finding a space downtown "effortless." Admittedly, the app does some things well. Paying from your phone is genuinely convenient, and I'll grant that the old coin meters were hardly a triumph of design. But let's look at the first three months. The council tells us that complaints are "broadly in line with expectations." (brief pause, dry) Presumably, then, the expectations were rather low: the help line received more than four thousand calls in March alone. I spoke to a café owner on Harbor Street who put it rather well. "It's a lovely app," she said, "if you don't need to park." And there's a deeper problem. The app was meant to be a bridge between drivers and the spaces they need; for many older residents it has become a locked gate. If you don't own a smartphone, you now have precisely two options: walk to one of the four remaining pay stations, or pay a fine. So here's my suggestion. Not that we scrap the app, which would be throwing out the baby with the bathwater, but that the council keep a simple card-payment meter on every block until the app actually works for everyone. That, surely, isn't too much to ask.

Pair Speaking Task · 9 minutes

Two learners sit with you. Give Card A to one and Card B to the other and allow one minute of silent preparation. Then check the instructions:

In Part 1, how many versions of your message do you give? (Two, in two different registers.) What has to change between them? (Everything that marks register, not just the vocabulary.) In Part 2, are you allowed to state the problem directly? (No, only by implication.) What does your partner do? (Decode it and respond.) In Part 3, do you have to argue your real opinion? (No, you can take the side on the card.) What do you need to reach by the end? (A decision you both accept.)

Part 1 (about 3 minutes): One message, two registers. Each learner delivers the message on their card twice: first in the formal register described, then informally. Listen for the whole cluster moving together (lexis, grammar, contraction, tone, delivery). Part 2 (about 2 minutes): Say it without saying it. Student A conveys the difficult message on their card entirely by implication; Student B responds naturally, then says what they understood ("So what you're really telling me is…"). Swap for B's message. Part 3 (about 4 minutes): give them the discussion card. Each opens with a short case (30–40 seconds) using one appeal and one figure; then they concede, refute, and reach a decision. If a pair doesn't want to choose sides, assign them: A argues for, B against. Step in only to restart a stuck pair. Don't correct during the task. Score each learner on the grid using the rubric in the key. Mixed-level pairs: score each person on what they did, not on the pair's result.

Discussion card · both students

Part 3 · The proposal

"Our organization should replace its printed annual report with a three-minute video."

  • Open with a short case (30–40 seconds): use at least one appeal (ethos, pathos, or logos) and one figure (a tricolon, an antithesis, a rhetorical question…).
  • Concede your partner's strongest fair point before you refute it.
  • Use at least one stance marker precisely: admittedly, arguably, if anything, to be fair…
  • Reach a decision you can both defend. No preference? Student A argues for, Student B against.
Teacher prompts (keep)

If a pair stalls

Part 1: "Now the same message, as if to a friend." · Part 2: "Try a hint." / "What did you understand?" · Part 3: "What's the strongest point on the other side?" / "So what have you decided?"

Stretch

"Give me the headline version of your decision, then the version for the minutes." (a register shift in both directions)

Support: a learner may glance at the card during Part 1; score what they produce. In Part 2, if the implication is so buried that the partner misses it, prompt once ("Try a hint"), and score the second attempt. Dominant speaker? Turn to the quieter learner: "And where do you stand on that?" If it continues, say "Let's hear from [name] first this time" and note it under Interaction. Don't lower the quieter learner's score because they got fewer chances. Near-native in some areas, fossilized in others: expect a learner whose idiom is flawless but whose register never moves, or whose irony lands but whose stress pattern is L1-driven. Score each criterion separately; that is exactly the profile the grid is designed to show.

After the test: close the loop in Week 7

Score the papers before Class 14 with the key. Add up each section across the class, because the weakest section tells you what to recycle. The warmers in Classes 14–19 are the place for it: a 5-minute "one message, four rooms" relay if the register rewrites (11–13) were weak; a "what did she really mean?" exchange with faint praise and hedged refusals if Part 1A and 3B were weak (it also sets up Lesson 7.1's cross-cultural politeness); a "strip the stance markers" task on a real column if Part 3C and Reading 2–4 were weak.
