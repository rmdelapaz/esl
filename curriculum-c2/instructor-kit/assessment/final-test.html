Final Test — C2 Exit Check

The end-of-course check on all eight modules, taken on the last day, which is also a celebration: the end of this course and, for many learners, of the whole A1–C2 ladder. It shows each learner what they can now do: hear attitude, irony, and implication in extended speech; read a literary text for theme, voice, and technique; write an op-ed, a review, or a synthesis with authority; control the fine points of usage; and sustain an individual interview with a long turn, an abstract discussion under push-back, and a negotiation or diplomatic task with a register shift. It also gives you the evidence for the next step: C2 achieved (with exam entry, professional, teaching, and translation pathways as options), C2 with a review plan, or consolidation at C1/C2 with support. It is never a pass/fail verdict on a person.

~62 min written + ~12 min interview each Written /85 · Speaking /15 · Total /100 Covers Modules 1–8
Frame it as "show what you can do"

By Class 26 your learners have met you 25 times, and yesterday they delivered and defended a masterwork. Use that: "Thirteen weeks ago we were arguing about the difference between frugal and stingy. Yesterday you held a room, took a hard question, conceded the fair part, and kept your argument standing." Tell them plainly that this is not a proficiency exam and doesn't predict one: the Use of English section is written in the style of such exams, with original items, so that anyone thinking of sitting one sees the task types. Do every example item together, keep the room calm, and plan the celebration so it happens whatever the scores are. Every learner gets a certificate of attendance. The test decides the next step, not who gets to celebrate.

What each part checks
  • Listening /15: a curator's talk heard for decision, stance, irony, concession, and image; a fast conversation between colleagues (sarcasm, understatement, a hedged refusal, slang); variety and contrastive stress
  • Reading /15: an original literary passage read for theme, narrative reliability, symbol, imagery, juxtaposition, irony, a periodic sentence, and authorial purpose
  • Use of English /25: original proficiency-style items: open cloze, word formation, five key word transformations (2 pts each), and the fine points of usage
  • Writing /30: an op-ed, a review, or a two-source synthesis (about 300 words), scored with the writing rubric
  • Speaking /15: an individual interview of about 12 minutes: a long turn, an abstract discussion with push-back, and a negotiation or diplomatic task with a register shift
Literacy-adapted version

Same paper, different delivery. Read all instructions and items aloud, enlarge the paper, and give extra time or split it over two sittings. Parts 1–3: accept spoken answers (for the transformations, the learner says the completed sentence and you check it). Part 2: read the passage aloud twice. Part 4: the learner dictates the piece while you scribe, and you score it with the same rubric. Mark the paper "Adapted". For these learners the interview and listening are the main evidence; the key explains how to recognize C2 oral command without holding someone back for reading and writing alone.

Your side, an assigned side, or an imagined example

The interview asks for opinions and, in the long turn, sometimes for an experience. Some learners' experiences of moving between languages involve loss, a profession left behind, or a hard journey. Say before you start: "Real or imagined, general or personal: your choice. And in the discussion I'll argue against you on purpose. That's my job today, not my real opinion." Never ask follow-up questions about home country, family members who are absent, or immigration status. If a learner says "I'd rather not talk about that," thank them and move to the next stage.

The Clock
an original short story, written for this test

¶1They gave me a clock when I retired, which I thought showed a certain sense of humor on the part of the accounts department. It is a handsome thing, walnut and brass, and it now stands on the mantelpiece, where it tells me, with great precision, the time I no longer need to know.

¶2I want to say at once that I did not mind leaving. Writing

WriteChoose one task. Write about 300 words (270–330). Plan for 3–4 minutes first, and leave 3 minutes at the end to edit.
Option A · An op-ed

Your city newspaper is inviting readers' op-eds on this question: "Should schools teach students to write with AI tools rather than ban them?" Write for the paper's general readership: open with a real hook, make your line unmistakable, concede the strongest objection and answer it, marry evidence to a human detail, and close on a line that lands. You may argue either side.

Option B · A review

A cultural magazine is publishing short reviews under the heading "The work that changed how I see." Review a book, film, exhibition, album, or podcast (real or invented). Describe only enough to orient the reader, evaluate through specific, grounded detail, balance praise and criticism, and end with a clear verdict. No spoilers.

Option C · A synthesis

Write an article for a school's newsletter for parents that brings together the two views below on handwriting and typing in class. Organize by idea, not source by source; attribute each view accurately with fair reporting verbs; show where the sources agree and diverge; and end with your own measured conclusion, clearly marked as yours.

Source 1 (a cognitive scientist, in a magazine interview): "When students write notes by hand, they cannot copy every word, so they are forced to decide what matters. That act of selection is where learning happens. Laptops make transcription easy, and understanding optional."

Source 2 (a learning-support teacher, on her blog): "For many of my students with dyslexia or motor difficulties, handwriting is not a route to understanding but a wall in front of it. Typing lets them keep up with the class. The question is not which tool is better, but which tool lets this student think."

Part 1 · Listening Scripts

Give 45 seconds to read the Part A questions and 30 seconds for each later section. Read each script twice at a natural pace, with about 10 seconds between readings. Read Part A as a real talk: warm, unhurried, with the pauses marked. Notice that it mentions the wrong option first ("my first instinct was…") before the real decision, exactly as Lesson 8.1 warned. Don't signal the switch with your voice beyond what the words do. For Part B, change your voice or position for each speaker, or read it with a colleague, at a brisk conversational speed, with Omar's sarcasm flat and deadpan. In Part C, say the number, then the sentence once per reading, with the marked stress. Don't read the answer choices aloud.

A · Dr. Elena Voss, "Why we kept the crack" (read the whole talk twice, then give 1 minute)
Good evening, and thank you for coming out on such a wet night. I'm told the umbrella stand downstairs is now, technically, an installation.
Tonight I want to talk about a single object: the blue-and-white bowl in Case Twelve, the one with the crack running right through the middle. When it came to us, three years ago, the obvious question was how to hide that crack. I'll be honest: my first instinct was to fill it and paint over it, so that visitors would see the bowl as it looked on the day it was made. In the end, though, we did the opposite. We kept the crack visible, and I want to explain why.
Arguably, a restorer's first duty is to an object's history, not to its original appearance. And this bowl's history includes that crack. The records suggest it was broken, and mended, at least twice, once by someone who, judging by the quality of the work, was not a professional, and cared about it a great deal. That repair is not damage to the story; it is part of the story.
Now, I'm not going to pretend everyone agreed. One of our trustees described the bowl as, and I quote, "a lovely piece, if you stand far enough away." (pause; dry) We took that under advisement.
Admittedly, there are objects where invisible restoration is the right choice: a painting, say, where the damage would distract from everything the artist intended. I'm not arguing that every crack is sacred. What I am arguing is that we should stop treating the marks of use as a kind of embarrassment. A museum that erases every scar is not preserving the past; it is airbrushing it.
Tellingly, the bowl has become one of the most photographed objects in the gallery. Visitors stand in front of it longer than almost anything else on that floor. Why? I suspect it's because a perfect object asks only to be admired, whereas a mended one asks a question: who loved this enough to fix it?
So when you walk through the galleries tonight, I'd invite you to look for the repairs rather than past them. They're the places where the objects are still talking.

Individual Speaking Interview · about 12 minutes

Sit beside the learner, not across a desk, with the grid on your lap. Speak naturally, at your normal pace. Ask the core question first; if there's no answer after about 5 seconds, use the support version; if the answer comes easily, add the stretch. At C2 you are listening for effortless precision and appropriacy: a long turn that is shaped and memorable, abstract reasoning that bends under challenge without breaking, idiom and register that feel chosen rather than learned, and tact that never blurs the message. Many C2 learners are near-native in some areas and fossilized in others (a stubborn article pattern, an L1 stress habit, one register they never leave). Score what you hear on each criterion separately. Don't correct during the interview. Before stage 1, let the learner choose a long-turn card and think about it quietly (about 1 minute) while you do the warm-up.

2

Long turn

~3½ min
Please talk about the card you chose, for about two and a half minutes. I'll just listen.

Don't interrupt. Time about 2½ minutes; stop kindly at 3. Then ask one follow-up question about the content. Support: if they freeze, "What's your main point?" then "Give me an example." · Stretch: "What's the most persuasive thing someone could say against you?" or "Say that last point again, as you'd put it to a skeptical expert." Listen for a hook and a resonant close, signposting that doesn't creak, a controlled figure or image, precisely placed stance markers (arguably, if anything, tellingly), and delivery that uses pause and stress for effect.

3

Abstract discussion with push-back

~3½ min

Read one statement below. Whatever the learner says, take the other side and push back twice, politely but firmly. Make the second push-back sharper: challenge an assumption, offer a counter-example, or slip in a mildly loaded question (Lesson 2.1) and see whether they refuse the premise. Your push-back is a role, not your opinion.

Here's a statement: [statement]. Where do you stand? … Why? … I'm not sure that follows. Surely [counter-argument]? … But isn't that just [loaded reformulation]? … So, on balance, where does that leave you?

Support: "Do you agree or disagree?" then "Why?" and one push-back only · Stretch: "Put the other side's case as fairly as you can." · "What would it take to change your mind?" Listen for concession without collapse, a refined final position, hedging calibrated to the argument, precise abstract vocabulary, and a presupposition noticed and declined ("That assumes…, which I'd question").

4

Negotiation or diplomatic task + register shift

~3½ min

The learner chooses one task card. Part 1 (about 2½ minutes): you play the other person and react naturally, with one real objection and one mild push. Part 2 (about 1 minute): ask for the outcome in two registers: the one-sentence version for the formal minutes, then the voice message to a friend.

(Festival director) Thanks for coming in. I have to say, our budget is basically fixed. What did you want to raise? … Twelve percent? That's a very hard sell for us. … What could you offer in return?
(Applicant) Thanks for calling. I've been waiting to hear. So, how did it go? … Oh. Can I ask what the reason was? … So is there any point applying again?
(New partner) Hi. Yes, sorry, I've been meaning to get back to you about Thursday. … Well… it's quite challenging. … What would you suggest?
(Switch) Thanks. Now, one sentence for the minutes, please. … And the voice message you'd send a friend about how it went?

Listen for the moves of Lessons 7.1–7.2: acknowledge → counter, a conditional trade ("If you were able to…, then we could…"), a face-saving bridge, a difficult message stated clearly and early, then cushioned, and the hedge decoded as a refusal and repaired without blame. In Part 2, listen for two genuinely different registers, each internally consistent. Support: model the first line ("I'll be straight with you: I need to raise something difficult"). Stretch: push back a second time, harder, and see whether they stay calm and assertive.

5

Closing + your question

~½ min
Thank you, that's everything. Before you go, is there anything you'd like to ask me? About the course, what comes next, anything at all.

Plain or ornate?

"The plainest word is almost always the best one." Is it? When does ornament (a figure, a rare word, a long periodic sentence) earn its place, and when does it get in the way? Use examples from writing or speech you know.

Long turn · Mediation

Something lost, something carried

A word, a joke, a proverb, or an idea that does not cross easily between two languages or cultures (yours, or ones you know about). What gets lost, and how would you carry as much as possible across?

Long turn · Interpretation

A text that changed how you see

A book, film, speech, poem, or article (it may be imagined or described in general terms). Not just what it said, but how its craft worked on you: its voice, an image, a turn, its ending.

Long turn · Evaluation

The measure of mastery

Can any exam truly measure mastery of a language? What does a test capture well, what does it miss, and what would a fairer measure look like?
