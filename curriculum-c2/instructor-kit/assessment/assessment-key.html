Scoring principles

Score the choice, not the key's wording. At C2 many items have several defensible answers: the register rewrites, the "what is really meant" items, the reading short answers, the gist and paraphrase. Award the point whenever the answer does the job the item names; the key gives a model and the line between "does it" and "doesn't". If a learner has written a margin note defending an unexpected answer, read it: a well-argued alternative is itself C2 evidence. Score meaning, not spelling, in Listening and Reading. In the one-word items (Progress Part 2B, Final Parts 3A–B) the word itself is being tested, so it must be right, though a pure spelling slip (resouding for resounding) still earns the point where the word is unmistakable. Circle items are right or wrong. Half points are not used. The key word transformations are scored in two parts (1 + 1), as proficiency exams do.

US and UK forms. The tests use American English; accept British forms everywhere. The online lessons are written in British spelling and use British examples freely (summarising, colour, programme, flat, lorry, cuppa, gutted, whilst, single quotation marks, "full stop" for period), and Lesson 5.1 teaches both systems. Three points to know: ① the mandative subjunctive (recommends that she step down) is standard in American English; British should step down is also correct; only the indicative steps earns no point in Final item 20. ② Collective nouns may take a plural verb in British English (the committee are); never penalize it. ③ Final item 14 turns on a real US/UK difference taught in Lesson 5.1 (first floor); it tests awareness, not preference.

Part 5 · Writing /12
  • Task & rhetoric 0–3. 3 = a real hook, a clear line, all three appeals, two named figures that do work, and a concession that is answered; 2 = one element weak (often the refutation, or a figure that decorates rather than argues); 1 = a list of reasons with no persuasive design, or under about 120 words; 0 = off task.
  • Organization & cohesion 0–3. 3 = the opening builds, turns, and lands, with invisible signposting; 2 = clear but mechanical ("Firstly… Secondly…"); 1 = points in no evident order; 0 = incoherent.
  • Precision & range 0–3. 3 = the mot juste throughout, connotation used deliberately, fresh images, no mixed metaphor or stale cliché; 2 = precise with one or two near-misses or a tired phrase; 1 = general vocabulary (good, bad, a lot of) or a mixed metaphor; 0 = too limited to judge.
  • Register & accuracy 0–3. 3 = a consistent spoken-public register and near-flawless control; 2 = one register slip or occasional minor errors; 1 = frequent slips or errors that distract; 0 = meaning often lost.

Model answer (12/12, about 185 words):

Last Sunday I timed my walk down Main Street. Four minutes to cover two blocks, not because of the crowds, but because I waited at three crossings for cars that were, for the most part, simply looking for somewhere to park. [hook]

I've run the bookshop on this street for eleven years [ethos], and I want to argue that one car-free day a week would be the best thing to happen to it in a decade. [line]

I'll grant that some of my neighbors fear lost trade: if customers can't park at the door, will they come at all? It's a fair question. [concession] But towns that have tried it tell a different story. In Riverton, Sunday footfall rose by almost a third in the first year. [logos · refutation]

Picture it for a moment: children chalking on the asphalt, café tables spilling into the road, an elderly couple crossing without a second glance. [pathos · tricolon] We are not closing a street; we are opening one. [antithesis]

So what, exactly, do we lose by trying it for one summer? [rhetorical question]

Invented but plausible figures are acceptable in a test task (the model's "Riverton" is invented); in real life, say so and cite.

Errors to expect (Progress Test 1)
  • Prosody and connotation near-misses: caused a breakthrough (item 2) and amused for bemused (item 3). These survive to C2 precisely because they never block meaning. Recycle Lesson 1.1's "hear it in stereo" test: meaning in one channel, company and feeling in the other.
  • Garbled or reordered chunks: mute point, large and by, null and invalid. The fix is a verbatim phrasebook, not a rule (Lesson 1.3).
  • Half-shifting in the rewrites: formal vocabulary with casual grammar ("Staff must make sure they've…"), or the reverse; and over-pitching to frozen legalese in item 11 or to cold impersonality in item 13. At C2 the register clang is the error that matters most, so name it clearly and kindly.
  • Literal readings of implicature items (14: "he was well dressed and punctual"). A learner who does this on paper usually hears the implication perfectly in conversation: reassure them that the skill is there and the task is to put the unsaid into words.
  • Stance marker slips: undoubtedly for a concession (18), plus in a formal essay (20). Reading: naming the topic instead of the line (item 1: "the library"), and choosing the patchwrite (item 11a) because it "sounds more formal."
  • Writing: figures bolted on and labeled but doing no work; a concession with no refutation; the throat-clearing hook ("In today's world, cars are a big issue"); and the mixed metaphor born of half-dead idioms ("we need to grab this opportunity before it sails away"). Praise the ambition, then ask: "Can you picture it?"

Model answer, Option A (C2 strong mastery, 30/30, about 300 words)

Let Them Write With the Machine, and Teach Them to Argue With It

Last spring my nephew handed in an essay on the causes of the First World War that was, by any measure, flawless. It was also, he admitted cheerfully, not his. The software had written it in eleven seconds. What struck me was not the cheating but the shrug: he genuinely could not see what he had lost.

The obvious reply is to ban the tools, and I understand the instinct. Schools exist to build minds, and a mind that outsources every sentence builds nothing. But a ban is a wall around a single classroom in a world where the machine sits in every pocket. Students will use it at home, at university, and at work; the only question is whether anyone ever teaches them to use it well.

What would that look like? Not "write me an essay," but "here is a draft the machine produced: find its three weakest claims, check its sources, and rewrite its conclusion so that it says something true." A student who can do that has learned more about argument than one who copies a paragraph from a textbook, and far more than one who is simply forbidden to look.

None of this is to deny the risk. Some students will take the shortcut whatever we do, and teachers will need time, training, and new kinds of assessment (more writing in class, more oral defense of written work) to tell thinking from copying. That is a real cost, and districts should fund it rather than pretend it away.

But the choice is not between the machine and the mind. It is between students who are quietly used by these tools and students who know how to use them, and to argue back. My nephew, to his credit, now does the second. It took one teacher who refused to shrug.

Model answer, Option C (C2 strong mastery, 30/30, about 290 words)

Pen or Keyboard? The Wrong Question

Few classroom debates generate as much heat as the one over laptops, and it is tempting to treat it as a contest with a winner. The two views gathered here suggest instead that the argument turns less on the tool than on what it does for a particular learner's thinking.

Both writers, notably, care about the same thing: understanding, not neat notes. A cognitive scientist argues that handwriting forces students to select, since no one can copy a lecture word for word by hand, and that this act of selection is where learning takes place. Typing, in her view, makes transcription effortless and comprehension optional.

A learning-support teacher does not dispute that selection matters. She points out, however, that for many of her students with dyslexia or motor difficulties, handwriting is less a route to understanding than an obstacle in front of it; typing is what allows them to keep pace with the class at all. Where the scientist sees a shortcut, the teacher sees a ramp.

Taken together, the two accounts suggest that the scientist's claim holds for the typical student but not for every student, and that the teacher's reframing ("which tool lets this student think?") absorbs the scientist's insight rather than contradicting it. A laptop used to transcribe everything may indeed make understanding optional; a laptop used by a student for whom a pen is a wall may be the only way understanding can happen.

My own conclusion, for what it is worth, is that schools should stop issuing blanket rules. Teach every student to take selective notes, whichever tool they use, and let the choice of tool follow the learner rather than the policy.

What makes these C2 strong mastery: a hook that earns the next line; a line or governing idea stated early; the best objection conceded and answered; reporting verbs that are fair to both sources (argues, points out, does not dispute); a controlled image that carries an argument ("a shortcut… a ramp"); and a close that lands. For Option B, look for the same qualities in review form: minimal description, grounded evaluation through a specific detail, calibrated praise and criticism, and a verdict the reader can act on (Lesson 6.1).

Errors to expect (Final Test)
  • Listening: choosing the first-mentioned option in item 1 ("fill and paint"), exactly the trap Lesson 8.1 describes; and literal readings of Omar's sarcasm (item 9) by learners who follow the words but not the flat delivery. The second is a delivery-reading issue (Lessons 2.2 and 5.3), not a vocabulary gap.
  • Reading: naming the subject instead of the theme (item 1: "a man retires"); technique-spotting without effect ("it is a simile"); and taking the narrator at his word (item 2). Learners who read a great deal in their first language often shine here; learners from strongly exam-driven systems may hunt for a "right answer." Reassure them that a well-supported reading is the right answer.
  • Use of English: that for what (2), rose for risen (13), willingness with the prefix forgotten (7), and transformations that exceed the word limit or alter the key word. These are technique leaks, fixable in a week of targeted practice if the learner plans to sit an exam.
  • Usage: "between you and I" (18) survives in many highly educated speakers of English, native and not; and the dangling participle (19) is often invisible to the writer. Point to Lesson 6.3's "whose action is it?" test.
  • Op-ed: essay register in disguise ("It is important to note that…"), a throat-clearing opening, and a close that summarizes instead of landing. Review: the plot recap, and unrelieved praise. Synthesis: the source-by-source march, loaded reporting verbs on the view the writer dislikes (claims, alleges), and a conclusion that simply picks a winner.
  • Across all options: over-ornamentation, the C2 version of over-formality: a sentence carrying a metaphor, an inversion, and a tricolon at once. Lesson 4.2's "kill your darlings" is the kindest possible correction.

Speaking Rubric · Both Tests

Five criteria, each 0–3 (total /15). 0 = no response, or only L1. The C2 column paraphrases the Council of Europe's C2 descriptors for spoken production and interaction, range, accuracy, fluency, coherence, sociolinguistic appropriateness, and phonological control (CEFR Companion Volume, 2020). The strong mastery column describes performance at the top of C2 as this course defines it. For the progress test, judge against Modules 1–4 only.

Task & coherence /3 Solid C1: well-structured and clear, but the long turn is organized rather than shaped; the argument holds under push-back by repetition rather than refinement; the diplomatic outcome is reached but blurred. Produces clear, smoothly flowing, well-structured speech with an effective logical structure that helps the listener notice and remember significant points. Creates coherent, cohesive discourse, making full and appropriate use of a variety of organizational patterns and cohesive devices. The talk is memorable as well as clear: a hook, a turn, and a close that lands. Under challenge, the position is refined with visible intellectual honesty; every task outcome is unmistakable.
Precision & range /3 A broad C1 range, but near-synonyms, collocations, and fixed expressions are sometimes approximate ("a loud success," "a mute point"); abstract ideas are expressed through general words; complex grammar slips when attention moves to the argument. Shows great flexibility in reformulating ideas in differing linguistic forms to convey finer shades of meaning precisely, give emphasis, differentiate, and eliminate ambiguity. The mot juste, reliably and apparently without search; idiom and restricted collocation used with a native speaker's exactness; a figure or image deployed once, at the right moment, for effect.

Register moves as a whole system, deliberately and invisibly; implicature, understatement, and irony are controlled and deniable; a loaded premise is noticed and courteously declined; firmness and warmth coexist.
Interaction /3 Takes part skillfully, but responds to the words rather than the intent; concedes too readily or not at all; needs help to steer toward an outcome. Can interact with ease and skill, picking up and using non-verbal and intonational cues apparently effortlessly. Can interweave their contribution into the joint discourse with fully natural turn-taking, referencing, and allusion. Reads the other speaker's hints and tone and answers the question beneath the question; concedes the fair point and narrows to the claim that survives; builds a face-saving bridge or a conditional trade that both sides can defend.
Fluency & delivery /3 Fluent and spontaneous on most topics, but noticeable searching on abstract ones; prosody is correct but seldom used for effect; irony or reservation is carried by words alone. Can express themselves at length with a natural, effortless, unhesitating flow, pausing only to reflect on precisely the right words. Can exploit the full range of phonological features (stress, rhythm, intonation) with a high level of control to convey finer shades of meaning. Delivery is part of the meaning: the planned pause, the contrastive stress, the flat irony, the warmth of the formal register and the ease of the informal one. Accent, whatever it is, has no effect on clarity or authority.

Writing Rubric · Final Test Part 4

Five criteria, each 0–3 (/15), then × 2 = /30. 0 = nothing written, or nothing in English. At C2 the learner can write clear, smoothly flowing, complex texts in an appropriate and effective style, with a logical structure that helps the reader find the significant points; can produce articles, reviews, and essays that present a case or give a critical appreciation of a work; and can summarize and synthesize information and arguments from different sources in a coherent presentation (paraphrased from the CEFR). An assigned side is fine: score the craft, never the opinion.

CriterionNot yet C2 (1)C2 (2)C2 strong mastery (3)
Task & content /3 Under about 220 words; a topic discussed rather than a line argued; a review that recaps; a synthesis that summarizes each source separately or distorts one. Op-ed: a sharp line, the best objection conceded and answered, evidence married to a human detail. Review: minimal description, grounded evaluation, a clear verdict. Synthesis: both sources represented accurately, agreement and divergence identified, a conclusion clearly marked as the writer's own. The piece has something to say that a thoughtful reader would remember: the concession is steel-manned, the verdict is calibrated, or the synthesis reframes the question rather than merely reporting it.
Organization & cohesion /3 Clear paragraphs but a mechanical architecture ("Firstly… In conclusion…"); a source-by-source march; connectors that assert relations the ideas don't have. A genre-appropriate shape (hook → line → concession → evidence → landing; description → evaluation → verdict; governing idea → agreement → divergence → conclusion); given-before-new flow; lexical chains and reference that carry the reader. The joins are felt rather than flagged; the close echoes or reframes the opening; the one-sentence-per-paragraph skeleton reads as a complete argument.
Range & precision /3 Accurate but general vocabulary; clichés (game-changer, at the end of the day); figures that decorate or collide; patchwriting from the sources. The mot juste, precise reporting verbs and stance markers, fresh images, fixed expressions exactly right, and a full range of structures used naturally rather than displayed. Word choice carries stance by itself (connotation used deliberately); one controlled image does argumentative work; nothing is ornament for its own sake.
Accuracy /3 Occasional errors that a careful reader notices: a dangler, a comma splice, a which for that that changes meaning, agreement after a long subject. Consistent grammatical and orthographic control of complex language; the fine points of usage and punctuation are right; errors are rare slips. Punctuation used for rhythm and emphasis (the semicolon that balances, the dash that turns); no errors a copy editor would change.
Register, tone & effect /3 Essay register in an op-ed, or a tone that wobbles between formal and chatty; claims over- or under-hedged; the reader's attention is not earned. Register and tone pitched to the named readership and held consistently; a distinct voice that serves the reader; hedging calibrated to the evidence. The voice is unmistakable yet never self-indulgent; the piece would stand publication in the outlet named; the last sentence is the one a reader carries away.

A note on IELTS equivalence (approximate only)

Learners will ask, "What IELTS band is this?" Answer honestly, and consistently with the C1 kit: as a rough guide only, a secure C1 corresponds to about IELTS 7.0–8.0, and C2 to about 8.5–9. Published tables differ slightly at the boundaries, which is why Lesson 8.1 speaks more loosely of "band 8 and above" as proficiency territory: a strong C1+ user may well score 8. These are broad, published alignments, not a conversion of this test's score. No classroom test can predict an official band or grade, and proficiency exams reward format knowledge and timing that this course only introduces. A learner who needs a specific result should practice with the official materials and take at least one full timed practice test before booking. This kit is independent and is not affiliated with or endorsed by Cambridge English, IELTS, or any exam board.

These are guidelines. Your thirteen weeks of observation count as much as one test on one day, especially the Class 12 and Class 25 masterworks: a learner who held a room and fielded a hard question yesterday has shown you what C2 looks like. A learner who was ill, anxious, or grieving on test day deserves a re-test or a quick re-check before any "consolidate" recommendation. Record the outcome and a one-line reason for your program.

Giving Feedback Kindly

Principles
  • Private, always. Never read scores aloud or return papers in score order.
  • Strength first, then one thing to refine. One precise next step, not a list of errors.
  • Name the progress. Compare with Week 1, not with classmates.
  • Precision, appropriacy, naturalness, effect. At C2 the useful feedback is almost never "that's wrong." It is "that's correct, and here is the version that would land harder, sit better in that room, or sound less translated."
  • Near-native and fossilized at once. Many C2 learners have one stubborn habit (an article pattern, a stress pattern, one register they never leave) inside otherwise flawless English. Name it once, precisely, with an example of their own, and don't let it overshadow everything else.
  • Respect the expert. Many learners are translators, teachers, or specialists. Ask what they noticed first, and give feedback as one professional to another.
  • A written note to take home, with the lesson number and URL.
  • Adults decide. Offer the recommendation and the reason, and let the learner choose, especially about exams.
Phrases that work at C2
You did an outstanding job. The way you conceded the fair point and then narrowed your claim was genuinely masterful. Your opening made the second sentence unavoidable. That image did real argumentative work; it wasn't decoration. One refinement: your register held beautifully until the last line. Read it aloud and you'll hear the clang. Do Lesson 1.2 again at home and keep collecting clangs from real emails. This is C2. If you want the certificate, you're ready to start exam practice. One more lap. This time it'll feel like polishing, not learning. It has been a privilege to teach you.
Thirteen weeks ago we were arguing about frugal and stingy. Yesterday you held a room and kept your argument standing under a hard question.
Your speaking is unmistakably C2. Your writing needs a little more time, so let's find a writing partner or a writing center to go with it.
Avoid

Red ink everywhere, percentages without meaning ("68%"), the word "fail", comparisons ("Ana got 92"), and correcting a learner's accent as though it were an error: at C2, intelligibility and control are the standard, not a "native" sound (Lesson 5.1). Don't grade opinions: a learner who argued the opposite of your view, or an assigned side, with craft and courtesy has done exactly what C2 asks. Don't push anyone toward an exam they don't need. Highly accomplished adults can take a "consolidate" recommendation hard; if a learner goes quiet or gets upset, pause the conversation and come back to it. The number can wait.
