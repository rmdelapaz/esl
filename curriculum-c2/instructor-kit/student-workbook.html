The Mot Juste — Precision & Nuance

Three lessons on choosing exactly. First the single word: its connotation, its force, and the company it habitually keeps. Then the whole manner of a message: register as a mixing desk, with tone as a dial of its own. No true synonyms. Near-synonyms differ in at least one dimension.

Forcetepid → warm → hot → scorching · decent → good → excellent → superb → sublime

Semantic prosody. set in keeps unpleasant company (rot, despair, winter), so "happiness set in" jars. cause leans negative (damage, delay, concern), so a success is better brought about. Likewise utterly (ruined), bordering on (rudeness), rife with (corruption), a spate of (burglaries), a breeding ground for (resentment).

Precision is not ornament. The mot juste is often the plain, specific word. She trudged home beats she walked slowly and wearily; use usually beats utilize.

field = what the text is about · tenor = the relationship between the people (power and distance) · mode = spoken or written, planned or spontaneous · a clang = one marker that belongs to a different register

Grammar box

One message, five levels. The fact stays the same; everything else moves together.

1 · FrozenNotice is hereby given that the premises will close at 3:00 p.m. on Friday, June 12.
2 · FormalPlease be advised that the community center will close early on Friday.
3 · NeutralJust a reminder that we're closing at 3 on Friday.
4 · InformalHeads up, we're shutting early Friday.
5 · IntimateFriday, 3 o'clock, doors shut. Don't be late!
Markers of HIGH registerLatinate words (ascertain, remuneration) · nominalization · passive and impersonal (It was decided that…) · hedging · long, subordinated sentences · no contractions · furthermore, notwithstanding, thereby
Markers of LOW registerphrasal verbs (sort out, put off) · contractions and ellipsis (Got a minute?) · you know, I mean, anyway · vague language (stuff, sort of) · idiom and slang · direct address and tags (…right?)

Tone is a second dial. Formality sets the ceremony; tone sets the attitude. Same level, opposite tone:

WarmThanks so much for flagging this; it's really helpful. I'll get it fixed today and let you know the moment it's done.
CoolNoted. This will be addressed in due course. You will be informed upon completion.

Other tones: ironic (over-grand language on a trivial subject), authoritative (boosters, imperatives, no hedging), deferential (hedges, questions, I wonder if…).

Move the whole cluster. One stray marker produces a clang: "Please find attached the aforementioned pics." Over-formality (utilize, commence, endeavor) sounds inflated rather than educated; slang in a serious setting punctures authority.

Every chunk has a register. to cut a long story short (spoken) · that said (safe in writing) · for what it's worth (modest, conversational) · at the end of the day (worn to cliché: ration it). Fixed similes (as easy as pie, as dry as a bone) are informal by nature.

Exact or nothing. Not a mute point but a moot point; not nip it in the butt but nip it in the bud; not peaked my interest but piqued; not on tender hooks but on tenterhooks. And ration them: rarely more than one fixed expression per sentence.

Grammar box — the machinery of the unsaid

Four expectations we have of a cooperative speaker, and what we infer when one is visibly broken:

Quantity: say enough"Is she good at her job?" "She's very punctual." → She has no better quality worth mentioning.
Quality: say what's trueIn a downpour: "Well, that was a lovely day out." → It was a disaster (irony).
Relation: be relevant"What did you think of my presentation?" "Shall we get coffee?" → I'd rather not say.
Manner: be clear"Did they sing?" "They produced a series of sounds corresponding to the score." → It was barely singing.

Presupposition triggers. A presupposition survives negation: "Your team is still behind" and "Your team isn't still behind" both assume it was behind.

stop · start · keepHave you stopped cutting corners? → you were cutting corners
again · still · yetAre you late again? → you've been late before
even · onlyEven Tom passed. → Tom was the least likely to pass
the · possessivesthe mistake in your figures → there is a mistake
realize · regret · admitWhen did you realize the plan was flawed? → it was flawed
manage toDid you manage to finish? → finishing was difficult

Refusing the premise. Never answer a loaded question with yes or no. Name the assumption, reject it, reframe: That question assumes X, which isn't the case. What I can tell you is…

Slant in writing comes from four levers: diction (insisted rather than said), omission (the question not asked), juxtaposition (two facts placed side by side) and distancing markers (reportedly, allegedly, apparently, so-called, scare quotes).

Two virtues. Ask first: is the wiring missing, or the thought?

Cohesive, not coherentThe committee approved the budget. However, the budget is a document. Moreover, paper comes from trees. (links everywhere; no thought)

Grammar box — Architecture and sources

An outline is an argument in miniature. Give every section a one-line claim, not a topic. Then read the claims in order: if you could reorder them without loss, you have a list, not an argument.

Backward signpostAs noted in the previous section, … · Having established that…, we now turn to…
Forward signpostThis section argues that… · The following section turns to… · This point is developed further in Section 4.
Locational signpostThe remainder of this report is organized as follows: … · At this point it is worth stepping back…

The Curse of Knowledge

Anyone who has asked a brilliant colleague for directions and received a baffling answer has met what psychologists call the curse of knowledge. (1) The expert has not become careless; the difficulty lies in the very thing that makes them expert.

Dialogue — a masterwork and its discussion · Listen · Read

Speaker: I've come to believe that fluency in a language is the least interesting thing about knowing it. [pause] Fluency is the door. What lies beyond it, the humor you can finally catch, the argument you can finally win on its own terms, the stranger who becomes a friend because you met them in their own words: that is the room. And I spent years, frankly, mistaking the door for the room.

Speaker: So when people ask me why anyone would climb all the way to mastery (why not stop at "good enough"?), I answer with a question of my own. Good enough for what? Good enough to be understood, yes. But not, perhaps, good enough to be moved, or to move someone else.

Discussant: That's a lovely image, but isn't there something a little elitist in it? Most people will never reach mastery, and they live perfectly rich lives in their language all the same.

Speaker: That's a genuinely important challenge, and you're right to press it; I'd hate for this to sound like a hierarchy of worth. Let me draw the distinction I actually mean. I'm not saying mastery makes a person richer; I'm saying it opens particular rooms: a poem in the original, a joke that doesn't survive translation. Those rooms aren't better than others. They're simply closed until you have the key.

Discussant: And for someone just beginning that climb, what would you say?

Speaker: That the summit they're dreading is closer than they think, and that the view, when they reach it, is not an ending but a door of its own.
