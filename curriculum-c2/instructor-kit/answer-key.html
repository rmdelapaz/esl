Module 1 — The Mot Juste: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the tuned texts, the five voices, the audits), and the errors and near-misses you can expect from C2 learners.

Lessons 1.1–1.3Classes C1–C39 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, naturalness, appropriacy and effect, not just accuracy. Most items in this module have several defensible answers, so the models below are examples. Accept any answer that (1) keeps the meaning and every fact, (2) is what a proficient user would actually say (the right partner word, the fixed order, no prosody clash), and (3) suits the reader and the room. When a learner defends an unexpected choice convincingly ("I chose curt because she's cross but controlled"), accept it: the ability to justify a choice is the skill. Where connotation labels are genuinely borderline (forceful, old-fashioned, anxious), mark the argument, not the label. American English is the model; accept British spellings and vocabulary (colour, utilise, judgement, touch wood).

1.1 · Fine Shades of Meaning — The Mot Juste (C1)

First-day writing sample

No key: score it with the four-row rubric (precision, range, naturalness, register & tone control) in the classroom materials, Set B. Never return a score to the learner; return a short, private note with one strength and one focus, quoting their own words.

Exercise 2 — Connotation sort
  1. inquisitive + (or 0) · curious 0 · nosy − · prying −
  2. assertive + · forceful 0 (or ±: praise in a debate, criticism in a meeting) · pushy − · domineering −
  3. traditional 0 · old-fashioned − (or 0: "old-fashioned courtesy" is praise) · dated − · classic +
  4. youthful + · childlike + (innocence, wonder) · childish − · immature −

Accept any borderline label with a convincing example. The teaching point: connotation often shifts with the collocate (old-fashioned values vs old-fashioned software).

Exercise 3 — Up the cline
  1. irritated → annoyed → furious → livid (circle livid: informal and emotive for a report)
  2. tepid → warm → hot → scorching
  3. am not keen on → dislike → detest / loathe (the last two are close; accept either order) (circle am not keen on: conversational, understated)
  4. decent → good → excellent → superb → sublime (sublime is rare in reports; accept a circle)

Wrong: putting annoyed below irritated is a common near-miss; they are very close, so accept it if the learner can argue it. Putting tepid above warm is a genuine error: tepid is lukewarm and, figuratively, unenthusiastic (a tepid response).

Exercise 4 — One exact word (models)
  1. muttered / grumbled
  2. chided / sighed / remarked
  3. exclaimed / cheered / whooped
  4. trudged / plodded
  5. glanced
  6. wolfed down / devoured
  7. scrutinized / pored over
  8. strolled / ambled / sauntered

Judge by manner and register. Near-misses: "whispered" for 1 (quiet, but not resentful), "stared" for 5 (long, not quick), "marched" for 4 (energetic, the opposite). Sauntered in 8 adds a hint of confidence or showing off; accept it and ask what it adds. In 6, "devoured the whole pizza" needs the word order changed: The children devoured the whole pizza.

Exercise 5 — Spot the prosody clash
  1. caused → brought about / secured / led to a landmark victory
  2. set in → settled over / descended on the valley (set in favors unpleasant, gradual states)
  3. a breeding ground for → a catalyst for / fostered / gave rise to (though gave rise to also leans slightly negative; sparked is ideal)
  4. rife with → teeming with / buzzing with / full of cheerful helpers
  5. a spate of → a string of / a run of awards

None of the originals is ungrammatical, and that is the point. If a learner argues that 3 or 5 is deliberately ironic ("a spate of awards" as a joke about too many), accept it as a stretch answer: prosody clash can be a stylistic device, provided it is chosen.

Dialogue — tuning one sentence

Omar's changes: very good → attentive, unhurried service (specificity, force) · cheap → reasonably priced or a bargain (connotation: cheap can imply poor quality) · sublime, magnificent and transcendent → sublime alone (restraint: overwriting weakens force). Note Omar's own utterly forgettable: utterly with its typical negative company, used exactly right. The test at the end, "Does the word serve, or does it show off?", is the workshop's peer-review question.

Speaking — Mot Juste Editing Workshop

Models for all four texts, with the gains labeled by dimension, are printed on the text cards (classroom materials, Set D). Check the log: four changes, each with a named dimension and a reason that goes beyond "sounds better." Fail a version that invents facts (numbers, names, events) or that reads as a thesaurus exercise. Strong log entry: "cheap → unpretentious: connotation; cheap suggests poor quality, unpretentious praises simplicity." Weak log entry: "nice → magnificent: more advanced word."

Self-check
Q1
Which word means "puzzled, bewildered" rather than "amused"?
A) astonished
B) bemused
C) startled
Why: bemused means bewildered; the confusion with amused is common even among native speakers. Astonished = very surprised; startled = suddenly, briefly alarmed.
Q2
Semantic prosody explains why which sentence sounds subtly wrong?
A) A deep contentment set in.
B) A cold, damp winter set in.
C) Rot had set in.
Why: set in habitually introduces unpleasant, gradual states (rot, despair, bad weather), so pairing it with contentment clashes. B and C are exactly the company it keeps.
Q3 · new
"The chair thanked the speaker in fulsome terms." In its careful sense, the praise was…
A) generous and entirely sincere
B) excessive, perhaps insincere
C) brief and formal
Why: traditionally, fulsome praise is overdone to the point of insincerity. Many speakers now use it to mean "generous," which is why careful writers avoid it when the sense matters. At C2, knowing both, and knowing the risk, is the point.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.1)

Prosody clashes, especially cause + a positive result ("caused a big success") · connotation false friends ("an aggressive, ambitious marketing" meant as neutral; "propaganda for the new product") · bemused for amused · thesaurus inflation (utilize, commence, endeavor, purchase in friendly writing) · overwriting: three superlatives where one would do · weak word + modifier ("very big," "walked slowly") · fossilized accuracy errors that survive to C2 (informations, advices, I am agree, since three years, discuss about): note them privately; don't let them crowd out the lesson's focus. For range, praise understatement (It isn't cheap) and the exact verb. For effect, praise any learner who changed a verdict with a single word.

Exercise 2 — Label the register and the tone (models)
  1. Formal / frozen · cool-neutral, business-polite · conditional inversion (Should you require), Latinate assistance, the undersigned, no contractions
  2. Informal · warm but rueful · Right, so, long story short, fell through, ellipsis, honestly as a tag
  3. Neutral · ironic (sarcastic) · flawless in loaded emphasis; "What could possibly go wrong" with no question mark, said flat
  4. Formal–neutral · deferential, tentative · I wonder if, might, the formal our looking (possessive + -ing), a question for a proposal

Accept any accurate level with a supporting marker. Judge item 3 by effect: learners who label it "positive" have missed the tone, which is exactly what Module 2 will train.

Exercise 3 — Slide it up and down the cline (models)

(a) Frozen: "Abstracts must be submitted no later than 5:00 p.m. on Friday. Submissions received after that time cannot be considered under any circumstances." (Passive, impersonal, no contractions, precise time, under any circumstances.) Alternative: "Notice is hereby given that the deadline for the submission of abstracts is Friday; late submissions will not be accepted."

(b) Informal: "Don't forget your abstract's due Friday! They're really strict; if it's late, it's out." (Contractions, exclamation, it's out, direct address.)

Check: does every marker agree? A frozen version with "we can't" or an informal one with "submissions" has half-shifted.

Exercise 4 — Find and fix the clang (models)
  1. you absolute legend clashes with the formal congratulation → "…after thirty years of distinguished service. Your colleagues will miss you greatly." (Or make the whole message informal: "Congratulations on thirty amazing years. You're a legend!")
  2. Attached please find… for your perusal sits between Hey and See ya → "Hi Sam! I've attached the updated files. Have a look and let me know what you think. Thanks!"
  3. no clash of level, but pompous → "The committee will use the feedback to start a review."
  4. Kindly be advised is frozen; the pizza's here, guys is intimate → "Pizza's here, everyone!"
  5. Hope you're good! Quick q… asap? Thx clashes with Dear Dr. Mensah (a student to a professor: tenor) → "Dear Dr. Mensah, I hope you are well. I have a question about my grade for the final essay. Would you be able to look at it when you have a moment? Thank you."

The principle: the fix is rarely to raise everything; it is to make the whole message agree with its reader. In 5, going down is not an option: the tenor decides.

Exercise 5 — Turn the tone dial (models)
  1. Warm: "Thank you for raising this; it's really helpful. We'll look into it straight away and keep you posted."
  2. Cool: "Received. Thank you." / "Thank you for the prompt submission."
  3. Deferential: "Would it be possible to have the final figures by noon? I know it's a tight turnaround." / "I wonder if you might be able to send the final figures by noon."

Check that the formality level hasn't moved: a warm version of 1 that says "No worries, will sort it!" has changed register too. For 2, a "cool" version that is rude ("Whatever.") has overshot: cool means distant, not hostile.

Dialogue — too stiff, too loose

The clang: a total bummer in a press release. Same formality, different tone: "The Riverside Bakery will close permanently on March 28, following the retirement of its founder" (formal, cool) → the same sentence with Rosa's quotation and "after forty-two years of serving the neighborhood" (formal, warm). The social post is deliberately informal; "Notice is hereby given" would only belong there as irony.

Speaking — Five Voices

A full model of Message 1 (the bakery) in all five voices is printed in the classroom materials (Set E), along with the voice specification sheet. For each voice, check: the facts match the card; every marker agrees with the voice; the justification names field, tenor and mode correctly; the delivery matches (the online project's rule: a perfect informal sentence read in a stiff cadence still fails). The tribute must celebrate the retirement or the closing. Typical clangs: a contraction in the notice ("won't"); "we're thrilled to announce" in the notice; "notwithstanding" or a subordinate clause in the post; "Hey guys" in the press release; "commence" or a joke at someone's expense in the tribute.

Self-check
Q1
Which is a marker of high, formal register?
A) Phrasal verbs
B) Discourse fillers
C) Nominalization
Why: turning actions into nouns (the implementation of the policy) is typical of formal writing. Phrasal verbs (sort out) and fillers (you know) mark informal speech.
Q2
Which sentence contains a register mismatch?
A) Please find attached the requested documents.
B) Here are those pics you wanted!
C) Please find attached the aforementioned pics.
Why: a legalistic frame (Please find attached the aforementioned) with a slang noun (pics). A is consistently formal, B consistently informal; both are fine in the right room.
Q3 · new
Which reply is professional in register and warm in tone?
A) Noted. This will be addressed in due course.
B) Thank you so much for letting us know; we'll have this resolved by Friday and will update you the moment it's done.
C) Cheers, will sort it.
Why: B is workplace-appropriate and warm (thanks, we, a specific commitment). A is professional but cool; C is warm but informal. Tone and formality are separate dials.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.2)

Half-shifting: raising the vocabulary but leaving casual grammar, or vice versa ("Notice is hereby given that we're closing, so don't forget!") · over-formality as the default for any writing (utilize, commence, in the event that, kindly) · translated letter formulas (Hoping for your kind reply, Please revert) · formal = cold: stripping every human marker from a formal message · dated or regional slang that doesn't fit the room (cheers, mate, chuffed in an American office) · irony missed or taken literally · tweets that are too long or stuffed with hashtags · tributes that tip into pomposity or into jokes. For range, praise a learner who re-keys mid-performance. For effect, praise a tribute line that moved the room and name its device.

Exercise 3 — Choose the strong collocate

  2. bitterly disappointed
  3. drives a hard bargain
  4. a moot point (mute is a classic garble)
  5. a resounding success
  6. few and far between

In each, the alternative is logical but not idiomatic: the collocation is restricted. Note on 4: in American English moot usually means "no longer relevant"; in British English it more often means "open to debate." Both senses fit the sentence.

Exercise 4 — Fix the garble
  1. For all intents and purposes, the project is finished.
  2. It's a dog-eat-dog world out there.
  3. We need to nip this problem in the bud.
  4. That documentary really piqued my interest.
  5. After the drought, the fields were as dry as a bone.
  6. We waited on tenterhooks for the decision.
  7. You should have told me earlier. (should've in speech)
  8. They made him the scapegoat for the whole delay.

Each correction is the only fixed form; the garbles are fluent and phonetically close, which is exactly why they are so revealing. Accept bone-dry for 5.

Self-check
Q1
Which is the correct, fixed order?
A) peace and quiet
B) quiet and peace
C) peace or quiet
Why: the binomial is fixed as peace and quiet; the reverse never occurs, and the conventional pair uses and.
Q2
Which word completes the restricted collocation "a ______ conclusion" (an outcome certain in advance)?
A) predetermined
B) foregone
C) inevitable
Why: a foregone conclusion is the fixed phrase. Predetermined and inevitable are logical but not the idiomatic partner: the collocation is restricted.
Q3 · new
Which sentence contains a garbled fixed expression?
A) It was touch and go for a while.
B) Once the contract expired, the old agreement was null and void.
C) Her question really peaked my interest.
Why: the verb is pique (to arouse): piqued my interest. Peaked sounds identical, which is why the garble is so common, even in published writing. A and B are correct binomials.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.3)

Reversed binomials, especially where the learner's language has the opposite order ("later or sooner," "forth and back") · logical-but-wrong partners ("a big / loud success," "harshly disappointed," "make a hard bargain," "strong rain") · garbles (mute point, peaked my interest, for all intensive purposes, should of) · full-form AND with equal stress, which makes a chunk sound assembled · fixed similes and clichés in formal writing · overstuffing, typically in speeches and toasts · over-flagging in the audit: marking correct chunks as errors because they are unfamiliar. For range, praise the learner who uses one precise chunk exactly where it belongs. For effect, praise any deliberate, witty bending of a chunk (Stretch).

Lessons 2.1–2.3Classes C4–C69 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark accuracy, precision, appropriacy and effect. Most items in this module are interpretations or rewrites with many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. For an interpretation, accept any reading that (1) identifies the withheld or implied verdict at roughly the right strength and (2) points to the words that produce it. Don't reward over-reading: an implication the text doesn't support is wrong, however clever. For a rewrite, accept any version that (1) keeps the message, (2) would be inferred by a proficient reader, and (3) fits the register: deniable for the implication tasks, aimed at a situation or the speaker for humor, coherent in one frame for metaphor. American English is the model; accept British spellings (humour, realise, programme, analyse).

2.1 · Implicature, Inference & Reading Between the Lines (C4)

Exercise 1 — The reference call (Script 2.1-A)
123456

Implications: 1 attendance is his only merit worth citing (quantity: faint praise) · 2 enthusiasm, not results (quantity) · 3 style over substance; possibly colorful = not serious or not accurate (quantity, and a loaded word) · 4 liked socially, which says nothing about leadership, the question asked (relation) · 5 an evasion: the honest answer would be no (relation / strategic vagueness) · 6 he missed deadlines (quantity; eventually is the lever). Exercise 2 — State the implicature (models)
  1. The content is weak (or the formatting is chaotic); the only remark is about fonts. Quantity (faint praise) / relation. Accept "the report looks unprofessional."
  2. She isn't handling the clients well: politeness on the phone is the only positive. Quantity.
  3. The coffee is bad. Quantity (the obvious thing a café should be praised for is missing), plus a hint.
  4. I don't want to discuss the budget (or: now is not the time). Relation.
  5. It was far too long. Understatement / faint praise (quantity); certainly + thorough is a classic polite complaint.
  6. There was a heated argument. Understatement / euphemism in the minutes (manner and quantity); lively is the lever.

Accept a different maxim label if the learner's reasoning is sound: items often flout two at once. Not yet: literal paraphrases ("She is polite on the phone") that miss the verdict.

Exercise 3 — What is taken for granted?
  1. the error · yet → there is an error in the invoice, and it should have been fixed by now
  2. Even → the new intern was the least likely person to spot it (so others should have)
  3. again → you have worked from home before (possibly: too often)
  4. regret (factive) → you signed the contract (and, as implicature, it may have been a mistake)
  5. only → second was less than expected of her
  6. manage to → speaking to the landlord was difficult
  7. Model: "That question assumes we're disorganized, which I don't accept. What I can tell you is that we've cut response times by a third this quarter."
  8. Model: "I don't accept that the team stopped following it. We had one missed step in March, and we've added a second sign-off since then."

For 7–8, accept any answer that names and rejects the premise before adding information. Not yet: "We're not so disorganized" (accepts the premise) or a defensive "Why do you say that?" alone.

Exercise 4 — The verdict between the lines

Levers (any five): much-anticipated (diction: sets up a fall) · reportedly (distancing: airs the crashes without vouching for them) · the juxtaposition of the crashes with "a game-changer" · the scare quotes around "a game-changer" and "well over two hundred" (distancing) · declined to take questions (omission: implies evasion) · A modest crowd of some forty people (diction) juxtaposed with the press release's figure. The verdict: the launch was a flop, and the company is exaggerating (or not being honest) about it. Neutral model: "The company launched its new app at an event attended by about forty people, according to reporters; its press release gave the figure as over two hundred. The chief executive described the app as a game-changer and did not take questions. Some attendees reported that the app crashed twice." (Every fact remains; did not take questions is more neutral than declined.)

Exercise 5 — Imply it, don't state it (models; judge by tact and deniability)
  1. "There's a lot of great material here. I wonder whether the audience will manage to read it all before you move on?" (hint + manage to)
  2. "It's certainly ambitious. Have we had a chance to stress-test the timeline?" (faint praise + presupposing question)
  3. "Ah, things have been a bit complicated with the car lately. I've had to become rather strict with myself about lending it." (strategic vagueness)
  4. "The location is excellent, and the staff clearly do their best. I'd suggest bringing your own cleaning wipes." (faint praise + a hint)

Accept any version a listener would decode but the writer could plausibly disown. Too bald: any sentence that states the message ("It's too long"). Too buried: praise with no hint at all.

Dialogue — decoding the director

Implying phrases: It's clear a great deal of work went into it · certainly comprehensive · a slightly different direction · for now · yet · Let's touch base next quarter. Three messages: (1) the proposal is rejected, at least for now; (2) it is too long; (3) she doubts the budget figures. Hardest to deny: the doubt about the budget, because yet presupposes that the check should already have happened (presuppositions are harder to disown than implicatures). Accept "the rejection" with good reasoning.

Speaking — Implicature Lab

Round 1: model readings for all four source texts are printed small at the foot of each card (classroom materials, set C). A good worksheet quotes the exact words in the Said column, keeps Meant to one short clause, and names a mechanism for every line. Round 2, model (encode card 1, "your slides have far too much text"): "Hi Tom, thanks for sharing the deck. There's clearly a lot of expertise in it. I couldn't help wondering whether the back row will manage to read slide 4 before you move on. You might want to keep the detail for the handout? Happy to look at a second version." Result: pass (message clear, every line defensible). A version that says "Slide 4 is unreadable" is too bald; one that only praises is too buried.

Self-check
Q1
"Did you enjoy the concert?" "The program notes were beautifully printed."
A) The speaker only cared about the printing.
B) It presupposes the concert was canceled.
C) The concert was disappointing, implied by praising only a trivial detail.
Why: the reply withholds everything relevant (quantity) and changes the subject (relation). A takes it literally; B confuses implicature with presupposition: nothing in the sentence takes a cancellation for granted.
Q2
Which question takes a damaging premise for granted?
A) "What did the committee decide about the data?"
B) "When will you admit the data was falsified?"
C) "Do you think the data might be reviewed?"
Why: admit is a factive verb: it presupposes that the data was falsified, and when assumes you will eventually confess. A and C are open questions.
Q3 · new
"Why is your team still behind schedule?" Best first move?
A) "We're not that far behind, honestly."
B) "That question assumes we're behind schedule. In fact, we're on track for the revised date. Let me show you."
C) "Sorry about that. We'll catch up soon."
Why: B names and rejects the premise before giving information. A and C both accept the premise carried by still (A partly, C completely).
Errors you will meet most (2.1)

Literal readings of faint praise ("The reference is positive: he is punctual") · over-reading (finding sarcasm in a neutral sentence) · "Only she got a B" for "She only got a B" · a mistake vs the mistake confused (accidental accusations) · "He pretended the scheme was a success" (for claimed) · "I have a doubt about the figures" (for a question) · answering a loaded question with yes/no first · encoded messages that are either bald or so buried the partner infers the opposite. For precision, praise insisted, reportedly, for now, certainly + adjective, and a well-placed yet.

Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect. Almost every task in this module has many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps every fact and the source's degree of certainty, (2) is accurate and idiomatic, (3) fits the register (a written report, a review, an academic synthesis) and (4) has the effect the task asked for: the stance a reader can name, the order a reader can follow, the attribution a reader can trace. The most revealing words are usually admittedly (fairness), frankly (amusement at the lamp and toaster) and tellingly (the untouched map room is the most popular: a quiet criticism of the new layout). Exercise 2 — Choose the stance
  1. admittedly (a concession against oneself; frankly is bluntness, undoubtedly full commitment)
  2. ironically (the outcome reverses the intention)
  3. reportedly (attributed to a source, not vouched for)
  4. notably (singles out one striking case)
  5. presumably (a reasonable inference; undoubtedly is too strong)
  6. ostensibly (the stated reason, with a hint of another)
  7. Technically (true by a strict definition)
  8. if anything (corrects toward the opposite of what was expected)

Near-misses to discuss: apparently in 3 is acceptable (it also distances), but reportedly is the precise choice for a claim from a source. Seemingly in 6 changes the meaning (it looks that way) and loses the suspicion.

Exercise 3 — Function and register
  1. furthermore: organizing (adding) · written / formal
  2. anyway: organizing (topic shift or closing) · spoken / casual
  3. arguably: stance (epistemic, softens a bold claim) · both, especially written argument
  4. the thing is: interactional (flags the crux) · spoken / casual
  5. to sum up: organizing (closing) · written / formal, or a prepared talk
  6. mind you: interactional / organizing (a qualifying afterthought) · spoken / casual
  7. be that as it may: organizing (conceding) · written / formal
  8. long story short: organizing (summarizing, closing) · spoken / casual
  9. tellingly: stance (attitude: the detail is revealing) · both, especially written commentary
  10. let's face it: interactional (presents an unwelcome truth as shared) · spoken / casual

Accept "stance" for 6 and 10 if the learner explains the attitude they carry. The point of the exercise is the register column: a learner who puts anyway or let's face it in "written / formal" needs a follow-up conversation.

Exercise 4 — Same facts, different temperature (models)

Approving: "The city's new central library, which opened in May, has been a remarkable success. It cost $14 million and, encouragingly, visitor numbers have already tripled. Admittedly, the café closes at 4 p.m., and the reading room can be too noisy to work in; these are, however, teething problems. Fittingly, the architect has won a national award."

Skeptical: "The city's new central library opened in May at a cost of $14 million. Visitor numbers have, admittedly, tripled. Yet the café closes, curiously, at 4 p.m., and the reading room is often too noisy to work in, which is, for a library, no small failing. The architect, predictably, has won a national award."

Check: all six facts present and unchanged; three to five markers; no marker stacking. Not yet: versions that add facts ("thousands of complaints"), change them ("only tripled" is fine as stance; "doubled" is not), or use spoken markers (anyway, let's face it) in a written review. Praise medial placement (Visitor numbers have, admittedly, tripled) and a single final-position verdict.

Exercise 5 — Read the slant, then strip it

1. so-called: the label "upgrade" is disputed, a raised eyebrow · supposedly: the writer doubts the stated purpose · Needless to say: presents the writer's verdict as obvious and pulls the reader along · To be fair: a token concession that makes the criticism look reasonable · tellingly: turns a small fact (the team doesn't use it) into damning evidence · (also though: contrast).

2. Strip test (model): "The office booking system was upgraded to save staff time. The upgrade did not save time. The new calendar view is an improvement. Members of the project team do not use it." Accept versions that drop "did not save time" as an unsupported opinion: that's an even sharper insight.

3. Very little: the only evidence against the upgrade is one unsupported claim and the fact that the project team doesn't use the calendar view. The persuasion lived in the framing.

4. Balanced (model): "The upgrade to the office booking system was intended to save staff time, although there is, as yet, little evidence that it has. The new calendar view is, admittedly, an improvement; that said, members of the project team do not appear to use it themselves."

Neutral facts: six-month trial of free Thursday evenings (5–9 p.m.) · average attendance 180 → 640 · 62% of visitors under 35 · gift-shop spending per visitor down 10%, total Thursday sales up · $22,000 staff overtime · 71% of 300 surveyed would not otherwise have visited · board votes in March.

Approving: "For six months, the museum opened free of charge on Thursday evenings, and the response was remarkable: average attendance more than tripled, from 180 to 640. Encouragingly, 62 percent of these visitors were under 35. Total Thursday sales in the gift shop rose, even though spending per visitor fell by 10 percent. The cost, $22,000 in overtime, was modest by any measure. Notably, 71 percent of 300 visitors surveyed said they would not otherwise have visited. The board will vote in March on whether to continue."

Judiciously balanced: "Over a six-month trial of free Thursday evenings, average attendance rose from 180 to 640, and 62 percent of visitors were under 35. Admittedly, spending per visitor in the gift shop fell by 10 percent, and the trial cost $22,000 in staff overtime; total Thursday sales, however, rose. Seventy-one percent of 300 visitors surveyed said they would not otherwise have visited, which suggests, broadly speaking, that the evenings reached a new audience. On balance, the evidence appears to favor continuing, although the board, which votes in March, may reasonably want a longer trial."

Reports 2–4, markers that work well: Open textbooks (costs down $310, pass rates unchanged 78% / 77%, 14% internet problems, 40 hours faculty time each): skeptical ostensibly, predictably, needless to say on "unchanged"; approving remarkably, if anything, mercifully; balanced admittedly, broadly speaking, on balance. Parking app ($140,000; enforcement time down a third; complaints 1,200 → 90; 8% without smartphones visited the office; paper option kept): skeptical supposedly, tellingly on the 8%; approving encouragingly, notably on the fall in complaints; balanced understandably, that said. Concerts (average paid $14 vs $45; capacity 55% → 96%; 30% first-timers; net revenue down 38%; donor covers this season only): skeptical conveniently, for now at least; approving remarkably, strikingly; balanced admittedly, it remains to be seen whether.

Stance Detective: no fixed answers. The detective should quote evidence. Typical markers: grudging admiration (I have to admit, credit where it's due, annoyingly) · polite skepticism (apparently, supposedly, I'm not sure) · wry amusement (ironically, of all things, needless to say) · enthusiastic but fair (remarkably, to be fair, admittedly) · weary disappointment (sadly, predictably, once again) · cautious optimism (hopefully, so far at least, encouragingly).

Exercise 2 — Reorder for coherence

Set A: b → c → a → d. Principle: chronological, reinforced by cause and effect (the gathering → clashing aims → a flawed treaty → a later consequence). Theme / rheme hand-off: Versailles → Each (the powers) → The result (a treaty) → it (the treaty).

Set B: b → d → c → a. Principle: chronological, with specific → general (the tools lent → the scheme's success → its spread). Clues: tools → Drills, ladders and sewing machines; The scheme's success sums up b and d; the idea and Within a year need the scheme to exist already. Accept b → c → d → a if the learner argues that c is the topic sentence of a success paragraph; point out that especially popular then drifts.

Words that make the order inevitable: Each, The result, it, Within two decades; The scheme's, the idea, Within a year. They are reference items and summary nouns, which point back and so cannot come first.

  3. No topic sentence; the main point is buried (the podcast is outperforming FM, but this emerges only in sentence 7). Coherence. Fix: open with the claim.
  4. Sentence 6, false connective ("Consequently, the station's volunteers are mostly retired teachers"): nothing causes this. Coherence disguised as cohesion. Fix: remove the link; move the fact to where it explains the training request, or cut it.
  5. Sentence 8, elegant variation ("The broadcaster's board… the outlet… the channel's… the organization"): Cohesion. Fix: "the station" and it / its.
  6. Sentences 9–10, new information in the theme slot and ambiguous This ("Training for volunteers in audio editing was requested by several of them. This is a concern…"): Cohesion (flow and reference). Fix: "Several volunteers have asked for training in audio editing" and a summary noun that names the right antecedent ("This worry about FM listeners…").

Model repair (about 120 words): "Riverside Community Radio's move online is succeeding, but it raises a question about the station's FM listeners. Over the past five years, the FM audience has fallen by 40%. In January, the station launched podcast versions of its three most popular programs, and in the first quarter these drew 12,000 downloads, more than the FM audience for the same shows. The board has welcomed the results and plans to add two more programs. Some of the station's older volunteers, however, worry that it is abandoning its FM listeners, and several have asked for training in audio editing so that they can take part in the new format. The board will need to address both this worry and the training request."

Skeleton of the model: The move online is working but raises a question. · FM has declined. · The podcasts outperform FM. · The board will expand. · Volunteers worry and want training. · The board must respond. Reads as one argument.

B) Cohesion

Why: the surface links (cohesion) are all present; the line of thought is not. The fix is rethinking order and relevance, not adding connectives. Reference is a kind of cohesion.

Q3 · new

B) A coherence problem: a step is repeated, the cause of the extra cost is missing, and the critics get no reply. Reorder and rebuild.

Errors you will meet most (3.2)

Polishing sentences when the order is the problem · false connectives (Consequently, Similarly) where no relation exists · synonym fever (five names for one thing) · the main point in the last sentence of the paragraph · "As for X, it…" opening every paragraph · paragraph-long sentences joined by commas · "Firstly… Secondly…" in a two-point text · a summary noun that doesn't match (This increase for a fall; This problem for a success) · this / it with two possible antecedents · reading aloud with no pitch reset, so paragraphs blur. For range, praise an it-cleft used to hand on the news (It is this unplanned expense that…), a confident repetition of the anchor term and a signpost-free transition that simply picks up the last paragraph's rheme.

Exercise 1 — The radio panel (Script 3.3-A)

Exercise 2 — One-sentence gist (models)
  1. "Although trustees defend the museum's new admission charge as an unavoidable response to falling subsidy, critics argue that a publicly owned collection should stay free and that the fee will deter the low-income visitors it aims to reach."

Not yet: "The museum now charges admission" (true to fact, false to the argument); "Community gardens don't work" (over-generalized); any version that adds a verdict ("…which is unfair").

Speaking — Synthesis Workshop (model, 200 words)

"The debate over fine-free libraries turns less on money than on whom the library is for. All three writers accept that fines raise relatively little: Ortega, director of the Brookhaven library, reports that collecting them cost almost as much as they earned, and even Hsu, a county budget analyst, calls the $180,000 they bring in 'modest but real.' Nor does anyone dispute that returns have held steady where fines were dropped. Where the writers diverge is on how much weight that evidence can bear, and on whether the lost revenue matters. Ortega points to her library's rising return rate and 3,000 returning borrowers as proof that fines mainly deterred families in need; Hsu counters that such figures are gathered by the libraries themselves, and only briefly, and urges an independently measured pilot first. Nwosu's interviews with low-income parents support Ortega's central point, suggesting that the dread of a penalty can deter families altogether, but they also qualify it: fines, she finds, are just one obstacle, alongside transport, opening hours and staff attitudes. Taken together, the three sources suggest that abolishing fines is likely to widen access, but that it is neither cost-free nor, on its own, sufficient."

Moves: governing sentence (not a list) → agreement (with a careful qualification from Hsu, quoted) → divergence on evidence → Nwosu as both support and qualification → a close that holds the views in tension. Whose voice? 1 writer · 2 all (A, B) · 3 all · 4 writer · 5 A, then B · 6 C · 7 writer. Highlighter test: only "modest but real," correctly quoted. Accept any synthesis of 180–220 words that organizes by idea and passes both tests. Common slips: "Ortega proves…" (too strong), "Hsu claims…" (loaded), "Nwosu says fines don't matter" (distortion: she says they are one barrier among several).

Paraphrase integrity strips (materials sheet, C9 early finishers)
  1. PW: same skeleton as Ortega's sentence, synonyms swapped.
  2. P: rebuilt and attributed.
  3. C: copied almost word for word with no quotation marks (plagiarism, even with the name).
  4. D: Nwosu says removing fines is likely to help but unlikely on its own to close the gap; "will close the gap" distorts her.
  5. Q: an exact quotation, marked and attributed: correct.
  6. NA: a genuine paraphrase with no attribution: the idea is still Hsu's.

Errors you will meet most (3.3)

Patchwriting presented as paraphrase, especially by strong writers who have always "reworded" this way · citation used as a license to copy · "According to X, she argues that…" · "discuss about / mention about / emphasize on" · backshifting what a text says ("Ortega argued") · claims as the default reporting verb · summaries that editorialize ("…which is clearly unfair") · hedges hardened (may → will) or bold claims softened · source-by-source syntheses · a governing sentence that is really a list ("This paragraph discusses three views on fines") · no divergence named, so the synthesis reads as agreement. For range, praise Y qualifies X's claim, for all their differences, a well-chosen concedes, a short exact quotation used where the wording itself matters, and a close that holds the sources in tension.

Exercise 2 — Name the appeal and the figure
  1. Ethos (forty years' experience), with pathos (the hands). Figures: anaphora (I have… I have…) and an antithesis (held vs only read about).
  2. Logos in form but really pathos by rhythm (accept logos with a reason). Figures: anaphora (We cannot…) + tricolon; the repeated our way to is parallel structure.

  4. Pathos framed as reasoning. Rhetorical question built on antithesis (strongest / weakest).

Exercise 3 — Recast a flat claim (models)
  1. Learning a language as an adult sharpens the memory, widens the circle, and humbles the ego in the best possible way.
  2. We should not wait for the problem to arrive and then fight it; we should build so that it never arrives. / Our task is not to cure the problem, but to prevent it.
  3. This library deserves hours that fit working lives. This library deserves seats for everyone who comes. This library deserves a connection that doesn't give up at the first download.
  4. How many more months can we afford to spend deciding whether to decide? / If not now, when?

Reward parallel grammar (three -s verbs in 1; This library deserves + noun + relative clause in 3), a clean landing on the strongest word, and a falling tone on 4 when said aloud. Wrong: a "tricolon" of four items or three items with different grammar (sharpens the mind, for friends, and being humble); an "antithesis" with no real opposition.

Exercise 4 — Name the fallacy and counter it (models)
  1. False dilemma. "Those aren't the only options: a cheaper tool, a shared license or a trial would all sit in between."
  2. Ad hominem (attacking her credentials rather than the advice). "Her restaurant history isn't the question; the advice is. Does the recipe work?" (Some learners call it the reverse of an appeal to authority: accept that as a thoughtful answer.)
  3. Slippery slope. "Each step would need its own justification. What mechanism makes flexible hours lead to nobody turning up?"
  4. Bandwagon. "Popularity isn't proof. What does the template actually do better than the alternatives?"
  5. Hasty generalization. "Two engineers aren't a pattern. What does a wider sample show?"
  6. Straw man. "That's not what I said. I suggested fewer meetings, not none."

Reward a counter that is calm and redirects to the actual claim. A counter that insults back ("Well, you can't even run a meeting") is itself an ad hominem: point that out with a smile.

Exercise 5 — Concede narrowly, refute decisively (models)
  1. I grant that some genuinely urgent problems will arise on a Wednesday afternoon. But an emergency channel handles those, and protecting four hours of focus each week doesn't stop anyone answering a real emergency.
  2. There is force in the objection that museums would lose some ticket income on those evenings. That may be so, yet it does not follow that they would lose money overall: first-time visitors buy memberships, coffee and gifts, and some come back and pay.
  3. I readily accept that most home cooks don't own a kitchen scale today. But a scale costs less than one ruined birthday cake, and a recipe can print both weights and cups while cooks make the switch.

Check the narrow concession (some, a few, today) and the decisive refutation (a reason, not just a repeated claim). Too wide: "I agree urgent issues can't wait, so maybe we should keep meetings" (the claim disappears). No concession at all: "Urgent issues are not important" (partisan; the audience discounts it).

Errors you will meet most (4.1)

Precision: "tricolon" for lists of four or two · anaphora and epistrophe confused · any repetition called "anaphora" · "rhetorical question" for a genuine question · "ad hominem" for any rude remark (it must replace the argument). Appropriacy: pathos that outruns the evidence; three rhetorical questions in a row; soaring figures in what should be a plain report; a "concession" that gives away the claim. Naturalness: "I will explain you why," "Let's discuss about," "as a conclusion," "actually" for currently, "eventually" for possibly, "according to me." Delivery: rising intonation on rhetorical questions; a tricolon with no pause before the last item. Style & Voice Workshop — one event, three voices (model, Event 4: the bus)

The Reporter. The 7:42 bus to the city center arrived at 7:40 on Monday and departed immediately. Twelve passengers walking toward the stop were left behind. The next service arrived at 8:05. Two of the twelve, who had not met before, said they had become friends while they waited.

The Lyric Essayist. Two minutes before its promise, lit and indifferent, humming with the warmth of people luckier than us, the 7:42 slid away from the curb. Twelve of us stood in the cold and watched it go. Then the waiting. Twenty-three minutes is a long time to share a bench with a stranger; long enough, it turns out, to stop being strangers.

The Deadpan Wit. The 7:42 bus, keen as ever, arrived at 7:40 and left without us. Twelve of us admired its punctuality from a short distance. The 8:05 was less ambitious. I would like to thank the transit authority for the twenty-three minutes it gave me to make a new friend, a service not listed on the timetable.

All four facts appear in every version. What to praise: the Reporter's discipline (no unfortunately, no sadly), the Essayist's periodic opening and quiet last line, the Wit's straight face and last-word twist. What to question: a Reporter who editorializes, an Essayist who is merely formal, a Wit who explains the joke or aims the irony at a person.

Exercise 2 — Name the mastery move
  1. Irony / understatement, with a stance hedge (shall we say); optimistic damns by understatement.
  2. Concession + cohesion: a stance-marked concessive turn (For all its flaws…) that binds and pivots; the parenthetical and there are many adds irony.
  3. Antithesis (not… the loudest voice, but… the last one still standing) carrying a metaphor (still standing); logos in shape.
  4. Implicature: damning with faint praise (energy is the only thing conceded, so everything else is implied to be lacking).
  5. Synthesis (across three reports) with an antithesis (incentives, not intentions).

Defensible variants exist: judge by which device does the heavy lifting.

Exercise 3 — Layer up a flat line (models)
  1. "Frankly, constant notifications don't merely interrupt our concentration; they auction it off, a few seconds at a time." Layers: stance marker (Frankly) · antithesis (don't merely…; they…) · metaphor (auction it off) · rhythm (the weighted final phrase).
  2. "To my mind, good teachers are the quiet architects of every profession, and we remember them far better than we reward them." Layers: stance marker · metaphor (quiet architects) · antithesis (remember / reward) · the mot juste (quiet).

Accept any version with three or more layers that work together. Flag over-orchestration (a metaphor, an idiom, a tricolon and a register drop fighting in one sentence) and mixed metaphors.

Exercise 4 — One idea, two rooms (models)
  1. Expert: "The evidence suggests that scheduled short breaks may substantially reduce error rates in data-entry tasks." · Lay: "Take a few minutes off every hour or so and you'll make fewer mistakes. It really works."
  2. Expert: "Shared reading in early childhood is strongly associated with gains in expressive vocabulary." · Lay: "Read to your kids out loud. It's one of the best ways to give them more words, and they'll love it."

Exercise 5 — Judge the adaptation
  1. B: warm, personal, humble, with a light touch of wit; A is clinical and wrong for a celebration.
  2. B: logos-forward, precise and hedged for scientists; A is pathos-heavy anaphora that a funding panel will distrust.

The written re-pitch — a model (Audience 5: a skeptical manager · about 170 words)

Subject: A one-line decision log: a four-week trial?
Dear Ms. Reyes,
I would like to propose a small experiment for our team: a shared decision log, with one line per decision recording what we decided, who decided, why, and when we will review it. On our ward, a three-month trial roughly halved the number of emails disputing earlier decisions.
One colleague questioned whether the log would simply add bureaucracy. It is a fair concern. However, each entry takes about ninety seconds, whereas reopening a settled decision typically costs us an hour of meeting time. If an entry ever takes longer, the format should be simplified, not the idea abandoned.
Another raised the concern that names in the log might be used to assign blame. The log would record decisions, not culprits; if anything, it would show that each decision was reasonable given the information available at the time.
Could we trial it for four weeks and review the results at our next monthly meeting?
Best regards,
Daniel

Why it works for this reader: evidence first, neutral register, both challenges reported fairly with precise reporting verbs (questioned whether, raised the concern that) and answered, a small, low-risk request. The speech's flourishes (Raise your hand…, a generous storyteller and a poor accountant) are gone because this reader wants a decision, not a performance. For a newspaper op-ed, the metaphor would come back.

Errors you will meet most (4.3)

Architecture: no genuine turn (a speech that never concedes) · a landing that simply stops ("So, yeah, that's it"). Adaptation: the written piece is a summary of the speech rather than a re-pitch · idioms and contractions carried into a formal re-pitch · condescension in the teenagers' version · the challenge omitted or reported unfairly ("Some people wrongly think…"). Fossils under pressure: "discuss about," "since three years," "informations," "I will explain you," "according to me." Exercise 2 — From technique to effect (models)
  1. foreshadowing → to create unease and prepare us for the bridge's collapse (or a later disaster).
  2. irony → to expose his ruthlessness by praising the very quality he lacks; the narrator invites us to judge him.
  3. motif (also accept symbolism) → to build a growing sense of confinement or secrecy across the novel.
  4. juxtaposition → to sharpen the contrast between life and illness, making both more vivid.
  5. allusion (to the Greek myth) → to suggest that once the policy is released, its many harms can't be contained.
  6. symbolism → to let one small image carry the whole decline of the relationship.

Mark the effect clause, not only the label. Wrong: "irony, because it's funny"; "the effect is to use a symbol" (circular). Naturalness: "the effect is to make us see / feel" (not "the effect is making to see").

Exercise 3 — Unpick the sentence (models)

Exercise 5 — Two roads (Frost, "The Road Not Taken")
  1. "Though as for that the passing there / Had worn them really about the same," and "And both that morning equally lay / In leaves no step had trodden black." (Also "as just as fair.")
  2. The poem has just told us the roads were equal, so "the one less traveled by" is not quite true: the speaker will later tell the story as if it were. The last stanza is in the future tense: it's a prediction of how he'll narrate the choice, not a record of what happened.
  3. Accept any two supported readings: nostalgia or regret for the road not taken; self-aware irony at his own future myth-making; weariness; contentment tinged with wistfulness. Evidence: "with a sigh," "ages and ages hence," the dash and the stutter "and I— / I took."
  4. Model: "The poem explores how people turn arbitrary choices into stories of deliberate, defining decisions." / "The poem suggests that the meaning of a choice is made afterwards, in the telling."

Both the popular celebratory reading and the ironic reading can be defended; the strongest answers acknowledge the tension and cite the second and third stanzas.

Close-Reading Seminar — model readings for the six texts

A1 · Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813), opening. Theme: marriage as an economic transaction dressed up as romance. Chain: the grand, philosophical phrase "a truth universally acknowledged" is applied to local gossip (irony, mock-heroic register) → the effect is comic deflation → which exposes how a community disguises its self-interest as universal truth. Key words: universally, must be in want of, rightful property. Voice: the narrator ventriloquizes society (free indirect style).

A2 · Jerome, Three Men in a Boat (1889), the British Museum. Theme: the comedy of self-deception and hypochondria. Chain: the narrator's casual understatement ("some slight ailment… hay fever, I fancy it was") and his inflated verbs ("plunged into," "fearful, devastating scourge") → the effect is ironic contrast between a trivial complaint and imagined catastrophe → which makes him a comically unreliable narrator whose fears we see through. Link to A1: both narrators state things we are meant to doubt.

B1 · Dickens, Bleak House (1853), the fog. Theme: a society (and its legal system) lost in confusion it has made itself. Chain: verbless sentences and the anaphora "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river… fog down the river" → the effect is to make the fog feel total and inescapable, like a camera panning with no action → which prepares the fog as a symbol of the Court of Chancery's obscurity. Allusion: "as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth" echoes the Flood in Genesis; the Megalosaurus waddling up Holborn Hill is comic prehistory, suggesting a city that hasn't evolved. Accept readings focused on pollution ("defiled," "waterside pollutions") and on the parenthesis "(and dirty)" as ironic.

B2 · Thoreau, Walden (1854). Theme: a deliberate life as a response to the fear of not having lived. Chain: the long periodic sentence, stacking purposes ("to live deliberately, to front… and see if… and not, when I came to die, discover…") → the effect is to hold the reader in suspense until the stark final clause → which makes "I had not lived" land with the full weight of the whole sentence. Second quotation, "The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation": juxtaposition of "quiet" and "desperation" (an oxymoron-like pairing) → despair that is invisible.

C1 · Dickinson, "Hope is the thing with feathers" (1891). Theme: hope as something self-sustaining that gives without demanding. Chain: extended metaphor of a small bird → the effect is to make an abstraction tender, physical and fragile-seeming → which heightens the surprise of its resilience ("never stops at all," "in the chillest land"). Syntax: inversion "sore must be the storm." Final turn: "Yet, never, in extremity, / It asked a crumb of me": the commas slow the line, and the shift to "me" makes it personal.

C2 · Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916). See Exercise 5. Theme: how we narrate chance as choice. Chain: the insistence on the roads' equality in stanzas 2–3, set against the confident future claim in stanza 4 → the effect is irony (the speaker will tell a better story than what happened) → which questions the idea that one decision "made all the difference." Link to C1: both poems end with a turn to the first person.

Accept any wording for 1 that includes "no / not realistic / unlikely." Item 4a is American; point out the British equivalent, Have I got the wrong end of the stick? (common slip: "wrong side of the stick"), and accept Did I misunderstand you? as well.

Exercise 2 — Name the strategy
  1. BR (urgent warning: softening would be absurd)
  2. NP (negative question, minimizing "ten minutes")
  3. PP (compliment, "shall we," shared task)
  4. OR (a hint; the speaker can deny it was a request)
  5. NP (apology, "would you mind," "terribly")
  6. PP ("let's," "just the two of us": solidarity)
  7. BR
  8. OR (an indirect complaint; also accept NP-flavored OR: the distancing "I'd have thought" hedges it)

Polite in one setting, abrupt in another: most often 7 (fine between close colleagues on a deadline day or in some efficient workplaces; curt to a stranger or a senior colleague) and 3 (warm among peers; over-familiar, even manipulative-sounding, to a new manager). Accept 1, 6 or 8 with a sound argument. Reject any answer that assigns a strategy to a nationality.

Exercise 4 — The euphemism ladder
  1. kicked the bucket → died → passed away
  2. got the boot → was fired → was let go
  3. the john → the bathroom → the facilities
  4. over the hill → older → senior
  5. lying through his teeth → not telling the truth → being economical with the truth
  6. cheap → inexpensive → budget-friendly

(a) passed away (also accept died: many people prefer the plain word, and grief counselors often recommend it; a good discussion point). (b) They are minced oaths: mild, disguised substitutes for swear words, acceptable in most settings. (c) The euphemism treadmill: a polite word gradually absorbs the associations of the thing it names, so it stops feeling polite and a newer word replaces it. Accept the restroom or the toilet as the middle rung in set 3 (British toilet is neutral); accept cheap as neutral in set 6 if the learner means price only, but point out its "low quality" connotation.

Exercise 5 — When humor misfires (models)
  1. "Sorry, I meant the opposite! It's a terrible day. Bad joke. Shall we make a run for it?"
  2. "Sorry, that came out wrong. I was only joking, and it wasn't a very good one. I'm glad you made it; we're just getting started."
  3. "Can I just clarify something? When I said I'm hopeless with numbers, I was being modest. I'm actually comfortable with budgets and I'd like to keep that task, if that's all right."
  4. "Never mind, obscure reference! Anyway, where were we?"

Good repairs are short, light and forward-looking, take responsibility, and don't explain the joke. Item 3 needs a clarifying repair: self-deprecation read literally has real consequences. Not yet: "It was a joke. You don't have a sense of humor?" (blames the listener) or a paragraph explaining irony.

Dialogue — the compliment and the pause

Deflected compliment: "Oh, no, it was very rough." Lena neither argues at length nor takes it as distress; she accepts the modesty lightly ("it didn't look rushed from where I was sitting") and moves on. Silence handled well: "Take your time. I know it's a tricky one." (she doesn't fill it or push). Hedge decoded: "difficult" → Lena reads it as a likely no. Clarifying check: "So when you say 'difficult,' you'd expect a no as it stands?" Speaking — Critical-Incident Clinic (model for Case 1, "The yes that wasn't")

Describe: Elena asked, "Can you finish the inventory by Friday?" Ravi said, "Yes, yes, I understand." On Friday the inventory wasn't finished; Ravi said he had told her it was difficult. Interpret: Ravi: (a) "yes" meant "I hear you," not "I agree"; (b) he signaled difficulty in a way that was clear by his norms (perhaps a pause, "I understand"); (c) refusing a new manager directly felt risky. Elena: (a) she heard an explicit "yes" and reasonably planned on it; (b) in her experience, anyone with a problem says so. Evaluate: directness (and the meaning of "yes"). Bridge: "Ravi, I think I may have misread you on Tuesday. When you said 'Yes, I understand,' did you mean you'd heard the request rather than that Friday was possible? That's on me for not checking. From now on, shall we agree that if something's tight, you tell me what date would work, and I'll ask rather than assume?" The good-faith test fails any analysis with "typical," "always," a nationality, or an adjective about character.

Q1
"That might be a little difficult." The safest reading is:
A) They're just being shy; press for a firm yes.
B) It may well be a polite "no": treat the hedge as the answer.
C) It's basically a yes with a small caveat.
Why: a heavy hedge is often a face-saving refusal; the softener carries the message. Pressing (A) forces the speaker to be blunter than their norms allow; C is the classic over-reading (Maya's error).
Q2
A joke has landed wrong. Best first move?
A) Explain what made it funny.
B) Say nothing.
C) Lightly acknowledge it and move on.
Why: a short, unfussy acknowledgement repairs the moment; explaining (A) deepens the awkwardness, and silence (B) leaves any offense unrepaired.
Q3 · new
A new colleague asks how much you earn. Most graceful response?
A) "That's none of your business."
B) "Oh, enough to keep me in coffee! How are you finding the new office so far?"
C) Tell them the exact figure.
Why: the question may be ordinary friendliness by the asker's norms. B declines lightly (self-directed humor), protects both people's face and redirects to safe small talk. A shames the asker; C gives up a boundary you're entitled to keep.
Errors you will meet most (7.1)

"I don't suppose you can…" (needs could) · "feel free to not answer / for not answering" (fixed frame: feel free not to) · "Have I got the wrong side of the stick?" · answering negative questions by the proposition ("You don't mind? — Yes.") · clarifying checks that accuse ("So you lied?") · explaining a failed joke at length · euphemisms in the wrong room (kicked the bucket in a condolence message) · generalizing about groups in analysis, which is the most important thing to catch and reframe gently. Dialogue — the golden bridge

Blaming the situation: "The supplier delays have been against us from the start." Crediting the reasoning: "Your plan was sound given what we knew in March." Golden bridge: "With the new delivery figures in, it would be completely reasonable to revisit it." Conditional trade: "If you were able to take the new date to the board, I could draft the explanation for you today." Speaking — Negotiation Simulation (scoring guide)

The Hall (Center = A, Club = B). BATNAs: A 55 points (the yoga studio), B 40 points (the library room). Integrative package ($55 a night, 2 nights, the storeroom, 12 months, the club cleans): A 100 · B 100. Split-the-difference deal ($55, 2 nights, the cabinet, 6 months, shared cleaning): A 75 · B 83. Rent-only haggle (the club pushes rent down to $40 and nobody discusses storage or cleaning: $40, 2 nights, no storage, 6 months, the center cleans): A 45 · B 80. That is below the Center's BATNA of 55, so a well-prepared A should refuse, and B has missed the storeroom it needed most.

The Coffee Cart (Library = A, Co-op = B). BATNAs: A 55 (a vending machine, with no plaza to manage), B 35 (the farmers' market). Integrative package (3 days including Saturday, $250 a month, cart paused during Saturday story time, the co-op sweeps the plaza, a 3-month trial with automatic renewal to 12 months): A 107 · B 72. Split deal (3 days, $250, no story-time pause, library staff clean, 3-month trial): A 50 · B 60, below the Library's BATNA of 55, so the library should refuse it. The co-op gains most by trading the two things that cost it little (the one-hour pause and the sweeping) for the automatic renewal it needs. Points tables are on the confidential briefs (classroom materials, set E). Points are not comparable across sides; judge each side against its own BATNA.

What good negotiators said (examples): "What matters most to you in the space itself?" · "If you were able to take on the cleaning, then we could clear the storeroom for you." · "Supposing we paused the cart during story time, would that make five days possible?" · "With a trial built in, it would be completely reasonable for your board to say yes." · "Can they sell it upstairs?": "We've got a year of steady income and a cleaner hall" / "We've got storage for the robots and two nights a week."

Self-check
Q1
The classic conditional trade?
A) "If you were able to move the deadline, then we could look again at scope."
B) "Take it or leave it: that's my final offer."
C) "It'd be completely reasonable to revisit this now."
Why: A ties a concession to a return and keeps the deal moving toward a package. B is an ultimatum that invites a standoff. C is a useful face-saving bridge, but not a trade.
Q2
The pitfall of over-softening a rejection?
A) It lands too harshly.
B) It's too short.
C) The listener may not realize they were turned down and may feel misled later.
Why: if the decision is buried in cushioning, the listener misses it, and the kindness backfires when the truth sinks in. Clarity is itself a form of respect.
Q3 · new
What is a BATNA?
A) Your ideal outcome.
B) Your best alternative if no agreement is reached.
C) Your first concession.
Why: the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (Fisher and Ury) is what you'll do if you walk away. It sets your walk-away point: never accept a deal worse than your BATNA. A is your opening position (or aspiration); C is a tactic.

"provided you will sign / as long as you will pay" · "If you would be able to…, we would can…" · "I'm afraid no" · with respect and correct me if I'm wrong used as sneers (weaponized courtesy) · revealing the BATNA as a threat ("Give us the room or we go to the hotel") · conceding without a return ("OK, we'll lower the price") · haggling one issue at a time and splitting every difference · over-softened refusals that never state the decision · compromise used where a solution or package is meant. Exercise 4 — The essay summary

Key points (model paraphrases; accept any accurate paraphrase that doesn't lift phrases):

Text 1 · aWe can only think with knowledge we already carry; recognizing patterns requires it, so looking everything up is not a substitute.
Text 1 · bThe problem is not memorizing as such but the tedious ways it has been taught.
Text 2 · aClass time spent on facts that can be retrieved instantly would be better used on judging and connecting information.
Text 2 · bWhen information is plentiful, the valuable skill is evaluating it, not remembering it; curricula that reward recall are out of date.

Where they agree: both accept that memory still has some value (Text 2: "Memory has not become worthless"); they disagree about how much school time it deserves and about whether it competes with understanding.

Model essay (about 270 words).

Few educational questions are as quietly contested as the place of memorization. The two texts agree that memory retains some value, yet they draw opposite conclusions about how much classroom time it deserves.

The first writer argues that dismissing memorization misreads how thinking operates. Reasoning, on this view, depends on knowledge already held in mind: expertise consists largely in recognizing patterns, which is impossible if every fact must be looked up. The writer's most persuasive move is to separate memorization itself from the tedious drills that have given it a bad name. Less convincing is the implied assumption that the knowledge worth storing is obvious; the text never says who decides.

The second writer contends that schools squander time on facts that are instantly retrievable, and that the capacity now in short supply is judging information rather than recalling it. The point is well made, particularly given how much unreliable material circulates. However, the argument risks a false opposition: one can hardly evaluate a source on a subject one knows nothing about.

In my view, the two positions are less incompatible than they first appear. Evaluation is not an alternative to a well-stocked memory but one of its fruits, since we detect a dubious claim precisely because it clashes with what we already know. Schools should therefore teach less by rote, but not ask students to remember less: they should choose a core of knowledge that makes judgment possible, and then train students to question it.

Check against the task: both texts summarized ✓ · both evaluated (one strength, one weakness each) ✓ · own view, weighed ✓ · no lifted phrases ✓ · formal, essay register ✓. Common problems: summarizing without evaluating; evaluating only one text; copying "the scarce skill is not recall but evaluation" word for word; an opening that says nothing ("Nowadays, with the development of technology…"); running far over the word limit.

Exercise 5 — The strategist's move

A. 1 Mark it, move on, come back later: never let one gap drain the time that banks five easy marks. · 2 Reject it: the "true but not there" trap; if you can't underline the evidence, it's wrong. · 3 Plan both parts first: an under-answered prompt caps the score however good part one is. · 4 Invite them in ("What's your take on…?"): the task rewards interaction; drawing a partner out scores more than filling the silence with a monologue.

Dialogue — the collaborative task

A1: "Shall we start with…?" organizes the discussion (accept I or C: it proposes a starting point) · B1: "That's a fair point, though I'd add…" B; "What's your take on bike lanes?" I · A2: "I see it slightly differently, in that…" D; "So, are we agreed that…?" C · B2: "Agreed. Let's recommend those two." C. The conclusion comes last because the task demands a decision: a discussion that never decides has not completed the task. Turns are short because the task assesses interaction; a long turn here would crowd the partner out.

Self-check
Q1
On the collaborative speaking task, what do examiners most reward?
A) One candidate delivering a fluent, uninterrupted speech
B) Genuine interaction: inviting, building, negotiating toward a decision
C) Reaching agreement as fast as possible with no discussion
Why: the collaborative task exists to assess interaction. A polished monologue misses the point; instant agreement skips the negotiation the task is designed to elicit.
Q2

Why: the "true but not there" option is the examiner's favorite distractor. World knowledge is not evidence. Leaving a blank never scores.

Use of English: changing the key word (soon for SOONER) · no inversion after a negative adverbial · dropped articles in fixed phrases (on brink of, in wake of) · counting a contraction as one word · the right root in the wrong form (reluctancy) · the wrong negative prefix (unconclusive, inforeseen) · choosing by meaning, not collocation (heavy possibility). Reading and listening: matching repeated words instead of paraphrase · the first-mentioned option in listening · "true but not there." Writing: the under-answered prompt · formulaic openings · lifted phrases. Speaking: a long turn with no close (the speaker runs out of time mid-point) · a collaborative task run as two monologues · "discuss about" · I am agree under pressure. For range, praise precise collocations (a double-edged sword, cast doubt on), invitations that genuinely hand over the floor, and closes that pull the long turn together.

Exercise 3 — Open, close, answer

1 (b): a hook that opens a curiosity gap; (a) and (c) waste the first fifteen seconds and lower expectations. · 2 (c): a resonant, decisive close on a full stop; (a) and (b) fizzle at the moment of highest attention. · 3 (b): concede the valid part, then narrow to the real claim; (a) is defensive and (c) evasive, and both cost credibility.

Exercise 4 — Mark it up for delivery (model)

Every expert you admire || was once catastrophically bad | at the thing they're now famous for. ||

Every single one. ||

We tell ourselves a comforting lie: | that talent is something you're handed | at birth.

It lets us off the hook. ||

The gap between you and the person you admire | is not talent. || It is time.

Accept other defensible choices (for example stress on comforting rather than lie). The longest pause belongs before "It is time": it primes the answer to the question the speech has been building, and the contrast talent / time needs the silence to land. Weak mark-ups: a pause after every two or three words (robotic), stress on function words (the, is, at), or no pause before the key line.

Exercise 5 — From defensive to poised (models)
  1. "That's a fair challenge: it's true that many jobs, in care or in retail for example, can't simply drop a day. What I'm arguing is narrower: that where the work is measured by output rather than hours, a four-day week is worth trialing."
  2. "I'm glad you asked. I don't have the source on hand, so let me not lean on that exact figure; I'll send it to you. The broader point doesn't depend on it, though: the trend in the studies I read was consistently upward."
  3. "That's a good question. You're right that most of my examples are large cities. I'd put it more cautiously, then: it works best where public transportation is already strong, and smaller towns would need a different first step."

Look for three moves: acknowledge, concede what is fair, narrow or bridge back. Reject rewrites that simply become polite without conceding anything ("Thank you for your question, but I think you are wrong"), and rewrites that collapse ("You're right, my speech was wrong").

Dialogue — the speech and its Q&A

1 The lie is that talent is innate, "handed at birth"; it lets us off the hook because it excuses us from trying ("I'm just not gifted"). · 2 Antithesis: "The gap… is not talent. It is time" (also: "Don't wait to be good before you begin. Begin."). The pauses sit around the highest-stakes lines: after the opening claim (to let it land), after "Think about that" (to give the audience thinking time), and before "Begin" (to prime the call to action). · 3 The speaker concedes the valid part ("the starting lines are not equal"), narrows the claim to one that survives the objection ("quitting early guarantees you stay behind it"; persistence is the controllable part), and checks that the answer landed ("Does that distinction land for you?"). It persuades because it takes the questioner seriously and leaves a stronger, smaller claim standing.

Speech plan and feedback record

Open tasks: no key. Check that the open and close are written in full and that the close ends on a full stop. The most common planning problem is a spine with five or six points: a three-minute speech holds two or three.

Self-check
Q1

A) It primes the audience's attention and lets the line land
B) It simply slows the talk down to fill time
C) It hides the fact that the speaker is unprepared
Why: the pause before says "listen, this matters"; the pause after says "let that sink in." It is a tool of emphasis, not filler, and it reads as confidence.
Q2
A questioner challenges your talk. The most poised response is to:
A) concede the valid part, then narrow to your actual point
B) insist firmly that they're mistaken
C) decline to engage with the question
Why: conceding what is fair keeps credibility; narrowing keeps the argument. B is defensive and C evasive: poise is the persuasion.
Q3 · new
You are preparing a three-minute speech. The best way to prepare the words is to:
A) memorize every word so that nothing can go wrong
B) memorize the open and the close, and speak the middle from a skeleton of key points
C) improvise all of it, so that it sounds natural
Why: automatic bookends are the best insurance against nerves and the fizzled ending; a skeleton keeps the middle alive. Full memorization sounds recited and collapses if you lose your place; pure improvisation rambles.
Errors you will hear most (8.2)

The apologetic open ("Sorry, I'm not a good speaker…") · the fizzled close ("So… yeah, that's it") · too many points for three minutes · stress on every word, or on function words · a rhetorical question with no pause after it · pauses that fall mid-phrase (breathing where the lungs run out, not where the idea ends) · speeding up under nerves · defensive or evasive answers in Q&A · discuss about, explain me, return back under pressure. For range, praise closes that echo the open, triads whose third beat falls, genuine concessions in Q&A, and feedback that quotes a line before judging it.

8.3 · Your Masterwork & Lifelong Command (C25)

Exercise 1 — Name the thread

1 mot juste · 2 register · 3 cohesion · 4 rhetoric · 5 personal voice. (From the online lesson.) A masterwork weaves all five together until the seams disappear.

Exercise 2 — Principles of lifelong command

1 reading · 2 teach (accept explain) · 3 garden · 4 door (accept graduation). (From the online lesson.)

Exercise 3 — Judge it against the C2 standard
  1. Below the bar. Grammatically flawless but flat: short, undifferentiated clauses that state without shading.
  2. Meets the bar. Precise diction ("ambitious to the point of recklessness"), a nuanced concession ("in spite of itself"), and a differentiated observation about the audience.
  3. Below the bar. A register mismatch and vague fillers ("like," "and stuff") that blur rather than sharpen the meaning.
  4. (new) Meets the bar. A negative inversion used for genuine emphasis, a precise and evaluative collocation ("quiet evasions"), and a hedged, ironic stance ("I suspect, precisely what its authors intended") that differentiates a fine shade of meaning: the evasions were deliberate.

The point to draw out: the C2 standard is met by precision and differentiated nuance, not by long words. Item 4 would fail if the inversion were decoration; here it enacts the delay it describes.

Exercise 4 — Concede, then narrow (models)
  1. "That's a fair challenge, and I'd hate to sound nostalgic; phones have given us a great deal. Let me draw the distinction I actually mean: I'm not saying screens are the problem; I'm saying constant interruption is, and the evidence on that is hard to ignore."
  2. "You're right to press that: the three studies are all from one country, and I should say so more clearly. So I'd narrow the claim: the findings are strong for that context, and they give us a reason to test the idea elsewhere, not a reason to assume it works everywhere."
  3. "Granted, real life is rarely that tidy, and I did consider a more open ending. What I was after is narrower than realism: the neatness is the character's wish, not the world's, and the last line is meant to make you doubt it. If that doesn't come through, that's something I'd want to fix."

Accept any answer that concedes something specific and restates the claim more precisely. Reject answers that concede everything ("You're right, it's just nostalgia") or nothing ("It's fiction").

Exercise 5 — From vague to precise feedback (models)
  1. "What landed for me was the line 'the choices we don't notice are the ones shaping us': it turned the whole talk back on the audience."
  2. "Your ending summarizes points we'd just heard. I wonder whether you could end on the image of the empty library instead; if this were mine, I might cut the last two sentences."
  3. "The door metaphor carries the piece for me. I wonder whether the river image in paragraph three competes with it: two journeys at once. If this were mine, I might keep only the door."
  4. "Someone might push back on the claim that all schools can afford this: how would you answer a principal with a fixed budget?"

Look for a quotation or precise reference, an effect, and either a question or one offered change. Disagreement (item 4) becomes useful when it turns into a challenge the writer can answer.

Dialogue — the masterwork and its discussion

1 The door and the room: fluency is only the entrance; the value lies in what fluency makes possible (catching the humor, being moved, meeting people in their own words). "Mistaking the door for the room" means treating basic fluency as the goal rather than the threshold. · 2 Something more precise: she concedes the risk ("you're right to press it; I'd hate for this to sound like a hierarchy of worth"), then narrows the claim (mastery opens particular rooms; it doesn't make anyone's life richer or their worth greater). · 3 Precise diction: moved set against understood, hierarchy of worth; rhetorical device: the rhetorical question "Good enough for what?", the door/room antithesis; personal voice: the candid "I spent years, frankly, mistaking the door for the room." · 4 The summit is "not an ending but a door of its own": mastery is a graduation into lifelong use, the lesson's central theme.

Open tasks: no key. When you read planning frames (C24) and pieces (after C25), use the format rubrics (materials set J).
