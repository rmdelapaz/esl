B · New Identity Cards (7.1 · 7.2 · 7.3)

Any learner may use a card instead of their own life, in any task, with no reason needed. In 7.1 they use the cross-cultural moment (journal, online project). In 7.2 they use the negotiation for the online project. In 7.3 they may convey the word on the card instead of one from their own language. The words are deliberately not linked to the names: a word is something anyone can learn and pass on.

C · Critical-Incident Clinic — Case Cards (7.1)

One card per group of 3. Every incident is invented; the people have names and jobs, never nationalities, and nobody is the villain. The small gray model at the bottom is for you: fold it back or cut it off before class. Accept any analysis that gives two good-faith readings for each person and any bridge with a softener, a clarifying check and a realigning line.

Case 1 · Directness

The yes that wasn't

Elena, a new warehouse manager, asks Ravi: "Can you finish the inventory by Friday?" Ravi pauses, then says, "Yes, yes, I understand." On Friday the inventory isn't finished. Ravi says, "But I told you it was difficult."

Readings: Ravi's "yes" = "I hear you"; the pause + "I understand" signaled difficulty; refusing a new manager felt risky. Elena heard an explicit yes and planned on it. Bridge: "Ravi, I think I may have misread you on Tuesday. When you said 'I understand,' did you mean you'd heard me rather than that Friday was possible? That's on me for not checking. Shall we agree that if something's tight, you tell me what date would work?"
Case 2 · Silence

The silent call

Owen chairs a video call with a partner organization. He asks, "Any objections?" After about four seconds of silence he says, "Great, agreed!" and moves on. The next week the partner sends a long email listing serious concerns.

Readings: the partners were thinking, or waiting to be invited individually, or felt objecting on a call was impolite; Owen heard silence as consent and was trying to be efficient. Bridge: "Thank you for the email, and sorry: I think I rushed you on the call. When I heard silence I assumed agreement. Could we take your points one by one? And next time I'll leave more time, or ask each of you in turn."
Case 3 · Compliments

The deflected compliment

Hana tells Jorge his report was excellent. Jorge says, "No, no, it's full of mistakes." Worried, Hana spends twenty minutes reassuring him and listing what was good. Jorge looks more and more uncomfortable.

Readings: Jorge's deflection was the polite way to receive praise, not distress; Hana, used to "Thank you," read it as low confidence and tried to help. Bridge: "I'll stop there. I think I made a fuss! I just wanted you to know the team found it really useful. Now, shall we look at the next section?"
Case 4 · Small talk & taboo

The welcome lunch

At a team welcome lunch, Bea asks the new colleague, Tariq, whether he's married and how much rent he pays for his apartment. She's being friendly. Tariq changes the subject and is quiet for the rest of the lunch.

Readings: for Bea these questions show warm interest; for Tariq, family and money are private with new colleagues, or simply painful topics. Bridge: "Tariq, I'm sorry if I was nosy at lunch; please tell me if I'm straying into things you'd rather not discuss. I'd love to hear how you're finding the neighborhood, though!"
Case 5 · Humor (banter)

Week one

In her first week, Sofia arrives five minutes late to a team meeting. Mark grins: "Nice of you to join us. Working part-time already?" Everyone laughs; Sofia doesn't. That afternoon she asks her manager if she's in trouble.

Readings: for Mark, teasing is how the team welcomes people; for Sofia, with no shared history, it was a public criticism from a stranger. Bridge (Mark): "Sofia, about this morning: that was meant as a joke, and not a good one, especially in your first week. You're not in trouble at all. We're glad you're here."
Case 6 · Directness in writing

The email

Lukas emails a colleague in another office: "Send me the updated figures by 3 p.m. Thanks." The colleague, Ama, forwards it to her manager and asks whether she has done something wrong.

Readings: for Lukas this is an efficient, neutral email between peers; for Ama, an unsoftened imperative from someone she barely knows reads as an order or as anger. Bridge (Lukas, by phone): "Ama, I think my email this morning came across as abrupt. I was rushing, and I'm sorry. I don't suppose there's any chance of the figures this afternoon? If three is tight, just tell me what works."
Case 7 · Humor (irony)

Lovely day for it

A visiting trainer, Grace, looks out at the flooded parking lot and says, "Well, lovely day for it!" Her host, Min-jun, apologizes at length for the weather, offers to reschedule, and looks embarrassed all morning.

Readings: Grace used ironic understatement to be friendly about a shared problem; Min-jun took it literally, or as a polite complaint he should fix as host. Bridge (Grace): "Oh, I'm sorry, I meant the opposite! It was a joke about the rain, and not a very clear one. I'm very happy to be here. Shall we get started?"
Case 8 · Indirect refusal

"I'll think about it"

Pat, a community theater director, asks Yusuf, who owns a local bakery, to donate cakes for the opening night. Yusuf says, "Interesting idea. I'll think about it." Pat prints "Cakes kindly donated by Yusuf's Bakery" on 300 programs.

Readings: Yusuf's reply was a courteous no (or a very uncertain maybe); Pat heard enthusiasm and a likely yes. Bridge (Pat, before printing): "Yusuf, just so I don't assume: when you said you'd think about it, should I plan on the cakes, or look for another option? Either is completely fine."

D · Euphemism Ladders — Sorting Strips (7.1)

Cut each ladder into three words and shuffle within the ladder. Pairs order each from dysphemism (blunt or jokey) → neutral → euphemism (gentle), then say where each belongs (a condolence card, a chat with close friends, an HR letter). This is taboo as a linguistic phenomenon: all examples are mild, and nobody is asked for examples from their own language.

F · Translation & Interpreting Lab — Ethics Dilemmas (7.3)

Each group takes two cards. For each: name the principle(s) at stake (accuracy/completeness · impartiality · confidentiality · transparency · role boundaries · competence), script the interpreter's exact words, and decide whether a professional interpreter should be there instead. The settings are deliberately everyday and low-stakes; medical, legal, police and immigration settings are discussed only in the abstract. Models: answer key, 7.3.

Dilemma 1

"What would you do?"

You're volunteering as an interpreter at a parent–teacher conference about a school trip. The parent turns to you and asks, "What would you do, if it were your child?"

Dilemma 2

The rude remark

At a community meeting about parking, a resident says, loudly, "This committee is completely useless." The committee members are waiting for your rendering.

Dilemma 3

The pun

A speaker at a neighborhood event makes a play on words that gets a big laugh. It depends entirely on two meanings of one word; there's no equivalent in the other language.

Dilemma 4

The curious neighbor

Last week you interpreted for a neighbor at a meeting with the building manager about a repair. Today another neighbor asks, "So what did they talk about?"

Dilemma 5

The missed number

The coordinator says the fee is "fifteen" or "fifty": you're not sure which. Everyone is looking at you.

Dilemma 6

Too fast, too technical

A store manager explains a laptop warranty very quickly, full of terms like "pro-rated," "accidental damage" and "authorized service provider."

Dilemma 7

Out of your depth

A friend asks you to interpret at her bank appointment about opening an account and a loan. You're fluent, but you don't know the financial vocabulary in either language.

Dilemma 8

"Don't translate that"

During a meeting, the person you're interpreting for says something critical, then tells you, "Don't translate that part."

F · Register Rescue, Sight Texts & the Untranslatables Gallery (7.3)

Register rescue: the rendering kept the facts and lost the tone. Groups restore the original register. Sight texts: the English-only route for Part 2 of the Lab: the mediator reads the dense notice silently for 30 seconds, then tells a "neighbor" what it says in friendly, plain spoken English. Fold back the models.

Register rescue 1 · casual → flattened

Original (a coach's voice message): "Hey, everyone! Huge well done on Saturday. Honestly, you were all amazing. Pizza's on me next week!"

Rendering: "The coach acknowledges the team's satisfactory performance on Saturday. Pizza will be provided next week."

Restored: "Hi, everyone! Great job on Saturday, really, you were all fantastic. Next week, pizza's on me!" (warmth, praise level and the personal offer all return; "satisfactory" was a mistranslation of the praise level too)
Register rescue 2 · formal → too casual

Original (a building notice): "Residents are respectfully reminded that the community room must be vacated by 10:00 p.m."

Rendering: "Hey guys, get out of the community room by ten, OK?"

Restored (formal but clear): "A reminder to all residents: please leave the community room by 10 p.m." (the courtesy and the official voice return; the meaning stays plain)
Sight text 1 · the pool

"Due to scheduled maintenance of the filtration system, the aquatic facility will be closed to patrons from Monday, June 3, through Wednesday, June 5, inclusive. Lap swimming will resume on Thursday at 6:00 a.m. Membership holders will receive a prorated credit."

"Heads up: the pool's closed Monday to Wednesday, June 3 to 5, while they fix the filters. It opens again Thursday at 6 a.m. for laps, and members get a bit of money back for the days they missed."
Sight text 2 · the library

"Effective immediately, overdue fines on juvenile and adult materials have been eliminated. Patrons remain responsible for the replacement cost of items not returned within 30 days of the due date."

"Good news: no more late fees, for kids' books or adults'. But if you keep something more than 30 days past the due date, you'll have to pay for it."
Sight text 3 · recycling

"Residents are advised that, commencing in September, collection of mixed recyclables will transition to a biweekly schedule. Glass must be separated and placed in the designated container."

"Starting in September, recycling will be picked up every two weeks instead of every week. Keep glass separate, in its own bin." Trap: "biweekly" can mean every two weeks or twice a week. A good mediator checks it, or flags it.
Your own text (optional)

A short (60–120 words), simple, public text in any language you read: a recipe, a museum sign, a product label, a children's news story, a tourist leaflet. Not a letter, not a medical or legal document, nothing about a real person you know.

Read it silently first · chunk it into ideas · start with the main point · paraphrase what won't cross · keep its tone.

H · Listening & Dictation Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

Read at a natural speed. For dialogues, change your voice for each speaker, or give the lines to confident learners. In this module the tune carries much of the meaning, so read the softeners slowly with a fall-rise. The Listen buttons in the online lessons give learners the dialogues again at home. The scripts follow the online lessons, with American wording where the lessons use British forms.

7.1-A · Maya and Jun: a misunderstanding, repaired (Workbook 7.1, Ex 1)

Maya: Great to finally meet you, Jun. So, can you have the first draft ready by Thursday? · Jun: Thursday… mm. That would be quite challenging, I think. There are a few other things on at the moment. · Maya: OK, "challenging" but doable. Great! I'll let the client know Thursday, then. · Jun: Ah, I, er… I'll certainly do my best. · Maya: Perfect. Thanks so much! · Jun: (silence) · Maya: Jun, you've gone quiet. Have I got the wrong end of the stick? I have a feeling I may have just talked over something. · Jun: No, no, it's fine. It's just… when I said "quite challenging," I meant that, realistically, Thursday isn't likely. I'm sorry, I should have been clearer. · Maya: No, that's on me. I hear "challenging" and I steamroll straight past it. Let me back up. What would be realistic? · Jun: Monday I could promise with confidence. Thursday, I'd only be setting us both up to disappoint the client. · Maya: Monday it is. A promise I can actually pass on is worth far more than an optimistic Thursday. Thank you for spelling it out; I'd genuinely rather you did. · Jun: And I'll try to be more explicit with you. I can see now that hints do you no favors. We'll get the hang of each other. · Maya: We will. And next time I hear "challenging," I'll take it as the polite red flag it clearly was.

Answers: 1 Thursday isn't realistic (an indirect refusal) · 2 "I'll certainly do my best" · 3 Jun went quiet · 4 end · talked · on · back · hang · 5 Monday: a promise that can be kept and passed on. Note "Have I got the wrong end of the stick?" is British; American: "Did I get the wrong idea?" The online lesson says "brilliant" where this script says "Great!"

7.1-B · What might they really mean? (C20 Meaning stage or C21 warmer · read each once, with the tune)

1. "That would be quite challenging." · 2. "I'll see what I can do." · 3. "It's an interesting idea." · 4. "It's not the worst plan I've heard." · 5. "We might have a slight issue with the timeline." · 6. "Well… I suppose that could work."

Learners say a possible meaning and a check: 1 probably no · 2 a courteous no or a weak maybe · 3 not keen · 4 it's weak (understatement) · 5 there's a real, possibly serious problem · 6 a reluctant yes. Ask: "Could any of these mean exactly what it says? How would you find out without accusing?"

7.2-A · Rosa and Daniel: a delicate negotiation (Workbook 7.2, Ex 1)

Rosa: Daniel, thanks for making time. I'll be straight with you: I need to raise something difficult about the pricing. · Daniel: Go on. Although I'll say now, the budget on our side is more or less locked. · Rosa: I understand, and I appreciate you saying so up front. The situation is that our material costs have risen sharply. It's hit the whole sector, not just us. As things stand, the current rate no longer covers delivery. · Daniel: I hear that, but I'm afraid a price rise is going to be a very hard sell internally. I've got my own people to answer to. · Rosa: Completely, and I don't want to put you in that position. So rather than a straight increase, let me put something on the table. If you were able to commit to a twelve-month term instead of six, then we could hold the rate much closer to where it is. · Daniel: That's more interesting. A longer term I can defend: it looks like stability, not a cost. What sort of number are we talking about? · Rosa: With a twelve-month commitment, I could keep the rise to three percent rather than the ten I'd otherwise be forced into. And we'd guarantee no further increase for the full term. · Daniel: Three, with a freeze after that… Let me think aloud. If I can take that upstairs as a locked-in rate with no surprises, that's a story I can actually sell. My concern is being back here in six months having the same conversation. · Rosa: Which is exactly what the freeze rules out. Neither of us wants to keep reopening this. Shall we say three percent, twelve months, rate frozen, and I'll put it in writing today so you've got something solid to show your team? · Daniel: I think we can work with that. Honestly, I came in braced for a fight, and this feels like something we've solved together rather than one of us losing. · Rosa: That's how I'd want it. You've got a rate you can defend and I've got a business that survives. I'll call that a good day's work for both sides.

Answers: 1 material costs up across the sector · 2 the budget is more or less locked · 3 commit · could · 4 10% · 3% · 5 predictability; something he can defend upstairs · 6 put it in writing today. Ask: "Where does Rosa acknowledge before she asks? Which line is the golden bridge?" ("it looks like stability, not a cost" / "a story I can actually sell").

7.2-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times · C21 drill or C22 warmer)

1. If you were able to move on the timeline, then we could look again at the price. · 2. We could offer weekend cover, provided you sign for a year. · 3. With respect, we may have to agree to differ on that particular point. · 4. I'm afraid I've got some disappointing news, and I'd rather be straight with you about it.

Check: were able to (not would be able to), provided you sign (no will), agree to differ. Ask: "Which move is each sentence?" (1 conditional trade · 2 conditional trade · 3 closing without a loser · 4 signposting a difficult message.)

7.3-A · Ana and Tom: sobremesa (Workbook 7.3, Ex 1)

Ana: There's a word in my language, sobremesa. Do you have it in English? · Tom: I don't think we've got a single word for that. What does it mean, literally? · Ana: Literally it's "over-the-table," but that doesn't really tell you anything. Roughly, it's the time you spend still sitting and talking after a meal: the plates are cleared, but nobody wants to get up and leave. · Tom: Ah, so it's a kind of relaxed, lingering conversation at the table. The closest we'd get is maybe "chatting over coffee after dinner," but that loses the sense that it's a whole custom. · Ana: Exactly. It literally names the after-meal talk, but really it's about not being in a hurry, about the meal not being over just because the food is. · Tom: That's lovely, actually. We do the thing; we just never gave it a name.

Answers: 1 sobremesa · 2 "over-the-table" · 3 the time spent still sitting and talking after a meal · 4 not being in a hurry · 5 "chatting over coffee after dinner" · 6 the sense that it's a whole custom. Ask: "Which frames did they use?" (I don't think we've got a single word… · Literally… · Roughly… · The closest we'd get… but that loses…)

7.3-B · The helpful volunteer (C22 Lab, Part 1 · count the breaches)

A community garden meeting. The gardener, Leila, speaks another language; a well-meaning volunteer, Sam, interprets for the coordinator. Read each pair: first what Leila said (given here in English), then what Sam said. Learners note each difference and the principle it breaks.

1. Leila: "I've been waiting three weeks for a plot." Sam (to the coordinator): "She says she's been waiting three weeks for a plot." · 2. Leila: "Also, the water tap by the gate has been broken since June." Sam: (says nothing about the tap) · 3. Leila: "Honestly, this is ridiculous." Sam: "She's a little concerned." · 4. Coordinator: "The fee is fifteen dollars for the season." Sam (to himself, then to Leila): "Fifteen or fifty? …Fifty, I think. It's fifty dollars." · 5. Coordinator: "Watering is best early in the morning." Sam (to Leila): "He says water in the morning, but honestly, just water in the evening, like I do. It's easier." · 6. (Leila and Sam talk together for thirty seconds.) Coordinator: "What was that about?" Sam: "Oh, nothing important." · Sam (leaving): "Don't worry, I'll tell everyone at the market how it went!"

Breaches: 1 third person instead of the first person ("I've been waiting…") · 2 omission (completeness) · 3 softening (accuracy: register lost) · 4 guessing a number instead of asking (transparency, accuracy) · 5 adding advice (impartiality, role boundaries) · 6 an unreported side conversation (transparency). Bonus: the parting remark breaks confidentiality. Ask: "Sam meant well. Which of his breaches could have real consequences?" (4 and 2 especially.)
