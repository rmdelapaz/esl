D · Gapped-Text Strips (8.1)

Cut into three paragraph strips and four sentence strips (A–D). Pairs lay out the paragraphs, place the sentences, and write the breadcrumb for each placement on the back of the sentence strip. Key: 1 B · 2 C · 3 A · D extra.

Paragraph 1

Anyone who has asked a brilliant colleague for directions and received a baffling answer has met what psychologists call the curse of knowledge. (1) ________ The expert has not become careless; the difficulty lies in the very thing that makes them expert.

Paragraph 2

Once we know something well, we lose access to the state of not knowing it. (2) ________ What was once a sequence of twelve deliberate steps has been compressed, through years of practice, into a single fluent gesture.

Paragraph 3

(3) ________ Asked to explain, the expert naturally describes the gesture rather than the steps, skipping precisely the moves a beginner most needs to see. The remedy, fortunately, is less mysterious than the problem: experts who watch a novice attempt the task, rather than simply describing it, quickly rediscover the steps they had forgotten they were taking.

Sentences

A This compression is what allows a surgeon or a pianist to work at speed, but it comes at a price.

B Such answers are rarely the product of impatience.

C Knowledge does not merely accumulate; it reorganizes itself.

D Beginners, by contrast, tend to overestimate how much they have understood.

E · Essay Summary Task Sheet (8.1)

Writing · the essay summary · original practice task

Task. Read the two texts below. Write an essay summarizing and evaluating the key points from both texts. Use your own words throughout as far as possible, and include your own ideas in your answer. Write about 240–280 words. (A typical format for this task type; always check the current official specification.)

Text 1 · In Defense of Memory. It has become fashionable to dismiss memorization as a relic of the schoolroom, made obsolete by the search engine. Yet this view misunderstands how thinking works. We can only reason with what we already hold in mind: a doctor who must look up every symptom cannot see a pattern, and a reader who must search for every reference cannot enjoy a poem. Far from competing with understanding, a well-stocked memory is its precondition. What we should abandon is not memorization but its dullest methods.

Text 2 · The Scarce Skill. Schools still devote hours to material that any student can retrieve in seconds. That time would be better spent teaching learners to judge sources, to connect ideas and to recognize what they do not know. Memory has not become worthless, but the premium has shifted: in a world of abundant information, the scarce skill is not recall but evaluation. A curriculum that rewards the student who remembers most, rather than the one who questions best, is preparing young people for a world that no longer exists.

Before you hand it in: ☐ both texts summarized ☐ both evaluated ☐ my own view, weighed ☐ my own words ☐ within the word count ☐ one register throughout

I · Masterwork Kit — Format Cards & Subject Starters (8.3)

Pass these round at the end of C23. Learners choose a format and a subject they genuinely have a view on; the starters are for anyone who is stuck. Subjects stay in workplace, education, science, arts and culture, technology, language and community territory.

Format · Speech

A talk of 4–5 minutes

Delivered in full from notes; open and close by heart. Built for the ear: a hook, a signposted spine, a resonant close.

Starters: Why every beginner is braver than every expert · The case for slower cities · What a second language taught me about the first (real or a new identity) · The skill no school teaches

Format · Op-ed

500–700 words for a named publication

An arguable claim, a reason to read it now, a fair concession, a memorable last line. In the salon: a 1-minute frame + the two strongest paragraphs, performed.

Starters: Every library needs a room without screens · Four-day weeks belong in the public sector too · Stop ranking universities · Workplaces should pay for language learning

Format · Creative piece

500–700 words

A short story, a personal essay, a sequence of prose poems or a dramatic monologue. In the salon: read aloud (or an excerpt) + a 30-second craft note.

Starters: A place at 6 a.m. · A conversation overheard on a bus · An object that outlived its owner · The first word I loved in English (real or invented)

Format · Research summary

500–700 words, 3+ real sources

One question, what the evidence says, how strong it is, what remains open. Sources listed; no invented figures. In the salon: a 4-minute spoken briefing from notes.

Starters: What we know about sleep and learning · Does bilingualism change how we think? · Do open-plan offices work? · What makes feedback effective?

L · Teacher Observation Checklist · C25 → C26

How to use it

Observe during the warmer, the reading and response preparation, the salons (you rotate between groups) and the lifelong-command exchange. Mark ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with visible effort, or with slips that affect precision · ○ not yet / not observed. You don't need a mark in every box; add items 1–2 from the piece itself after class. Then use it to (1) plan each learner's C26 assessment. The C2 final assessment is an individual speaking interview (about 12 minutes) plus listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's C2 final assessment; match these notes to its speaking criteria and decide where each learner should start, (2) choose the two weakest items for a short C26 warm-up, (3) name one real strength for each learner on their returned piece and fill in their fossilization slip, and (4) note who needs encouragement. This is not a grade.

  • 1 Piece: a clear controlling idea, fit for its format and audience
  • 2 Precision: the exact word, sound collocation, no near-misses
  • 3 Register held throughout; shifts are deliberate
  • 4 Cohesion across the whole piece or talk
  • 5 Rhetoric and imagery used with purpose; seams hidden
  • 6 Delivery: pause, stress, pace; performed, not read
  • 7 Discussion: understands searching questions; concedes and narrows with poise
  • 8 As respondent: quotes precisely; genuine question; fair challenge
  • 9 Accuracy under pressure: errors rare (fossilized items noted)
  • 10 Lifelong plan: specific, self-diagnosed, realistic

M · Listening Scripts (Teacher)

How to read these scripts

Read at natural speed: the pace of an articulate adult in a real conversation, a real interview or a real talk, not a slowed-down "teacher voice." At C2, learners should hear connected speech, reductions and genuine hesitation where the script marks it. Where a script has two voices, use a small change of position or pitch, or ask a confident learner to rehearse one part with you. Stage directions are in square brackets: perform them, don't read them.

8.1-A · Two pairs, one task (Speaking Circuit set-up: read Pair 1, then Pair 2; "Which pair would score higher? Why?")

Examiner: A town wants to make its center more welcoming. Talk together about how effective each idea might be, and then decide which two to recommend. · Pair 1, Candidate A: Well, I think the park is the best idea, because parks are good for everyone, children, older people, and green spaces reduce stress, and also the market is good because people like buying locally, and the buses are important too because many people don't have cars, and the outdoor seating is nice in summer, so I think the park. · Candidate B: Yes. I also think the park. And the garden. The garden is good because people can grow vegetables and meet their neighbors, and it's cheap, and the school can use it too. · Examiner: Thank you. So which two do you recommend? · A: The park. · B: And the garden, I think.
Pair 2, Candidate A: Shall we start with the market? I'd say it's the quickest win: it brings people in on a fixed day. What's your take? · Candidate B: I'm with you on the foot traffic, though I'd add that it only helps one day a week. The outdoor seating might do more for the everyday feel of the place. · A: That's a fair point. I see the garden slightly differently from the park, in that people build it themselves, so they feel it's theirs. Would you agree? · B: Up to a point. The upkeep worries me, but if the school's after-school program ran it, that would solve two problems at once. · A: Now that's clever. So, are we agreed on the seating and the garden, with the school involved? · B: Agreed. Let's recommend those two.

Pair 1 has good ideas but runs two parallel monologues: nobody invites, builds or differs, and the decision is two separate answers. Pair 2 organizes (Shall we start with…?), invites (What's your take? · Would you agree?), builds (I'd add that…), differs softly (I see it slightly differently · Up to a point), combines ideas, and decides together (are we agreed…?).

8.1-B · Listen past the distractor (Workbook 8.1 Ex 5B: read each extract once, at natural speed)

1. We were all sure the delay was down to the budget; that's what everyone assumed. But when we actually went through the figures, the money was fine. It came down to timing: the contractor simply couldn't start until March.
2. Was the new scheduling system a success? Well… it was… [pause] fine. I mean, it does what it says it does. I just can't say I've noticed anyone's life getting any easier.
3. My first instinct was to recommend the cheaper supplier, and on paper they're excellent. But having visited both, I'd go with the local firm without hesitation. Their after-sales support is in a different league.
4. People assume I took up the cello to relax. Quite the reverse, actually. I took it up because I wanted something I'd be bad at for years. Something to keep me humble.

Answers: 1 timing, not the budget (matter-of-fact) · 2 unconvinced: "fine" is faint praise, delivered hesitantly (lukewarm) · 3 the local firm, for its after-sales support (decisive) · 4 to be bad at something for a long time, to stay humble (wry). In each, the first thing mentioned is the trap.

8.1-C · A model long turn (optional: read if learners ask what two minutes sounds like; about 2 minutes)

Candidate: How far should we trust expert advice? That's an interesting one, and I'd like to take it from two angles: the kind of expert, and the stakes. [pause] First, the field. An expert is only an expert within a boundary. A cardiologist's view on your heart deserves a great deal of weight; the same cardiologist's view on the housing market deserves exactly as much as anyone else's. So the first question to ask isn't "Is this person an expert?" but "An expert in what?" [pause] Second, the stakes. When the cost of being wrong is small, I'd happily follow advice I haven't checked. When it's high, a medical decision, say, or a large investment, trust shouldn't mean blind acceptance; it should mean asking the expert to explain their reasoning, and perhaps seeking a second opinion. And that, I think, is where disagreement between experts becomes useful rather than alarming: it shows us where the evidence runs out. [pause] So, to pull that together: we should trust expert advice generously within its field, cautiously outside it, and actively when the stakes are high.

Point out: a framing sentence with a map ("two angles"), two signposted points, precise lexis (deserves a great deal of weight, blind acceptance, where the evidence runs out), a tricolon in the close with a falling third beat, and a deliberate ending ("So, to pull that together…").

8.2-A · Same words, two deliveries (lead-in: read version 1, then version 2; the words are identical)

Version 1 [fast, even speed, no pauses, every word at the same volume, eyes on the page]: Most of us will spend around twenty-five years of our lives asleep. We treat that time as lost. It isn't. It's when the brain files the day, repairs the body and, quite often, solves the problem we went to bed with. So tonight, don't think of sleep as the end of your day. Think of it as the part where the work gets done.
Version 2 [look up; slower; pauses marked]: Most of us will spend around twenty-five years of our lives || asleep. || We treat that time as lost. || It isn't. || It's when the brain files the day ↗ | repairs the body ↗ | and, quite often, solves the problem we went to bed with ↘. || So tonight, | don't think of sleep as the end of your day. || Think of it as the part | where the work | gets done. ↘

8.2-B · The speech on talent and its Q&A (the workbook dialogue: two voices; perform the pauses)

Speaker: Every expert you admire was once catastrophically bad at the thing they're now famous for. [pause] Every single one. We tell ourselves a comforting lie: that talent is something you're handed at birth. It lets us off the hook. But I want to leave you with three thoughts, and here's the first: the people we call gifted are, almost always, just the people who refused to quit while they were still terrible. Think about that. [pause] The gap between you and the person you admire is not talent. It is time, and the willingness to be bad in public for longer than felt comfortable. So if you take one thing from this room today, let it be this: don't wait to be good before you begin. [pause] Begin, and let the being-good arrive on its own schedule. That's how every one of your heroes did it. And it's how you will too. · Audience member: That's inspiring, but isn't it a bit easy to say? Some people really do start with more advantages than others. · Speaker: That's a fair challenge, and you're absolutely right: the starting lines are not equal, and I'd never pretend otherwise. What I'm arguing is narrower: that whatever your starting line, quitting early guarantees you stay behind it. Advantage helps. Persistence is the part that's yours to control. Does that distinction land for you? · Audience member: It does, actually. Thank you.

Ask: "What does the speaker do with the challenge?" (Concedes the valid part, narrows the claim, checks that the answer landed.) Note that the speech promises "three thoughts" but this excerpt gives only the first: a good moment to discuss whether a promised number must always be delivered (it must).

8.2-C · I never said she stole the money (Workbook 8.2 Ex 1B: read in this order, stressing the word in capitals, with a short pause between readings)

1. I never said she stole the MONEY. · 2. I never SAID she stole the money. · 3. I never said she stole the money. [stress on I] · 4. I never said SHE stole the money. · 5. I NEVER said she stole the money.

Answers: 1 MONEY (e) · 2 SAID (c) · 3 I (a) · 4 SHE (d) · 5 NEVER (b). Then ask learners to read one aloud for the class to identify.

8.3-A · A masterwork and its discussion (the workbook dialogue: two voices; Listen 1 for gist, Listen 2 for Ex 1 and the questions)

Speaker: I've come to believe that fluency in a language is the least interesting thing about knowing it. [pause] Fluency is the door. What lies beyond it, the humor you can finally catch, the argument you can finally win on its own terms, the stranger who becomes a friend because you met them in their own words: that is the room. And I spent years, frankly, mistaking the door for the room. So when people ask me why anyone would climb all the way to mastery (why not stop at "good enough"?), I answer with a question of my own. Good enough for what? Good enough to be understood, yes. But not, perhaps, good enough to be moved, or to move someone else. And it is the moving, in the end, that makes a language worth the climb. · Discussant: That's a lovely image, but isn't there something a little elitist in it? Most people will never reach mastery, and they live perfectly rich lives in their language all the same. · Speaker: That's a genuinely important challenge, and you're right to press it; I'd hate for this to sound like a hierarchy of worth. Let me draw the distinction I actually mean. I'm not saying mastery makes a person richer; I'm saying it opens particular rooms: a poem in the original, a joke that doesn't survive translation. Those rooms aren't better than others. They're simply closed until you have the key. My argument is only that, if you want those rooms, the climb is the price, and it's a fair one. · Discussant: And for someone just beginning that climb, what would you say? · Speaker: That the summit they're dreading is closer than they think, and that the view, when they reach it, is not an ending but a door of its own. The language doesn't stop giving once you've mastered it. That, if anything, is when it begins.

8.3-B · A model response round (the protocol stage: four voices, or you read all four with a change of position; learners name the four moves)

Chair: Thank you, Sofia. Respondent 1, what landed for you, and what would you like to ask? · Respondent 1: What landed for me was the phrase "information's impersonation." It made a phrase we hear every day, "the age of information," suddenly suspicious, and it did it in one word. My question: what made you choose "impersonation" rather than, say, "imitation"? · Sofia: Good question. "Imitation" can be innocent; children imitate. "Impersonation" implies intent to deceive, and that's the charge I wanted to make. · Chair: Respondent 2, your challenge, please. · Respondent 2: Someone might push back on "we have never known more and understood less." Isn't that a claim every generation makes about the next? · Sofia: That's a genuinely important challenge, and you're right that it's an old complaint. Let me narrow it: I'm not saying people understand less than they used to; I'm saying the gap between what we can access and what we've actually thought about has never been wider. That's the claim I'd defend. · Chair: One offer each. · Respondent 1: If this were mine, I might end the opening one sentence earlier, on "It is an invitation." · Respondent 2: If this were mine, I might put the narrowed version of the antithesis into the piece itself. · Sofia: Thank you.

The four moves: reflect (quotes "information's impersonation" and names its effect) · probe (a genuine question about a word choice) · challenge (fair, about the claim, not the person) · offer ("If this were mine…"). Note that Sofia answers the challenge by conceding and narrowing, and says only "Thank you" to the offers.
