C · Stance Workshop — Neutral Reports (3.1)

Round 1. One report per group of 3; give neighboring groups different reports. Each is a flat, factual summary of an invented trial. Groups rewrite it three times: skeptical, approving and judiciously balanced, without changing or adding a single fact. The small gray model at the bottom is a balanced version for you: cut it off before class. Three-stance models for Report 1 are in the answer key.

Report 1 · Culture

Harbor Street Museum: free Thursday evenings

For six months, the museum opened free of charge from 5 to 9 p.m. on Thursdays. Average Thursday attendance rose from 180 to 640 visitors. Sixty-two percent of Thursday visitors were under 35. Spending in the gift shop per visitor fell by 10 percent, but total Thursday sales rose. The trial cost $22,000 in staff overtime. In a survey of 300 Thursday visitors, 71 percent said they would not otherwise have visited. The board will vote in March on whether to continue.

Over a six-month trial, average Thursday attendance rose from 180 to 640, and 62 percent of visitors were under 35. Admittedly, spending per visitor fell by 10 percent and the trial cost $22,000 in overtime; total sales, however, rose. The survey suggests, broadly speaking, that the evenings reached a new audience, which the board will weigh when it votes in March.
Report 2 · Education

Northgate Community College: open textbooks

Last year, Northgate Community College replaced printed textbooks with free online materials in twelve introductory courses. Students' average spending on course materials fell by $310 per semester. The pass rate in the twelve courses was 78 percent, compared with 77 percent the year before. Fourteen percent of students reported problems accessing the materials at home because of unreliable internet. Instructors spent an average of 40 hours each adapting the materials. The college plans to extend the program to twenty courses next year.

Replacing printed textbooks with free online materials cut students' spending by $310 a semester, while pass rates held broadly steady (78 percent, against 77). Admittedly, 14 percent of students struggled to access the materials at home, and instructors each invested some 40 hours in adapting them. On balance, the results appear to support the plan to extend the program to twenty courses.
Report 3 · Technology

City of Linden: the parking-permit app

In January, the city of Linden replaced paper residential parking permits with a smartphone app. The app cost $140,000 to develop. Time spent by officers checking permits fell by a third. The city received 1,200 complaints in the first month and 90 in the fourth. Eight percent of permit holders, most of them over 70, had no smartphone and had to register in person at City Hall. The council has said it will keep a paper option.

Linden's $140,000 parking app has cut the time officers spend checking permits by a third. Complaints, understandably numerous at first (1,200 in the first month), had fallen to 90 by the fourth. That said, 8 percent of permit holders, most of them over 70, had to register in person, which presumably explains the council's decision to keep a paper option.
Report 4 · Arts

Lakeshore Chamber Orchestra: pay what you can

This season, the Lakeshore Chamber Orchestra ran four "pay what you can" concerts. Audiences paid an average of $14 per ticket, compared with the usual price of $45. The hall was 96 percent full, compared with an average of 55 percent for regular concerts. Thirty percent of the audience were attending the orchestra for the first time. Net revenue per concert fell by 38 percent. A private donor has covered the shortfall for this season only.

The four pay-what-you-can concerts filled the hall to 96 percent of capacity, against an average of 55, and 30 percent of the audience were newcomers. Admittedly, the average ticket brought in $14 rather than $45, and net revenue fell by 38 percent. The experiment has, broadly speaking, won a new audience; whether it can survive once the donor's support ends remains to be seen.

D · Cohesion & Coherence Lab — The Wrenfield Article (3.2)

Round 1. An invented magazine article of about 700 words in seven paragraphs, printed here in scrambled order with scrambled letters. Cut along the dashed lines and make packets: groups of 3, A = M, K, R · B = D, H · C = T, W; pairs, A = M, D, T, K · B = H, W, R. For the first five minutes, learners may only read aloud or describe their paragraphs, not show them. The intended order and the clues are in the answer key (3.2).

MSmall Forests, Big Questions

It is this unplanned expense that critics have seized on. The city's budget office calculates that each pocket-forest tree has cost around $38 to plant and keep alive, compared with roughly $12 for a sapling in a conventional park. Several members of the parks committee argue that the same money would shade more sidewalks and cool more homes if it were spent on mature street trees in the neighborhoods that currently have the fewest of them. A street tree, they concede, costs more to plant than either, but it begins to cast useful shade almost at once.

DSmall Forests, Big Questions

The early results were striking. According to the city's own monitoring, the first twelve forests grew, on average, about a meter a year, and surveys recorded nearly three times as many insect species in them as in the mown grass nearby. Residents, too, seem to have taken the plantings to heart. Attendance at volunteer planting days doubled in the second year, and two elementary schools now use their neighboring forest as an outdoor classroom for science lessons. Local businesses noticed as well: a café beside the first forest on Carver Street began hosting monthly "forest breakfasts," and several property owners asked, somewhat unexpectedly, whether a forest could be planted on their own block.

TSmall Forests, Big Questions

Supporters of the scheme reply that the comparison is unfair. A pocket forest, they point out, is not a collection of individual trees but a small ecosystem, and its benefits (habitat for wildlife, healthier soil, the absorption of storm water, a place for children to learn) do not show up in a cost-per-tree figure. An independent ecologist commissioned by the city broadly agrees, though with a caveat of her own. The forests' ecological value, she concludes, is real, but it will not be reliably measurable for another decade, and in the meantime the city should claim no more than its evidence can support.

KSmall Forests, Big Questions

When the city of Wrenfield announced three years ago that it would plant forty "pocket forests" (dense patches of native trees, none larger than a tennis court) on vacant lots and neglected corners, the plan attracted more curiosity than controversy. It seemed modest, even charming: a few thousand saplings, a few hundred volunteers, a little more green in a city that badly needed it. Yet the pocket forests have since become the most argued-about line in Wrenfield's environmental budget, and the argument turns on a question that reaches well beyond one mid-sized city: how, exactly, should a city measure the value of a tree?

HSmall Forests, Big Questions

What the Wrenfield experiment offers, then, is less a verdict than a lesson in bookkeeping. If the only measure is the price of a single tree, the pocket forests look extravagant; if the measure includes what a decade of dense, self-sustaining woodland may give back to the neighborhoods around it, they may yet look like a bargain. Neither measure is dishonest, but each answers a different question, and a city can choose which question it wants answered. The council's next budget will reveal which of these two ledgers it has chosen to read.

WSmall Forests, Big Questions

These gains, however, were put to the test in Wrenfield's second summer, the driest since records began. Without regular watering, almost a fifth of the saplings in the newest sites died, and the volunteer teams that were supposed to fill the gap proved difficult to organize in the heat. Some residents carried buckets from their own kitchens, and a retired firefighter lent his street a water tank, but goodwill could not cover forty sites. The city eventually hired a contractor to water the surviving sites twice a week until the fall, at a cost that had not appeared anywhere in the original budget.

RSmall Forests, Big Questions

The method behind the forests is simple to describe, if laborious to carry out. Volunteers first break up and enrich the compacted soil, then plant saplings of up to thirty native species at a density of three or four per square meter, far closer together than any conventional park would allow. Crowded in this way, the young trees compete fiercely for light and grow upward quickly, forming a closed canopy within a few seasons. After two or three years of weeding and watering, the plantings are meant to need no further care at all.

E · Synthesis Workshop — Source Cards A/B/C (3.3)

One set per group of 3; each learner gets one card. All three sources and writers are invented. Learners read for two minutes, turn the card over, and give a 30-second gist from memory. Then all cards go face up for the idea grid and the writing. A model 200-word synthesis is in the answer key (3.3).

Source A · Library newsletter

Marisol Ortega, Director, Brookhaven Public Library

"When we eliminated overdue fines two years ago, several board members predicted that books would simply stop coming back. The opposite happened. Our return rate rose slightly, from 94 to 95 percent, and more than 3,000 lapsed cardholders, many of them children whose accounts had been blocked over a few dollars, started borrowing again. Fines were never a serious source of income: collecting them cost us almost as much in staff time as they brought in. What they did very efficiently was keep away the families who most needed the library."

Source B · Report to the county board

Daniel Hsu, Budget Analyst, County Finance Office

"The case for abolishing library fines is often presented as cost-free. It is not. In our county, fines bring in about $180,000 a year, a modest but real contribution to the budget for new books, and that sum would have to be found elsewhere. Libraries that have gone fine-free report stable return rates, but the data are drawn largely from their own records, over short periods. Before the county follows their lead, it should fund a two-year pilot in a limited number of branches and collect independent data on returns, replacement costs and new registrations."

Source C · Journal article

Dr. Amara Nwosu, family-literacy researcher

"Our interviews with sixty low-income parents suggest that the fear of fines, more than the fines actually owed, keeps many families from borrowing at all. Several parents described avoiding the library entirely after a single overdue notice. However, fines were only one barrier among several: transportation, opening hours and whether staff seemed welcoming mattered as much to the families we spoke to. Removing fines is therefore likely to help, but it is unlikely on its own to close the gap in library use between wealthier and poorer neighborhoods."

G · Listening & Dictation Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

Read at a natural speed. Read each script twice: once for the main idea, once to check. Change your voice for each speaker, or give the lines to confident learners. The 🔊 buttons in the online lessons give learners extra listening at home.

3.1-A · The museum commentary (Workbook 3.1, Ex 1)

Read it like a relaxed podcast host. Pause after each fronted marker and use a fall-rise on admittedly and arguably.

Right, so, the Harbor Street Museum has finally reopened, and I went on Saturday. To be honest, I went in expecting to be disappointed. Admittedly, the building looks wonderful: the new glass roof floods the main hall with light. That said, the big change is the layout. The old galleries were arranged by century; the new ones are arranged by theme: "water," "work," "home." Arguably, that makes the collection far more approachable for younger visitors. Mind you, it also means a Roman oil lamp now sits beside a 1970s toaster, which, frankly, made me laugh out loud. Tellingly, the busiest room on Saturday was the old map room they left untouched. Anyway, long story short: go, but go with an open mind.

Answers: 1 Right, so (O) · 2 To be honest (I) · 3 Admittedly (S) · 4 That said (O) · 5 Arguably (S) · 6 Mind you (I) · 7 frankly (S) · 8 Tellingly (S). Attitude: amused, mildly skeptical, fair; a qualified recommendation. Ask: "What is tellingly saying about the new layout without saying it?" (Visitors still prefer the old way.)

3.1-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. Admittedly, the sample was small; that said, the trend is hard to ignore. · 2. The results, remarkably, held up in every region. · 3. Presumably, the committee will want to see the full figures. · 4. He said nothing about the budget, tellingly.

Check the commas (they mark the pause you read) and the position of each adverbial: front, medial, front, final. Ask: "Which one sounds like a verdict?" (4.)

3.2-A · The lecture opening (Workbook 3.2, Ex 1)

Read the first paragraph, pause, say "Twenty minutes later…", then read the second on a noticeably higher starting pitch (pitch reset).

Good morning, everyone. Today's lecture will argue that the history of the city park is, at heart, a history of public health. I'll begin with the crowded industrial city of the 1840s, then turn to the reformers who campaigned for the first public parks, and finally ask what their example means for cities today. · [Twenty minutes later] · So far, then, we've seen that the first parks were designed as the "lungs" of the city, places where workers could breathe clean air on their one free day. This raises a further question: who were these parks actually for? A very different picture emerges when we look at the rules posted at their gates: no running, no ball games, and, in several cities, no entry for anyone "improperly dressed."

Answers: 1 will argue that · 2 I'll begin with · 3 then turn to · 4 finally ask · 5 So far, then, · 6 This raises a further question · 7 A very different picture emerges when. Ask: "What do you predict the lecture says next?" (That parks were designed for some of the public more than others.) That prediction is what good signposting makes possible.

3.2-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. This tension runs through the whole report, and it is where we must begin. · 2. Such objections, however, miss the deeper point I raised a moment ago. · 3. The result was chaos; that chaos, in turn, bred a hunger for order. · 4. It is this single thread, trust, that stitches the whole argument together.

Ask: "Which words point back to something earlier?" (1 This tension · 2 Such objections, I raised a moment ago · 3 that chaos, in turn · 4 this single thread.) In 3, check that learners de-accent the second chaos (given) and stress order (new).

3.3-A · The radio panel wrap-up (Workbook 3.3, Ex 1)

Host: We're nearly out of time, so let me try to pull the threads together. Priya Raman, who runs a café on Main Street, argues that closing the street to cars on weekends would bring in more shoppers and cut pollution. Tom Becker, who owns the hardware store, concedes that the street is badly congested on Saturdays, but he insists that businesses like his need delivery access, and he wants a three-month trial before anything permanent. Dr. Lee, a transport researcher, questions the city's footfall figures, which, she points out, were collected on a single sunny weekend. So all three of our guests agree that the street is overcrowded on weekends and that something has to change. Where they diverge is on how confident we can be about the benefits, and how quickly the city should act. Thank you all.

Answers: Priya · argues · closure brings shoppers, cuts pollution · Tom · concedes / insists / wants · congestion is real, but deliveries matter; a trial first · Dr. Lee · questions / points out · the figures are weak (one sunny weekend). Agreement: overcrowded on weekends; change is needed. Ask: "Was the host fair? Which verb would have made the host sound biased against Dr. Lee?" (claims / alleges.)

3.3-B · Gist it (C9 summarizing stage · read twice; learners write a one-sentence gist)

A frequently cited study found that students who took lecture notes by hand remembered the key ideas better than those who typed on laptops, apparently because writing slowly forced them to summarize rather than transcribe. Later attempts to repeat the study, however, have produced weaker and less consistent results. Some researchers now argue that what matters is not the tool but the habit: laptop users who are trained to summarize as they type appear to do about as well as those who write by hand.

Model gist: "Although an early study suggested that handwritten notes beat laptops for learning, later studies are mixed, and some researchers argue that the habit of summarizing, not the tool, is what matters." Check: the concession survives (although), the hedges survive (suggested, some researchers), and no one writes "Handwriting is better" (distortion) or "Laptops are fine" (over-generalization).
