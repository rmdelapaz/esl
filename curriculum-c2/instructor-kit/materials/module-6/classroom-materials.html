B · Writer Identity Cards (6.1 · 6.2)

Any learner may write as one of these personas instead of themselves, in any task, with no reason needed. Each gives a beat (what they write about), a voice, and an anecdote they can use as a hook. In C18 the "question I'd research" line gives an alternative topic for the Architecture Lab.

Writer identity · 1

Inés — food critic, city magazine

Voice: warm, precise, a little mischievous. Never uses "delicious."

Anecdote: A chef once came out of the kitchen to ask why she had left half a dessert. She told him the truth; he changed the recipe.

A question I'd research: Do open kitchens change how diners judge food?

Writer identity · 2

Kwame — tech columnist, weekend paper

Voice: skeptical, funny, allergic to marketing language.

Anecdote: His smart doorbell once announced a visitor who turned out to be a cat.

A question I'd research: Do smart-home devices save energy in practice?

Writer identity · 3

Mei — city-life blogger

Voice: first person, observant, loves small details: bus stops, benches, corner shops.

Anecdote: She once rode every bus route in the city in a single weekend.

A question I'd research: Do public benches make neighborhoods friendlier?

Writer identity · 4

Tomasz — arts reviewer, cultural weekly

Voice: measured, generous, allergic to hype; finds one good thing in every show.

Anecdote: He fell asleep in the front row of a three-hour opera and wrote his best review about why.

A question I'd research: Do free museum evenings bring in new visitors or the same ones?

Writer identity · 5

Aisha — HR trade-magazine writer

Voice: brisk, practical, evidence-first; writes for busy managers.

Anecdote: She once counted how many "quick questions" she answered in a day: forty-one.

A question I'd research: Does the four-day week change how teams share knowledge?

Writer identity · 6

Rafael — museum educator and blogger

Voice: curious, patient, explains without talking down.

Anecdote: A six-year-old once asked him why a famous painting "looks unfinished," and he still hasn't found a better question.

A question I'd research: Do audio guides help visitors look longer or less?

C · Commission Cards (6.1)

One card per learner (the three members of a group take different cards). Each names the outlet, the reader, the genre, the topic and, for op-eds, the assigned side. Target: 200–250 words in class, 250–350 at home.

Commission 1 · Review

Harbor Hall, the new downtown food hall

Outlet: City Life magazine (lifestyle). Reader: 25–45, eats out weekly, reads on a phone.

Facts: 14 stalls; hand-pulled noodles and dumplings are the stars; 40 stools for hundreds of people; loud; lines at noon.

Commission 2 · Op-ed

Phone-free concerts

Outlet: the arts section of a serious daily newspaper. Reader: informed, skeptical, time-poor.

Side (assigned): FOR venues asking audiences to lock phones in pouches during shows.

Objection to grant: people paid for the ticket and may want a memory.

Commission 3 · Feature

The night buses

Outlet: a city magazine. Reader: residents who have never ridden a bus after midnight.

Facts: route 47 runs 12:30–5 a.m.; riders are nurses, bakers, cleaners, students; the driver has worked the route for 11 years.

Commission 4 · Product review

A "smart" kitchen scale with an app

Outlet: a tech blog. Reader: keen home cooks, curious but wary of gadgets.

Facts: accurate to 1 g; the app suggests recipes from what you weigh; setup took 25 minutes; costs three times a normal scale.

Required: an actionable verdict (who should buy it, who shouldn't).

Commission 5 · Op-ed

Free Thursday evenings at the art museum

Outlet: the local newspaper. Reader: residents of all ages; many have never visited.

Side (assigned): FOR keeping the free evenings, even though they cost the museum money.

Objection to grant: free entry means crowds and lost ticket income.

Commission 6 · Feature

The four-day week at a design studio

Outlet: an HR trade magazine. Reader: HR managers considering a trial.

Facts: 30-person studio; six-month trial; clients were told in advance; Fridays covered by a rotating pair; staff turnover fell.

Commission 7 · Review

A neighborhood bakery's sourdough

Outlet: a food blog. Reader: bread enthusiasts who will travel for a good loaf.

Facts: dark, blistered crust; tangy crumb; sells out by 10 a.m.; the rye is disappointing; tiny shop, no seating.

Commission 8 · Op-ed

Reply-all culture

Outlet: the workplace column of a business magazine. Reader: managers and office workers.

Side (assigned): AGAINST using reply-all by default.

Objection to grant: reply-all keeps everyone informed and avoids people being left out.

C · Pitch Cards — Writer (A) & Editors (B) (6.1)

Information gap. The writer (A) knows the idea; the editor (B) knows what the outlet's readers want and one thing the editor will not print. A pitches in three sentences; B may ask only three questions, then says yes, no, or "yes, if…". Match the editor card to the outlet on A's Commission card (lifestyle and food blog → B1; national and local papers → B2; tech blog → B3; city and trade magazines → B4).

Pitch · A · Writer

Your three sentences

  1. Your hook, exactly as you'd write it.
  2. Your line (op-ed) or verdict (review) or payoff (feature), in one breath.
  3. Why this outlet's reader will care this week.

Then answer the editor's questions. If the answer is "yes, if…", write the condition at the top of your draft.

Pitch · B1 · Editor, lifestyle magazine / food blog

What our readers want

Readers skim on phones, love one strong recommendation, and hate being lectured. Last month they complained a review "never said whether to go."

Won't print: anything over 350 words, or a review with no verdict in the first paragraph.

Pitch · B2 · Opinion editor, national / local paper

What our readers want

Readers are skeptical and well informed; they respect a writer who grants the other side its best argument. They dislike hype and exclamation marks.

Won't print: an op-ed whose line can't be said in one sentence, or one with no evidence at all.

Pitch · B3 · Editor, tech blog

What our readers want

Readers are curious but have been burned by gadgets before. They want to know who a product is for, and they notice marketing language instantly.

Won't print: "game-changer," "revolutionary," or a verdict that says "it depends."

Pitch · B4 · Features editor, city / trade magazine

What our readers want

Readers want a human story and a practical takeaway. Trade readers need one number they can quote to their boss; city readers need one place they can visit.

Won't print: a feature whose payoff doesn't arrive in the first paragraph.

Pitch · Editor's three questions (any card)

If you're stuck, ask:

  • What's the one thing you want our reader to remember?
  • What's the strongest objection, and do you grant it?
  • What's the specific detail that proves your point?

C · Annotated Models (6.1) — teacher copy

Use these to show "what a 4 looks like" after the critique, or project one if learners ask for a model. The op-ed is the workbook's Exercise 2 text with its moves labeled.

Model op-ed · workplace column · "Give Us Back the Lunch Hour"

[HOOK · vivid particular + comic twist] At 12:47 last Tuesday I ate a cheese sandwich over my keyboard while answering an email about a meeting about lunch. [recruits the reader] I suspect you have done something similar this week, and I want to suggest that it is making us worse at our jobs. [LINE] The desk lunch is not a sign of dedication; it is a false economy, and it is time offices treated the lunch hour as part of the working day rather than a gap in it.

[CONCESSION] The obvious reply is that busy people are simply busy: the work has to be done, and an hour away from it is an hour lost. [TURN] That is true, and it is exactly the problem. [EVIDENCE] In one office survey, more than half of employees said they rarely took a full break, yet the same employees rated the afternoon as their least productive time of day. [ANECDOTE] A colleague of mine, a data analyst, started walking to the river every day at one o'clock; within a month she was solving in ten minutes the problems that used to take her the whole afternoon. [REBUTTAL, landed with a short sentence] Rest, it turns out, is not the opposite of work. It is part of how work gets done.

[CLOSE · echoes the hook, turns it into an instruction] So take the hour. Leave the building. And next Tuesday at 12:47, let the email about lunch wait until after lunch.

Model review paragraph · food blog · evaluation + calibrated verdict

[DESCRIPTION · minimal] Crumb & Co. is a shop the width of a hallway, and by ten on a Saturday it has sold out of the one thing you came for. [GROUNDED STRENGTH] What makes its sourdough remarkable is the crust: dark, blistered, almost bitter at the edges, giving way to a crumb with a sharp, clean tang. [GROUNDED WEAKNESS] Where the bakery falls short is everything else on the shelf; the rye is dense in a way that feels accidental rather than traditional. [VERDICT · calibrated and actionable] Go early, buy the sourdough, and leave the rest: for one loaf alone, it is worth crossing the city.

Model feature opening · city magazine · headline + standfirst + front-loaded payoff

The Most Interesting Room in the City Has Wheels
After midnight, route 47 becomes a moving neighborhood of nurses, bakers and students. We rode it for a week.

[PAYOFF EARLY] At 2 a.m. the 47 bus is the friendliest place in town, and almost nobody who sleeps at night knows it exists. [HUMAN STORY] Rosa has driven the route for eleven years; she knows which stop the night baker needs and which student will fall asleep past it. [SUBHEADING follows] Who rides at 2 a.m. …

D · Source Packs A–D (6.2) — fictional sources for practice

One pack per group of 3. Each pack has four short invented sources with a finding, a quotable line and a limitation, chosen so that learners must synthesize: the sources partly agree, partly diverge, and leave a gap. One source in each pack deserves a distancing verb (claims). Cut each pack as one card.

Source Pack A

Does background music help people concentrate at work?

  • Rossi (2021), lab study, 120 office workers. Music with lyrics impaired reading; instrumental music had no measurable effect. "It is the words, not the music, that compete for the reader's attention." Limit: all under 40; lab, not office.
  • Kim (2022), field study, six open-plan offices. Instrumental music improved self-reported focus on routine tasks; no effect on complex tasks. Limit: self-report only.
  • Haddad (2023), review of 30 studies. Effects depend on the task and the listener; introverts are affected more. Concedes most studies are small.
  • Brennan (2024), white paper by a music-streaming company. "Focus playlists raise productivity by 25%." Limit: no method published; commercial interest.
Source Pack B

Are public libraries becoming community hubs, and what happens to reading?

  • Garcia (2020), 12 city libraries. Visits rose 40% after libraries added classes, makerspaces and meeting rooms. "The library has become the city's living room."
  • Nwosu (2022), 20 small-town libraries. A similar rise in visits, strongest among families. Limit: two years of data.
  • Chen (2023), national borrowing data. Loans fell about 3% a year over the same period, including in "hub" libraries.
  • Adeyemi (2024), four libraries that routed every event past the shelves and added "take one home" displays. Loans recovered within a year. Limit: four cases only.
Source Pack C

Are food-delivery apps good for neighborhood restaurants?

  • Moreau (2021), survey of 200 restaurants. 60% said apps brought new customers, especially on weeknights.
  • Okafor (2022), report funded by a delivery app. "Apps doubled sales for partner restaurants." Limit: partners chosen by the app.
  • Lindqvist (2023), accounts of 50 small restaurants. Commission fees of 25–30% left profits flat even when sales rose. "Busier is not the same as better off."
  • Tan (2024), case study of one district. "Dark kitchens" (delivery-only) replaced three sit-down restaurants; street life at night declined.
Source Pack D

Do open-plan offices help people collaborate?

  • Weber (2019), sensor badges in two firms. Face-to-face conversation fell by about two thirds after the move to open plan; email rose.
  • Suárez (2021), survey of 900 employees. Staff report that open plan makes their work more "visible" to managers.
  • Park (2022), design study. Quiet rooms and team "neighborhoods" restored focus; acknowledges that costs rose. "Open is not a layout; it is a set of choices."
  • Ahmed (2023), hybrid-work study. With staff in the office 2–3 days a week, open plan worked well when those days were planned for meetings.

D · Supervision Cards A/B (6.2)

Pairs, 2 minutes each way. The supervisor may only ask, never rewrite. Give the supervisor card to a dominant speaker first.

Supervision · A · Writer

Hand over your outline

Give your partner the outline planner. Say your through-line once, aloud. Then listen while your partner reads your section claims in order.

Answer each question in one or two sentences. Don't defend; decide. Write one change you will make at the top of your plan before you start drafting.

Supervision · B · Supervisor

Read, then ask four questions

Read the writer's section claims aloud in order, without the headings. Then ask:

  1. Could anyone disagree with your through-line? Who?
  2. Could any two sections swap places without loss?
  3. Where is the strongest objection, and which section grants it?
  4. Which key term will you hold constant from start to finish?

E · Style-Sheet Cards A/B (6.3) — information gap

A British author's novel is being published in the United States. In 4 minutes the pair agree a five-line house style (quotation marks, dashes, the Oxford comma, spelling, collective nouns) and write it in the workbook. Each card has one non-negotiable; everything else can be traded. Useful language: Would you be open to…? · I could live with… if… · Where I can't move is…

Style sheet · A · US copy editor

Northgate Books, New York

  • Our house style: "double" quotation marks, commas and periods inside.
  • Closed em dashes (word—word).
  • Oxford comma: non-negotiable (our legal team insists after a misread contract).
  • American spelling (-ize, color, center).
  • Collective nouns singular (the team is).

You can be flexible on dashes and on spelling inside dialogue.

Style sheet · B · British author

Novelist, Manchester

  • You prefer 'single' quotation marks, but you don't feel strongly.
  • You like spaced en dashes (word – word); they "breathe."
  • You never use the Oxford comma, out of habit.
  • Non-negotiable: your British characters' dialogue keeps British spelling and words (colour, pavement, the team are).

You can be flexible on everything outside the dialogue.

G · Game — The Editing Race (6.3 · C20 warmer)

How to play (teams of 3, one race sheet per team): four stations around the room, one passage each. Each passage has 8 faults and 2 traps (correct sentences that break a myth). 4 minutes per station, 1 minute to move. Roles rotate at each station: editor (marks the passage), namer (writes the name of each point), judge (rules on fault vs myth after hearing both teammates). Scoring: +1 fix · +1 name · −1 false alarm · +2 one justified style improvement per station. Ties go to the team with fewer false alarms. Print one copy of each station per team; keep the answer strips.

Station 1 · Café review · 8 faults · 2 traps

Lantern Café, which opened in March on Elm Street, is the kind of place you want to really like. Walking through the door, the smell of cardamom buns hits you first. The owners, who trained in Stockholm, bake everything on site, the buns sell out by ten. There are less tables than there should be, and the staff, which are clearly overworked, rarely have time to clear them.

The menu has one real weakness, the coffee. Between you and I, the espresso tastes as though it was made yesterday. The buns, however, are worth crossing town for; soft, fragrant and generous. Go for the buns, for the quiet mornings and to watch the bakers at work. It is a café I will keep going back to.

Answer strip · Station 1
1 Walking through the door, the smell… → Walking through the door, you smell the cardamom buns first (dangling modifier) · 2 …on site, the buns… → on site; the buns… (comma splice) · 3 less tables → fewer tables (fewer / less) · 4 the staff, which are → who are (who for people) · 5 one real weakness, the coffee → one real weakness: the coffee (colon delivers the promise) · 6 Between you and I → me (object after a preposition) · 7 crossing town for; soft… → crossing town for: soft… or a dash (semicolon misused before a phrase) · 8 for the buns, for the quiet mornings and to watch → for the buns, the quiet mornings and the sight of the bakers at work (parallelism). Traps (no change): to really like (split infinitive) · going back to (final preposition). Note: as though it was made is fine; it is not a contrary-to-fact wish.
Station 2 · Research paragraph · 8 faults · 2 traps

There is broad agreement that background music effects concentration, but studies diverge on why. Rossi (2021), whose study involved 120 office workers, found that music with lyrics impaired reading, instrumental music had no measurable effect. Each of the later studies have confirmed this pattern for reading tasks. Kim (2022) whose participants worked in open-plan offices reports a different picture for routine work. Neither Rossi nor Kim control for age, and each study reports it's own measure of concentration.

Having reviewed these findings, a clear gap emerges: little attention has been paid to older workers, to people who work from shared homes, and whether volume matters. This report aims to directly address the first of these gaps, which earlier studies have simply not looked into.

Answer strip · Station 2
1 effects → affects (confusable: verb affect) · 2 impaired reading, instrumental… → impaired reading, whereas instrumental… or ; instrumental (comma splice) · 3 Each… have confirmed → has (agreement with each) · 4 Kim (2022) whose… offices reports → Kim (2022), whose… offices, reports (non-restrictive commas) · 5 Neither Rossi nor Kim control → controls (nearer subject) · 6 it's own → its own (confusable) · 7 Having reviewed these findings, a clear gap emerges → Having reviewed these findings, we can identify a clear gap (dangling modifier) · 8 to older workers, to people…, and whether volume matters → …, and to the role of volume (parallelism). Traps: to directly address (split infinitive) · looked into (final preposition).
Station 3 · Night-bus feature · 8 faults · 2 traps

At 2 a.m. the 47 bus is the most interesting room in the city. Riding it for a week, the driver became my guide. Her name is Rosa, she has driven the route for eleven years. The route serves three hospitals; two bakeries, a fish market and the university. The number of riders have doubled since 2019. "People think it's empty at night," Rosa says. "It's the opposite."

Riders describe the bus as safe, as warm, and they feel it is friendly. Rosa says the passengers want one thing above all; to get home safely. The city, that funds the service, is now reviewing its budget. 'It's my living room on wheels,' says one nurse. And then, at 2:40, the bus reaches the end of the line. It is a service worth fighting for.

Answer strip · Station 3
1 Riding it for a week, the driver became my guide → Riding it for a week, I came to know the driver, who became my guide (dangling modifier) · 2 Her name is Rosa, she has driven → ; she has or a period (comma splice) · 3 three hospitals; two bakeries → three hospitals, two bakeries (semicolon misused; items contain no commas) · 4 The number… have doubled → has (head noun number) · 5 as safe, as warm, and they feel it is friendly → as safe, warm and friendly (parallelism) · 6 one thing above all; to get home → : to get home (colon) · 7 The city, that funds → The city, which funds (non-restrictive: which) · 8 'It's my living room on wheels,' → "It's my living room on wheels," (inconsistent house style: the piece uses double quotation marks). Traps: And then… (starting with And) · worth fighting for (final preposition).
Station 4 · Office memo · 8 faults · 2 traps

To all staff: from Monday, the third-floor kitchen will close for repairs, staff should use the second-floor kitchen instead. The new coffee machine, which arrived last week, will move with it. Please make sure that dishes are washed, dried and you put them away. There will be less chairs than usual. We want to really thank you for your patience. Each team have been given a new cleaning day. The schedule, that is posted on the fridge, lists every team's day.

Management recommends that everyone brings their own mug. Having read this memo, the new arrangements should be clear. If you have questions, please contact Sam or myself. And if the repairs finish early, we will let you know.

Answer strip · Station 4
1 …for repairs, staff should use… → for repairs; staff should use… (comma splice) · 2 washed, dried and you put them away → washed, dried and put away (parallelism) · 3 less chairs → fewer chairs · 4 Each team have → has (agreement: each is singular) · 5 The schedule, that is posted → The schedule, which is posted (non-restrictive) · 6 recommends that everyone brings → bring (mandative subjunctive, formal memo) · 7 Having read this memo, the new arrangements should be clear → Having read this memo, you should find the new arrangements clear (dangling modifier) · 8 contact Sam or myself → Sam or me (hypercorrect reflexive). Traps: to really thank (split infinitive) · And if the repairs… (starting with And). Not a fault: singular their (accepted in US and UK style guides).

Start with the chef tearing the bread at your table. That's your hook. · Tomás: Got it. When do you need it? · Maya: Thursday, and it's in.

Answers: Published? Yes, next week, if revised by Thursday. 1 cut the recap of the evening to two sentences · 2 anchor "amazing" to a dish (the flatbread) · 3 give a clear verdict (go / what to order / what to skip) · 4 add a reservation (the noise or the forty-minute wait) · 5 replace the opening with a hook (the chef tearing the bread). Ask: "Which of Maya's changes is about structure, which about grounding, which about trust?"

6.1-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. It's tempting to think the problem is new, but the evidence points the other way. · 2. What makes the book remarkable is not its answers but the sharpness of its questions. · 3. For all its flaws, it is a far better film than most that are flawless. · 4. None of this is to deny the difficulty; it is to insist that we stop pretending it away.

Ask: "Which sentence concedes, which evaluates, which gives a verdict?" (1 and 4 concede → turn · 2 evaluates · 3 verdict.) Check the semicolon in 4 and the comma after flaws in 3.

6.2-A · The supervision meeting (Workbook 6.2, Ex 1)

Dr. Lewis: Come in, Amina. I've read the chapter on library hubs. First, the good news: it's very well written, line by line. The sentences are clean and confident. · Amina: But? · Dr. Lewis: But I got lost around page nine. By then I couldn't tell where we were in the argument. You stop telling the reader what's been settled and what's coming. · Amina: I think I knew where I was, so I assumed the reader did. · Dr. Lewis: Classic. Open each section with a backward look and a forward one: "Having established that visits are rising, this section turns to borrowing." Second problem: Section 3, the history of the building. It's interesting, but what does it have to do with your through-line? · Amina: Honestly, not much. I just found the archive material fascinating. · Dr. Lewis: Then either cut it to a paragraph or reframe it, so it explains why the building's design matters for the hub question. Third, your literature review is a queue: Garcia found this, Nwosu found that, Chen found something else. · Amina: So group them? · Dr. Lewis: Exactly. Where do they agree, where do they part company, and what has nobody asked yet? End on the gap; that's where your chapter comes in. Oh, and one small thing: you call the same idea "community use," "social function" and "hub activity" in three different sections. Pick one. · Amina: "Community use." I'll hold it all the way through.

Answers: happy with the sentences, not the structure. Problems → fixes: 1 reader lost, no signposts → backward + forward signpost at each seam · 2 Section 3 off the through-line → cut to a paragraph or reframe · 3 literature review is a queue → group by agreement / divergence, end on the gap. Extra: terminological drift → hold "community use."

6.2-B · Dictation (read each sentence 3 times)

1. As noted in the previous section, the productivity gains are real; this chapter argues that they are not the whole story. · 2. The remainder of this report is organized as follows: three strands of evidence, then their implications for design. · 3. Smith demonstrates the productivity effect; in contrast to that view, Lee contends that the gains are overstated. · 4. What remains unclear is whether the new visitors ever borrow books.

Ask: "Which way does each signpost look?" (1 backward then forward · 2 locational · 3 attribution: demonstrates = accepted, contends = a position · 4 the gap.) Check the stress on deMONstrates, conTENDS.

6.3-A · Hear the punctuation (Workbook 6.3, Ex 1 · read with clear pauses and pitch)

1. The proposal | (lower) after much debate | was approved. · 2. The café was full ‖ the bakery next door was empty. · 3. She had one goal ↗ ‖ to win. · 4. They had planned for everything — | and then the power failed. · 5. My colleague | (lower) who speaks four languages | translated the menu. · 6. We visited Lisbon, Portugal ‖ Accra, Ghana ‖ and Hanoi, Vietnam.

| = short pause · ‖ = longer pause, voice not fully falling. Answers: 1 commas (or dashes / parentheses) · 2 semicolon · 3 colon · 4 dash · 5 commas around the non-restrictive clause · 6 semicolons between the items. Ask: "In 5, how many colleagues does the speaker have who could have translated it?" (The clause adds information about one colleague.)

6.3-B · One or several? (C19 lead-in and listening stage · read each once)

1a. My brother who lives in Berlin is a doctor. · 1b. My brother | who lives in Berlin | is a doctor. · 2a. The students | who passed the test | received certificates. · 2b. The students who passed the test received certificates. · 3a. The laptops that were left in the office were stolen. · 3b. The laptops | which were left in the office | were stolen.

Learners say "one / all" or "several / some": 1a several brothers · 1b one brother · 2a all the students passed · 2b only some passed · 3a only the ones in the office · 3b all the laptops (and they were in the office). Ask: "Which ones did I read with a lower voice and pauses?" (1b, 2a, 3b: the non-restrictive ones.)
