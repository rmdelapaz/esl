A · Flashcards — Appeals, Figures & Fallacies (4.1)

The three appeal cards (blue = ethos, red = pathos, green = logos) go on the wall in C10 and stay there all module. In the appeals stage, read an extract and ask learners to point at the dominant appeal. The purple figure cards are for the Form stage, the C11 warmer ("Recast this claim with the card I'm holding") and the Device Hunt. The amber fallacy cards are for Script 4.1-B and the C11 warmer: hold one up and ask for an example, or read an example and ask for the card.

C · Persuasion Lab — Topic Cards (4.1)

Each learner draws one topic card and one side card. They argue the card, not their convictions; one free swap is allowed. The "evidence" lines are invented for practice: learners may use them (hedged: one survey suggests…) or their own knowledge. Model spines are in the answer key (4.1).

Topic · 1

Every office should protect one meeting-free afternoon a week.

Evidence you might use: in one team's count, two of eleven Wednesday meetings produced a decision.

Expect this objection: urgent problems don't keep office hours.

Topic · 2

Handwriting still deserves a place in adult education.

Evidence you might use: learners in one pilot remembered more new words they had written by hand than words they had typed.

Expect this objection: adults write almost everything on screens now.

Topic · 3

Museums should be free one evening a month.

Evidence you might use: a gallery that tried it saw a third of evening visitors come for the first time.

Expect this objection: museums need the ticket income.

Topic · 4

Every neighborhood should have a tool library.

Evidence you might use: in one street survey, most drill owners had used theirs fewer than three times a year.

Expect this objection: tools will come back broken, or not at all.

Topic · 5

Recipes should give weights, not cups.

Evidence you might use: a "cup" of flour can vary by a quarter depending on how it is scooped.

Expect this objection: most home cooks don't own a kitchen scale.

Topic · 6

Commuter trains should keep one quiet carriage.

Evidence you might use: a rail operator's passenger survey rated the quiet carriage its most popular feature.

Expect this objection: the rule is impossible to enforce.

Topic · 7

Adult evening classes should run on a four-day timetable.

Evidence you might use: one college found attendance was lowest on Fridays by a wide margin.

Expect this objection: longer sessions tire learners who have worked all day.

Topic · 8

Every town should host a monthly repair café.

Evidence you might use: one repair café reports fixing about two-thirds of the items brought in.

Expect this objection: it takes business from professional repairers.

Topic · 9

Teams should rotate who chairs their meetings.

Evidence you might use: in one department, quieter members spoke twice as often in meetings they had chaired.

Expect this objection: not everyone is good at chairing.

Topic · 10

Every adult should learn a musical instrument.

Evidence you might use: adult beginners in one study reported better concentration after six months.

Expect this objection: adults have no time, and some have no ear.

D · Style & Voice Workshop — Event Cards & Voice Cards (4.2)

One event card per pair (it matches a picture card in Set A). The four facts must appear, unchanged, in all three versions. The voices change the telling, never the event.

Event · 1

The suitcase that went to the wrong city

  • A flight landed at 11:20 p.m.
  • One suitcase was sent to another city.
  • It came back three days later.
  • Inside, everything was exactly as it had been packed.
Event · 2

The umbrella that turned inside out

  • A job interview was at 9:00 a.m.
  • The wind turned the umbrella inside out at the door.
  • The interviewer had the same thing happen that morning.
  • The interview lasted forty minutes.
Event · 3

The cake that collapsed

  • An office party was held for a colleague's fiftieth birthday.
  • The three-tier cake leaned, then collapsed, during the speech.
  • Nobody was hurt.
  • Everyone ate it anyway, with spoons.
Event · 4

The bus that left early

  • The 7:42 bus arrived at 7:40.
  • It left at once, with twelve people still walking toward the stop.
  • The next bus came at 8:05.
  • Two of the twelve became friends while they waited.
Event · 5

The last loaf

  • A Saturday market, 8:30 a.m.
  • Two customers reached for the last sourdough loaf at the same moment.
  • The baker cut it in half.
  • Neither customer paid full price.
Event · 6

The smoke alarm at dinner

  • A dinner for six friends, 8:00 p.m.
  • The smoke alarm went off during the main course.
  • The cause was the bread, forgotten in the oven.
  • The guests ate dessert first.

Core voice cards (every learner writes all three)

Voice · Core 1

The Reporter

Sentences: short, declarative, past simple. Words: exact times, numbers, places; no opinion adjectives. Stance: invisible; third person or impersonal. Figures: none.

Sounds like: "The 7:42 bus departed at 7:40. Twelve passengers were left at the stop. The next service arrived at 8:05."

Voice · Core 2

The Lyric Essayist

Sentences: at least one periodic sentence; a short one or a fragment for contrast. Words: concrete, sensory, one image. Stance: first person, reflective. Ending: a quiet line that means more than it says.

Sounds like: "Two minutes before its promise, lit and indifferent, the bus slid away from us."

Voice · Core 3

The Deadpan Wit

Sentences: plain, level, formal-ish. Tools: understatement, irony at the situation (never at a person), a comparison that is really a joke. Ending: the twist in the last few words. Never explain the joke.

Sounds like: "The bus, keen as ever, left without us. We admired its punctuality for twenty-three minutes."

Genre voice cards (pastiche · stretch and early finishers)

Pastiche imitates a manner, not anyone's actual sentences. After writing in a genre voice, stretch learners write once more, keeping one habit of the genre and replacing the rest with their own.

Genre · 1

The noir detective

First person, world-weary, present-tense feel in the past; short sentences; hard-boiled similes ("The rain came down like it had a grudge").

Genre · 2

The nature-documentary narrator

Hushed present tense; humans described as a species; patient suspense ("Here, at the edge of the platform, the commuter waits").

Genre · 3

The sports commentator

Present tense, rising excitement, names and numbers, rhetorical questions ("Can she reach the loaf? She can! She has!").

Genre · 4

The nineteenth-century letter-writer

Long, courteous periodic sentences; formal address; understatement ("I must confess the afternoon was not without incident").

Genre · 5

The breathless friend on the phone

Informal, run-on, discourse markers (so, anyway, honestly, and then), rhetorical asides ("you will not believe this").

Genre · 6

The minimalist

Very short sentences and fragments; concrete nouns; no adjectives of feeling; the meaning in what is left out.

Critique Circle protocol card (1 per group of 4)

Critique Circle · about 3 minutes per writer · the writer stays silent until step 2
  1. Blind reading. Someone else reads the writer's three pieces aloud, in random order, unlabeled. The group names each voice and two features that gave it away.
  2. What stayed with me. Each critic quotes one phrase that stayed with them and says why. No suggestions yet.
  3. The writer's question. The writer asks the group one question about the draft. ("Did the Wit version land, or did I explain it?")
  4. Neutral questions. Critics ask questions with no opinion hidden inside. Yes: "Why did you choose slid?" No: "Isn't slid a bit much?"
  5. One darling, one fix, with permission. "Would you like a suggestion?" If yes: quote one possible darling and offer one specific fix. The writer says "Thank you," not a defense.

Last critic: speaks after everyone else and may not repeat a point already made.

Audience cards for the written re-pitch (150–180 words)

Audience · 1

A specialist panel

A briefing note. Formal, dense, hedged, logos-forward. No contractions or idioms; one controlled image at most.

Audience · 2

A board of trustees (a hospital, a museum, a library)

A short recommendation. Formal but readable; costs, risks and a clear decision to take.

Audience · 3

The general readers of a newspaper

An op-ed paragraph. Vivid opening, one appeal to shared values, a fair concession, a memorable last line.

Audience · 4

A volunteers' newsletter

Warm, plain, "you" and "we," concrete examples, an invitation to act.

Audience · 5

A skeptical manager

An email. Neutral register, diplomatic, evidence first, the objection met head-on, a small first step proposed.

Audience · 6

A class of fifteen-year-olds

A short talk script or blog post. Plain and lively, no condescension, a question or a story, accurate in every claim.

G · Game — Layer It! (Modules 1–4 review · C12 warmer · C13 station)

How to play (groups of 3–4, one die, one counter each): roll and move. Do the task on your square aloud, in a full sentence. The group judges with the cheat sheet (below): good = stay; not yet = go back 1; masterful (the group agrees it was better than the cheat sheet) = move on 1. Squares go in a snake: 1–6 left to right, 7–12 right to left, and so on. The module is in brackets. First to FINISH wins.

H · Listening & Dictation Scripts (teacher reads aloud)

Read at a natural speed, with conviction: at this level the delivery is part of the text. Read each script twice unless the notes say otherwise. Give speaking parts to confident learners if you like. The 🔊 buttons in the online lessons give learners extra listening at home.

4.1-A · "Lend It, Don't Buy It" (Workbook 4.1, Ex 1 · about 2½ minutes)

Rosa, a retired carpenter, at a neighborhood association meeting. Read it as a real speech: pause after the questions, stress the opposed words, slow down for the last line. The survey figures are invented for this script.

Good evening, everyone. For thirty-one years I earned my living with a saw in my hand, so when I talk about tools, I hope you'll allow that I know a little about them. · Here is my proposal: this association should open a tool library in the basement of the community center, run by volunteers and free to every household on the street. · Consider the evidence from our own survey last month. Forty-two households told us they own an electric drill. Thirty-five of them had used it fewer than three times in the past year. We buy it, we use it once, and we store it for a decade. Multiply that by the ladders, the sanders and the pressure washers sleeping in garages up and down this street. · Why does that matter? I'll tell you why. A tool that sits in a garage is not an asset; it is a cost with a handle. It costs money we didn't need to spend and space we don't have. · Picture, for a moment, Mrs. Okafor at number twelve, who wanted to fix her own fence last spring and couldn't justify buying a post-hole digger for one afternoon. She waited three months for a handyman. Three months, with a fence leaning into the street. · Now, I know the objection before you raise it. What if the tools come back broken? What if they don't come back at all? It's a fair question, and I won't pretend otherwise. I grant that a few tools will come back worse than they left. But tool libraries in other towns report that borrowers return what they borrow, because they know their neighbors are waiting. And a small repair fund costs far less than forty-two drills. Besides, everyone I've spoken to on this street already agrees with me, so the matter is really settled. · So I'm not asking you to spend more; I'm asking you to own less. Share the drill, share the ladder, share the know-how. Isn't that what a neighborhood is for? Lend it, don't buy it, and let that basement become the busiest room on the street.

Answers: see Guide 4.1 Check and Answer Key 4.1 Ex 1. After the second listening ask: "Which line did you believe the first time and doubt the second?" (usually the bandwagon line).

4.1-B · Spot the counterfeit (C10 controlled practice · read each once, pause for the name and a counter)

1. "Either we cancel the staff party or we go over budget. There's no other way." · 2. "Why should we listen to his plan for the parking lot? He can't even park straight." · 3. "If we let one team work from home on Fridays, soon nobody will come in at all, and the office will close." · 4. "Every other company in town has an open-plan office, so it must be the best design." · 5. "My cousin tried a standing desk and hurt her back, so standing desks are dangerous." · 6. First voice: "Maybe we could send fewer internal emails." Second voice: "So you want to ban email altogether? That's absurd."

Answers: 1 false dilemma (a cheaper party, a potluck, a later date) · 2 ad hominem (his parking isn't the question; the plan is) · 3 slippery slope (each step needs its own justification) · 4 bandwagon (popularity isn't proof; what does the evidence on open-plan offices show?) · 5 hasty generalization (one case isn't a pattern) · 6 straw man ("fewer," not "none": that's not what I said).

4.2-A · The fire drill in three voices (Workbook 4.2, Ex 1 · C11 lead-in)

Read A briskly and neutrally, B slowly with long breaths, C completely flat (no smile in the voice).

A. At 10:14 on Tuesday, a scheduled fire drill interrupted the quarterly budget meeting on the fourth floor. Staff left the building in six minutes and assembled in the parking lot. The meeting resumed at 10:40. No decisions had been made. The facilities manager described the evacuation as orderly. · B. Deep in the third hour of the quarterly budget, somewhere in that long gray corridor of spreadsheets through which we walk each season as if toward a promised door, we heard the bell. Then the stairs. We filed down four flights into a morning none of us had planned to see, and stood among the painted lines of the parking lot like people released early from a sentence. Twenty-six minutes later we went back up. Nothing had been decided. Something, I think, had been. · C. The quarterly budget meeting was going about as well as these meetings usually go when, at 10:14, the fire alarm offered us a way out. It was, I'm told, only a drill. Nobody mentioned this at the time. We reached the parking lot in six minutes, which is faster than we have reached any decision this year. The meeting resumed at 10:40, and I am pleased to report that it picked up exactly where it had left off: nowhere.

Answers: A exact times and numbers, impersonal, short sentences, no figures · B periodic opening sentence, metaphor ("corridor of spreadsheets") and simile ("like people released early from a sentence"), fragment ("Then the stairs."), first person, closing twist · C exact times, first person, understatement and irony, a comparison that is really a joke, closing twist ("nowhere"). Voice names: e.g. A the Reporter · B the Lyric Essayist · C the Deadpan Wit. Ask: "Which one would you trust in a safety report? Which one would you read for pleasure?"

4.2-B · Rhythm dictation (C11 drill · read each sentence 3 times)

1. The rain fell. It fell hard. It fell all night, and we did not sleep. · 2. Good prose is not written; it is rewritten, line by stubborn line. · 3. She wrote the way she walked: quickly, lightly, and never once looking back. · 4. When the lights came back on, when the music started again, when at last we could see each other's faces, nobody said a word.

After writing, learners mark the stressed words and the pauses, and name the device: 1 short sentences building by repetition, ending on a strong word (sleep) · 2 antithesis (written / rewritten) · 3 a tricolon with a simile of manner (the way she walked) · 4 a periodic sentence built with anaphora (when…, when…, when…). Then they read all four aloud in pairs.

4.3-A · A decision log for every team (Workbook 4.3, Ex 1 · about 3 minutes + Q&A)

Daniel, a hospital pharmacist, speaking to colleagues from several departments. Two learners read Priya and Tom. Pause after the opening question, deliver the understatement flat, and slow down for the last sentence. The trial figure is invented for this script.

Daniel: Raise your hand if you have ever left a meeting certain that something had been decided, and arrived at the next one to discover that nobody agrees what. … I thought so. Keep that hand in mind. · I've spent eleven years in hospital pharmacies, where a forgotten decision is not an inconvenience; it's a risk. So my proposal is modest. Every team should keep a decision log: one shared page, one line per decision. What we decided, who decided it, why, and when we'll look at it again. Not a transcript of everything that was said, but a ledger of what was settled. · Why does this matter? Because memory is a generous storyteller and a poor accountant. Six weeks after a meeting, each of us remembers the version in which we were right. Our last reorganization was, shall we say, memorable. For some of the wrong reasons. · We trialed a log on our ward for three months. By and large, the results were undramatic, which is exactly the point. The number of emails beginning "But I thought we agreed…" fell by roughly half. I counted. Frankly, it was the most satisfying spreadsheet of my career. · Admittedly, nobody went into healthcare to fill in forms, and I'd be the last person to add paperwork to a nurse's shift. That said, a line in the log takes about ninety seconds. Compare that with the hour we lose re-arguing a decision we've already made. In plain English: we stop arguing about what we argued about. · To my mind, a decision log is part and parcel of treating each other's time with respect. It won't make us wiser. But it will make us honest: about what we chose, why we chose it, and when we'll look again.
Priya: So your proposal is a one-line record of every decision. Isn't this just more bureaucracy?
Daniel: If I read your question rightly, the worry is that the log becomes the work instead of recording it. That's a fair challenge. What I'd say is: one line, ninety seconds, and if it ever takes longer than that, we've designed it badly and we should change it.
Tom: You said the log records who decided. What happens when someone's name is next to a decision that goes wrong?
Daniel: Let me answer the question beneath the question, which I think is: will this be used to blame people? It shouldn't be, and it mustn't be. The log records decisions, not culprits. If anything, it protects people: it shows that a decision was reasonable at the time, with the information we had.

Answers: see Guide 4.3 Check and Answer Key 4.3 Ex 1. Point out that Priya paraphrases before she challenges: that's the C12 rule for challengers.

4.3-B · Two rooms (C12 clinic or C13 re-pitch station · read both, then ask for five shifts)

Room 1, a university library committee: Our observational data suggest that students working on designated quiet floors sustain attention for markedly longer periods, on average some twenty minutes more per session, than those in mixed-use areas. We would therefore recommend that the current provision be extended, subject to a review in the spring. · Room 2, a students' association meeting: Here's the thing. When you sit on the quiet floor, you actually get stuff done: about twenty minutes more every time you sit down, we found. So we want more quiet floors. Sound good? We'll check in the spring that it's working.

Shifts to elicit: vocabulary (sustain attention for markedly longer periods → actually get stuff done) · hedging (our data suggest → we found) · density (one long sentence with a parenthetical figure → short sentences) · voice and address (passive, subjunctive that the provision be extended → active we want, direct "you") · rhetoric (logos-forward → a hook, Here's the thing, and a rhetorical question) · contractions. The substance (twenty minutes, extend, review in spring) is identical: that's the point.
