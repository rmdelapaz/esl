Module 6 — Writing with Authority

Three writing workshops for learners who already write correctly and now need to write well. First, the op-ed, review and feature lab: writing for a reader who owes you nothing and can leave at any line (the hook, the line, the concession, the grounded verdict, the close that lands, and a voice matched to the publication). Then the long-form and research-writing lab: architecture over 1,500+ words (the through-line, the argued outline, signposting across sections, and literature-review moves that synthesize sources instead of listing them), with a plan and one section drafted in class. Last, a clinic on the fine points: semicolons, colons and dashes, restrictive vs non-restrictive clauses, parallelism, dangling modifiers, rule vs myth, and US vs UK conventions, ending in an Editing Race.

3 lessons Lessons 6.1–6.3 Classes C17–C19 Weeks 9–10 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. In a writing module the "drill" is short and oral: learners say the working moves of the genre aloud until they sound like reflexes, because prose that cannot be read aloud comfortably is rarely good prose.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-6-writing-authority.html. Print materials/module-6/classroom-materials.html before C17. It holds the flashcards, the writer identity cards, the Commission cards and Pitch cards (A/B), the annotated model texts and the Write–Critique–Revise rubric (C17); the four Source Packs, the Supervision cards (A/B) and the outline planner (C18); the Style-Sheet cards (A/B), the Rule, Convention or Myth? sort and the Editing Race passages and scoreboard (C19); and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home, and each ends in a writing project that extends the in-class workshop.
  • Protect the workshop. Every class gives about a third of its time to one extended product: a drafted, critiqued and revised piece for a named publication (C17); an argued plan for a 1,500–2,500-word piece plus one drafted section that synthesizes sources (C18); a timed editing race in which every correction must be justified by name (C19). If you're running late, cut the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop. Writing classes die when writing is always "for homework."
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. These learners met essay structure, hedging, cohesion and summary at C1 (C1 Modules 3 and 6), and in this course they have already worked on discourse markers, long-text cohesion and synthesis (C2 3.1–3.3) and rhetoric and voice (C2 4.1–4.2). Don't re-teach topic sentences or linking words. The C2 step is authority: the reader's trust earned by a clear line, grounded judgment, a plan that carries them, and a surface so clean that nothing snags.
  • Feedback on four things. At C2, accuracy is rarely the most useful column. In every workshop take notes under precision (the exact word or mark: concedes vs claims, a semicolon where a colon was needed), appropriacy (right for this publication and reader: a broadsheet op-ed that opens "Hey guys," a research section that says "I reckon"), naturalness (grammatical but not how an expert writer would put it: "It is important to note that," "In today's fast-paced world," "the author says" six times) and effect (does it work on a reader: a hook that grabs, a verdict that persuades, a signpost that orients). Board 2–3 items per column, without names, and ask the class to improve each one.
  • Useful feedback for near-native writers. C2 learners are often near-native in one area and fossilized in another (flawless argument, but informations; elegant syntax, but a comma splice in every paragraph). Generic praise ("Very good, very fluent") wastes their time. Always (1) quote the exact words, (2) name the effect on a reader ("I stopped trusting the verdict here because it isn't anchored to a detail"), and (3) offer one alternative, not a rewrite. For fossilized errors, agree on a personal "watch list" of two or three items per learner and check only those in the homework; C2 writers improve fastest when they proofread for their own patterns.
  • Peer critique, every class. Each workshop has a structured critique protocol with its own test: the "Where did you stop?" test (C17: the reader marks the exact line where their attention dipped, then scores the rubric), the "Read the claims in order" test (C18: a partner reads only your section claims aloud; if they can be reordered without loss, you have a list, not an argument), and the "Name it" rule (C19: no correction scores unless the team can name the point at issue). Critics always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, quoting the text. Writers may only say "thank you" or ask a question until all feedback is in: no defending the draft.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes often include academics, editors and senior professionals who are used to being the expert in the room. Give them roles that need restraint: in C17 the editor in the pitch (who may ask only three questions) or the timekeeper-reader who marks where attention dipped; in C18 the supervisor with the checklist (who must not rewrite the outline, only question it); in C19 the judge for another team's claims. Say it kindly: "Your eye is excellent. I want it on someone else's draft this round." In whole-class feedback use "a new voice first."
  • Topics stay safe. Op-eds and research questions in this module stay in arts, food, technology, city life, work culture, education and science: no party politics, religion or immigration-policy debates. Every Commission card with an argument has an assigned side, so nobody has to reveal a personal opinion. Learners who prefer not to write personally can take a writer identity card (a food critic, a tech columnist, a city-life blogger…) and write in that persona's voice.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and words (organisation, criticise, sceptical, rigour, marshalled, honour, centre, flat, full stop, humour). Teach organization, criticize, skeptical, rigor, marshaled, honor, center, apartment, period, humor, and accept British forms whenever a learner uses them consistently. Lesson 6.3 turns the difference into content: learners compare US and UK conventions (quotation marks, the Oxford comma, dash spacing, collective nouns, that / which) and learn that the professional rule is pick one house style and hold it.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Earn the reader → carry the reader → never let the reader snag. In C17 learners write for someone free to leave: every sentence must earn the next, and the voice must fit the room (a broadsheet, a food blog, a trade magazine). In C18 the problem is distance: over 1,500+ words the reader can no longer see the whole, so the writer must build an architecture (a through-line, argued sections, signposts) and bring sources in without surrendering the argument to them. In C19 the scale shrinks to the single mark: a comma that rewrites a family, a dangler that makes the rain walk to work, a semicolon that balances two thoughts. Authority is all three at once: a reason to read, a structure to follow, and a surface the careful reader trusts.

Trauma-informed: persona, assigned sides, private drafts

Audience writing loves the personal anecdote, and anecdotes drift toward difficult moments. In class, never require a true personal story. Offer three routes for every anecdote: a real one, an invented one, or one from a writer identity card. Keep model texts light (lunch at your desk, a noisy food hall, night buses, a museum's free evenings). Drafts are read by one partner or a small group, never displayed to the whole class without the writer's consent; ask "May I share this line?" before boarding anything. Some learners come from places where publishing an opinion carried real risk: make it explicit that op-ed practice is a craft exercise on an assigned side, not a statement of belief. In C19, never board a learner's own error with their name, and never make a learner's accent or L1 "the example" of a punctuation habit.

Colored pens help: the critic writes in a different color.
  • For C18: one Source Pack per group of 3 (4 different packs, each with four short fictional sources); an outline planner and a section-draft frame per learner; Supervision cards A/B per pair.

6.1 · C17

Writing for an Audience — Op-eds, Reviews & Articles

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain how writing for a free reader differs from writing for a marker (opening, voice, evidence, sentence rhythm, close) and diagnose "essay register in disguise"
  • open with a hook that makes the second sentence unavoidable (a vivid particular, a provocation, a counter-intuitive claim, an anecdote) and cut throat-clearing (In today's fast-paced world… · This article will discuss…)
  • state an op-ed's line in one breath, concede the strongest objection fairly (The obvious reply is that… · None of this is to deny that…) and rebut it with one statistic and one story
  • write a review that moves from minimal description to grounded evaluation to a calibrated verdict (What makes it remarkable is… · Where it falls short is… · For all its flaws…), without spoilers and without mush
  • match headline, structure and voice to a named publication and its reader, and close on a line that lands rather than summarizes

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the contract. An academic marker is paid to read every word; a reader of an op-ed, review or feature is not. Everything else (the hook, the voice, the varied rhythm, the landing) follows from that one fact.

"Your reader owes you nothing." Can they stop reading in the middle? (Yes, at any line.) Who is the marker? (Someone paid to read the whole thing.) So what is the job of every sentence? (To earn the next one.) · "In today's fast-paced modern world, technology is changing fast." Is it wrong? (No, it's grammatical.) Does it make the second sentence unavoidable? (No: it's throat-clearing.)

Meaning: the line and the concession. An op-ed argues one thing, sayable in a breath. Conceding the strongest objection early is not weakness; it earns a skeptical reader's trust.

"The obvious reply is that busy people are simply busy. That's true, and it's exactly the problem." Is the writer agreeing with the objection? (Partly: they grant it, then turn it.) Why grant it? (So a skeptic feels understood.) Could you persuade someone you seem not to have understood? (No.) · Is an op-ed a balanced essay? (No: it's a case made with conviction, fairly.)

Meaning: the calibrated verdict. Unrelieved praise reads as a press release; unrelieved scorn reads as a grudge. Authority comes from a grounded strength in a pan and an honest reservation in a rave.

"For all its flaws, it is a far better film than most that are flawless." Did the reviewer like the film? (Yes, mostly.) Does the film have problems? (Yes.) Which is more credible: this or "It's perfect, go now"? (This: the praise is believable because the criticism is there.) · "The food was amazing and the vibe was great." What's missing? (Grounding: which dish, what detail?)

Form: the genre toolkit.

Pronunciation: prose lives in the ear. Teach the read-aloud test as a revision tool. Concede-then-turn sentences depend on contrastive stress and a fall-rise on the concession: "It's TEMPTing to think the problem is NEW ↘↗ | but the EVidence points the OTHer way ↘." The praise move puts the nucleus on the evaluative word: "What makes the book reMARKable | is not its ANswers | but the SHARPness of its QUEStions." Short "punch" sentences after long ones need a real pause before them. Watch op-ed (OP-ed, two syllables, not "oped") and verdict (VERdict).

Drill script
  1. Concede, then turn (choral, then individual; exaggerate the fall-rise): T: "remote work / lonely" → S: "It's tempting to think remote work is simply freedom, but for many people it's lonely." T: "food hall / loud" → "None of this is to deny that the food hall is loud; it's to insist that loud isn't bad." T: "phones at concerts" → "The obvious reply is that people paid for the ticket, and they can film what they like. And yet…"
  2. Praise move: T: "the novel / restraint" → S: "What makes the novel remarkable is its restraint." T: "the café / its bread" → "What makes the café remarkable is its bread." T: "the app / what it leaves out" → "What makes the app remarkable is what it leaves out."
  3. Measured criticism: T: "final act / ambition" → S: "The final act falls short of its ambition." T: "the second half / its promise" → "The second half never quite earns its promise."
  4. Calibrated verdict: T: "flawed but good film" → S: "For all its flaws, it's a far better film than most that are flawless." T: "overpriced but one brilliant dish" → "Worth the price of admission for the flatbread alone."
  5. Hook it (individual, fast): T reads a flat opening ("Libraries are important for communities.") → S offers a hook of a named type ("vivid particular, please"). Accept anything concrete; praise the ones that make you want the second sentence.
Write–Critique–Revise Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 3; each member holds a different Commission card (outlet, reader, genre, topic, assigned side where relevant, 250–350 words). Topics: a new downtown food hall (review, lifestyle magazine), phone-free concerts (op-ed, arts section), night buses (feature, city magazine), a smart kitchen gadget (product review, tech blog), free Thursday evenings at the art museum (op-ed, local paper), the four-day week at a design studio (feature, HR trade magazine), a neighborhood bakery's sourdough (review, food blog), reply-all culture (op-ed, workplace column). Anyone may write as a writer identity card persona.
  • Pitch (4 min): in pairs with Pitch cards A/B. A (writer) pitches in three sentences: the hook, the line or verdict, and why this outlet's reader will care. B (editor) has the outlet's reader profile on the card and may ask only three questions (give this role to a dominant speaker). Then swap. The third member of the group joins a pair as a second editor.
  • Draft (12 min): silent writing, 200–250 words: the hook, the line or verdict, the body (op-ed: concession + rebuttal with one statistic and one story; review: minimal description → grounded evaluation → verdict; feature: payoff early, one subheading). No dictionaries for the first 8 minutes: write first, polish later. The close can be a placeholder.
  • Critique (8 min): pass the draft to the left. The reader (1) reads once at normal speed and puts a small mark at the exact line where attention dipped, the "Where did you stop?" test; (2) scores the six rubric criteria 1–4; (3) writes one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text, in a different color. Writers listen and may only say thank you or ask a question.
  • Revise (6 min): writers rewrite three things only: the hook, the close, and the paragraph where the reader stopped. Two volunteers read "before" and "after" hooks aloud (with permission).
  • Keep it honest: a score of 2 is useful information, not a failure. At C2, the most valuable critique is usually about effect ("I stopped here because the example is general") rather than grammar.
ICQs before the Lab
In the pitch, how many sentences? (Three.) How many questions can the editor ask? (Three.) · Can you write your own opinion on an op-ed card? (Write the assigned side; it's a craft exercise.) Must the anecdote be true? (No: real, invented or from an identity card.) · In the critique, where do you put the mark? (Where my attention dipped.) Can the writer argue with the critic? (No: only thank you or a question.) · What do you revise? (The hook, the close, and the paragraph where the reader stopped.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 speakers are far stronger orally than on the page (often learners who acquired English through work and immersion rather than schooling). Let them draft aloud first: they tell the piece to a partner for two minutes, the partner notes the best sentence, and that sentence becomes the hook. Give them a move frame strip (Hook: … · The line: … · The obvious reply is that… · And yet… · For all its flaws… · Close: echo the hook) and reduce the target to 150 words. In the critique they make excellent "Where did you stop?" readers: honest attention is the whole test. Fewer items: Ex 3 items 1–2, Ex 4 items 1–3.

Stretch

Voice under constraint. Ask strong writers to produce the same opening for two outlets on the same Commission card (the broadsheet and the lifestyle blog) and to annotate every choice that changed (person, sentence length, metaphor, humor, hedging). Then set the harder genre problem: a mixed review whose verdict is genuinely balanced but still actionable ("Go for the bread; skip the mains"), or an op-ed whose close echoes the hook with a twist. For editors and academics: write the standfirst (one-line summary under the headline) and cut the draft by 20% without losing a single move.

L1 watch-outs
  • The exam-essay opening: learners trained for IELTS, TOEFL or national exams often open with a memorized frame ("Nowadays, with the development of technology…," "It is a well-known fact that…"). It is a genre transfer, not an error: praise the discipline, then show that a free reader leaves at that sentence.
  • Delayed thesis: many Chinese, Japanese and Korean writing traditions build toward the main point (and ki-shō-ten-ketsu structures use an unexpected turn before the conclusion). English op-eds and features front-load. Frame it as a reader-responsibility difference: in English audience writing, the writer does the work of making the point findable.
  • Ornate openings and long periodic sentences: formal Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic and Persian prose often values elaborate, rhythmic introductions. At C2 this can be a real asset for voice; the fix is proportion and one short sentence after a long one.
  • Reluctance to use "I": learners schooled in French, German, Russian or many Asian academic traditions may feel the first person is unprofessional. In audience writing it is expected, used with judgment. Conversely, learners from some US-influenced blogging cultures may overuse it.
  • Evaluation adjectives: interesting, nice, amazing, great, very good carry no grounding. Push for the anchored detail and precise evaluative language (restrained, overworked, generous, uneven, assured).
  • False friends in reviews: Spanish actual / French actuel ("the actual menu" = current), sensible ("a sensible performance" = sensitive), German sympathisch ("a sympathetic chef" = likable), eventual (= possible). Circle and ask "Is that what you mean in English?"

6.2 · C18

The Long Form — Extended & Research Writing

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain why length changes writing in kind (the reader can't see the whole; the writer can't hold it) and plan before drafting
  • write a through-line that makes a contestable claim, and build an argued outline of 5–7 sections whose one-line claims, read in order, form a necessary sequence, with informative headings
  • orient a reader across sections with backward, forward and locational signposts (Having established that… · The following section turns to… · The remainder of this report is organized as follows)
  • integrate sources by paraphrasing by default and quoting by exception, avoid patchwriting, and choose attribution verbs that encode stance (demonstrates · claims · concedes · suggests · draws on)
  • use literature-review moves to synthesize rather than list sources (consensus, divergence, building on, identifying a gap, positioning your own contribution), and hold one term per concept across the piece
Materials
  • Workbook 6.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Architecture Lab frame, self-check)
  • Slides 8–13 · Attribution-verb flashcards (color-coded by stance) · Signposting cards · Source Packs A–D (1 per group of 3) · outline planners · section-draft frames · Supervision cards A/B · Writer identity cards
  • Listening scripts 6.2-A (the supervision meeting) and 6.2-B (dictation)

Left: architecture. Through-line (a claim someone could disagree with) → 5–7 section claims, not topics → headings a skimmer can read the argument from. Test: read the claims in order. Could you reorder them without loss? → a list ("and also"), not an argument. Repair: the reverse outline (one sentence per paragraph: what does it actually do?).

Center: signposts at every seam. Backward: Having established that… · As noted in Section 2… Forward: This section argues that… · The following section turns to… Locational: The remainder of this report is organized as follows… · At this point it is worth stepping back… One term per concept (no drift).

Right: sources serve the argument. Paraphrase (default) · quote (the wording is the point) · patchwriting (swapped synonyms = plagiarism). Stance verbs: demonstrates (accept) · argues (a position) · claims (distance) · concedes (against interest) · suggests (tentative) · draws on (extends). Lit-review moves: There is broad agreement that… · Whereas A…, B… · Building on… · Little attention has been paid to… · This study therefore… Shopping list ✘ → synthesis ✔.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the argued outline. An outline is not a list of topics; it is the argument in miniature. Each section states what it claims. If the claims, read in order, tell a coherent story that could not be reordered, the piece will hold.

"1. History of cars in cities. 2. Traffic. 3. Pollution. 4. Businesses." Is that an argument? (No, a list of topics.) Could you swap sections 2 and 3? (Yes, nothing breaks.) What does that tell you? (There's no through-line.) · "The business objection, granted: retailers fear lost customers." Is that a topic or a claim? (A claim: it says something someone could check or dispute.)

Meaning: signposting. The writer can hold the whole piece in mind; the reader can't. Signposts tell the reader where they have been, where they are going, and where they are in the whole.

"Having established that remote work raises individual output, we now turn to the hidden cost." Which way does "Having established" look? (Backward.) And "we now turn to"? (Forward.) Why both at the start of a section? (To connect what's settled to what's next.) · Does a 600-word op-ed need "The remainder of this report is organized as follows"? (No: too heavy for the length.)

Meaning: attribution verbs and synthesis. The verb that introduces a source is never neutral: it tells the reader how far you accept the claim. A literature review is not a list of summaries; it shows how the sources relate and where the gap is.

"Okafor claims the app doubled sales." Does the writer fully accept that? (No: claims keeps a skeptical distance.) "Okafor demonstrates…"? (The writer accepts it as shown.) "Even Okafor concedes the sample was small." Is that against Okafor's interest? (Yes: an admission.) · "Smith found X. Lee found Y. Park found Z." Do we know how the three studies relate? (No: it's a shopping list.) "Whereas Smith and Lee report gains, Park's larger study finds none." Now? (Yes: they diverge, and we know why it matters.)
Paraphrase or patchwriting? Original: "Background music with lyrics significantly impaired performance on reading tasks." Version: "Background songs with words considerably damaged performance in reading activities." (Patchwriting: same structure, swapped synonyms; it counts as plagiarism even with a citation.) What's a real paraphrase? (Lyrics seem to get in the way of reading, Rossi (2021) found, though instrumental music did not.)

Pronunciation. Attribution verbs are often read but rarely heard, so learners guess the stress. Drill: conTEND, mainTAIN, deMONstrate, esTABlish, asSERT, alLEGE, acKNOWLedge, SPECulate, proPOSE. Research is accepted with either stress in US English (REsearch / reSEARCH); don't correct it. When presenting an outline aloud, pause at each seam and use a rising "list" tone on section claims until the last, which falls.

Architecture Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one Source Pack per group; each pack holds four short fictional sources (author, year, type, key finding, one quotable line, a limitation). Say clearly that they are invented for practice and must not be cited in real work. Learners may also plan a piece from their own field instead (academics and professionals often prefer this); they still use the pack for the drafted section, or bring two real sources they know well.
  • Plan (9 min): on the outline planner: the through-line in one sentence (a claim someone could disagree with), then 5–7 sections, each with an informative heading and a one-line claim, and a word budget per section adding up to 1,500–2,500 words. Mark where each source enters and the attribution verb it will get.
  • Supervision (4 min): pairs with Supervision cards. The supervisor reads the writer's section claims aloud in order, without the headings, then asks the card's four questions (Is the through-line contestable? Could any two sections swap? Where is the strongest objection? Which term will you hold constant?). The supervisor may not rewrite; only question. Swap roles. Give the supervisor role to a dominant speaker first.
  • Draft (10 min): each learner drafts one section of 250–300 words (the literature-review section is recommended). Frame checklist: ☐ opens with a backward and a forward signpost ☐ synthesizes at least two sources (consensus, divergence, or gap) ☐ at least three different attribution verbs, each chosen for stance ☐ at least one paraphrase and at most one short quotation ☐ one key term held constant ☐ ends by pointing forward.
  • Reverse-outline check (3 min): swap drafts. The partner writes one sentence per paragraph describing what it does ("summarizes Garcia; contrasts Chen; names the gap") and ticks the checklist. A paragraph whose sentence is "summarizes a source" and nothing more = a shopping-list flag.
  • The rest of the piece is not the homework; the online project is the outline and an abstract. Learners who want to write the full piece can bring it to C25 (the review project) or the 8.3 masterwork.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Long-form planning is a visual task: let these learners plan on sticky notes, one claim per note, and physically reorder them on the desk; the "read the claims in order" test becomes a real move they can see. Give them a move strip of five literature-review frames to copy and complete (There is broad agreement that… · Whereas…, … · Building on… · Little attention has been paid to… · This report therefore…). Reduce the plan to 4 sections and the draft to one 120-word synthesis paragraph using two sources. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 4 items 1–2.

Stretch

The whole piece in miniature. Ask strong writers to write the abstract (150–200 words: context → problem → method → findings → meaning) for their planned piece before leaving class, then check it against the outline: any finding in the abstract that no section delivers is a structural hole. Researchers can add a positioning paragraph that states the gap and the contribution in two sentences, and test terminological drift in their own real draft (circle every name for the key concept). Offer the harder synthesis: three sources that agree on the finding but disagree on the cause.

L1 watch-outs
  • Source use and "respectful copying": in some educational traditions (for example, parts of China, the Gulf, South and Southeast Asia, and Eastern Europe), reproducing an authority's exact words is a sign of respect and learning. Treat patchwriting as a convention to learn, not a moral failing, but be completely clear that in English-medium academic and professional writing it counts as plagiarism.
  • Reader-responsible prose: Japanese, Korean and Chinese academic writing often expects the reader to infer connections; learners from these traditions may drop signposts because stating them feels condescending. The supervisor dialogue ("I knew where I was, so I assumed the reader did") is for them.
  • Digressive architecture: German, Russian and some Spanish-language academic traditions tolerate (and sometimes admire) digressions and long theoretical preambles. The "read the claims in order" test shows where a digression breaks the sequence.
  • "Says" and "according to" as the only reporting tools, and the double attribution "According to Smith, he says…" (common from Spanish según, Arabic wafqan li, Chinese 根据…说). Push for stance verbs.
  • Tense of reporting: learners from languages without the present perfect (Chinese, Russian, Arabic) or with different reporting norms may write "Many studies showed" for a body of research. Teach the three-way choice on the board plan.
  • Elegant variation: French and Spanish school writing often penalizes repetition, so learners rename the key concept every paragraph (knowledge-sharing → informal learning → tacit exchange). In long-form English, a load-bearing term stays constant.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: restrictive vs non-restrictive. A restrictive clause identifies which one; a non-restrictive clause adds information about something already identified. The commas, not just the word, carry the difference.

"The report that I sent yesterday is final." How many reports are there? (More than one.) Which one is final? (The one I sent yesterday.) · "The report, which I sent yesterday, is final." How many reports? (One.) Is "I sent it yesterday" needed to know which report? (No: it's extra.) · "The employees, who worked overtime, got a bonus." Did everyone get a bonus? (Yes, all of them; and all of them worked overtime.) Without commas? (Only the ones who worked overtime.)

Meaning: the marks.

"The rain stopped; the match resumed." Two sentences or one? (Two independent clauses, held together.) Could we use a comma? (No: that's a comma splice.) A period? (Yes, but the link is weaker.) · "She had one goal: to win." Does the colon promise something? (Yes: the goal.) "Her goals are: to win." (No: the clause before the colon isn't complete.) · "The proposal (after much debate) was approved." / "The proposal — after much, much debate — was approved." Which aside is louder? (The dashes.) Which almost whispers? (The parentheses.)
"Walking to the office, the rain soaked me." Who was walking? (Me, but the sentence says the rain!) Fix? (Walking to the office, I was soaked by the rain.) · "We almost drove 500 miles." Did we drive? (As written, no: we almost did.) What did the writer mean? (We drove almost 500 miles.) · "To boldly go." Rule, convention or myth? (Myth: splitting an infinitive is fine.)

Pronunciation: punctuation is prosody. A non-restrictive clause is read on a lower pitch with a pause either side ("My brother | who lives in Berlin | is a doctor"); a restrictive clause runs straight through with no pause. A colon gets a slight rise and a held pause (suspense); a dash a sharper, shorter break; a semicolon a pause longer than a comma but with the voice not fully falling. Reading aloud is the best splice detector: at a comma splice the voice falls to a full stop and the ear hears the error. Model, don't lecture: learners copy you.

Editing Race — how to run it
  • Set up before class: four stations around the room (tables or wall spots), each with one passage (set G): Station 1 a café review (8 faults), Station 2 a research paragraph (8 faults), Station 3 a feature on night buses (8 faults), Station 4 an office memo (8 faults). Each passage also contains two traps: a correct "myth" sentence (a split infinitive, a final preposition, a sentence starting with And) that must not be changed.
  • Teams of 3, one race sheet per team, one pen. Roles rotate at each station: editor (marks the passage), namer (writes the name of each point: "comma splice," "dangling modifier," "non-restrictive commas"), judge (decides whether a change is a real fault or a myth). Give the judge role to a dominant speaker: they must listen to both teammates before ruling.
  • Timing: 4 minutes per station, 1 minute to move (use a visible timer). Teams start at different stations. After four rounds, 2 minutes for scoring: read the answer strips aloud; teams self-score on the race sheet and you board the totals.
  • Scoring: +1 each fault correctly fixed · +1 each correct name · −1 each false alarm (a myth "corrected," or a correct sentence changed) · +2 per station for one style improvement the team can justify (e.g. joining two related short sentences with a semicolon; a colon before a payoff). Ties are broken by the fewest false alarms.
  • Why the "name it" rule: at C2 the goal is not to fix by instinct but to be able to explain a change to a colleague, a client or a student. A fix without a name scores half.
  • If time is short: run three stations (3 × 5 min) and do Station 4 as homework. Early finishers: rewrite the station passage's weakest paragraph for rhythm (a long sentence, then a short one that lands).

Give these learners the quick-test card (he/him · drop the other person · can you count it? · identify or add? · whose action? · read it aloud) and let them work by ear: in the race, the namer role suits a strong reader, while the learner who hears well reads the passage aloud and says "stop" at every snag. Pre-mark the number of faults per paragraph on their copy. Reduce the controlled practice to Ex 2 items 1–3 and Ex 4 items 1–3; target: hear and fix the comma splice and the dangler.

Stretch

Punctuation as music. Give strong writers a flat paragraph of eight even sentences (the "reads flat" paragraph from the online lesson's editor dialogue) and ask them to rewrite it for rhythm using exactly one semicolon, one colon, one dash and one short sentence, then justify each mark in a margin note. For editors and translators: compare the house styles of two real publications (a US magazine and a UK newspaper) and write a six-line style sheet. For the hardest usage cases, discuss none is / none are, singular they (accepted in US and UK style guides), and data is / data are by register.

L1 watch-outs
  • Comma splices by transfer: Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Russian, Vietnamese and Chinese all allow (or tolerate) joining independent clauses with a comma. This is the most frequent fossilized error at C2. Put it on each learner's personal watch list.
  • German and Russian commas: German requires a comma before dass and every relative clause, Russian before что and который, so learners write "I think, that…" and "The report, that I sent yesterday, is final" (a restrictive clause with non-restrictive commas). Contrast with English, where commas around a relative clause change the meaning.
  • French spacing: French puts a space before : ; ? ! ("Attention : …"). Learners who write French professionally carry it over. Also French and Russian guillemets « » and German „…“ quotation marks.
  • Dashes for dialogue: Spanish, Russian and French fiction use a dash to open dialogue. In English quoted speech uses quotation marks.
  • Hypercorrection is native too: "between you and I" and "whom shall I say is calling?" are errors of educated native speakers. Tell learners so; it is a relief for them to know that some "native" usage they have copied is itself the error.
  • Arabic punctuation: Arabic uses the comma (،) and wa ("and") far more freely between clauses, so long comma-linked chains are a natural transfer. Also watch the inverted comma form and question mark (؟) in typed work.

Closing the Module

End of C19: Module 6 wrap-up

Recap on slide 19, "earn, carry, polish": a hook that makes the second sentence unavoidable, a line in one breath, a grounded verdict (earn the reader) → a through-line, claims in a necessary order, signposts at every seam, sources synthesized with stance verbs (carry the reader) → the comma that carries meaning, the dangler repaired, the myth left alone, one house style held (never let the reader snag). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook and write their personal watch list (two or three fine points they will proofread for in every piece from now on) on the inside cover. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you; revisit it in the C20 warmer and in C25.

Watch for in Module 7: 7.1 (cross-cultural pragmatics) returns to audience from a new angle: the same op-ed hook or joke lands differently across cultures, so the "picture the reader" habit from C17 transfers directly.
