Writing with Authority

Three workshops on the difference between prose that is correct and prose that is trusted. Write for a reader who owes you nothing and can leave at any line; build a long piece whose architecture carries that reader across thousands of words; and polish the surface until nothing snags a careful eye.

How this module works

Each task is labeled Read, Listen, Write, Say or Pair work. Nearly every writing task has many good answers; judge yours by its effect on a reader, not by whether it matches a model.

You decide what to share. Any anecdote may be real, invented, or borrowed from a writer identity card, and every argument task comes with an assigned side: you are practicing a craft, not declaring a belief. Drafts are read by one or two classmates only, and nothing you write is shown to the class without your permission.

the line = the single thing an op-ed argues, sayable in one breath · throat-clearing = an opening that announces a subject instead of engaging the reader · to calibrate = to adjust a judgment precisely, neither inflated nor deflated · a standfirst = the one-sentence summary printed under a headline

Grammar box — The genre toolkit

The contract has changed. An examiner is paid to read every word; your reader is free to leave at any line. Every sentence must earn the next.

Op-ed archook → the line → the strongest objection, granted → rebuttal (one statistic, one story) → a close that lands, often echoing the hook
Review arcdescription (minimal, no spoilers) → evaluation (every judgment anchored to a detail) → a calibrated verdict the reader can act on
Feature arca specific headline and standfirst → the payoff early → one idea per paragraph → subheadings → written to one reader
Concede → turnIt's tempting to think…, but · The obvious reply is that… · None of this is to deny that…; it is to insist that…
ReframeThe real question is not X but Y. · Here is what we too often forget: … · If that sounds alarmist, consider…
EvaluateWhat makes X remarkable is… · Where it falls short is… · It never quite earns its… · The result is more X than Y.
VerdictFor all its flaws, … · worth the trip for … alone · a must-read · where lesser work would…, this…

Delete on sight: In today's fast-paced world… · This article will discuss… · It is important to note that… · In conclusion, …

Read — Exercise 2: Anatomy of an op-ed

Read the opinion piece. In the margin, label the seven moves: hook · line · concession · rebuttal · evidence · anecdote · close.

Give Us Back the Lunch Hour

(1) At 12:47 last Tuesday I ate a cheese sandwich over my keyboard while answering an email about a meeting about lunch. (2) I suspect you have done something similar this week, and I want to suggest that it is making us worse at our jobs. (3) The desk lunch is not a sign of dedication; it is a false economy, and it is time offices treated the lunch hour as part of the working day rather than a gap in it.

(4) The obvious reply is that busy people are simply busy: the work has to be done, and an hour away from it is an hour lost. (5) That is true, and it is exactly the problem. (6) In one office survey, more than half of employees said they rarely took a full break, yet the same employees rated the afternoon as their least productive time of day. (7) A colleague of mine, a data analyst, started walking to the river every day at one o'clock; within a month she was solving in ten minutes the problems that used to take her the whole afternoon. (8) Rest, it turns out, is not the opposite of work. It is part of how work gets done.

(9) So take the hour. Leave the building. And next Tuesday at 12:47, let the email about lunch wait until after lunch.

Model text written for this course; the survey is invented for practice.

Read and say — Dialogue: the voice is wrong

Editor: I like the argument, but the voice is all wrong for us. Who did you picture reading this?

Writer: Honestly? My old professor. I think I was still writing an essay.

Editor: That's the tell. Our reader is on a train, half-distracted, deciding by the second paragraph whether to bother.

Writer: So I cut "It is important to note that" and open on the bench by the river?

Editor: Exactly. And your verdict is hiding in paragraph four. Bring it up.

Writer: Less "one might argue," more "here's what nobody says out loud"?

Editor: Same rigor, human temperature. And keep the joke about the pigeons; it's the only line I read twice.

a through-line = the single controlling claim every section serves · a seam = the join between two sections · patchwriting = "paraphrase" that keeps the source's structure and swaps a few words: a form of plagiarism · terminological drift = one concept acquiring several names across a long text

Grammar box — Architecture and sources

An outline is an argument in miniature. Give every section a one-line claim, not a topic. Then read the claims in order: if you could reorder them without loss, you have a list, not an argument.

Backward signpostAs noted in the previous section, … · Having established that…, we now turn to…
Forward signpostThis section argues that… · The following section turns to… · This point is developed further in Section 4.
Locational signpostThe remainder of this report is organized as follows: … · At this point it is worth stepping back…

The attribution verb carries your stance.

you accept itdemonstrates · shows · establishes
a defended positionargues · contends · maintains
you keep your distanceclaims · asserts · alleges
an admissionconcedes · acknowledges · grants
tentativesuggests · proposes · speculates
extends earlier workdraws on · builds on
neutral factnotes · observes · points out

Synthesize; don't list. A literature review shows how sources relate and where the gap lies.

ConsensusThere is broad agreement that… · A growing body of work suggests… · Most studies concur that…
DivergenceWhereas A reports…, B finds… · Studies diverge, however, on…
Building onBuilding on A's framework, B… · B extends this finding to…
The gapLittle attention has been paid to… · What remains unclear is whether…
PositioningThis report therefore asks… · The present study addresses…

Tense. A source's argument: present (Rossi argues). A specific study's result: past (Rossi's 2021 study found). A body of research: present perfect (several studies have shown).

Read and say — Dialogue: page nine

Supervisor: Your chapter is well written line by line, but I got lost around page nine. Where's the reader supposed to be by then?

Student: I think I stopped signposting. I knew where I was, so I assumed the reader did.

Supervisor: That's the classic long-form trap. You can hold the whole in your head; your reader can't.

Student: So a "Having established X, this section turns to Y" at each seam?

Supervisor: Exactly. And your review of the literature reads like a queue: Smith found, Lee found, Park found. Tell me where they agree, where they part company, and what nobody has asked yet.

Student: And the gap is where my chapter comes in.

Supervisor: Now you're writing an argument, not a bibliography.

Pair work: read it twice. Which two problems does the supervisor name? Which signpost does the student offer?

a comma splice = two independent clauses joined by a comma alone · a dangling modifier = an opening phrase with no proper subject to attach to · hypercorrection = an error made by trying too hard to be correct ("between you and I") · house style = a publication's chosen set of conventions

Grammar box — Marks, tests and conventions
Semicolon ;joins two closely linked independent clauses · separates list items that contain commasThe café was full; the bakery next door was empty.
Colon :after a complete clause, delivers what it promisedThe menu has one weakness: dessert.
Em dash —an emphatic aside or a sudden turn; use sparinglyThey had planned for everything — and then the power failed.
Three volumescommas (neutral) · dashes (loud) · parentheses (quiet)The proposal (after much debate) was approved.
Restrictiveidentifies which one: that / who, no commasThe report that I sent yesterday is final.
Non-restrictiveadds information: , which / , who, with commasThe report, which I sent yesterday, is final.
who / whomanswer with he or him; him → whom (always after a preposition in formal writing)
you and medrop the other person: between me and the wall → between you and me
fewer / lesscan you count it? fewer errors · less traffic (but less than five miles)
danglerswhose action is it? The opening phrase must fit the subject.
only · almost · evenplace right before what they limit: We sell only organic eggs.
parallelismsame form in a series and after not only… but also, either… or
subjunctiveIf I were you · I insist that he be present

Rule, convention or myth? Rules protect meaning (danglers, restrictive commas, the comma splice). Conventions signal register (whom, the subjunctive were). Myths may be ignored: you may split an infinitive, end a sentence with a preposition, and begin one with And or But.

AmericanBritish
Quotation marks"double," with commas and periods inside'single' is common; punctuation inside only if it belongs to the quotation
Dashem dash, closed in book style (word—word), often spaced in journalismspaced en dash (word – word)
Oxford commastandard in most style guidesoptional; many newspapers omit it
Collective nounsThe team is winning.The team is / are winning.
TitlesMr. · Dr.Mr · Dr

Whichever you choose, choose once: consistency is the professional rule.

Read and say — Dialogue: it reads flat

Editor: The argument's fine, but it reads flat. Every sentence is the same length.

Writer: They're all grammatically correct, though.

Editor: Correct isn't the same as alive. Join those two short ones with a semicolon; they're one thought. Then let a short sentence land after your long one.

Writer: And the dash in paragraph two?

Editor: Keep it. It's the only one on the page, which is why it works. The five in paragraph four, though…

Writer: So the marks aren't just about avoiding errors; they're how the sentence moves.

Editor: Now you're hearing it. The rules keep you correct; the rhythm keeps you read.
