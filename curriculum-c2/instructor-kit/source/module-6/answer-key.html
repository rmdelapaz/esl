Module 6 — Writing with Authority: Answers

Answers to every Module 6 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (hooks, calibrated verdicts, the synthesized paragraph, the lab products), the Editing Race answers, and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect, not accuracy alone. Most items in 6.1 and 6.2 are rewrites with many good answers: the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps the facts and the assigned position, (2) is accurate at the level of the exact word and mark, (3) fits the named outlet and reader, and (4) works on a reader: the hook makes you want the next sentence, the verdict is grounded, the synthesis shows how sources relate. When a version is correct but flat, say so and name the effect ("accurate; it announces rather than arrests"). In 6.3, a correction scores fully only when the learner can name the point. American English is the model; accept British spelling and punctuation (organise, criticise, sceptical; single quotation marks; spaced en dashes) when used consistently. Mixed styles within one text are the error, not either style.

6.1 · Writing for an Audience (C17)

Exercise 1 — The editor's call (Script 6.1-A)

Published? Yes, next week, if the writer revises by Thursday.

  1. Cut the step-by-step account of the evening (the recap) to two sentences of description.
  2. Anchor "amazing" to a specific dish: say what makes the flatbread good.
  3. Give a clear verdict the reader can act on (go or don't; what to order).
  4. Add one honest reservation (the noise / the forty-minute wait) so the praise is credible.
  5. Replace the opening "In this review I will describe…" with a hook (the editor suggests the chef tearing the bread at the table).

Accept any wording that captures each change. Common mishearing: learners note "make it shorter" for 1; push for what is shortened (the recap, not the evaluation).

Exercise 2 — Anatomy of an op-ed
Hook(1) the 12:47 cheese sandwich over the keyboard: a vivid particular with a comic twist (the meeting about lunch)
Line(3) the desk lunch is a false economy; offices should treat the lunch hour as part of the working day. (2) prepares it and recruits the reader ("I suspect you have done something similar")
Concession(4) The obvious reply is that busy people are simply busy…
Rebuttal(5) That is true, and it is exactly the problem. (8) Rest… is part of how work gets done.
Evidence(6) the office survey (statistic)
Anecdote(7) the data analyst who walks to the river
Close(9) So take the hour… let the email about lunch wait until after lunch.

The line in one sentence (model): Eating lunch at your desk makes you less productive, so offices should protect the lunch hour as part of the working day. The echo: the close returns to the exact time (12:47), the day (Tuesday) and the "email about lunch," turning the opening's comic image into an instruction: the loop closes. Accept "rebuttal" for (8) or label (8) as a second close-like line; both readings are defensible.

Exercise 3 — Hook clinic (models; many good versions)
  1. (vivid particular) "At the new downtown food hall, the loudest sound at noon is not the crowd; it is a woman at the dumpling counter, pleating dough faster than anyone can order it."
  2. (counter-intuitive claim) "The best thing my smartwatch ever did for my health was run out of battery."
  3. (anecdote) "The first time I went to the art museum on a free Thursday, a security guard told me which painting to look at last, and he was right."
  4. (provocation) "The five-day week is not a law of nature; it is a habit, and most habits deserve an audit."

Check: the hook is concrete or surprising, does not announce the subject ("This review will…"), and makes a promise the next sentence can keep. Not yet: rhetorical-question openings with obvious answers ("Have you ever wondered about smartwatches?"), dictionary definitions, "Nowadays…". Clickbait flag: a hook the piece could not honestly deliver.

Exercise 4 — Calibrate the verdict
1press release → "What makes the album remarkable is its restraint: eleven songs, not one wasted. Only the closing track overstays its welcome."
2ungrounded → "The flatbread arrives blistered and still steaming, and the room hums with the kind of noise that makes you want to stay."
3spoiler → "The last chapter asks you to reread everything before it, and it is worth the effort; I won't say why."
4mush → "Buy it for the camera; if you mostly text and browse, last year's model does everything this one does for half the price."
5grudge → "The exhibition has one extraordinary room, the textiles, and eleven that feel assembled from a storage inventory."

Accept any rewrite that anchors the judgment to a detail and, where relevant, adds a calibrating concession. Learners may label 1 "press release" or "ungrounded" (both true); accept either with the reason.

Dialogue — the voice is wrong

The three instructions: open on the bench by the river and cut "It is important to note that" (voice / effect: the hook), bring the verdict up from paragraph four (structure: front-loading), keep the joke about the pigeons (voice / effect: it's the line the editor read twice). Accept other categorizations with reasons; the point is that the editor never mentions grammar.

Write–Critique–Revise Lab — what a 4 looks like

Model opening (Commission 1: review of a downtown food hall, lifestyle magazine): "By 12:15 on a Friday, the line at Harbor Hall's noodle counter reaches the door, and nobody in it looks unhappy. That tells you most of what you need to know about the city's newest food hall: it is loud, crowded and, at its best, genuinely thrilling. What makes it remarkable is not the range of stalls but their confidence; the hand-pulled noodles are thick, glossy and fiercely peppered, and the dumpling stall next door pleats its dough in front of you. Where it falls short is the seating: forty stools for four hundred people. Go early, go hungry, and eat standing up if you must."

Rubric reading: Hook 4 (a vivid particular with a promise) · Verdict 4 (clear, early, actionable) · Grounding 4 (noodles, dumplings, the stools) · Voice 4 (warm, second person for a lifestyle reader) · Close 4 (an instruction that lands) · Precision 4. Typical 2s: a hook that is a topic sentence; a verdict hidden in the last paragraph; "amazing, great, nice"; a serious newspaper piece that says "guys."

Self-check 6.1
Q1
What is the single defining job of an op-ed's opening hook?
A) To state the topic and thesis plainly up front
B) To make the second sentence unavoidable
C) To summarize everything the piece will cover
Why: the reader is free to leave, so the first sentence must earn the second. A describes an academic introduction; C is throat-clearing.
Q2
Which move most signals a trustworthy reviewer?
A) Anchoring each verdict to a specific detail, and noting a flaw even in a rave
B) Praising the work warmly from start to finish with no reservations
C) Retelling the plot thoroughly and in order
Why: grounded, calibrated judgment makes praise and criticism credible. B reads as a press release; C is a recap full of spoilers, with no evaluation.
Q3 · new
Which closing verdict for a restaurant review is both calibrated and actionable?
A) "Overall, Ember has some strengths and some weaknesses, so readers should decide for themselves."
B) "Ember is, without doubt, the finest restaurant this city has ever seen."
C) "Go for the flatbread, forgive the service, skip the desserts: for all its flaws, Ember is the most interesting new kitchen downtown."
Why: C gives specific instructions, concedes the flaws, and still takes a stance. A is mush (no verdict); B is a press release (uncalibrated, ungrounded).
Errors you will meet most (6.1)

"In today's fast-paced world…" · "This article will discuss…" · "It is important to note that" · "falls short to / from its ambition" (→ of) · "What makes the novel remarkable, it is its restraint" (doubled subject) · "a must-to-read" · "for all of its flaws it is good" (no contrast) · "the actual menu" (= current) · "a sensible performance" (= sensitive) · "one might argue" in a lifestyle blog · a verdict in the last line only · every paragraph starting with "I". For range, praise None of this is to deny that…; it is to insist that…, a hook that is echoed in the close, and evaluative adjectives with teeth (assured, overworked, uneven, generous).

6.2 · The Long Form (C18)

Exercise 1 — The supervision meeting (Script 6.2-A)

The supervisor is happy with the sentences ("well written line by line") but not the structure.

ProblemFix
1. The reader gets lost around page nine: the signposts stop.Open each section with a backward and a forward signpost ("Having established X, this section turns to Y").
2. Section 3 (the history of the building) has drifted off the through-line.Cut it to a paragraph, or reframe it so it serves the argument (why the building's design matters to the hub question).
3. The literature review is a queue of summaries ("Garcia found… Nwosu found…").Regroup sources by where they agree and where they diverge, and end on the gap the chapter fills.

Extra detail learners may catch: the student called the key idea "community use," "social function" and "hub activity" in different sections (terminological drift); the supervisor asks them to pick one.

Exercise 2 — Choose the attribution verb
  1. demonstrate / show / establish (plural subject data; accept demonstrates / shows with singular data)
  2. claims / asserts / alleges
  3. concedes / acknowledges / grants
  4. suggests / proposes
  5. draws / builds (on)
  6. argues / contends / maintains
  7. notes / observes / points out

Wrong: "The brochure demonstrates…" (endorses a doubtful claim) · "Garcia claims that her sample was small" (claims distances you from an admission: odd) · "Rossi speculates that most participants were under thirty" (a fact, not a speculation) · "Nwosu's model draws from" (possible, but draws on is the standard collocation). speculates for 4 is acceptable but stronger in its tentativeness than cautiously needs.

Exercise 4 — Paraphrase, quotation or patchwriting?

Exercise 5 — Shopping list into synthesis (model)

"There is broad agreement that libraries which add classes and meeting rooms attract more visitors: Garcia (2020) reports a 40% rise across twelve city libraries, and Nwosu (2022) finds a similar pattern in smaller towns. Borrowing tells a different story. Chen (2023) shows that loans have continued to fall over the same period. What remains unclear, and what no study has yet examined, is whether the new visitors borrow books at all."

Check: consensus move + both citations grouped · divergence move (tells a different story / whereas / however) · the gap stated as a question the review opens · stance verbs varied · every finding kept (12 libraries, 40%, smaller towns, falling loans). Not yet: the same list with linking words added ("Firstly, Garcia… Secondly, Nwosu… Finally, Chen…"): still a shopping list.

Dialogue — page nine

The two problems: dropped signposts (the reader gets lost) and a literature review that "reads like a queue" of summaries. The student's signpost: "Having established X, this section turns to Y." The supervisor's line "an argument, not a bibliography" is a good one to board.

Architecture Lab — what a strong plan and section look like

Model plan (Source Pack B, libraries): Through-line: Libraries that reinvent themselves as community hubs gain visitors but lose readers unless the hub is deliberately linked to borrowing. 1 A false choice: books or community (the debate is framed wrongly) · 2 Full buildings (visits rise with classes and rooms: Garcia, Nwosu) · 3 Empty shelves (loans keep falling: Chen) · 4 The case for the hub anyway (a public good even without loans: granted) · 5 Wiring the hub to the books (where events route past the shelves, loans recover: Adeyemi) · 6 A design question for library boards (recommendations). About 2,000 words: 250 · 350 · 350 · 300 · 450 · 300.

Model section opening (Section 3): "Having established that the hub model fills buildings, we now turn to what the new visitors actually do there. The evidence here is less encouraging. Whereas Garcia (2020) and Nwosu (2022) measure success by foot traffic, Chen (2023) tracks loans and finds that they have continued to fall in the same libraries… The following section considers whether this matters at all." Check against the frame's checklist.

Self-check 6.2
Q1
What is the surest test that an outline is a genuine argument rather than a list?
A) It has enough sections to fill the word count
B) Every section is labeled with a neutral category heading
C) You could not reorder the sections without breaking the argument
Why: a true through-line makes the sequence necessary. Reorderable sections joined by "and also" are a list. B describes exactly the weak, generic headings the lesson warns against.
Q2
When should you write the abstract, and what should it do?
A) First, as a promise of what the piece will cover
B) Last, but withholding the findings to make the reader read on
C) Last, and it should summarize the whole, delivering the actual findings
Why: you only know what the piece became once it is written, and an abstract summarizes rather than advertises (context → problem → method → findings → meaning).
Q3 · new
Which sentence synthesizes sources rather than listing them?
A) "Garcia (2020) studied visits. Chen (2023) studied loans. Both are interesting."
B) "Whereas Garcia (2020) reports a sharp rise in library visits, Chen (2023) finds that loans continue to fall; what remains unclear is whether new visitors ever borrow books."
C) "According to Garcia (2020), she says that visits rose, and according to Chen (2023), she says that loans fell."
Why: B relates the two findings (divergence) and identifies the gap. A lists with an empty evaluation; C lists with a doubled attribution ("according to… she says").
Errors you will meet most (6.2)

"According to Smith, he says…" · "the author says" as the only reporting verb · "Smith claims" for a finding the writer accepts · "Many studies showed" for a body of research (→ have shown) · "researches" as a count noun · "informations, evidences, literatures" · "draws from / bases on" (→ draws on / is based on) · patchwriting with a citation, believed to be safe · headings like "Analysis (2)" · signposts so heavy in a short piece that they become throat-clearing · the key term renamed in every section. For range, praise What remains unclear is whether…, Building on X's framework…, concedes used precisely, and a through-line a reader could argue with.

6.3 · Fine Points of Usage & Punctuation (C19)

Exercise 1 — Hear the punctuation (Script 6.3-A)
  1. The proposal, after much debate, was approved. (Accept dashes or parentheses if the learner heard more or less volume; ask which they heard.)
  2. The café was full; the bakery next door was empty. (A period is also correct; a comma is a splice.)
  3. She had one goal: to win.
  4. They had planned for everything — and then the power failed. (US book style closes the dash: everything—and.)
  5. My colleague, who speaks four languages, translated the menu.
  6. We visited Lisbon, Portugal; Accra, Ghana; and Hanoi, Vietnam.
Exercise 2 — One or several?
  1. No: only the employees who worked overtime (restrictive).
  2. Yes: all the employees received a bonus, and (by the way) they all worked overtime (non-restrictive).
  3. No: only the ones left in the office.
  4. Either two people (my parents = the head chef and the night manager, an appositive) or four (my parents + two others). Add the Oxford comma: my parents, the head chef, and the night manager, or reorder: the head chef, the night manager and my parents.
  5. One (the clause adds information; it doesn't pick one app out of several).
Exercise 3 — Choose the mark
  1. The reviews were glowing; the bookings were not.
  2. The new policy has one flaw: nobody asked the staff.
  3. The launch — after three delays and one lawsuit — went smoothly.
  4. The launch (after three delays) went smoothly.
  5. It rained all morning; we canceled the tour. / It rained all morning, so we canceled the tour. (Also: … morning. We canceled…)
  6. Our speakers are Ana Ruiz, a chef; Tom Park, a designer; and Lin Wei, an architect.

Accept closed dashes (US book style) or spaced dashes in 3, consistently. In 5, cancelled is British: accept. A dash in 2 is possible but less precise than the colon's "delivers the promise."

Exercise 4 — Danglers, stray modifiers and parallelism
  1. Rushing to catch the train, I spilled my coffee everywhere.
  2. Having finished the report, I found that the printer had jammed. / When I had finished the report, the printer jammed.
  3. We drove almost 500 miles that day.
  4. The manual explains how to install, configure and maintain the device. (Oxford comma optional.)
  5. The role calls for someone who is diligent, creative and able to lead a team.
  6. To succeed, you must be not only skilled but also disciplined.

Not yet: "Rushing to catch the train, my coffee was spilled by me" (grammatical attachment, but absurd) · "The manual explains installing, configuring and maintenance" (still not parallel).

Dialogue — it reads flat

The three changes: join the two short sentences with a semicolon (they're one thought); let a short sentence land after the long one; keep the single dash in paragraph two but cut most of the five in paragraph four.

Self-check 6.3
Q1
"My sister, who lives in Madrid, is a lawyer." What does the pair of commas tell the reader?
A) The writer has several sisters; the Madrid one is the lawyer
B) The writer has one sister; that she lives in Madrid is extra information
C) Nothing; the commas are optional and change nothing
Why: the commas make the clause non-restrictive: it adds a detail about a sister already identified. A describes the version without commas; C ignores the meaning the commas carry.
Q2
Which "rule" is actually a myth a C2 writer may freely ignore?
A) "Never end a sentence with a preposition"
B) "An introductory participle must attach to the subject of the main clause"
C) "Non-restrictive clauses take commas; restrictive ones don't"
Why: "the report I told you about" is perfect English. B and C are genuine rules: breaking them changes or muddles the meaning.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is free of faults?
A) "Having checked the figures twice, the report was approved by the analyst, the board, which met on Friday, agreed."
B) "Having checked the figures twice, the analyst approved the report, the board which met on Friday agreed."
C) "Having checked the figures twice, the analyst approved the report; the board, which met on Friday, agreed."
Why: C attaches the participle to the analyst, joins the clauses with a semicolon and fences the non-restrictive clause with commas. A dangles (the report checked the figures) and splices; B splices and drops the commas around a non-restrictive clause (there is only one board).
Errors you will meet most (6.3)

Comma splices (the most frequent fossilized error at C2) · "The report, that I sent yesterday, is final" · "I think, that…" (German / Russian comma) · a space before : ; ? ! (French) · "between you and I" · "whom shall I say is calling?" · "less mistakes" · "Each of the proposals have" · "Neither the chair nor the members was" (verb agrees with the nearer subject: were) · "I insist that he is present" (formal: be) · "Walking into the meeting, the tension was obvious" · a colon after an incomplete clause ("The reasons are: …"; tolerated in some US styles, but The reasons are three: … is cleaner) · mixed US and UK quotation marks in one text · "correcting" a split infinitive into something stiffer. For range, praise a semicolon that balances, a colon that delivers a punchline, and one well-placed dash.
