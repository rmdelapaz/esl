Module 3 — Discourse Mastery: Structure & Cohesion

Three classes that lift learners from the well-made sentence to the well-made text. First, the small words that steer and color discourse: organizing markers (that said, turning to, in short), stance adverbials (admittedly, arguably, tellingly, ostensibly) and interactional markers (the thing is, mind you, let's face it), used to signal attitude on purpose and to read the attitude other writers smuggle in. Then the architecture of long texts: cohesion versus coherence, theme and rheme (given before new), lexical chains without "elegant variation," topic sentences and the one-sentence-per-paragraph skeleton. Last, the three condensing arts at the heart of C2 reading-into-writing: the faithful summary, the genuine paraphrase (not patchwriting) and the multi-source synthesis, organized by idea and attributed with reporting verbs that are fair to every source.

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-discourse.html. Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C7. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Stance Workshop neutral reports and Stance Detective A/B cards (C7), the cut-up Wrenfield article and the Riverside Radio broken draft for the Cohesion & Coherence Lab (C8), the three library sources and the paraphrase integrity strips for the Synthesis Workshop (C9), the Thread It! board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and shadow the intonation.
  • This module is a writing-and-reading lab. Each class spends roughly a quarter to a third of its time on one extended product that another learner reads, hears or checks: a neutral report rewritten in three stances (C7), a reconstructed and repaired long text (C8), a 200-word attributed synthesis of three sources (C9). Protect that time. If you're running late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. At C1 learners met linking and signposting (C1 6.2), reference, substitution and ellipsis (C1 3.2), hedging (C1 3.3), cleft sentences and end-weight (C1 2.3), critical reading for bias (C1 6.1) and note-taking and summarizing a lecture (C1 7.1). Don't re-teach any of it. The C2 step is deliberate effect at the scale of the whole text: choosing the marker that plants exactly the attitude you intend, engineering flow so the reader never stumbles, and restating other people's thought so faithfully and so freshly that nothing is distorted and nothing is borrowed.
  • Feedback on four things. C2 learners rarely make the errors a C1 checklist catches; what they need is feedback on effect. In every workshop, take notes in four columns: precision (the word that is nearly right: ostensibly where apparently was meant, claims where notes is fair, Consequently where no consequence exists), appropriacy (the register seam: anyway in a report, furthermore in a chat, the thing is in a synthesis), naturalness (grammatical but no native writer would do it: three stance adverbials in one sentence, "Firstly… Secondly… Thirdly…" in a two-point email, five synonyms for one noun) and effect (did the reader get the attitude, the order, the attribution you intended?). In the feedback stage, board one or two items from each column, without names, and ask the class to explain the effect before anyone fixes it.
  • Fossilized and near-native at once. Many C2 learners write more elegantly than their teachers in one register and repeat the same fossilized habit in another: "On the other hand" with no first hand, "In my opinion, I think," "Actually" meaning "currently," a comma-less "Frankly I…," a synthesis that marches "Source A says… Source B says…". Name the habit privately and specifically ("You use moreover in speech about once a minute: try and on top of that today") and give it a one-class target. Praise the near-native range out loud; correct the fossil in writing, on a sticky note.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured peer-review step that has its own test: the "Name the attitude" + "Strip" tests (C7: can a reviewer name the stance of each version, and when every marker is stripped out, are the facts identical?), the "Skeleton" test (C8: one sentence per paragraph, read as a list: does it make an argument?) and the "Highlighter" + "Whose voice?" tests (C9: any run of three or more words borrowed from a source is highlighted; every claim is tagged with its source or "writer"). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote the text instead of summarizing it ("In line 4, claims makes you sound doubtful of Hsu").
  • Critique protocol at C2. Strong writers can be bruised by peer comments and can bruise others. Run every review as describe → ask → suggest: first describe what the text does ("Your second version reads as warmly approving because of remarkably and mercifully"), then ask a real question ("Did you intend mercifully to sound relieved?"), and only then suggest. No verdicts ("It's bad," "It's perfect") and no rewriting the writer's text for them.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes often include senior professionals, academics and translators who are used to being the best writer in the room. Give them the roles that require listening and restraint: in C7 the stance detective (who may only name the attitude and quote the evidence), in C8 the skeleton writer (who reduces each paragraph to one sentence and reads the list aloud without comment), in C9 the source checker (with the highlighter). Say it kindly: "Your ear is the best in the group. I need it on the evidence this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first."
  • Stretching the strongest. Each lesson has a stretch box. The direction for this module is controlled effect: moving stance adverbials into medial and final position for rhythm and irony (C7), mixing constant-theme and linear progression deliberately and delaying a point for a "reveal" (C8), and writing a synthesis whose own verdict is visible only in its ordering and emphasis, never in a loaded verb (C9). Ask strong learners to explain the effect of every choice, not just make it.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and vocabulary too (organize, summarize, color, skepticism, trucks, dollars, field). Accept British forms (organise, colour, lorries, pounds, pitch) whenever a learner uses them, and point out that précis keeps its accent in both varieties.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Steer it → build it → distill it. In C7 learners steer a stretch of discourse: organizing markers tell the reader where they are, stance adverbials tell the reader how to take it, and interactional markers keep a conversation reciprocal. They also learn to read the same devices working on them. In C8 they build a whole text that holds together: cohesion on the surface, coherence underneath, information flowing from given to new, one lexical chain, one through-line. In C9 they take other people's texts and distill them, into a gist, a paraphrase and a synthesis, without distorting a source or borrowing its words. Each class depends on the one before: stance language returns as reporting verbs in C9; flow and ordering return as "organize by idea."

Topic care: invented texts, assigned stances, low personal stakes

Stance and synthesis are practiced on invented, neutral texts: a museum's free evenings, open textbooks at a college, a parking app, an orchestra's pay-what-you-can concerts, pocket forests, a community radio station, fine-free libraries. Keep every example and discussion on language, not politics. Every stance in the workshop is assigned on a card: a learner writing the "skeptical" version is practicing a register, not declaring a belief. The C9 journal asks learners to look honestly at their own past paraphrasing. Treat patchwriting as a convention to learn, never as a moral failing: in several educational traditions, reproducing an authority's exact words is a mark of respect and diligence. Any "write about you" task allows a new identity card or an invented answer, and anyone can say "Pass."

  • For C9: the three library sources as A/B/C expert cards (one set per group of 3), one idea grid per group, the paraphrase integrity strips (one set per pair) and a highlighter per group.

Discourse Markers & Stance

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • choose organizing markers that fit the function (open, add, concede, shift, close) and the mode, and switch a text's register by swapping its hinges (That said → Nevertheless; Anyway → Turning to…)
  • use stance adverbials from four families to plant a precise attitude with one word, distinguishing near-neighbors: admittedly (concede against yourself) vs arguably (defensible but contestable) vs undoubtedly (full commitment); apparently vs ostensibly vs reportedly
  • punctuate and position a stance adverbial so it comments on the whole clause (Frankly, I disagree vs She spoke frankly) and use medial and final positions for rhythm (The result, remarkably, held. · He said nothing, tellingly.)
  • use interactional markers (the thing is, I mean, mind you, let's face it) to manage rapport in speech, and keep them out of formal writing
  • read the stance that others encode (supposedly, so-called, needless to say, as everyone knows) and strip it away to test whether an argument lives in the facts or only in the framing

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: organizing markers. They change nothing in the propositional content; they tell the reader where we are in the discourse. Their register is their most visible feature.

"That said, we learned a lot." Does that said add a fact? (No.) What does it do? (Concedes / qualifies what came before.) Would you write it in a formal report? (Yes, it's neutral; nevertheless is more formal.) · "Anyway, moving on." Chat or essay? (Chat or a relaxed talk.) What would the essay say? (Turning to… / As for…)

Meaning: stance adverbials. A fronted word, fenced by a comma, comments on the whole clause and reveals the speaker's attitude to it. It is outside the grammar of the clause.

"Admittedly, my figures were incomplete." Is the speaker attacking someone? (No.) Whose position does the admission weaken? (Their own.) Why do it? (To look fair and win trust.) · "Arguably the finest novel of the decade." Is the speaker certain? (Fairly confident, but they accept someone could disagree.) Is arguably weaker than undoubtedly? (Yes, and safer.)
"Ostensibly, it was a courtesy call." Was it a courtesy call? (That was the stated reason.) Does the speaker believe it? (Probably not: there was another, hidden reason.) · "Apparently the deal collapsed." Does the speaker know for sure? (No: they heard it, or it seems so.) · "Reportedly, talks have stalled." Who says so? (A source; the speaker doesn't vouch for it.)
"Tellingly, he said nothing." Is silence important here? (Yes: it reveals something bigger.) Positive or negative? (It depends on context, but usually critical.) · "Curiously, sales rose." Surprised or angry? (Surprised, puzzled.) "Ironically, the diet made her hungrier." What's ironic? (The result is the opposite of the intention.)

Meaning: reading slant. supposedly and so-called plant doubt; needless to say and as everyone knows assume agreement; to be fair stages a concession. Strip them out and the "argument" often disappears.

"The so-called upgrade slowed every laptop in the office." Does the writer think it was an upgrade? (No: the label is ironic.) · "Needless to say, the old system worked better." If it's needless to say, why say it? (To present an opinion as obvious and pull the reader along.)

Form.

Front (default)adverbial + comma + clauseFrankly, the scheme was doomed.
Medialbetween subject and verb, fenced by commasThe result, remarkably, held.
Finalafter the clause, with a commaHe said nothing, tellingly.
Manner vs stanceno comma, after the verb = mannerShe spoke frankly. (in a frank way) vs Frankly, she lied. (I'm being blunt)
Phrase formsto + adj · adj + enough · -ly + speakingto be fair · interestingly enough · broadly speaking · strictly speaking
Reading framesso-called + noun · supposedly · needless to saythe so-called reforms · it was supposedly designed to…

Pronunciation. A fronted stance adverbial is its own intonation unit: a short pause after it and, usually, a fall-rise on the stressed syllable that says "there's more to this": adMITtedly↘↗ | the evidence is thin. Know the stress: ARguably, adMITtedly, preSUmably, osTENsibly, conCEIvably, unDOUBTedly, TELLingly, reMARKably, FRANKly. Medial and final adverbials are said on a lower, quieter pitch, like an aside: "The result, remarkably, held." Interactional markers are unstressed and quick (I mean, you know); stressing them sounds impatient ("LOOK, …").

Drill script
  1. Which attitude? (choral then individual; exaggerate the fall-rise): T: "The trial was small. (concede against yourself)" → S: "Admittedly, the trial was small." T: "No one was hurt. (relief)" → "Mercifully, no one was hurt." T: "The minutes don't mention the budget. (this reveals something)" → "Tellingly, the minutes don't mention the budget." T: "It's a fruit. (strict definition)" → "Technically, it's a fruit." T: "The venue will change. (a reasonable guess)" → "Presumably, the venue will change."
  2. Switch the room: T reads a spoken hinge, S gives a written equivalent: "On top of that" → "Furthermore / What is more." "That said" → "Nevertheless / Be that as it may." "Anyway, moving on" → "Turning to… / As for…" "Long story short" → "In short / In sum." Then the reverse, written → spoken.
  3. Move it: T: "Remarkably, the result held." → S (medial): "The result, remarkably, held." T: "Tellingly, he said nothing." → S (final): "He said nothing, tellingly." Ask: "Which feels more like an aside? Which feels like a verdict?"
  4. Strip it (reading): T reads, S says the bare fact: "Needless to say, the so-called improvement supposedly saved time." → "The change was meant to save time." "Surprisingly enough, the cheaper option, arguably, performed better." → "The cheaper option performed better."
Stance Workshop + Stance Detective — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 with one neutral report. Each learner takes one stance to draft first: skeptical (doubt, distance, a raised eyebrow: ostensibly, supposedly, predictably, needless to say, tellingly), approving (warmth, admiration: remarkably, encouragingly, mercifully, notably, if anything) and judiciously balanced (a scholar weighing evidence: admittedly, arguably, broadly speaking, that said, on balance). Draft alone for 5 minutes, then polish all three together for 7.
  • The rules are what make it C2: ☐ every number and fact identical to the neutral report ☐ nothing added (no invented quotes, no new facts) ☐ 3–5 markers per version, no stacking (never three in one sentence) ☐ at least one medial or final adverbial ☐ written-report register (no anyway, you know, let's face it). A learner who "cheats" by changing a fact (a mere 71% is fine; only 60% is not) must put it back.
  • Peer review (6 min): swap the three versions, headings folded under. Reviewers write the stance they read for each and quote the two words that told them; then they choose one version and run the Strip test, crossing out every marker and checking the bare text against the neutral report. Mismatches are the most useful feedback of the class: "We read your balanced version as skeptical because of ostensibly."
  • Stance Detective (7 min): pairs. A takes a topic card and a secret attitude card (grudging admiration, polite skepticism, wry amusement, enthusiastic but fair, weary disappointment, cautious optimism) and speaks for 90 seconds, spoken register, interactional markers welcome. B listens only for stance, then names the attitude and quotes two markers. Swap once. The detective role goes to your most dominant speaker first.
  • Share: one group reads its three versions aloud in random order; the class names each stance. Ask: "Which version would you trust most as a reader, and why?" (Usually the balanced one, because admittedly makes the criticism look fair.)

Can you change a number? (No.) Can you add a new fact or a quote? (No.) How many markers in each version? (Three to five.) Three in one sentence? (No.) Is this a chat or a written report? Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 speakers are far stronger orally than in writing (often learners with interrupted schooling who have lived and worked in English for years). Give them a marker strip sorted by stance (skeptical / approving / balanced, four words each) and let them write one version only, the one they choose, while the group writes the others. In Stance Detective they are often the best detectives: their ear for attitude is excellent. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–6, Ex 3 items 1–6. Target: one clean version with three well-placed markers, read aloud with the pause after each fronted adverbial.

Stretch

Stance by position and scarcity. Ask strong learners to write a fourth, wry version of their report in which the irony lives in a single final-position adverbial (The board, which had predicted disaster, voted to continue the trial, unanimously) and to explain why one well-placed word is more damning than three. Then the reverse skill: give them a real published review and ask them to rewrite it as a neutral report, cataloguing every stance device they removed. Finally, hopefully: write the same sentence for a text message, a university report and a conservative style editor.

L1 watch-outs
  • False-friend markers: actually for "currently" (Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, Portuguese atualmente, Italian attualmente, German aktuell); eventually for "possibly" (German eventuell, Spanish eventualmente, Dutch eventueel); also for "so / therefore" (German also); in fine for "finally" (French enfin). These fossilize even in near-native speakers; board them once and keep a tally.
  • "On the other hand" as "also": very common across L1s. In English it needs a genuine contrast, ideally with a first hand. For addition, use moreover, what is more, on top of that.
  • Formal markers in speech: learners from exam-focused systems (Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Russian, and many South Asian and Arabic-medium schools) often say Firstly… Secondly… Moreover… In conclusion in conversation. Praise the control, then point out the register: "Perfect in your report. At the coffee machine: and another thing… anyway, that's about it."
  • "Of course" and "naturally": Russian конечно, Spanish claro, Portuguese claro, Arabic ṭabʿan are neutral "yes, sure"; English of course can sound as if the listener's question was obvious. Offer sure, certainly, absolutely.
  • "In my opinion, I think…" and "Honestly speaking / Frankly speaking" as fixed openers (common in Chinese-, Hindi-, Urdu- and Arabic-influenced English, and in many exam-trained learners). Neither is wrong, but at C2 one stance marker per clause is enough.
  • Comma omission: languages without a strong comma convention for sentence adverbs (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese) produce "Frankly I disagree," "Hopefully it works." Tie the comma to the pause they already hear.
  • Stance adverbials that don't translate one-to-one: Spanish de hecho and French en fait map to "in fact," which English uses more sparingly and often for correction; German eigentlich, Dutch eigenlijk are wider than "actually." Encourage learners to find the function first, then the English word.

3.2 · C8

Cohesion & Coherence Across Long Texts

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • distinguish cohesion (surface links you can point to) from coherence (a line of thought the reader infers), and diagnose a text that has one without the other
  • control information flow: open each sentence on given information (theme) and end on the new (rheme), using linear ("staircase") and constant-theme progression on purpose
  • build a lexical chain (repetition, synonym, superordinate, general word, collocation) that binds and develops a topic, and avoid the elegant-variation trap by repeating the anchor term
  • use meta-discourse sparingly (preview, topic sentence, internal summary, transition, cross-reference) and signpost the turns, not every step
  • reconstruct and repair a long text: choose an ordering principle, write topic sentences and test the whole with the one-sentence-per-paragraph skeleton
Materials
  • Workbook 3.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Lab frame, self-check)
  • Slides 8–12 · Cohesion and coherence flashcards · Wrenfield article cut-up sets (7 paragraphs, A/B/C packets, in envelopes) · Riverside Radio broken drafts · fault logs · New identity cards

Left: two virtues. Cohesion = on the page, you can point to it (reference, connectives, lexical links). Coherence = in the reader's mind, you infer it (order, relevance, a through-line). ✘ "The committee approved the budget. However, the budget is a document. Moreover, paper comes from trees." Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: cohesion vs coherence. Cohesion is the visible wiring; coherence is the thought the wiring should carry. Editing the wrong layer is the commonest waste of an editor's time.

"The gallery reopened in March. Consequently, the café serves excellent coffee." Is there a connective? (Yes.) Is it true? (No: the coffee isn't a consequence.) Cohesion or coherence problem? (Coherence: a false link.) Will a better connective fix it? (No: we need a real relation, or no link at all.) · "The program failed. Nobody had been consulted." Can you follow it? (Yes.) What's missing? (A little wiring: because.)

Meaning: theme / rheme. The theme is the point of departure, usually given; the rheme is the news, usually at the end, where attention rests (end-focus, which learners met as end-weight at C1).

"The director unveiled a radical proposal. This proposal alarmed her own board." Where does sentence 2 start? (On this proposal, the news of sentence 1.) Given or new? (Given.) Where's the new information? (At the end: alarmed her own board.) · "…Alarm among her own board was the immediate reaction." What's wrong? (Brand-new information in the theme slot: the reader has to hold it in suspense.)

Meaning: lexical chains and elegant variation. A chain binds and develops; variation for its own sake confuses.

"The spaniel… the dog… the animal." Same creature? (Yes.) Is the lens getting wider or narrower? (Wider: specific to general.) · "The novel… the book… the work… the volume… the tome." How many books? (One, but the reader has to keep checking.) Fix? (Repeat novel once and let it do the rest.) Is repeating a key word bad style? (No: repeating the anchor is a virtue.)

Meaning: meta-discourse and the skeleton. The writer steps outside the content to orient the reader. Too much, and the text reads like its own table of contents.

"So far, then, the evidence suggests…" Is this new content? (No, a summary of what came before.) Why use it? (To consolidate before a turn.) · "Firstly, I will now begin by introducing the first point." How many signposts? (Too many for one step.) · If the one-sentence skeleton stutters, should you polish sentences? (No: fix the order or relevance first.)

Pronunciation. Flow is audible. Given information is de-accented and the nuclear stress falls on the new: "The lecture introduced a new THEory. This theory challenges the STANdard model" (theory in sentence 2 is low and unstressed because it's given). When reading a long text aloud, use a pitch reset: start each new paragraph or topic on a noticeably higher pitch, and let the last sentence of a paragraph fall low. Signposts such as So far, then, and Against this backdrop, are their own intonation units with a pause after them.

Drill script
  1. Hand it on (the staircase): T says a sentence; S starts the next with a summary-noun theme that picks up the rheme. T: "Sales fell sharply in March." → S: "This decline…" T: "The council closed the pool for repairs." → "The closure…" T: "Staff proposed a four-day week." → "The proposal…" T: "The river flooded twice in one winter." → "These floods…" Stress the new information at the end of each S sentence.

Cohesion & Coherence Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, packets A, B and C (the paragraph split is on the materials sheet). In pairs, use the two-packet version. The rule is no showing in the first five minutes: learners may read a paragraph's first and last sentences aloud, or describe it, but not pass it over. This forces them to listen for cohesive clues (These gains, however,… · It is this unplanned expense… · Supporters of the scheme reply…).
  • Reconstruct (10 min): after five minutes, lay all seven paragraphs on the table and finalize the order. On the Lab frame they write: the order (letters), the ordering principle, and three clues that decided it. Any defensible order with a coherent skeleton earns credit, but the article was written with one clear order in mind (answer key).
  • Diagnose & repair (9 min): the Riverside Radio draft (a one-paragraph board report, about 160 words) has six planted faults: empty over-signposting, an irrelevant detail, a false connective, a buried main point with no topic sentence, elegant variation, and new information in the theme slot with an ambiguous This. Groups log each on the fault log as cohesion or coherence before rewriting, then repair it in about 120 words.
  • Skeleton test (6 min): swap repairs. The skeleton writer (give this role to a dominant speaker) writes one sentence per move and reads the list aloud without commentary. The rest of the group answers: Does it make one argument? Is anything redundant or missing? Then one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text.
  • Share: one group reads its repaired draft aloud with pitch reset; the class runs the skeleton test live on the board.

Stretch

Structure as strategy. Ask strong learners to re-order the Wrenfield article for a different effect: specific → general (open on the drought, the "reveal" later), or compare/contrast (critics and supporters point by point), and to write the new topic sentences so the joins still feel inevitable. Then ask them to mix constant-theme and linear progression in one paragraph on purpose and to explain where each serves. For exam candidates: the CPE Writing Part 1 essay rewards exactly this, a single through-line across two texts.

L1 watch-outs
  • Delayed main point: several rhetorical traditions build toward the point rather than opening with it: the Chinese and Japanese four-part qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé / ki-shō-ten-ketsu structure, some Arabic and Persian persuasive prose, and much Romance-language academic writing. Learners may write a beautiful paragraph whose topic sentence arrives last. Frame the English preference for topic sentence first as a genre convention, not a better way of thinking; the "specific → general reveal" is a legitimate choice when it's deliberate.
  • Synonym fever: French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and Russian school writing often penalizes repetition, so learners produce the elegant-variation muddle with pride. Reassure them that repeating the anchor term is good English style.
  • Additive chains: Arabic wa, Persian va and oral narrative styles in many languages link long runs of clauses with and. In English, a run of ands hides the logical relations that coherence depends on.
  • Theme and word order: speakers of free word-order languages (Russian, Polish, Czech, Ukrainian, Finnish, Hungarian) put new information last through word order and may try the same in English ("In the corner stood a man" is fine; "The report wrote the manager" is not). Offer English's tools instead: passive, there is, clefts, a summary noun.
  • Topic-comment openers: Chinese, Japanese and Korean topic-prominence produces "As for the budget, it…," "Regarding the report, it…" at the start of every paragraph. One is fine; five read as a list.
  • Paragraph length and comma splices: German and Spanish academic writing tolerates very long paragraphs and sentences joined by commas. English readers expect one idea per paragraph and a period between independent clauses.

Summarizing, Paraphrasing & Synthesis

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • sort a dense source into main idea, supporting detail, example and aside, and reduce it to a faithful one-sentence gist that keeps its logical relations (although, but, because) and its proportions
  • paraphrase by rebuilding the sentence (change word class, voice, clause order, subject), and recognize patchwriting (same skeleton, swapped synonyms) as a form of plagiarism, even when cited
  • choose reporting verbs that carry the right stance (notes · argues · concedes · counters · claims) and distance a source only when the doubt is earned
  • write a synthesis organized by idea, not by source: a governing sentence, agreement, divergence, qualification, and a close that holds the sources in tension
  • check a text for integrity: every claim attributed, no run of borrowed words, no distortion of a source's certainty or scope

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: summary. A miniature of the argument, not a bag of surviving facts. Fidelity of proportion matters as much as fidelity of fact.

"Though the high-speed line has cut journey times, its huge overspend has led economists to argue the money would have helped more people elsewhere." What survived? (The concession, the charge, the counter-argument.) What vanished? (The doubled estimate, the commuters, the rural districts: detail and example.) If I write "The line cut journey times and cost a lot," what's lost? (The logic: though, and the economists' argument.) · Can I add "which was a mistake"? (No: that's my opinion, not the source's.)

Meaning: paraphrase vs patchwriting. A genuine paraphrase rebuilds the architecture; a patchwrite keeps it and swaps the furniture.

"The quick growth of social media has basically changed the way young individuals develop their political views." Same subject, verb, object and order as the original? (Yes.) What changed? (Only synonyms.) Is it a paraphrase? (No: patchwriting.) If I add "(Smith, 2024)", is it OK? (No: it's still the author's expression. Quote it or rebuild it.) · Which should you change first, the structure or the words? (The structure.)

Meaning: reporting verbs. The verb is where the writer's stance toward the source lives.

"As Halpern shows, the policy failed." Does the writer agree with Halpern? (Yes: shows endorses.) "As Halpern claims, the policy failed." (The writer doubts it.) "Ortega concedes that fines brought in some money." Is Ortega happy to say so? (No: it's a point against her own position.) · Is it fair to write "Hsu alleges…"? (Only if you can justify the doubt; otherwise it's bias.)

Meaning: synthesis. Organized by idea, not source. If the paragraph could be reordered source by source without loss, it isn't a synthesis.

"Source A says… Source B says… Source C says…" Is that a synthesis? (No: a list of summaries.) What's the first question to ask? (Where do they agree, disagree, or qualify each other?) Must I be neutral? (Fair, not necessarily neutral: your view shows through order and emphasis, openly marked.)

Synthesis Workshop — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one source each (A Ortega, library director · B Hsu, budget analyst · C Nwosu, literacy researcher). The sources are short (about 90 words each) and invented, and none is simply "right": Ortega has good local results but a stake in them; Hsu is careful about evidence but frames the issue as cost; Nwosu widens the lens to other barriers.
  • Experts (6 min): read your source for 2 minutes, underline its main claim and its hedges, then turn the card over. Each learner gives a 30-second oral gist from memory. Speaking without the text makes patchwriting almost impossible, which is the point. Listeners may ask one clarifying question each.
  • Idea grid (5 min): cards face up. Fill three rows: agree (for example: fines raise little money; returns stay stable or rise), diverge (whether the lost revenue matters; whether current evidence is good enough), qualify / extend (Nwosu: fines are one barrier among several). Agree a governing sentence that is not a list: "The case for fine-free libraries turns less on money than on who the library is for."
  • Write (11 min): 180–220 words. Checklist: ☐ governing sentence first ☐ agreement before divergence ☐ each source named at least once, with a fair reporting verb ☐ at least one whereas / by contrast and one qualifies / extends move ☐ a close that holds the views in tension (the writer's own view, if any, clearly marked) ☐ no run of three or more borrowed words ☐ nothing sharper or softer than the source said. Those who finish early count words and cut to exactly 200.
  • Integrity review (5 min): swap. The source checker highlights borrowed runs and writes A / B / C / all / writer beside each sentence. The writer must fix any highlight and any sentence whose voice the checker couldn't label. One strength and one suggestion, quoting the text.
  • Share: one synthesis read aloud with the name-stress technique; the class says whose voice is speaking at each point. Early finishers: paraphrase integrity strips, then Thread It!

Stretch

Exam precision and authorial voice. L1 watch-outs
  • Different conventions of borrowing: in many educational traditions (for example, parts of East and South Asia, the Middle East and Eastern Europe), reproducing an authority's words shows respect and mastery, and quotation marks are used less. Explain the Anglo-American convention clearly and without judgment: in English academic and professional writing, you either quote exactly (with marks) or rebuild completely.
  • Preposition after reporting verbs: "discuss about," "mention about," "emphasize on," "stress on," "criticize about," "explain me" are common in South Asian, Arabic, Chinese and Spanish-influenced English, and often fossilized at C2. Drill discuss / mention / emphasize + noun, explain (to me) that.
  • Automatic backshift: learners trained on reported speech write "Ortega argued that fines were…" for a text that still argues it. In English academic writing the present tense is normal for what a text says.
  • "says" for everything, or the opposite: one learned verb (claims, often) used for every source, unaware of its distancing effect. Build a personal bank of six verbs with their stance.
  • Double attribution: "According to Hsu, he thinks that…" (common across L1s). One frame is enough.
  • Precision of certainty: modal systems don't map one-to-one (German sollen, Spanish conditional of rumor habría, French conditional aurait all mark "reportedly"). Learners may harden or soften a source's certainty in translation; check every may / suggests / shows against the original.
