Module 3 — Discourse Mastery: Answers

Answers to every Module 3 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the three-stance rewrite, the reconstructed article and repaired draft, the 200-word synthesis) and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 3.1–3.3Classes C7–C99 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect. Almost every task in this module has many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps every fact and the source's degree of certainty, (2) is accurate and idiomatic, (3) fits the register (a written report, a review, an academic synthesis) and (4) has the effect the task asked for: the stance a reader can name, the order a reader can follow, the attribution a reader can trace. When a learner's version is good but different from the model, say what it does well and, if useful, show one alternative ("Lovely. Tellingly in final position would make it even drier."). American English is the model; accept British spellings (organise, summarise, colour, scepticism). Contractions are fine in the spoken tasks and dialogues, not in the formal written ones.

3.1 · Discourse Markers & Stance (C7)

Exercise 1 — The museum commentary (Script 3.1-A)
1Right, soO (opening, spoken)
2To be honestI (also accept S: candor)
3AdmittedlyS (epistemic: concedes a point against the speaker's own expectation)
4That saidO (conceding / qualifying)
5ArguablyS (softens a bold claim)
6Mind youI (qualifying afterthought; also accept O)
7franklyS (attitude: bluntness)
8TellinglyS (attitude: a detail that reveals something larger)

Attitude: amused, mildly skeptical but fair; the speaker recommends the museum with reservations ("go, but go with an open mind"). The most revealing words are usually admittedly (fairness), frankly (amusement at the lamp and toaster) and tellingly (the untouched map room is the most popular: a quiet criticism of the new layout). The close is "Anyway, long story short" (O, spoken). Spelling errors to expect: admitedly, arguabley, tellinly.

Exercise 2 — Choose the stance
  1. admittedly (a concession against oneself; frankly is bluntness, undoubtedly full commitment)
  2. ironically (the outcome reverses the intention)
  3. reportedly (attributed to a source, not vouched for)
  4. notably (singles out one striking case)
  5. presumably (a reasonable inference; undoubtedly is too strong)
  6. ostensibly (the stated reason, with a hint of another)
  7. Technically (true by a strict definition)
  8. if anything (corrects toward the opposite of what was expected)

Near-misses to discuss: apparently in 3 is acceptable (it also distances), but reportedly is the precise choice for a claim from a source. Seemingly in 6 changes the meaning (it looks that way) and loses the suspicion.

Exercise 3 — Function and register
  1. furthermore: organizing (adding) · written / formal
  2. anyway: organizing (topic shift or closing) · spoken / casual
  3. arguably: stance (epistemic, softens a bold claim) · both, especially written argument
  4. the thing is: interactional (flags the crux) · spoken / casual
  5. to sum up: organizing (closing) · written / formal, or a prepared talk
  6. mind you: interactional / organizing (a qualifying afterthought) · spoken / casual
  7. be that as it may: organizing (conceding) · written / formal
  8. long story short: organizing (summarizing, closing) · spoken / casual
  9. tellingly: stance (attitude: the detail is revealing) · both, especially written commentary
  10. let's face it: interactional (presents an unwelcome truth as shared) · spoken / casual

Accept "stance" for 6 and 10 if the learner explains the attitude they carry. The point of the exercise is the register column: a learner who puts anyway or let's face it in "written / formal" needs a follow-up conversation.

Exercise 4 — Same facts, different temperature (models)

Approving: "The city's new central library, which opened in May, has been a remarkable success. It cost $14 million and, encouragingly, visitor numbers have already tripled. Admittedly, the café closes at 4 p.m., and the reading room can be too noisy to work in; these are, however, teething problems. Fittingly, the architect has won a national award."

Skeptical: "The city's new central library opened in May at a cost of $14 million. Visitor numbers have, admittedly, tripled. Yet the café closes, curiously, at 4 p.m., and the reading room is often too noisy to work in, which is, for a library, no small failing. The architect, predictably, has won a national award."

Check: all six facts present and unchanged; three to five markers; no marker stacking. Not yet: versions that add facts ("thousands of complaints"), change them ("only tripled" is fine as stance; "doubled" is not), or use spoken markers (anyway, let's face it) in a written review. Praise medial placement (Visitor numbers have, admittedly, tripled) and a single final-position verdict.

Exercise 5 — Read the slant, then strip it

1. so-called: the label "upgrade" is disputed, a raised eyebrow · supposedly: the writer doubts the stated purpose · Needless to say: presents the writer's verdict as obvious and pulls the reader along · To be fair: a token concession that makes the criticism look reasonable · tellingly: turns a small fact (the team doesn't use it) into damning evidence · (also though: contrast).

2. Strip test (model): "The office booking system was upgraded to save staff time. The upgrade did not save time. The new calendar view is an improvement. Members of the project team do not use it." Accept versions that drop "did not save time" as an unsupported opinion: that's an even sharper insight.

3. Very little: the only evidence against the upgrade is one unsupported claim and the fact that the project team doesn't use the calendar view. The persuasion lived in the framing.

4. Balanced (model): "The upgrade to the office booking system was intended to save staff time, although there is, as yet, little evidence that it has. The new calendar view is, admittedly, an improvement; that said, members of the project team do not appear to use it themselves."

Dialogue — after the exhibition

Stance: honestly · to be honest (also interactional) · arguably · technically · frankly · to be fair · curiously · tellingly. Interactional: well · the thing is · I mean · mind you · let's face it · (you know what I mean). Theo's final "Tellingly." suggests, without saying so, that the visitors care more about shopping than the art, or that the exhibition itself failed to hold them: a one-word verdict. Good learners notice that an adverbial can stand alone as a reply.

Speaking — Stance Workshop (models for Report 1: Harbor Street Museum)

Neutral facts: six-month trial of free Thursday evenings (5–9 p.m.) · average attendance 180 → 640 · 62% of visitors under 35 · gift-shop spending per visitor down 10%, total Thursday sales up · $22,000 staff overtime · 71% of 300 surveyed would not otherwise have visited · board votes in March.

Skeptical: "For six months, the museum opened its doors free of charge on Thursday evenings. Attendance, predictably, rose, from an average of 180 to 640, and 62 percent of these visitors were under 35. Spending per visitor in the gift shop, tellingly, fell by 10 percent, although total sales rose. The trial cost $22,000 in staff overtime. Seventy-one percent of 300 visitors surveyed said they would not otherwise have come; whether they will ever come back and pay is, needless to say, another question. The board votes in March."

Approving: "For six months, the museum opened free of charge on Thursday evenings, and the response was remarkable: average attendance more than tripled, from 180 to 640. Encouragingly, 62 percent of these visitors were under 35. Total Thursday sales in the gift shop rose, even though spending per visitor fell by 10 percent. The cost, $22,000 in overtime, was modest by any measure. Notably, 71 percent of 300 visitors surveyed said they would not otherwise have visited. The board will vote in March on whether to continue."

Judiciously balanced: "Over a six-month trial of free Thursday evenings, average attendance rose from 180 to 640, and 62 percent of visitors were under 35. Admittedly, spending per visitor in the gift shop fell by 10 percent, and the trial cost $22,000 in staff overtime; total Thursday sales, however, rose. Seventy-one percent of 300 visitors surveyed said they would not otherwise have visited, which suggests, broadly speaking, that the evenings reached a new audience. On balance, the evidence appears to favor continuing, although the board, which votes in March, may reasonably want a longer trial."

Reports 2–4, markers that work well: Open textbooks (costs down $310, pass rates unchanged 78% / 77%, 14% internet problems, 40 hours faculty time each): skeptical ostensibly, predictably, needless to say on "unchanged"; approving remarkably, if anything, mercifully; balanced admittedly, broadly speaking, on balance. Parking app ($140,000; enforcement time down a third; complaints 1,200 → 90; 8% without smartphones visited the office; paper option kept): skeptical supposedly, tellingly on the 8%; approving encouragingly, notably on the fall in complaints; balanced understandably, that said. Concerts (average paid $14 vs $45; capacity 55% → 96%; 30% first-timers; net revenue down 38%; donor covers this season only): skeptical conveniently, for now at least; approving remarkably, strikingly; balanced admittedly, it remains to be seen whether.

Stance Detective: no fixed answers. The detective should quote evidence. Typical markers: grudging admiration (I have to admit, credit where it's due, annoyingly) · polite skepticism (apparently, supposedly, I'm not sure) · wry amusement (ironically, of all things, needless to say) · enthusiastic but fair (remarkably, to be fair, admittedly) · weary disappointment (sadly, predictably, once again) · cautious optimism (hopefully, so far at least, encouragingly).

Self-check
Q1
"Ostensibly, the visit was a courtesy call." What does ostensibly add?
A) It confirms the reason beyond any doubt.
B) It expresses the speaker's relief about the visit.
C) It signals the stated reason, hinting that the true reason is otherwise.
Why: ostensibly presents the declared reason while implying a different, undisclosed one. A describes undoubtedly / clearly; B describes mercifully.
Q2
Which marker belongs to spoken, casual register and would jar in a formal essay?
A) furthermore
B) anyway
C) nevertheless
Why: anyway is a spoken topic-shifter or closer. Furthermore and nevertheless are formal written markers, at home in an essay.
Q3 · new
Which sentence keeps a consistent register for a formal written report?
A) The trial, admittedly, was small; anyway, the results were encouraging.
B) The trial was, admittedly, small; nevertheless, the results were encouraging.
C) The trial was small, you know, but, mind you, the results were encouraging.
Why: B pairs a register-neutral stance adverbial with a formal concessive marker. A's anyway is spoken (and dismissive); C's you know and mind you are interactional markers that belong in conversation.
Errors you will meet most (3.1)

"Actually" for "currently" · "eventually" for "possibly" · "On the other hand" meaning "also" · "In my opinion, I think…" · comma omitted ("Frankly I disagree") · ostensibly used as a fancy apparently · arguably used as "certainly" · marker stacking ("Honestly, frankly, to be fair,…") · anyway / you know / let's face it in written versions · a "balanced" version that reviewers read as skeptical because of one loaded word · changed facts smuggled in as stance ("a mere 40%" when it was 62%). For range, praise medial and final placement, if anything used precisely, be that as it may, and a single final tellingly doing the work of a whole sentence.

3.2 · Cohesion & Coherence Across Long Texts (C8)

Exercise 1 — The lecture opening (Script 3.2-A)
1will argue thatpreview (thesis)
2I'll begin withpreview / sequence
3then turn topreview / sequence
4finally askpreview / sequence
5So far, then,internal summary
6This raises a further questiontransition
7A very different picture emerges whentransition (contrast)

Through-line: the history of the city park is, at heart, a history of public health (and, the lecture hints, of who counted as "the public"). Accept I'll start with for 2 and then move on to for 3 if heard that way. Deletable without loss: arguably none; this is a well-signposted lecture. In writing, 2–4 could be compressed into one sentence.

Exercise 2 — Reorder for coherence

Set A: b → c → a → d. Principle: chronological, reinforced by cause and effect (the gathering → clashing aims → a flawed treaty → a later consequence). Theme / rheme hand-off: Versailles → Each (the powers) → The result (a treaty) → it (the treaty).

Set B: b → d → c → a. Principle: chronological, with specific → general (the tools lent → the scheme's success → its spread). Clues: tools → Drills, ladders and sewing machines; The scheme's success sums up b and d; the idea and Within a year need the scheme to exist already. Accept b → c → d → a if the learner argues that c is the topic sentence of a success paragraph; point out that especially popular then drifts.

Words that make the order inevitable: Each, The result, it, Within two decades; The scheme's, the idea, Within a year. They are reference items and summary nouns, which point back and so cannot come first.

Exercise 3 — Improve the information flow (models)
  1. "The trouble began with a leak in the roof. The leak damaged the archive's oldest manuscripts, and that damage finally prompted the board to order a complete overhaul of the building's drainage."
  2. "In September, the school introduced a new timetable. Its early start provoked complaints from parents, and those complaints led the school to settle on a compromise start time of 8:45."

Check: each sentence opens on the given (The trouble → the leak → that damage; timetable → its early start → those complaints) and closes on the new. Several orderings work; the test is whether the reader is ever asked to hold new information in suspense. Not yet: versions that simply turn every sentence passive without moving the news to the end.

Exercise 4 — Wiring or thought?
  1. A: coherence (cohesive but incoherent). The connectives assert relations that don't exist. Fix: not better connectives but a real line of thought: group the genuinely related facts ("The gallery reopened in March with an exhibition of Dutch masters; tickets are timed.") and cut the café or give it its own purpose.
  2. B: elegant variation (a cohesion fault that damages clarity). Five names for one piece. Fix: "The sonata is demanding. It tests any pianist; indeed, few tackle it in public, and its finale defeats all but the finest. The piece is, quite simply, a peak."
  3. C: cohesion (coherent but under-cohesive). The causal chain is implied but bare. Fix: "When sales fell, three senior staff left, and the manager was replaced; under new leadership, profits recovered." Also accept "coherence" if the learner argues the causal link is unclear; the useful discussion is whether the writer can prove the causes.
  4. D: over-signposting (meta-discourse with no content: in effect, a coherence failure, as nothing is said). Fix: cut every signpost and write a topic sentence with a claim: "Sleep is the cheapest performance enhancer most of us ignore."
Exercise 5 — Build the chain (model)

Repetition: the heat wave · synonym: the hot spell, the extreme heat · superordinate: the weather, the extreme weather event · general word: the crisis, the whole episode · collocations: soaring temperatures, heat stroke, cooling centers, air conditioning, water restrictions, sweltering. Paragraph: "The heat wave arrived in the second week of July. Within days, temperatures had passed 40°C, and the city opened cooling centers in every library. By the time the heat wave broke, the crisis had exposed how few homes in the older neighborhoods had any air conditioning at all." Check for one confident repetition of the anchor and no more than two variations.

Dialogue — the editor's verdict

Coherence: the order (the drought paragraph comes after the critics, though it caused the cost they attack); Ruth's skeleton test exposes it. Cohesion: the elegant-variation muddle in paragraph four ("the plantings," "the woodlands," "the green patches," "the mini-forests"). Ruth's key line: "No amount of polishing fixes a paragraph that's in the wrong place."

Speaking — Cohesion & Coherence Lab

Round 1, the Wrenfield article. Paragraph labels on the cut-up cards are scrambled. The written order is K → R → D → W → M → T → H.

KIntro: the plan and the governing question (how should a city measure the value of a tree?)Opens a text: nothing is given yet; "three years ago"
RThe method (dense planting)The method behind the forests: needs the forests to be introduced
DEarly resultsThe early results: after the method
WThe drought and the unbudgeted watering contractThese gains, however, sums up D; second summer
MThe critics' cost argumentIt is this unplanned expense (summary noun + it-cleft) picks up W's rheme
TSupporters' reply and the ecologist's caveatthe comparison is unfair answers M's cost-per-tree comparison
HVerdict: a lesson in bookkeepingthen (concluding) · these two ledgers returns to K's question

Ordering principle: general → specific framing, then chronological (plan → method → results → setback), then problem → debate (claim → counterclaim → qualification) → verdict. Accept "problem → solution" or "claim → evidence" if well argued. Lexical chain: pocket forests → the forests → the plantings → the saplings → a small ecosystem → woodland. Accept R and D swapped only if the group argues it convincingly; point out that "The early results" presupposes a method already described.

Round 2, the Riverside Radio draft: the six faults

  1. Sentence 1, over-signposting ("In this section, I will now begin by turning to the question of what this report will discuss…"): empty meta-discourse. Coherence (it says nothing) / meta-discourse. Fix: replace with a topic sentence.
  2. Sentences 2–3, irrelevant detail (the 1902 building and its copper roof): a relevance failure. Coherence. Fix: cut.
  3. No topic sentence; the main point is buried (the podcast is outperforming FM, but this emerges only in sentence 7). Coherence. Fix: open with the claim.
  4. Sentence 6, false connective ("Consequently, the station's volunteers are mostly retired teachers"): nothing causes this. Coherence disguised as cohesion. Fix: remove the link; move the fact to where it explains the training request, or cut it.
  5. Sentence 8, elegant variation ("The broadcaster's board… the outlet… the channel's… the organization"): Cohesion. Fix: "the station" and it / its.
  6. Sentences 9–10, new information in the theme slot and ambiguous This ("Training for volunteers in audio editing was requested by several of them. This is a concern…"): Cohesion (flow and reference). Fix: "Several volunteers have asked for training in audio editing" and a summary noun that names the right antecedent ("This worry about FM listeners…").

Model repair (about 120 words): "Riverside Community Radio's move online is succeeding, but it raises a question about the station's FM listeners. Over the past five years, the FM audience has fallen by 40%. In January, the station launched podcast versions of its three most popular programs, and in the first quarter these drew 12,000 downloads, more than the FM audience for the same shows. The board has welcomed the results and plans to add two more programs. Some of the station's older volunteers, however, worry that it is abandoning its FM listeners, and several have asked for training in audio editing so that they can take part in the new format. The board will need to address both this worry and the training request."

Skeleton of the model: The move online is working but raises a question. · FM has declined. · The podcasts outperform FM. · The board will expand. · Volunteers worry and want training. · The board must respond. Reads as one argument.

Self-check
Q1
A report is full of connectives, yet the reader can't say what it argued. What is most likely missing?
A) Coherence
B) Cohesion
C) Reference
Why: the surface links (cohesion) are all present; the line of thought is not. The fix is rethinking order and relevance, not adding connectives. Reference is a kind of cohesion.
Q2
Which pair shows the best information flow?
A) … Alarm among her own board was the immediate reaction.
B) The director unveiled a radical proposal. This proposal alarmed her own board.
C) … Board meetings are on Wednesdays.
Why: sentence 1 ends on its news (a radical proposal) and sentence 2 opens on it. A puts brand-new information in the theme slot; C has no link at all.
Q3 · new
A five-paragraph skeleton reads: planted forests · they grew fast · critics object to cost · they grew fast and attracted insects · the council is pleased. What does it reveal?
A) A cohesion problem: add connectives.
B) A coherence problem: a step is repeated, the cause of the extra cost is missing, and the critics get no reply. Reorder and rebuild.
C) Nothing: every sentence is accurate.
Why: the skeleton stutters (paragraphs 2 and 4 repeat), a necessary step is missing, and an objection is raised and dropped. Connectives (A) would only disguise the fault; accuracy (C) is not coherence.
Errors you will meet most (3.2)

Polishing sentences when the order is the problem · false connectives (Consequently, Similarly) where no relation exists · synonym fever (five names for one thing) · the main point in the last sentence of the paragraph · "As for X, it…" opening every paragraph · paragraph-long sentences joined by commas · "Firstly… Secondly…" in a two-point text · a summary noun that doesn't match (This increase for a fall; This problem for a success) · this / it with two possible antecedents · reading aloud with no pitch reset, so paragraphs blur. For range, praise an it-cleft used to hand on the news (It is this unplanned expense that…), a confident repetition of the anchor term and a signpost-free transition that simply picks up the last paragraph's rheme.

3.3 · Summarizing, Paraphrasing & Synthesis (C9)

Exercise 1 — The radio panel (Script 3.3-A)
Priya RamanarguesClosing Main Street to cars on weekends would bring in more shoppers and cut pollution.
Tom Beckerconcedes · insists · wantsThe street is badly congested on Saturdays, but businesses like his need delivery access; he wants a three-month trial before anything permanent.
Dr. Leequestions · points outThe city's footfall figures are unreliable as evidence because they were collected on a single sunny weekend.

Agreement: the street is overcrowded on weekends and something has to change. Divergence: how confident we can be about the benefits and how quickly to act. Discussion point: the host's questions is fair to Dr. Lee; claims or alleges would have implied the host doubted her.

Exercise 2 — One-sentence gist (models)
  1. "Although trustees defend the museum's new admission charge as an unavoidable response to falling subsidy, critics argue that a publicly owned collection should stay free and that the fee will deter the low-income visitors it aims to reach."
  2. "Although community gardens appear to make neighborhoods feel safer and more connected, a three-year study suggests that these benefits fade unless someone is paid to coordinate the volunteers."

Check: the logical relation survives (although; however → yet); examples and numbers are compressed; the source's certainty is kept (appear, suggests, not prove). Not yet: "The museum now charges admission" (true to fact, false to the argument); "Community gardens don't work" (over-generalized); any version that adds a verdict ("…which is unfair").

Exercise 3 — Paraphrase or patchwrite?
  1. A: PW. The skeleton is untouched; each word is swapped (reluctance → unwillingness, inspect → examine, dangerous → hazardous, flourish → thrive, unchecked → unrestrained).
  2. B: P. The nominal "reluctance to inspect" becomes a clause, the subject changes to managers, and a causal frame replaces "allowed."
  3. C: PW. Same structure and order as Hsu's sentence, with brief for short and figures for data; "from their own records" is copied outright. Also unattributed.
  4. D: P. Attributed, rebuilt around "the evidence," with a fair reporting verb.
  5. E: D (distortion). Hsu does not say libraries lose books; he says the evidence of stable returns is limited. Shows also wrongly implies the writer endorses a claim Hsu never made.

Model repairs: A → "Left unchecked by hesitant managers, the supply chain has seen its most dangerous habits take hold." · C → "According to Hsu, the evidence that returns hold steady without fines comes mainly from the libraries themselves and covers only a short time."

Exercise 4 — Choose the reporting verb
  1. notes / observes / reports / found (past, for the research act)
  2. concedes / acknowledges / admits / grants
  3. argues / maintains / insists / contends
  4. points out / shows / demonstrates
  5. disputes / challenges / rejects / questions
  6. claims / alleges / asserts
  7. describes / characterizes / presents / portrays
  8. extends / builds on / develops / reinforces

Watch the patterns: describe / characterize + noun + as; dispute / challenge + noun (no that); point out is fine after As Nwosu… (not As Nwosu argues, which is possible but weaker). Wrong: "discusses about," "mentions about," "emphasizes on," "explains that to us" in 1.

Exercise 5 — Synthesize two statements (models)
  1. "Whereas Voss defends standardized testing as the one objective yardstick of genuine learning, Ahmed counters that such tests reveal little more than a family's wealth and a talent for exam technique." Also: "Voss's faith in testing as an impartial measure is directly challenged by Ahmed, who contends that scores track income rather than learning."
  2. "Whereas Kim credits open-plan offices with sparking the casual exchanges from which ideas emerge, Patel counters that they chiefly breed noise and interruption, driving colleagues to email one another instead of talking."

Judge by three things: each view attributed to the right person, the opposition made explicit by the connector, neither view distorted. Reporting verbs must be fair to both; no scornful claims on the side the learner dislikes.

Dialogue — a list or a synthesis?

Organization: Luis's draft is source-by-source ("Source A says… Source B says…"); Nadia asks him to organize by idea, starting with agreement. Fidelity: "Hsu claims the data are unreliable": the verb is loaded, and "unreliable" is Luis's word, not Hsu's (Hsu says the data come from the libraries' own records over short periods). Also note Luis's own catch: "not much money" overstates the agreement; Hsu says "modest but real."

Speaking — Synthesis Workshop (model, 200 words)

"The debate over fine-free libraries turns less on money than on whom the library is for. All three writers accept that fines raise relatively little: Ortega, director of the Brookhaven library, reports that collecting them cost almost as much as they earned, and even Hsu, a county budget analyst, calls the $180,000 they bring in 'modest but real.' Nor does anyone dispute that returns have held steady where fines were dropped. Where the writers diverge is on how much weight that evidence can bear, and on whether the lost revenue matters. Ortega points to her library's rising return rate and 3,000 returning borrowers as proof that fines mainly deterred families in need; Hsu counters that such figures are gathered by the libraries themselves, and only briefly, and urges an independently measured pilot first. Nwosu's interviews with low-income parents support Ortega's central point, suggesting that the dread of a penalty can deter families altogether, but they also qualify it: fines, she finds, are just one obstacle, alongside transport, opening hours and staff attitudes. Taken together, the three sources suggest that abolishing fines is likely to widen access, but that it is neither cost-free nor, on its own, sufficient."

Moves: governing sentence (not a list) → agreement (with a careful qualification from Hsu, quoted) → divergence on evidence → Nwosu as both support and qualification → a close that holds the views in tension. Whose voice? 1 writer · 2 all (A, B) · 3 all · 4 writer · 5 A, then B · 6 C · 7 writer. Highlighter test: only "modest but real," correctly quoted. Accept any synthesis of 180–220 words that organizes by idea and passes both tests. Common slips: "Ortega proves…" (too strong), "Hsu claims…" (loaded), "Nwosu says fines don't matter" (distortion: she says they are one barrier among several).

Paraphrase integrity strips (materials sheet, C9 early finishers)
  1. PW: same skeleton as Ortega's sentence, synonyms swapped.
  2. P: rebuilt and attributed.
  3. C: copied almost word for word with no quotation marks (plagiarism, even with the name).
  4. D: Nwosu says removing fines is likely to help but unlikely on its own to close the gap; "will close the gap" distorts her.
  5. Q: an exact quotation, marked and attributed: correct.
  6. NA: a genuine paraphrase with no attribution: the idea is still Hsu's.
Self-check
Q1
"Some critics worry the reform may raise costs" is summarized as "The reform is a costly failure." What has gone wrong?
A) It over-generalizes and distorts the source's cautious claim.
B) It is too short to count as a summary.
C) It fails to reword the original.
Why: some critics became everyone, may became is, a worry became a verdict. Length (B) isn't the problem, and it is reworded (C).
Q2
Which is a genuine paraphrase of "The scheme's rapid rollout caused widespread confusion"?
A) The scheme's fast launch produced broad bewilderment.
B) The rapid rollout of the scheme caused confusion that was widespread.
C) Because the scheme was introduced so quickly, many people were left unsure how it worked.
Why: C changes the subject, turns the noun rollout into a clause and rebuilds the sentence. A is patchwriting (synonym swaps); B is a near-copy.
Q3 · new
Which reports Dr. Okafor's finding neutrally?
A) Okafor alleges that the new schedule reduced absences.
B) Okafor notes that the new schedule reduced absences.
C) Okafor would have us believe that the new schedule reduced absences.
Why: notes reports without judging. Alleges and would have us believe both signal the writer's doubt, which must be earned.
Errors you will meet most (3.3)

Patchwriting presented as paraphrase, especially by strong writers who have always "reworded" this way · citation used as a license to copy · "According to X, she argues that…" · "discuss about / mention about / emphasize on" · backshifting what a text says ("Ortega argued") · claims as the default reporting verb · summaries that editorialize ("…which is clearly unfair") · hedges hardened (may → will) or bold claims softened · source-by-source syntheses · a governing sentence that is really a list ("This paragraph discusses three views on fines") · no divergence named, so the synthesis reads as agreement. For range, praise Y qualifies X's claim, for all their differences, a well-chosen concedes, a short exact quotation used where the wording itself matters, and a close that holds the sources in tension.
