Mastery English Workbook · Module 3

Discourse Mastery: Structure & Cohesion

Three lessons that move from the sentence to the whole text. Steer a stretch of discourse with the small words that organize it and color it with attitude, and learn to hear the same words working on you. Engineer long texts that a reader follows without effort. Then give back other people's meaning, as a faithful summary, a genuine paraphrase and a fair synthesis, without distorting a source or borrowing its words.

How this module works

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Read, Listen, Write, Say or Pair work. Most tasks here have several good answers. Judge yours by three questions: Is it faithful to the facts? Does it fit its reader and its register? Does it have the effect you intended?

Every stance you write in class is assigned: writing the skeptical version of a report is practice in a register, not a statement of belief. When a task draws on your own life or writing, you decide what to share. Use your real experience, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good, and you can always say "Pass."

stance = the attitude a speaker or writer takes toward what they say: certainty, evaluation, viewpoint · slant = an attitude built into a text that presents itself as neutral · to editorialize = to insert your own opinion into what should be a report

Grammar box

Organizing markers change nothing in the facts; they tell the reader where we are. Their register is their most visible feature.

FunctionSpoken / casualWritten / formal
OpenRight, so… · Now, the thing is…To begin with · Let us first consider
AddOn top of that · Plus…Furthermore · What is more
ConcedeThat said · Mind you · To be fairNevertheless · Admittedly · Be that as it may
ShiftAnyway · By the way · Speaking of whichTurning to… · As for… · With regard to…
CloseLong story short · So there you have itIn short · All in all · In conclusion

Stance adverbials comment on the whole clause. One word can turn a report into an opinion.

Epistemic (how sure)admittedly · arguably · presumably · apparently · seemingly · undoubtedly · conceivably · ostensibly
Attitude (how I feel)frankly · honestly · regrettably · remarkably · curiously · ironically · mercifully · understandably · tellingly
Viewpoint (from where)personally · technically · theoretically · broadly speaking · to my knowledge · reportedly
Focus (what, exactly)notably · specifically · at least · if anything · for that matter
FrontFrankly, the scheme was doomed. (the comma marks a comment on the whole clause)
MedialThe result, remarkably, held. (an aside, said on a lower pitch)
FinalHe said nothing, tellingly. (a quiet verdict)
Not stanceShe spoke frankly. (manner: in a frank way)

Use them sparingly. Weak: Frankly, honestly, to be fair, the plan arguably failed. Stronger: The plan, arguably, failed. One well-placed adverbial colors a sentence; four cancel one another out.

Architecture — Cohesion & Coherence Across Long Texts

Class C8
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • cohesion
  • coherence
  • theme
  • rheme
  • information flow
  • a lexical chain
  • a superordinate
  • elegant variation
  • meta-discourse
  • a topic sentence
  • a through-line
  • an ordering principle
This raises a further question:So far, then, …Against this backdrop, …A very different picture emerges when…None of this, however, addresses…Having established that…, we can now turn to…as we shall seeAll of which brings us to…

cohesion = the visible links between sentences: reference, connectives, related words · coherence = whether the text makes sense as one connected line of thought · theme / rheme = the starting point of a clause (usually given) / the rest of it, which carries the news · elegant variation = renaming the same thing so often that the reader loses track

Grammar box

Two virtues. Ask first: is the wiring missing, or the thought?

Cohesive, not coherentThe committee approved the budget. However, the budget is a document. Moreover, paper comes from trees. (links everywhere; no thought)
Coherent, not cohesiveThe scheme failed. Nobody had been consulted. (sound logic; bare joins) → The scheme failed because nobody had been consulted.

Given before new. Start each sentence on familiar ground; end on the news, then hand the news forward.

Linear ("staircase")The lecture introduced a new theory. This theory challenges the standard model. The standard model had gone unquestioned for decades.
Constant themeThe novel divided critics. It sold well but baffled some. Its admirers hailed it as a landmark.
Tools to move news to the enda summary noun (This decline…) · the passive · there is · an it-cleft (It is this single thread, trust, that…)

Lexical chains. Repetition · synonym · superordinate · general word · collocation: the drought → the dry spell → the water shortage → the crisis. Repeat the anchor term with confidence. Weak: the novel… the book… the work… the volume… the tome.

Macro-structure. Topic sentence → development → link. Ordering principles: chronological · general → specific · specific → general · problem → solution · claim → evidence · compare / contrast. Signpost the turns, not every step. The skeleton test: reduce each paragraph to one sentence and read the list. Does it make an argument?

Read and say — Dialogue: the editor's verdict

Ruth: I've read your piece on the pocket forests. The sentences are lovely.

Sam: I sense a "but" coming.

Ruth: You sense correctly. Try the skeleton test: one sentence per paragraph, out loud.

Sam: Fine. The city planted forests. They grew fast. Critics object to the cost. The summer was dry. Supporters reply…

Ruth: Hear that? The drought arrives after the critics, but the drought is what caused the cost they're attacking.

Sam: So it's the order, not the wording.

Ruth: Exactly. No amount of polishing fixes a paragraph that's in the wrong place. And one more thing: in paragraph four you call them "the plantings," "the woodlands," "the green patches" and "the mini-forests."

Sam: I was avoiding repetition.

Ruth: You were avoiding clarity. Pick "pocket forests" and let "they" do the rest.

Which of Sam's problems is cohesion and which is coherence? Practice the dialogue, starting Ruth's key sentences on a higher pitch.

Distilling Other Voices — Summarizing, Paraphrasing & Synthesis

Class C9
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • a précis
  • the gist
  • a paraphrase
  • patchwriting
  • a synthesis
  • attribution
  • a reporting verb
  • fidelity
  • proportion
  • to distort
  • to over-generalize
  • to concede
In essence, …Boiled down to a sentence, …As X points out, …X concedes that…X counters that…Both X and Y concur that…Whereas X…, Y…Y qualifies X's claim, noting…Taken together, …A more nuanced picture emerges when…

patchwriting = keeping a source's sentence structure and swapping in synonyms; it is treated as plagiarism, even with a citation · fidelity = faithfulness to what the source actually says, including how certain it is · proportion = giving a minor point minor space

Grammar box
Summarizesame meaning, far fewer words: delete · generalize · supply a topic sentence · keep the logical relations (although, but, because)
Paraphrasesame meaning, similar length, new structure: change word class · voice · clause order · subject · then, last, words
Synthesizeseveral sources, one account organized by idea: agree · diverge · qualify · extend

Rebuild, don't re-skin. Original: The rapid expansion of social media has fundamentally altered the way young people form their political opinions.

PatchwritingThe quick growth of social media has basically changed the way young individuals develop their political views. (same skeleton, new synonyms)
Genuine paraphraseWhere young people once inherited their politics from family and community, many now form those views through the fast-growing world of social media.

Reporting verbs carry stance.

Neutralstate · note · observe · remark · describe
Committed sourceargue · contend · maintain · insist · hold
You lean toward itpoint out · show · demonstrate · establish
Reluctant admissionconcede · acknowledge · admit · grant
Oppositioncounter · dispute · challenge · reject · refute
You doubt itclaim · allege · purport · would have us believe

Use the present tense for what a text says (Hsu argues) and one attribution frame per claim. Weak: According to Hsu, he argues that…
