Module 1 — The Mot Juste: Answers

Answers to every Module 1 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the tuned texts, the five voices, the audits), and the errors and near-misses you can expect from C2 learners.

Lessons 1.1–1.3Classes C1–C39 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, naturalness, appropriacy and effect, not just accuracy. Most items in this module have several defensible answers, so the models below are examples. Accept any answer that (1) keeps the meaning and every fact, (2) is what a proficient user would actually say (the right partner word, the fixed order, no prosody clash), and (3) suits the reader and the room. When a learner defends an unexpected choice convincingly ("I chose curt because she's cross but controlled"), accept it: the ability to justify a choice is the skill. Where connotation labels are genuinely borderline (forceful, old-fashioned, anxious), mark the argument, not the label. American English is the model; accept British spellings and vocabulary (colour, utilise, judgement, touch wood).

1.1 · Fine Shades of Meaning — The Mot Juste (C1)

First-day writing sample

No key: score it with the four-row rubric (precision, range, naturalness, register & tone control) in the classroom materials, Set B. Never return a score to the learner; return a short, private note with one strength and one focus, quoting their own words.

Spelling to watch: meticulous, inquisitive, peculiar, obstinate. Accept economical for 1 if the learner heard "the most economical supplier" as the key word, but point to frugal. Most damaging: any answer with a reason. Nosy and obstinate are the usual picks.

Exercise 2 — Connotation sort
  1. inquisitive + (or 0) · curious 0 · nosy − · prying −
  2. assertive + · forceful 0 (or ±: praise in a debate, criticism in a meeting) · pushy − · domineering −
  3. traditional 0 · old-fashioned − (or 0: "old-fashioned courtesy" is praise) · dated − · classic +
  4. youthful + · childlike + (innocence, wonder) · childish − · immature −

Accept any borderline label with a convincing example. The teaching point: connotation often shifts with the collocate (old-fashioned values vs old-fashioned software).

Exercise 3 — Up the cline
  1. irritated → annoyed → furious → livid (circle livid: informal and emotive for a report)
  2. tepid → warm → hot → scorching
  3. am not keen on → dislike → detest / loathe (the last two are close; accept either order) (circle am not keen on: conversational, understated)
  4. decent → good → excellent → superb → sublime (sublime is rare in reports; accept a circle)

Wrong: putting annoyed below irritated is a common near-miss; they are very close, so accept it if the learner can argue it. Putting tepid above warm is a genuine error: tepid is lukewarm and, figuratively, unenthusiastic (a tepid response).

Exercise 4 — One exact word (models)
  1. muttered / grumbled
  2. chided / sighed / remarked
  3. exclaimed / cheered / whooped
  4. trudged / plodded
  5. glanced
  6. wolfed down / devoured
  7. scrutinized / pored over
  8. strolled / ambled / sauntered

Judge by manner and register. Near-misses: "whispered" for 1 (quiet, but not resentful), "stared" for 5 (long, not quick), "marched" for 4 (energetic, the opposite). Sauntered in 8 adds a hint of confidence or showing off; accept it and ask what it adds. In 6, "devoured the whole pizza" needs the word order changed: The children devoured the whole pizza.

Exercise 5 — Spot the prosody clash
  1. caused → brought about / secured / led to a landmark victory
  2. set in → settled over / descended on the valley (set in favors unpleasant, gradual states)
  3. a breeding ground for → a catalyst for / fostered / gave rise to (though gave rise to also leans slightly negative; sparked is ideal)
  4. rife with → teeming with / buzzing with / full of cheerful helpers
  5. a spate of → a string of / a run of awards

None of the originals is ungrammatical, and that is the point. If a learner argues that 3 or 5 is deliberately ironic ("a spate of awards" as a joke about too many), accept it as a stretch answer: prosody clash can be a stylistic device, provided it is chosen.

Dialogue — tuning one sentence

Omar's changes: very good → attentive, unhurried service (specificity, force) · cheap → reasonably priced or a bargain (connotation: cheap can imply poor quality) · sublime, magnificent and transcendent → sublime alone (restraint: overwriting weakens force). Note Omar's own utterly forgettable: utterly with its typical negative company, used exactly right. The test at the end, "Does the word serve, or does it show off?", is the workshop's peer-review question.

Speaking — Mot Juste Editing Workshop

Models for all four texts, with the gains labeled by dimension, are printed on the text cards (classroom materials, Set D). Check the log: four changes, each with a named dimension and a reason that goes beyond "sounds better." Fail a version that invents facts (numbers, names, events) or that reads as a thesaurus exercise. Strong log entry: "cheap → unpretentious: connotation; cheap suggests poor quality, unpretentious praises simplicity." Weak log entry: "nice → magnificent: more advanced word."

Self-check
Q1
Which word means "puzzled, bewildered" rather than "amused"?
A) astonished
B) bemused
C) startled
Why: bemused means bewildered; the confusion with amused is common even among native speakers. Astonished = very surprised; startled = suddenly, briefly alarmed.
Q2
Semantic prosody explains why which sentence sounds subtly wrong?
A) A deep contentment set in.
B) A cold, damp winter set in.
C) Rot had set in.
Why: set in habitually introduces unpleasant, gradual states (rot, despair, bad weather), so pairing it with contentment clashes. B and C are exactly the company it keeps.
Q3 · new
"The chair thanked the speaker in fulsome terms." In its careful sense, the praise was…
A) generous and entirely sincere
B) excessive, perhaps insincere
C) brief and formal
Why: traditionally, fulsome praise is overdone to the point of insincerity. Many speakers now use it to mean "generous," which is why careful writers avoid it when the sense matters. At C2, knowing both, and knowing the risk, is the point.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.1)

Prosody clashes, especially cause + a positive result ("caused a big success") · connotation false friends ("an aggressive, ambitious marketing" meant as neutral; "propaganda for the new product") · bemused for amused · thesaurus inflation (utilize, commence, endeavor, purchase in friendly writing) · overwriting: three superlatives where one would do · weak word + modifier ("very big," "walked slowly") · fossilized accuracy errors that survive to C2 (informations, advices, I am agree, since three years, discuss about): note them privately; don't let them crowd out the lesson's focus. For range, praise understatement (It isn't cheap) and the exact verb. For effect, praise any learner who changed a verdict with a single word.

1.2 · Register Agility & Tone (C2)

Exercise 1 — The architect's call (Script 1.2-A)
1234
c · you'll get the permita · as long as the engineer says the building is safed · you can open once you've got the certificateb · anything they ask us to fix

The signal: Joe's request for plain English. The dimension that changed is tenor (Nadia re-keys for a client rather than a fellow professional); the field stays the same, and the mode (a phone call) stays the same. Note that Nadia will still email "the formal version": the written record keeps its register.

Exercise 2 — Label the register and the tone (models)
  1. Formal / frozen · cool-neutral, business-polite · conditional inversion (Should you require), Latinate assistance, the undersigned, no contractions
  2. Informal · warm but rueful · Right, so, long story short, fell through, ellipsis, honestly as a tag
  3. Neutral · ironic (sarcastic) · flawless in loaded emphasis; "What could possibly go wrong" with no question mark, said flat
  4. Formal–neutral · deferential, tentative · I wonder if, might, the formal our looking (possessive + -ing), a question for a proposal

Accept any accurate level with a supporting marker. Judge item 3 by effect: learners who label it "positive" have missed the tone, which is exactly what Module 2 will train.

Exercise 3 — Slide it up and down the cline (models)

(a) Frozen: "Abstracts must be submitted no later than 5:00 p.m. on Friday. Submissions received after that time cannot be considered under any circumstances." (Passive, impersonal, no contractions, precise time, under any circumstances.) Alternative: "Notice is hereby given that the deadline for the submission of abstracts is Friday; late submissions will not be accepted."

(b) Informal: "Don't forget your abstract's due Friday! They're really strict; if it's late, it's out." (Contractions, exclamation, it's out, direct address.)

Check: does every marker agree? A frozen version with "we can't" or an informal one with "submissions" has half-shifted.

Exercise 4 — Find and fix the clang (models)
  1. you absolute legend clashes with the formal congratulation → "…after thirty years of distinguished service. Your colleagues will miss you greatly." (Or make the whole message informal: "Congratulations on thirty amazing years. You're a legend!")
  2. Attached please find… for your perusal sits between Hey and See ya → "Hi Sam! I've attached the updated files. Have a look and let me know what you think. Thanks!"
  3. no clash of level, but pompous → "The committee will use the feedback to start a review."
  4. Kindly be advised is frozen; the pizza's here, guys is intimate → "Pizza's here, everyone!"
  5. Hope you're good! Quick q… asap? Thx clashes with Dear Dr. Mensah (a student to a professor: tenor) → "Dear Dr. Mensah, I hope you are well. I have a question about my grade for the final essay. Would you be able to look at it when you have a moment? Thank you."

The principle: the fix is rarely to raise everything; it is to make the whole message agree with its reader. In 5, going down is not an option: the tenor decides.

Exercise 5 — Turn the tone dial (models)
  1. Warm: "Thank you for raising this; it's really helpful. We'll look into it straight away and keep you posted."
  2. Cool: "Received. Thank you." / "Thank you for the prompt submission."
  3. Deferential: "Would it be possible to have the final figures by noon? I know it's a tight turnaround." / "I wonder if you might be able to send the final figures by noon."

Check that the formality level hasn't moved: a warm version of 1 that says "No worries, will sort it!" has changed register too. For 2, a "cool" version that is rude ("Whatever.") has overshot: cool means distant, not hostile.

Dialogue — too stiff, too loose

The clang: a total bummer in a press release. Same formality, different tone: "The Riverside Bakery will close permanently on March 28, following the retirement of its founder" (formal, cool) → the same sentence with Rosa's quotation and "after forty-two years of serving the neighborhood" (formal, warm). The social post is deliberately informal; "Notice is hereby given" would only belong there as irony.

Speaking — Five Voices

A full model of Message 1 (the bakery) in all five voices is printed in the classroom materials (Set E), along with the voice specification sheet. For each voice, check: the facts match the card; every marker agrees with the voice; the justification names field, tenor and mode correctly; the delivery matches (the online project's rule: a perfect informal sentence read in a stiff cadence still fails). The tribute must celebrate the retirement or the closing. Typical clangs: a contraction in the notice ("won't"); "we're thrilled to announce" in the notice; "notwithstanding" or a subordinate clause in the post; "Hey guys" in the press release; "commence" or a joke at someone's expense in the tribute.

Self-check
Q1
Which is a marker of high, formal register?
A) Phrasal verbs
B) Discourse fillers
C) Nominalization
Why: turning actions into nouns (the implementation of the policy) is typical of formal writing. Phrasal verbs (sort out) and fillers (you know) mark informal speech.
Q2
Which sentence contains a register mismatch?
A) Please find attached the requested documents.
B) Here are those pics you wanted!
C) Please find attached the aforementioned pics.
Why: a legalistic frame (Please find attached the aforementioned) with a slang noun (pics). A is consistently formal, B consistently informal; both are fine in the right room.
Q3 · new
Which reply is professional in register and warm in tone?
A) Noted. This will be addressed in due course.
B) Thank you so much for letting us know; we'll have this resolved by Friday and will update you the moment it's done.
C) Cheers, will sort it.
Why: B is workplace-appropriate and warm (thanks, we, a specific commitment). A is professional but cool; C is warm but informal. Tone and formality are separate dials.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.2)

Half-shifting: raising the vocabulary but leaving casual grammar, or vice versa ("Notice is hereby given that we're closing, so don't forget!") · over-formality as the default for any writing (utilize, commence, in the event that, kindly) · translated letter formulas (Hoping for your kind reply, Please revert) · formal = cold: stripping every human marker from a formal message · dated or regional slang that doesn't fit the room (cheers, mate, chuffed in an American office) · irony missed or taken literally · tweets that are too long or stuffed with hashtags · tributes that tip into pomposity or into jokes. For range, praise a learner who re-keys mid-performance. For effect, praise a tribute line that moved the room and name its device.

1.3 · Fixed Expressions, Binomials & Advanced Collocation (C3)

Spelling to watch: tenterhooks (not tender hooks), foregone (forgone is an accepted variant spelling; accept it). Is it overstuffed? Not really: twelve chunks spread over six turns, all in a register that fits two friends, and never two clichés in one sentence. Compare Aiko's first toast in the workbook dialogue.

Exercise 2 — Complete the chunk
  1. parcel
  2. large
  3. later
  4. sinker
  5. void
  6. quiet
  7. tear
  8. cons
  9. ends
  10. stock

The order is fixed throughout: "large and by," "later or sooner," "cons and pros" are impossible. Spelling: tear here rhymes with wear (/ter/), not with fear.

Exercise 3 — Choose the strong collocate
  1. a foregone conclusion
  2. bitterly disappointed
  3. drives a hard bargain
  4. a moot point (mute is a classic garble)
  5. a resounding success
  6. few and far between
  7. a vested interest
  8. a burning ambition

In each, the alternative is logical but not idiomatic: the collocation is restricted. Note on 4: in American English moot usually means "no longer relevant"; in British English it more often means "open to debate." Both senses fit the sentence.

Exercise 4 — Fix the garble
  1. For all intents and purposes, the project is finished.
  2. It's a dog-eat-dog world out there.
  3. We need to nip this problem in the bud.
  4. That documentary really piqued my interest.
  5. After the drought, the fields were as dry as a bone.
  6. We waited on tenterhooks for the decision.
  7. You should have told me earlier. (should've in speech)
  8. They made him the scapegoat for the whole delay.

Each correction is the only fixed form; the garbles are fluent and phonetically close, which is exactly why they are so revealing. Accept bone-dry for 5.

Dialogue — the farewell toast

Aiko's first version has six fixed expressions in one sentence: by and large, through thick and thin, sooner or later, give and take, at the end of the day, part and parcel. The final version keeps two, in separate sentences: part and parcel (true and apt for a school) and through thick and thin (saved for the end, where it can land). "Raise your glasses" is a fixed formula of toasts: exactly right in that room.

Speaking — Naturalness Audit and Odds and Ends

Keys to all four audit texts (7 problems each, with the correct chunks that should not be flagged) are printed on the teacher copies in the classroom materials, Set F. A complete audit finds all seven, labels them correctly (accept PARTNER / GARBLE overlap where a garble is also a wrong word, e.g. few and rare), and fixes them without introducing new problems. The Seams test passes if listeners can't point to a chunk that sounds bolted on. For the game, the cheat sheet (Set G) gives the order, meaning and a natural sample sentence for every binomial.

Self-check
Q1
Which is the correct, fixed order?
A) peace and quiet
B) quiet and peace
C) peace or quiet
Why: the binomial is fixed as peace and quiet; the reverse never occurs, and the conventional pair uses and.
Q2
Which word completes the restricted collocation "a ______ conclusion" (an outcome certain in advance)?
A) predetermined
B) foregone
C) inevitable
Why: a foregone conclusion is the fixed phrase. Predetermined and inevitable are logical but not the idiomatic partner: the collocation is restricted.
Q3 · new
Which sentence contains a garbled fixed expression?
A) It was touch and go for a while.
B) Once the contract expired, the old agreement was null and void.
C) Her question really peaked my interest.
Why: the verb is pique (to arouse): piqued my interest. Peaked sounds identical, which is why the garble is so common, even in published writing. A and B are correct binomials.
Errors and near-misses you will meet most (1.3)

Reversed binomials, especially where the learner's language has the opposite order ("later or sooner," "forth and back") · logical-but-wrong partners ("a big / loud success," "harshly disappointed," "make a hard bargain," "strong rain") · garbles (mute point, peaked my interest, for all intensive purposes, should of) · full-form AND with equal stress, which makes a chunk sound assembled · fixed similes and clichés in formal writing · overstuffing, typically in speeches and toasts · over-flagging in the audit: marking correct chunks as errors because they are unfamiliar. For range, praise the learner who uses one precise chunk exactly where it belongs. For effect, praise any deliberate, witty bending of a chunk (Stretch).
