Module 1 — The Mot Juste: Precision & Nuance

Three classes that open the C2 level. Your learners already have a vast vocabulary and control of register; the C2 frontier is discrimination. First, the single word: denotation and connotation (frugal vs stingy), the myth of the synonym (surprised · astonished · taken aback · bemused), semantic prosody (why rot sets in but happiness doesn't) and the specificity dividend (walked slowly and wearily → trudged). Then the whole manner: register agility across field, tenor and mode, with tone as a second, independent dial. Last, the prefabricated layer: binomials in their fixed order (by and large, odds and ends), restricted collocations (a foregone conclusion, bitterly disappointed), lexical bundles, fixed similes, and the garbles that betray a speaker (for all intensive purposes).

3 lessons Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C1 (the first class) replaces the warmer with a welcome routine that doubles as a C1 diagnostic.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-1-mot-juste.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the force-cline picture cards and word cards, the welcome question cards, the C1 diagnostic grid and writing-sample rubric, the new identity cards, the Mot Juste Editing Workshop texts, the Five Voices message cards and voice specifications, the Naturalness Audit texts, the Odds and Ends binomial card game and all the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons (with a UK or US voice), so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • These are workshops, not lectures. At C2 nobody needs the concept explained twice; they need to use it under pressure and be held to a high standard. Every class spends roughly a quarter of its time on one extended product: a tuned text with a justification log (C1), one message written in five voices and performed (C2), an audited near-native text plus a binomial card game (C3). Protect that time. If you run late, cut the controlled practice (set it for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not re-teaching. At C1 your learners met connotation and collocation (C1 4.2), register and nominalization (C1 3.1) and softening (C1 3.3). Do not re-teach any of it. The C2 step is deliberate effect: choosing a word, a register or a chunk on purpose, being able to say which dimension you adjusted, and hearing the near-imperceptible clash that separates a very good speaker from an indistinguishable one.
  • Feedback on four things. In every workshop, take notes in four columns: precision (the vague or approximate word: a lot of bad problems, very big), naturalness (grammatical but not what a proficient user would say: caused her success, a loud success, make a hard bargain, quiet and peace), appropriacy (right word, wrong room: utilize in a friendly email, as easy as pie in a report, a stray notwithstanding in a tweet) and effect (does the choice do what the writer wants: flatter, damn, amuse, move?). Keep a small side note for fossils: the one or two persistent accuracy errors (informations, I am agree, since three years) that survive to C2. In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names.
  • Giving C2 learners genuinely useful feedback. C2 learners are often near-native in some areas and fossilized in others, and most have heard "Your English is excellent" so often that it no longer helps them. Be specific and ambitious: quote their exact words, name the dimension ("caused leans negative here, so your success sounds like an accident"), and offer the proficient alternative. Praise precise choices by name, not "good job." For fossils, agree a private target with each learner (one error, one term) and flag it only when it appears, never in front of the class. Many C2 learners are language professionals: invite them to argue with your suggestion. "Convince me" is a legitimate C2 reply to feedback, and defending a choice is itself the skill.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured peer-review test: the "Justify or cut" test (C1: every change must name its dimension, and the skeptic may challenge any change as ornament), the "Clang hunt" (C2: listeners raise a hand at any stray marker that belongs to another register) and the "Seams test" (C3: can you hear where a chunk was bolted on?). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote the text rather than summarize it.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes attract confident professionals (lawyers, academics, translators, managers) who are used to being the best English speaker in the room. Give them the roles that require listening and restraint: in C1 the skeptic (who may only ask "Does this serve or show off?"), in C2 the clang hunter for another group's performance, in C3 the auditor who must justify every flag with the category (order / partner / garble / register / stuffing). Say it kindly: "You've got the sharpest ear in the room; I need it on the checklist this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first."
  • Stretching the strongest learners. Each lesson has a stretch box. The direction for this module is connotation as persuasion and controlled mismatch: writing the same fact to flatter and to damn (C1), deliberate register clash for irony (C2: the mock-legalistic notice about a missing stapler) and knowingly bending a fixed expression for wit, which only works once you own the original (C3). This leads straight into Module 2 (irony, humor and implicature).
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and a good deal of British vocabulary (colour, behaviour, judgement, recognise, utilise, endeavour, humour, nominalisation, finalised; in 1.2 solicitor, vendor, completion, bloke, chuffed, knackered, gutted, soz, cheers, mate, give me a shout; in 1.3 touch wood). Teach color, behavior, judgment, recognize, utilize, endeavor, lawyer / attorney, seller, closing, knock on wood. At C2, treat the British forms as receptive knowledge worth having (Module 5 covers varieties of English): accept them whenever a learner uses them, but point out that British slang in an American workplace is itself a register choice.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Choose the word → choose the voice → choose the chunk. In C1 learners weigh a single word on every axis at once (denotation, connotation, force, register, specificity, prosody) and tune a vague text until every word earns its place. In C2 they zoom out to the whole manner of a message, sliding one proposition across five registers and learning that tone (warm, cool, ironic, authoritative, deferential) is a separate dial from formality. In C3 they meet the layer below the word: the ready-made chunks that proficient users retrieve whole, in their fixed order and with their fixed partners. Every class produces something another learner reads, hears or judges.

Trauma-informed: tributes, notices and bereavement

Register work easily drifts toward heavy occasions (eviction notices, condolences). For learners who have been displaced, lost housing or recently lost someone, these can land hard. Keep examples light, as the online lesson 1.2 does: the cline example is a community center closing early, and the "eulogy-style tribute" in the Five Voices workshop is always a tribute to a retiring person or a place that is closing (a bakery, a movie theater, a pool), never to someone who has died. Learners never write about their own bereavement, and anyone can take a different message card or say "Pass." If a learner chooses to mention a personal loss, thank them simply and move on; don't build a task on it.

Before the Module

  • Placement. Ideally, learners finished the C1 course or took the toolkit placement test (../../instructor-toolkit/placement-test.html). Either way, the C1 welcome mingle and the 8-minute writing sample double as a C1 diagnostic (see Lesson 1.1). It won't replace a placement test, but it will show you, within the first hour, each learner's profile across precision, range, naturalness and register control, which is exactly what this course trains.
  • Print the welcome question cards (Set B, one set per 16 learners), one copy of the C1 diagnostic grid and writing-sample rubric for you on a clipboard, and name tents (folded paper is fine). The writing-sample page is in the workbook (Lesson 1.1).
  • Print the flashcards (Set A, one set for the board): picture force-cline cards (8), connotation and prosody word cards (C1), register-room picture cards and tone cards (C2), binomial picture cards and strong-collocation cards (C3).
  • For C1: one Mot Juste Editing Workshop text per group of 3 (4 texts; Set D) and one justification log per group (in the workbook).
  • For C2: the Five Voices message cards (6; Set E), one voice specification sheet per group, and a Clang hunt slip per listener.
  • For C3: one Naturalness Audit text per pair (4 texts; Set F) and one set of Odds and Ends cards per group of 3–4 (Set G). Cut the game cards before class and keep each set in an envelope.
  • Print the new identity cards (Set C) and keep them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking.
  • Optional but powerful: bring two real texts that describe the same thing with different connotation (a glowing and a lukewarm review of the same restaurant; a company's press release and a news report on the same announcement). C2 learners enjoy dissecting real language far more than invented examples.

1.1 · C1

Welcome to C2 · Fine Shades of Meaning — The Mot Juste

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • distinguish a word's denotation (dictionary sense) from its connotation (the feeling and value judgment it carries), and choose positive, neutral or negative near-synonyms deliberately (thrifty / careful with money / stingy)
  • order near-synonyms on a cline of force (tepid → warm → hot → scorching) and explain what else separates them: register, collocation, specificity (surprised vs startled vs taken aback)
  • use the careful senses of commonly misused words (bemused = bewildered; fulsome = excessive; enormity = great wickedness)
  • recognize semantic prosody (the good or bad company a word habitually keeps) and repair a prosody clash (caused a victory → brought about / secured)
  • replace a weak word plus a modifier with one specific word (walked slowly and wearily → trudged), and stop before precision turns into ornament

Left: one fact, three judgments. careful with money (0) → frugal / thrifty (+) → stingy / tight-fisted (−). determined → resolute (+) / obstinate, pig-headed (−). confident → self-assured (+) / cocky, smug (−). Denotation = the dictionary · Connotation = the verdict.

Center: no true synonyms. Force: decent → good → excellent → superb → sublime. Register: kids → children → offspring. Specificity: walk → stride · amble · trudge · saunter. Careful senses: bemused = puzzled (not amused) · fulsome = excessive · enormity = wickedness.

Right: the company words keep (prosody). set in: rot, despair, winter (not happiness) · cause: damage, delay, concern (success? → bring about, lead to) · a breeding ground for: crime, resentment · rife with: corruption · a spate of: burglaries. Test: Force? Connotation? Register? Prosody? Specific? Then: does it serve or show off?

If you run long

The welcome mingle is the most important part of C1: the group needs it, and you need the diagnostic. Don't cut it. If time is short, the writing sample becomes homework (same prompts, "no dictionary, 10 minutes, please"), the drill shrinks to "Flatter it, damn it," and the workshop share is dropped. Keep at least 14 minutes for the Mot Juste Editing Workshop.

Running the diagnostic — what to listen for

The mingle is a friendly first conversation, not an exam. Learners must never feel watched. Hold the grid on a clipboard, stand near a pair for 30–60 seconds, and join in sometimes ("What's the difference, for you, between assertive and pushy?"). Mark each learner ✓ (masterful: consistent and effortless) · ~ (strong, with gaps or occasional misfires) · ✗ (avoids it or can't produce it yet) in four columns:

  • PRECISION (LOOK CLOSER cards): Does the learner reach for the exact word (trudged, cluttered, faintly musty) or circle with weak word + modifier (very big, a bit bad, walked slowly)? Do they paraphrase gracefully around a missing word?
  • RANGE (SHADES OF MEANING and TAKE A POSITION cards): Can they explain a connotation difference and illustrate it? Do they use idiom, advanced collocation, cleft and fronted structures, nuanced hedging and concession (granted…, that said…) without effort?
  • NATURALNESS (all cards): Do the chunks sound native-like (by and large, a foregone conclusion, bitterly disappointed) or subtly homemade (a big success, strong rain, make a photo, in the other hand)? Note any fossils (informations, I am agree, since three years, discuss about).
  • REGISTER CONTROL (SAY IT THREE WAYS cards): Does the whole cluster of markers move between the friend, the colleague and the written notice, or only the vocabulary? Can they sound warm and formal? Many C1 graduates are accurate but tonally flat or over-formal: that is exactly what C2 trains.

The writing sample. Score it with the four-row rubric in the materials (Set B): precision, range, naturalness and register & tone control, 0–3 each. As a rough guide: 10–12 = already performing at C2 in writing (give Stretch tasks and ask them to lead peer review); 6–9 = the typical C2 entry profile; 5 or less and mostly ✗ in the speaking grid = check the placement privately (the toolkit's oral interview takes 5 minutes); the learner may be better served by the C1 course. A spiky profile (for example, superb academic precision but an intimate register that sounds like a textbook) is normal at this level; note it and target the weaker dimension.

What to do with the results. Write one short, private, specific note to each learner by C3: one strength and one focus, quoting their own words ("A faintly institutional smell is exactly right. Focus: since three years → for three years, and reserve utilize for technical contexts."). Never share the grid with learners. Keep it; compare it with Progress Test 1 in C13.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: denotation and connotation. Two words can point at the same thing and pass opposite verdicts on it. The choice of word is the message.

"She's frugal." "She's stingy." Is she spending a lot of money in either sentence? (No.) Same behavior? (Yes.) Which speaker likes her? (The first.) So what changed: the facts or the judgment? (The judgment.) · "He's resolute." "He's pig-headed." Would you put either in a job reference? (Resolute, yes; pig-headed, never.)

Meaning: no true synonyms. Near-synonyms almost always differ in at least one dimension: force, register, collocation, specificity or connotation.

"I was startled." Long or short? (Short, sudden.) Physical or mental? (Often physical: a noise.) · "I was taken aback by his tone." Pleasant surprise? (No, usually disconcerting.) · "I was bemused by the instructions." Was I laughing? (No.) Was I confused? (Yes.) · "Absolutely flabbergasted." Would you write it in a report? (No: very strong, informal, faintly comic.)

Meaning: semantic prosody. Some words carry an aura of approval or disapproval simply from the company they keep. Ignoring it produces sentences that are grammatical but subtly foreign.

"Rot had set in." "A deep contentment set in." Which sounds natural? (Rot.) Is the second grammatically wrong? (No.) So why does it grate? (Set in usually introduces something unpleasant and gradual.) · "Years of practice caused her success." What does cause usually go with? (Damage, delay, problems.) Better verb? (brought about, led to, paved the way for.)

Meaning: specificity and restraint. One exact word often beats a weak word plus modifiers, but precision is not ornament.

"She trudged home." Fast or slow? (Slow.) Energetic or tired? (Tired, heavy.) Why is it better than "walked slowly and wearily"? (Shorter, stronger, more exact.) · "I shall endeavor to utilize the facility." Precise or pompous? (Pompous: I'll try to use the gym.) Is the grand word ever the mot juste? (Yes, when the room calls for it.)

Pronunciation. Model the terms learners will use all term: con·no·TA·tion, de·no·TA·tion, PROS·o·dy, SYN·o·nym but sy·NON·y·mous; mot juste is French: /moʊ ˈʒuːst/, "moh ZHOOST." Target words: FRU·gal, a·STON·ished, be·MUSED, FLAB·ber·gast·ed, SUB·tle (silent b), NU·ance. Then the key delivery skill: contrastive stress carries the distinction in speech. "She isn't STINGY; she's FRUGAL." "I was beMUSED, not aMUSED." A flat reading loses the point.

Drill script
  1. Flatter it, damn it (fast, individual): T: "careful with money" → S1: "frugal" → S2: "stingy." T: "determined" → "resolute / obstinate." T: "confident" → "self-assured / cocky." T: "curious" → "inquisitive / nosy." T: "unusual" → "distinctive / peculiar." T: "relaxed about rules" → "easygoing / lax."
  2. Up the cline (choral, then individual): T: "tepid" → S: "warm, hot, scorching." T: "irritated" → "annoyed, furious, livid." T: "decent" → "good, excellent, superb, sublime." Ask: "Which of those would you never write in a report?" (livid, perhaps sublime.)
  3. One exact word: T: "walked slowly and wearily" → S: "trudged." T: "looked quickly" → "glanced." T: "said quietly and unwillingly" → "muttered." T: "ate very fast" → "wolfed down / devoured." T: "very, very big" → "vast / colossal / enormous."
  4. Contrastive stress (choral, then pairs): "She isn't stingy; she's frugal." · "I was bemused, not amused." · "He didn't just walk home; he trudged." Listen for the stress falling on the contrasted word.
Mot Juste Editing Workshop — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 with three roles: the editor (holds the pen), the advocate (proposes substitutions and fills in the log) and the skeptic (may only ask questions: "Is that the same fact? Does it serve or show off? Which dimension?"). Give the skeptic role to a dominant speaker.
  • The texts (Set D) are 80–100 words of deliberately adequate English: correct, clear and lifeless (very good, a lot of, nice, bad, big, things). Each card has a gray model version for you at the bottom: cut or fold it off before class.
  • Tune (10 min): at least 10 substitutions, including at least one gain in force, one in connotation, one in specificity and one in register; remove any prosody clash. Facts unchanged: learners may not invent numbers, names or events. Log four changes in the workbook: original → choice → dimension → why.
  • Justify or cut (6 min): groups swap texts. The reviewing group reads the tuned version aloud. Its skeptic may challenge up to three changes; the authors must name the dimension and defend the choice in one sentence, or cut it. Every group must revert at least one change that turned out to be ornament. Reviewers then give one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text.
  • Share (4 min): two groups read their before and after versions. The class votes for the single best substitution of the day, and you board it.
  • Keep it honest: there is no single right version. The standard is that every change earns its place. A text with twelve grand words and no restraint has failed the task.
ICQs before the workshop
How many changes at least? (Ten.) · Can you add a new fact, like a number or a name? (No.) · Which four dimensions must appear? (Force, connotation, specificity, register.) · What does the skeptic ask? (Does it serve or show off? Which dimension?) · If you can't justify a change, what happens? (We cut it.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 speakers, often those who acquired English by living and working in it, are more precise orally than on the page, or read more slowly than they speak. Give them the force-cline picture cards as anchors and a strip with ten "tuning" words to choose from (sturdy, spacious, dimly lit, modest, sluggish, a handful of, shortly, frequently, heartfelt, remarkable). In the workshop they make an excellent advocate: they propose substitutions orally while the editor writes. Fewer items: Ex 2 rows 1–2, Ex 4 items 1–5. Target: five precise substitutions, justified aloud.

Stretch

Connotation as persuasion. Strong learners write the same 50-word description of a colleague or a city twice, once as a glowing reference and once as a damning one, changing only word choice, never the facts; the partner must find every loaded word. Then ask them to find one real example of semantic prosody the online lesson doesn't list (commit + crime, error, offense; rife with; perpetrate) and check it against a corpus or a search engine's usual pairings. Finally: when is the plainest word the mot juste? (A condolence card, a safety instruction, a child's bedtime.)

L1 watch-outs
  • False friends that differ in connotation, not just meaning: Spanish/Portuguese propaganda (= advertising) vs English propaganda (manipulative); Russian амбициозный (can suggest arrogance) vs English ambitious (usually positive in US workplaces); French prétendre (= claim) vs pretend; German konsequent (= consistent) vs consequent; Spanish sensible (= sensitive). At C2 these survive as connotation errors: "our aggressive colleague" meant as praise, "propaganda for the new product."
  • Prosody transfer: Spanish causar, French causer, Italian causare and Portuguese causar are more neutral than English cause, so "caused a big success" is common. Spanish provocar → "provoked an improvement." Offer brought about, led to, resulted in, gave rise to (the last also leans negative).
  • Thesaurus inflation: learners who built their vocabulary from bilingual dictionaries or exam word lists (common in Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian and Arabic educational traditions) may reach for the rarest synonym: utilize, commence, endeavor, purchase in a friendly email. Praise the range; then ask, "Is it the exact word, or the impressive one?"
  • Careful senses: bemused is confused with amused by native speakers too; eventual (Spanish/French/German eventual, éventuel, eventuell = possible) → "an eventual problem" meaning a possible one; actual/actually (= current/currently in Spanish, French, German, Russian, Italian and Romanian) still appears at C2.
  • Contrastive stress: speakers of syllable-timed and tone languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog) may not shift sentence stress to the contrasted word, so "She isn't stingy, she's frugal" sounds like two unrelated claims. Drill it.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: field, tenor, mode. Register is a mixing desk, not a single knob. The relationship (tenor) is the dimension learners misread most, and it is the one that makes a speaker sound either presumptuous or oddly servile.

"Pass the salt." · "Could you pass the salt?" · "Sorry to bother you, would you mind passing the salt?" Same field? (Yes: dinner.) Same mode? (Yes: spoken.) So what changes? (The relationship: a sibling, a colleague, a stranger.) Is the third one "better English"? (No, just right for a different person.) · "The patient's in AF." Who says this, to whom? (A doctor to another doctor.) To the patient's family? (No: jargon excludes them. "Her heart rhythm is irregular.")

Meaning: the cluster. When register moves, everything moves together: vocabulary, grammar, voice, contractions, length and stance.

"Notice is hereby given that the premises will close at 3 p.m. on Friday. Don't forget!" Is there a problem? (Yes: "Don't forget!" is informal.) Is it a grammar mistake? (No: a register clang.) · "Heads up, we're shutting early Friday. Kindly be advised." Where's the clang? (Kindly be advised.) What's the fix: raise everything or lower one phrase? (Lower one phrase: move the whole message to one level.)

Meaning: tone is a separate dial. Formality sets the ceremony; tone sets the attitude.

"Noted. This will be addressed in due course." Formal? (Yes, fairly.) Warm? (No: cool.) "Thanks so much for flagging this; I'll get it sorted today." Informal? (Neutral, professional.) So can formal be warm? (Yes.) Can informal be cold? (Yes: "Whatever. Fine.") · "Ah yes, another flawless plan." Does the speaker think the plan is flawless? (No: irony.)

Meaning: over-formality. The pompous clang sounds inflated, not educated.

"The committee will utilize the feedback to commence a review." Wrong? (Not wrong: inflated.) Plainer? ("use the feedback to start a review.") Is plain less intelligent? (No: usually more masterful.) When is commence right? (A formal ceremony or a legal document: "Proceedings will commence at ten.")

Pronunciation. Register lives in the voice as much as in the words. Frozen / formal: slower pace, full forms (do not, it is), even rhythm, falling tones, pauses at clause boundaries. Informal / intimate: faster, weak forms and contractions (gonna, wanna, dunno are receptive at C2: understand them; use them only when the tenor invites it), wider pitch range, rising tags ("…yeah?"). Warm vs cool: warmth = wider pitch range and a rise-fall on key words ("Thanks so much"); coolness = narrow range, clipped syllables, a flat fall ("Noted."). Irony: exaggerated stress or a deadpan flat delivery on an over-positive word ("another FLAWless plan"). The online project's key test applies in class: a perfect informal sentence read in a stiff, formal cadence still fails.

Drill script
  1. Slide it (choral, matching the voice): T: "I disagree: frozen." → S: "I must respectfully take issue with the foregoing." T: "Formal." → "I'm afraid I can't agree with that assessment." T: "Neutral." → "I see it a bit differently, actually." T: "Informal." → "Yeah, I'm not so sure about that." T: "Intimate." → "Nah, you're way off there." Repeat with "please wait": "Kindly be advised that a brief delay is anticipated." → "Thank you for your patience; this won't be long." → "Bear with me one moment." → "Hang on a sec." → "Gimme a second."
  2. Jump the ladder (individual): T calls a level at random ("please wait, formal!" "I disagree, informal!"); a learner answers with the right line and the right voice.
  3. Turn the tone dial (individual, same words): "The report is ready." T holds up a tone card: WARM (bright, rising: "The report's ready!") · COOL (flat fall, clipped) · IRONIC ("The report is finally ready.") · AUTHORITATIVE ("The report is ready. We present it on Monday.") · DEFERENTIAL ("I think the report might be ready, if you'd like to take a look?"). The class names one marker each time.
Five Voices — how to run it
  • The message cards (Set E) each give one piece of community news with fixed facts: a bakery closing as its founder retires, a 1931 movie theater closing for restoration, a school librarian retiring after 35 years, a public pool closing for rebuilding, a local newspaper ending its print edition, a community jazz night ending after 25 years. All are deliberately warm, non-political and low-stakes. Every card carries dates, numbers, one quotation and one "what happens next," so that five voices share identical facts.
  • The five voices (specifications on Set E): (1) Official notice: frozen / formal, impersonal, passive, no contractions, dates in full, Notice is hereby given… · (2) Friendly email to a friend who loves the place: informal, warm, contractions, a shared memory, a suggestion · (3) Press release: formal–neutral, third person, headline, city and date, one quotation, "About…" paragraph · (4) Social post: at most 280 characters, informal-public, energetic, one hashtag at most · (5) Tribute: eulogy-style, ceremonial and warm, elevated but never pompous, concrete detail, rhythm (a tricolon, a closing line meant to be spoken).
  • A model, if you need one: Script 1.2-B is one piece of news (the pool) in all five voices. If your group seems unsure what a press release or a tribute sounds like, read it before the Plan stage and take the 2 minutes from the Share stage; in that case, don't hand out Message 4 (the same pool). Otherwise, keep it for the C4 warmer ("Name the voice and one marker").
  • Plan (4 min): the group underlines the facts that must appear in every version. Each learner takes one voice (in groups of 3–4, the strongest writers take two). Draft (10 min): each learner writes their voice on the Five Voices planner, with one line of justification: field, tenor, mode and two markers.
  • Perform + Clang hunt (12 min): groups pair up. Group A reads its five versions in a random order, each in a matching voice (grave for the notice, brisk for the press release, quick and bright for the post, slow and warm for the tribute). Listeners write the voice number on their Clang hunt slip and raise a hand at any stray marker from another register. Then they give one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text. Swap.
  • Share (2 min): one tribute and one social post to the whole class. Ask: "Same facts? Which words carried the tone?"
  • The tribute needs care (see the trauma-informed note at the top of this module): it celebrates a retirement or a closing, never a death. If a learner wishes, any group may swap its card.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Give learners who write slowly the social post or the friendly email (short, spoken-like), with the voice specification as a frame and three starter lines on a strip ("Just heard that… · Can you believe it? · We have to go before…"). They can perform another learner's tribute aloud: delivery is half the task, and many strong speakers are superb readers of an emotive text. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–2, Ex 4 items 1–3. Target: one voice where every marker agrees, delivered with the right tone.

Stretch

Controlled mismatch. Strong learners write a sixth version: a mock-legalistic notice about something trivial ("Notice is hereby given that the stapler on the third floor has been removed from the premises without authorization…"), where the frozen register on a trivial field produces irony. Then ask them to re-key their tribute for two different tenors: spoken by a grandchild of the founder, and spoken by the mayor. What changes? (Pronouns, anecdote, formality of address; the warmth stays.) This leads straight into Module 2 (irony and implicature).

L1 watch-outs
  • Tenor through pronouns: speakers of languages with a T–V distinction (French tu / vous, Spanish tú / usted, German du / Sie, Russian ты / вы) and with honorific systems (Japanese, Korean, Javanese, Thai) are used to marking distance in a pronoun or verb ending. English marks it with modals, indirectness, past-tense distancing and vocabulary. Show the salt ladder: the pronoun you never changes, everything else does.
  • Translated letter formulas: Spanish Sin otro particular, le saluda atentamente, French Veuillez agréer…, Russian bureaucratic style and some South Asian English formulas (Kindly do the needful, Please revert) produce a frozen register where American English expects neutral-professional. Treat these as regional register norms, not errors, but show the American equivalent (Thanks in advance · Please reply by…).
  • Diglossia: Arabic speakers (Modern Standard Arabic vs dialect) and speakers of other diglossic languages may equate "written" with "very high register." English neutral-professional writing is lower than they expect: contractions are acceptable in most workplace emails.
  • Dated or regional slang: slang learned from textbooks, TV or a previous posting (British chuffed, knackered, cheers, mate; dated American groovy, far out) can clang in an American office. Slang is a tenor signal: it claims membership of a group.
  • Directness and tone: German, Dutch, Hebrew and Russian speakers may produce accurate but cool-sounding neutral English; Japanese, Korean, Thai and Indonesian speakers may default to deferential. Neither is wrong. Teach tone as a choice, and let learners practice the dial they use least.
  • Exclamation marks and capitals: Spanish and Portuguese writers may use !!! freely; in an English press release or notice they read as unprofessional, while ALL CAPS in a social post reads as shouting.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: binomials and fixedness. A binomial is a fixed pair joined by and or or, always in the same order. No grammar rule forbids the reversal; convention does, and a proficient ear notices at once.

"By and large, the plan worked." Did it work completely? (No, generally, on the whole.) Can I say "large and by"? (No, never.) · "Late nights are part and parcel of the job." Can you avoid them? (No, they're an unavoidable part of it.) · "It was touch and go." Did it end well? (We don't know; it was uncertain for a while.) · "I cleared out the odds and ends." Important things? (No, small miscellaneous items.)

Meaning: restricted collocation. At the rigid end of the collocation continuum, a word accepts essentially one partner, and a logical synonym still sounds wrong.

"The result was a foregone conclusion." Did anyone know before? (Yes, it was certain in advance.) Can I say "a predetermined conclusion"? (Understandable, but not the fixed phrase.) · "Whether he knew is now a moot point." Important or no longer relevant? (No longer relevant, or debatable.) Spelling? (m-o-o-t; not "mute.") · "She has a vested interest in the project." Neutral observer? (No: she gains if it succeeds.)

Meaning: register and rationing. Every chunk carries a register stamp, and some are worn to cliché.

"The results were as clear as day." Where would you hear it? (Conversation, a lively review.) In a lab report? (No: unambiguous, self-evident.) · "At the end of the day…" three times in one talk. Natural? (No: autopilot.) Once? (Fine.) · "That said, the costs remain high." Formal or informal? (Neutral: safe anywhere.)

Pronunciation. Binomials are said as one tone unit with a weak and (/ən/ or just /n/) and linking: odds_and_ends /ˌɑdzənˈendz/, by 'n' LARGE, PEACE 'n' QUIet, salt 'n' PEPper, fish 'n' CHIPS. Garbles are often pronunciation-driven: moot /muːt/ vs mute /mjuːt/; tenterhooks (TEN-ter-hooks, not "tender"); piqued /piːkt/, which sounds like peaked but isn't spelled like it.

Drill script
  1. Binomial ping-pong (fast, choral then individual, with the rhythm): T: "by and…" → S: "large." T: "part and…" → "parcel." T: "sooner or…" → "later." T: "null and…" → "void." T: "wear and…" → "tear." T: "odds and…" → "ends." T: "lock, stock and…" → "barrel." T: "hook, line and…" → "sinker."
  2. Reverse alarm: T says some binomials in the wrong order ("quiet and peace," "cons and pros," "forth and back," "tired and sick"); learners call "Alarm!" and say the right order. Mix in correct ones so they have to listen.
  3. One partner only: T: "a ___ conclusion (certain in advance)" → S: "a foregone conclusion." T: "___ disappointed (deeply)" → "bitterly." T: "a ___ success (huge, clear)" → "a resounding success." T: "___ a hard bargain" → "drive." T: "a ___ point (no longer relevant)" → "a moot point." T: "a ___ interest (personal stake)" → "a vested interest."
  4. Fix it fast: T: "for all intensive purposes" → S: "for all intents and purposes." T: "nip it in the butt" → "nip it in the bud." T: "on tender hooks" → "on tenterhooks." T: "it peaked my interest" → "it piqued my interest."
Naturalness Audit — how to run it
  • The texts (Set F) are about 130 words each and read like the work of a very proficient non-native writer: fluent, accurate, ambitious, and subtly homemade in exactly seven places. Each hides one or two of every problem type: ORDER (a reversed binomial), PARTNER (a logical but non-idiomatic collocate), GARBLE (an eggcorn), REGISTER (a chunk in the wrong room), STUFFING (too many chunks crowded into one sentence). The key is on the teacher's copy of each card.
  • Pairs with two roles: the reader reads each sentence aloud (many problems are heard before they are seen), the auditor marks and labels. Swap roles halfway. Give the auditor role to a dominant speaker: they must justify every flag with a category.
  • Audit (9 min): find, label and fix on the audit log. Warn learners that the texts also contain many correct chunks; over-flagging correct language is also an error ("Is it wrong, or just not how I'd say it?").
  • Cross-check (6 min): two pairs with the same text compare. Where they disagree, each side argues its case in one sentence; you arbitrate only if they can't agree.
  • Seams test (5 min): one learner per group reads the repaired text aloud; the others listen for a seam, a chunk that sounds placed rather than arisen. If they hear one, the group removes or replaces it.
  • Real-life link: many C2 learners edit colleagues' English at work. Remind them that an audit comment should be specific and kind: "The fixed phrase is a moot point" rather than "wrong word."
ICQs before the audit and the game
How many problems are hidden in your text? (Seven.) · What are the five labels? (Order, partner, garble, register, stuffing.) · Are all the chunks in the text wrong? (No, most are correct.) · In the cross-check, if you disagree, what do you do? (Argue it in one sentence.) · In the game, how do you keep a pair? (Say it in the right order and use it in a sentence.) · Wrong order? (The cards go back.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give learners who read more slowly Audit text C (the restaurant review: shorter sentences, most of its problems audible) and let them take the reader role first: hearing the text aloud is where most garbles surface. Pair picture cards with binomials (bread and butter, salt and pepper, fish and chips) for the game's first round. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–6, Ex 3 items 1–6. Target: find five of the seven problems and fix them correctly.

Stretch

Bending the chunk on purpose. Once you own a fixed expression, you can play with it: headline writers do it constantly (a foregone contusion for a boxing story; lock, stock and two smoking budgets). Ask strong learners to write two "bent" chunks for a headline and explain why the joke only works if the reader knows the original. Then have them write a 150-word toast that uses at least six fixed expressions and passes the Seams test for the whole class. This connects to Module 2 (wordplay and humor).

L1 watch-outs
  • Reversed binomials from L1: Spanish tarde o temprano ("late or early") → "later or sooner"; Chinese 东西南北 (east, west, south, north) → "east, west, south and north" instead of north, south, east and west; Spanish blanco y negro happens to match black and white, but negro sobre blanco ("black on white") does not map to in black and white. Many pairs match, though: Portuguese mais cedo ou mais tarde, French tôt ou tard, German Salz und Pfeffer. Ask learners which pairs match their language and which don't: the mismatches are the ones to drill.
  • Collocation calques that survive to C2: German, Russian and Polish make a photo; Spanish, French and Italian do a mistake; Chinese and Japanese strong rain (→ heavy); Spanish big success (→ great / resounding); Arabic and French open / close the light; Portuguese and Spanish win money (→ earn, unless it's a prize). Treat each as a chunk to replace, not a rule to learn.
  • Pronunciation-driven garbles: speakers who don't distinguish /uː/ and /juː/ or /ʊ/ may write mute point; learners of all backgrounds write peek / peak / pique interchangeably. Show the spelling alongside the sound.
  • Full-form "and": syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Tagalog, Turkish) lead learners to say by AND large with equal stress. Drill the weak form and the linking.
  • British vs American fixed expressions: the online lesson uses touch wood (American: knock on wood) and lists knife and fork, which is the dominant order in both varieties. Some binomials differ: British fish and chips vs American burger and fries are cultural, not grammatical. Accept both varieties; point out the American one.
