The Mot Juste — Precision & Nuance

Three lessons on choosing exactly. First the single word: its connotation, its force, and the company it habitually keeps. Then the whole manner of a message: register as a mixing desk, with tone as a dial of its own. Finally the ready-made chunks that proficient speakers retrieve whole, in a fixed order and with a fixed partner, and that betray a speaker the moment they are slightly wrong.

How this module works

Each heading tells you what to do: Read and say, Listen, Write, With a partner or Self-check. Few tasks here have a single right answer. What matters is that your choice is precise, natural, appropriate to its reader, and that you can say why you made it.

When a task draws on your own experience, you decide what to share. Use your real life, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good. In role-plays you play the card, not yourself, and you can always say "Pass."

Fine Shades of Meaning — The Mot Juste

Class C1
Welcome to C2

You have long since been able to say what you mean in English. At C2 the question is rarely "Which word is correct?" and almost always "Which word is exactly right?" Progress now looks like refinement: a hundred small calibrations rather than one large leap. Expect feedback on four things throughout the course: precision (the exact word), naturalness (what a proficient user would actually say), appropriacy (whether a choice suits the reader and the room) and effect (whether it does what you intend).

denotation = a word's literal, dictionary meaning · connotation = the associations and value judgment it carries · semantic prosody = the favorable or unfavorable aura a word acquires from the company it habitually keeps · a cline = a scale of gradual differences

Grammar box

One fact, three verdicts.

NeutralPositiveNegative
careful with moneythrifty, frugalstingy, tight-fisted, miserly

No true synonyms. Near-synonyms differ in at least one dimension.

Forcetepid → warm → hot → scorching · decent → good → excellent → superb → sublime
Registerkids → children → offspring · try → attempt → endeavor
Specificitywalk → stride, amble, trudge, saunter · look → glance, peer, scrutinize
Collocationstartled by a noise · taken aback by his rudeness · astonished at the sheer scale
Careful sensesbemused = bewildered, not amused · fulsome praise = excessive, insincere · enormity = great wickedness

Semantic prosody. set in keeps unpleasant company (rot, despair, winter), so "happiness set in" jars. cause leans negative (damage, delay, concern), so a success is better brought about. Likewise utterly (ruined), bordering on (rudeness), rife with (corruption), a spate of (burglaries), a breeding ground for (resentment).

Precision is not ornament. The mot juste is often the plain, specific word. She trudged home beats she walked slowly and wearily; use usually beats utilize.

Read and say — Dialogue: tuning one sentence

Lena: Could you look at the opening line of my review? "The restaurant was very good and the food was nice."

Omar: It's accurate. It's also utterly forgettable. What was actually good about it?

Lena: The service was quick without feeling rushed, and the soup was astonishing.

Omar: Then say that. "Attentive, unhurried service" does more than "very good."

Lena: And the prices? I wrote "cheap."

Omar: Careful. "Cheap" can suggest poor quality. Do you want "reasonably priced" or "a bargain"?

Lena: "A bargain," I think. What about "The dessert was sublime, magnificent and transcendent"?

Omar: Now you've overwritten it. Choose one. The strongest word works best alone.

Lena: "Sublime," then. It sounds a little grand, but it was a remarkable dessert.

Omar: Then it has earned its place. That's the test: does the word serve, or does it show off?

Underline every change Omar suggests. Which dimension does each one improve: force, connotation, register, specificity or prosody?

Register Agility & Tone

Class C2
Read and say — New words and chunks
registerfieldtenormodethe formality clinefrozentonedeferentialauthoritativea register clangcode-switchingjargonan archaism
Notice is hereby given that…Please be advised that…failing which…without further noticeJust a reminder that…Heads up,Bear with me one moment.Hang on a sec.I see it a bit differently.I must respectfully take issue with…

field = what the text is about · tenor = the relationship between the people (power and distance) · mode = spoken or written, planned or spontaneous · a clang = one marker that belongs to a different register

Grammar box

One message, five levels. The fact stays the same; everything else moves together.

1 · FrozenNotice is hereby given that the premises will close at 3:00 p.m. on Friday, June 12.
2 · FormalPlease be advised that the community center will close early on Friday.
3 · NeutralJust a reminder that we're closing at 3 on Friday.
4 · InformalHeads up, we're shutting early Friday.
5 · IntimateFriday, 3 o'clock, doors shut. Don't be late!
Markers of HIGH registerLatinate words (ascertain, remuneration) · nominalization · passive and impersonal (It was decided that…) · hedging · long, subordinated sentences · no contractions · furthermore, notwithstanding, thereby
Markers of LOW registerphrasal verbs (sort out, put off) · contractions and ellipsis (Got a minute?) · you know, I mean, anyway · vague language (stuff, sort of) · idiom and slang · direct address and tags (…right?)

Tone is a second dial. Formality sets the ceremony; tone sets the attitude. Same level, opposite tone:

WarmThanks so much for flagging this; it's really helpful. I'll get it fixed today and let you know the moment it's done.
CoolNoted. This will be addressed in due course. You will be informed upon completion.

Other tones: ironic (over-grand language on a trivial subject), authoritative (boosters, imperatives, no hedging), deferential (hedges, questions, I wonder if…).

Move the whole cluster. One stray marker produces a clang: "Please find attached the aforementioned pics." Over-formality (utilize, commence, endeavor) sounds inflated rather than educated; slang in a serious setting punctures authority.

Read and say — Dialogue: too stiff, too loose

Priya: I'm drafting the press release about the bakery closing. How does this sound? "The bakery is shutting its doors, which is a total bummer."

Marco: Warm, but "a total bummer" belongs in a text to a friend, not a press release.

Priya: Fair. "The Riverside Bakery will close permanently on March 28, following the retirement of its founder."

Marco: Much better. Now it's the right register, but it's a little cold for a place people love.

Priya: So I keep the formality and change the tone?

Marco: Exactly. Add the quotation from Rosa, and "after forty-two years of serving the neighborhood."

Priya: And the social post can be the loose one: "Last call for Rosa's cinnamon rolls! Riverside Bakery closes March 28 after 42 amazing years. Go say goodbye."

Marco: Perfect. Just don't put "Notice is hereby given" in the post.

Priya: Only if I'm being ironic.

Marco: Which, for a bakery's farewell, you're not.

Find the clang Marco spots. Which of Priya's lines are the same formality but a different tone?

Class C3

Grammar box

Binomials and trinomials: the order is fixed.

Restricted collocation: one partner only.

NaturalLogical, but not idiomatic
a foregone conclusiona predetermined conclusion
a moot pointa discussable point
a vested interestan invested interest
drive a hard bargainmake a hard bargain
a resounding successa loud success
bitterly disappointedharshly disappointed

Every chunk has a register. to cut a long story short (spoken) · that said (safe in writing) · for what it's worth (modest, conversational) · at the end of the day (worn to cliché: ration it). Fixed similes (as easy as pie, as dry as a bone) are informal by nature.

Exact or nothing. Not a mute point but a moot point; not nip it in the butt but nip it in the bud; not peaked my interest but piqued; not on tender hooks but on tenterhooks. And ration them: rarely more than one fixed expression per sentence.

Read and say — Dialogue: the farewell toast

Aiko: I'm giving the toast at Mr. Haddad's retirement party. Can you listen to my opening?

Ben: Of course. Go ahead.

Aiko: "By and large, through thick and thin, sooner or later, give and take, at the end of the day, Karim has been part and parcel of this school."

Ben: Every chunk is correct, and together they sound like a phrasebook. Can you hear the seams?

Aiko: I can now. Too many in one breath. Which should I keep?

Ben: "Part and parcel" is lovely: it's true, and it fits a school. Drop the rest.

Aiko: "For thirty years, Karim has been part and parcel of this school." Better?

Ben: Much. And you could save "through thick and thin" for the end, where it can land on its own.

Aiko: "He stood by us through thick and thin. Please raise your glasses."

Ben: Now nobody will notice the chunks, which is exactly the point.

How many fixed expressions were in Aiko's first version? How many survive in the final one, and why those?
