Module 5 — Interpreting the Real World: Answers

Answers to every Module 5 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model readings for the interpretive tasks (the Frost stanzas, the seminar texts, the Varieties Lab and the Media Decoding Lab), and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 5.1–5.3Classes C14–C169 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect. Many items in this module are interpretive, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any response that (1) is accurate about the variety, the text or the reference, (2) is supported by evidence (a word, a structure, a described sound, a quotation), and (3) is expressed in precise, natural English that fits the task. In 5.1, never mark an established variety feature as an error, and accept British spellings throughout (recognise, colour, humour): the kit models American English. In 5.2, a reading that differs from the model but is defended with the words of the text is correct. In 5.3, accept any accurate gloss, including other well-known meanings of a slang term, and allow for slang that has shifted since the kit was written.

Accept itself for 1 (Priya also uses it as an emphasis marker). Accept aye as an extra Scottish item and braw (= fine, excellent) if learners add it. Likely to confuse a newcomer: go-slow (British English also uses it for a protest in which workers deliberately slow down), ken (looks like a name), emphatic only (can sound like "merely"). Exercise 2 — Two Englishes, one meaning
  1. elevator
  2. line
  3. gas (gasoline)
  4. cell phone (cellphone; also just "phone" or "cell")
  5. check (Americans also say "bill," especially in some regions; check is the classic answer)
  6. yard (backyard). In American English a garden is the planted part (a vegetable garden).
  7. British: one flight (the ground floor is at street level). American: none (the first floor is the ground floor).
  8. In American English, to table a proposal means to postpone or shelve it; in British English it means to put it on the agenda for discussion. The British writer is saying it will be discussed on Monday.
  9. In British English, quite + a gradable adjective often means "fairly, moderately" (quite good = decent, could be better), especially with a falling tone. In American English, quite usually intensifies (= very good). Accept answers noting that intonation and the writer's habits matter.

Exercise 5 — Put the responsibility where it belongs (models)
  1. "Her accent is one I'm less familiar with, so I need a few minutes to tune in." / "I found her accent hard to follow at first."
  2. "He speaks English as an additional language, and he's a very good engineer." / "His English is fluent and clear, and he's a good engineer." (Also drop the "but": it implies the English is a defect.)
  3. "Indian English is an established variety with its own norms, including words like prepone."
  4. "Could you say that again a little more slowly, please?" / "Sorry, I missed that last part. Could you repeat it?"
  5. "She has an accent I find very easy to follow." / "She speaks very clearly." (Everyone has an accent; "no accent" usually means "an accent like mine.")

Look for: the listener as the subject ("I found…"), precise description instead of judgment, and polite repair requests (say that again, a little more slowly). Appropriacy flag: over-correction into something vague or patronizing ("His English is so cute") is not respectful either.

Dialogue — Tabled

False friends: table (a proposal) (UK: discuss / US: postpone) and quite good (UK: fairly good / US: very good). Accommodation: "Reply in plain international English: 'Great, we look forward to discussing it on Monday. Is there anything you'd like us to revise first?'" It removes the ambiguous words and invites specific feedback. Hedging: Arjun hedges because the meaning of quite good depends on the writer and the tone; he can't be certain, and pretending to be would be a C2 mistake.

Varieties Lab — Round 2 info gap (the six traps)
  1. "Your desk is on the first floor" → one flight up, not street level.
  2. "Take the lift, not the stairs" → the elevator.
  3. "We'll table your proposal on Monday" → it will be discussed, not shelved.
  4. "Your first draft was quite good" → probably "decent, needs work," not "excellent."
  5. "Bring a jumper; the office is cold" → a sweater (not a dress or a person who jumps).
  6. "The chemist's is opposite the car park" → the pharmacy / drugstore is across from the parking lot.

Also acceptable if learners find them: fortnight (two weeks), ring me on my mobile (call my cell), the loo (restroom). Praise pairs who propose an accommodated rewrite of the email.

Varieties Lab — Round 3 (what good answers include)

There are no right answers, but strong contributions: (1) take a clear position with a reason; (2) distinguish intelligibility (can I be understood?) from prestige (is my accent socially valued?); (3) cite work, media or research rather than people in the room (e.g., expectation shapes perception, as in Rubin's 1992 study; everyone has an accent; accommodation is two-way); (4) use precise, neutral language (an accent I'm less familiar with, a variety, an additional language). Flag in feedback: "heavy / thick accent," "broken English," "normal English," "proper English," and generalizations about any national group.

Self-check
Q1
A speaker pronounces the r in car and hard, and taps the t in water so it sounds like "wader." Which variety are they most likely speaking?
A) American English
B) Southern British (RP)
C) Australian English
Why: Rhoticity plus t-flapping is the classic General American combination. RP and Australian English are non-rhotic (Australian English does flap t, but it drops the r in car).
Q2
You hear "The committee are still arguing" and "Have you got the post?" Which is the best conclusion?
A) The speaker has made two grammar mistakes.
B) The speaker is a non-native learner making errors.
C) These are standard British English features: variety, not error.
Why: Plural agreement with collective nouns and have got for possession are standard British grammar; the post is British for the mail. Assume variety, not error.
Q3 · new
A colleague in Lagos messages: "There's a serious go-slow on the expressway, so I'll reach by ten." What does she mean?
A) Workers are protesting by working slowly, and she has made an error with "reach."
B) The traffic is badly jammed, so she will arrive by ten.
C) She is driving slowly on purpose and may not arrive at all.
Why: In Nigerian English a go-slow is a traffic jam, and intransitive reach (= arrive) is standard there. A applies the British meaning of go-slow and treats a variety feature as an error; C misreads both.
Errors you will meet most (5.1)

"strong r / American t" instead of rhotic / flap · "American language" · "English English" · "heavy accent," "broken English," "normal English," "no accent" · treating outer-circle features (prepone, reach, only) as mistakes · mixing colour and color in one text · "In America they say gotten, it's wrong" · stressing va-RI-e-ty wrongly (VA-ri-e-ty) or in-TEL-li-gi-BI-li-ty flat. For range, praise tune in to, accommodate, an accent I'm less familiar with, place a speaker, variety, not error, and any learner who explains a feature with its rule.

Exercise 2 — From technique to effect (models)
  1. foreshadowing → to create unease and prepare us for the bridge's collapse (or a later disaster).
  2. irony → to expose his ruthlessness by praising the very quality he lacks; the narrator invites us to judge him.
  3. motif (also accept symbolism) → to build a growing sense of confinement or secrecy across the novel.
  4. juxtaposition → to sharpen the contrast between life and illness, making both more vivid.
  5. allusion (to the Greek myth) → to suggest that once the policy is released, its many harms can't be contained.
  6. symbolism → to let one small image carry the whole decline of the relationship.

Mark the effect clause, not only the label. Wrong: "irony, because it's funny"; "the effect is to use a symbol" (circular). Naturalness: "the effect is to make us see / feel" (not "the effect is making to see").

Exercise 3 — Unpick the sentence (models)
  1. Spine: I went to the woods. (Because I wished to live deliberately.) Paraphrase: Thoreau moved to the woods to live on purpose and deal only with what truly matters, so that he wouldn't reach the end of his life and find he had never really lived. (front = face, confront; it = life.)
  2. Spine: The storm must be sore. (Inversion: sore must be the storm.) Paraphrase: Only a truly terrible storm could silence the small bird of hope that has comforted so many people. (sore = severe; abash = silence, disconcert; that kept so many warm refers to the bird.)
  3. Spine: This truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families that he is considered as the rightful property of one of their daughters. Paraphrase: Even if nobody knows what a newly arrived rich bachelor thinks or wants, the local families are so sure he needs a wife that they regard him as already belonging to one of their daughters.

Accept any accurate paraphrase. In 3, praise learners who notice the irony of rightful property: the man, not the women, becomes the property, reversing the usual economics of marriage in the period.

Exercise 5 — Two roads (Frost, "The Road Not Taken")
  1. "Though as for that the passing there / Had worn them really about the same," and "And both that morning equally lay / In leaves no step had trodden black." (Also "as just as fair.")
  2. The poem has just told us the roads were equal, so "the one less traveled by" is not quite true: the speaker will later tell the story as if it were. The last stanza is in the future tense: it's a prediction of how he'll narrate the choice, not a record of what happened.
  3. Accept any two supported readings: nostalgia or regret for the road not taken; self-aware irony at his own future myth-making; weariness; contentment tinged with wistfulness. Evidence: "with a sigh," "ages and ages hence," the dash and the stutter "and I— / I took."
  4. Model: "The poem explores how people turn arbitrary choices into stories of deliberate, defining decisions." / "The poem suggests that the meaning of a choice is made afterwards, in the telling."

Both the popular celebratory reading and the ironic reading can be defended; the strongest answers acknowledge the tension and cite the second and third stanzas.

Dialogue — A friendly disagreement

Disagreeing politely: "I'd read it slightly differently." · "But couldn't the sigh be…?" Conceding: "Fair point." · "It could." · "Now that I'd sign." Evidence: "Look at the second stanza…" "The future tense matters." Lina moves from a moral ("it's about courage") to an arguable theme grounded in the text ("how we turn chance into a story about choice").

Close-Reading Seminar — model readings for the six texts

A1 · Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813), opening. Theme: marriage as an economic transaction dressed up as romance. Chain: the grand, philosophical phrase "a truth universally acknowledged" is applied to local gossip (irony, mock-heroic register) → the effect is comic deflation → which exposes how a community disguises its self-interest as universal truth. Key words: universally, must be in want of, rightful property. Voice: the narrator ventriloquizes society (free indirect style).

A2 · Jerome, Three Men in a Boat (1889), the British Museum. Theme: the comedy of self-deception and hypochondria. Chain: the narrator's casual understatement ("some slight ailment… hay fever, I fancy it was") and his inflated verbs ("plunged into," "fearful, devastating scourge") → the effect is ironic contrast between a trivial complaint and imagined catastrophe → which makes him a comically unreliable narrator whose fears we see through. Link to A1: both narrators state things we are meant to doubt.

B1 · Dickens, Bleak House (1853), the fog. Theme: a society (and its legal system) lost in confusion it has made itself. Chain: verbless sentences and the anaphora "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river… fog down the river" → the effect is to make the fog feel total and inescapable, like a camera panning with no action → which prepares the fog as a symbol of the Court of Chancery's obscurity. Allusion: "as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth" echoes the Flood in Genesis; the Megalosaurus waddling up Holborn Hill is comic prehistory, suggesting a city that hasn't evolved. Accept readings focused on pollution ("defiled," "waterside pollutions") and on the parenthesis "(and dirty)" as ironic.

B2 · Thoreau, Walden (1854). Theme: a deliberate life as a response to the fear of not having lived. Chain: the long periodic sentence, stacking purposes ("to live deliberately, to front… and see if… and not, when I came to die, discover…") → the effect is to hold the reader in suspense until the stark final clause → which makes "I had not lived" land with the full weight of the whole sentence. Second quotation, "The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation": juxtaposition of "quiet" and "desperation" (an oxymoron-like pairing) → despair that is invisible.

C1 · Dickinson, "Hope is the thing with feathers" (1891). Theme: hope as something self-sustaining that gives without demanding. Chain: extended metaphor of a small bird → the effect is to make an abstraction tender, physical and fragile-seeming → which heightens the surprise of its resilience ("never stops at all," "in the chillest land"). Syntax: inversion "sore must be the storm." Final turn: "Yet, never, in extremity, / It asked a crumb of me": the commas slow the line, and the shift to "me" makes it personal.

C2 · Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916). See Exercise 5. Theme: how we narrate chance as choice. Chain: the insistence on the roads' equality in stanzas 2–3, set against the confident future claim in stanza 4 → the effect is irony (the speaker will tell a better story than what happened) → which questions the idea that one decision "made all the difference." Link to C1: both poems end with a turn to the first person.

Self-check
Q1
A story's subject is a family dinner; it keeps returning to unspoken resentment and the things no one will say. What is its theme?
A) A family eating dinner together
B) The failure of communication within a family
C) Families should always be kind
Why: B names the recurring concern. A is the subject (what happens); C is a bland moral the story doesn't state.
Q2
A first-person narrator repeatedly insists on his own honesty while the events suggest otherwise. This is best described as…
A) an unreliable narrator
B) an omniscient narrator
C) an allusion
Why: The gap between his claims and the events is the signature of unreliability. An omniscient narrator knows all and is usually third-person; an allusion is a reference to another text.
Q3 · new
Pride and Prejudice opens: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." What is the best reading?
A) Austen sincerely believes that every wealthy man wants to marry.
B) It is ironic: the "universal truth" is really the local families' wish to marry their daughters to a rich man.
C) It is an allusion to a philosophical treatise on marriage.
Why: The grand, philosophical phrasing is applied to local gossip, and the next sentence reveals who actually "wants": the surrounding families. A confuses the narrative voice with the author's sincere belief; C mistakes a mock-philosophical register for a specific allusion.
Errors you will meet most (5.2)

"The author wants to say…" · "The poem speaks about…" · "The theme is the fog / the sea" (subject) · "It's about love" (too broad) · "There is a metaphor" with no effect · "irony" for coincidence · narrator = author · "the author makes a metaphor" (uses / builds / sustains) · paraphrases that add ideas not in the text · stress errors: AL-lu-sion (→ al-LU-sion), jux-TA-po-si-tion with the wrong main stress (→ jux-ta-po-SI-tion). For range, praise evokes, juxtaposes, foreshadows, the tone shifts from… to…, sustains both readings, is answerable to the text, I'd read it slightly differently.

5.3 · Decoding Authentic Media (C16)

Exercise 1 — The coffee-shop run-in (Script 5.3-A)
  1. Marcus, at a coffee shop (while Jordan was getting coffee).
  2. He had cut off all contact with the friend group without any explanation.
  3. No. "Cool cool cool" (repeated, rushed) is ironic: Jordan was trying to look unbothered while feeling very awkward. The repetition and the "I'm just standing there like…" frame signal it.
  4. "I bet ten dollars" = I'm confident that…
  5. "No lie," "honestly," "I'm not exaggerating."
  6. Something socially awkward or hurtful happened at a birthday party and led Marcus to withdraw. The details are a shared reference between the friends: a listener can't know them, and doesn't need to.

Accept "lowkey" = slightly or quietly, and "iconic" = typical of you in an admirable way, if learners comment on Sam's line. Both the online version and the kit's script use American bucks and apartment; accept British quid and flat if learners know them.

Exercise 2 — Headlines (models)
  1. City Hall has approved (the plan for) night buses. (give the green light = approve)
  2. Scientists are investigating a mysterious signal.
  3. The museum will open a new wing in the spring.
  4. Library fines have been abolished.
  5. A chef has sharply questioned the mayor about the rules for food trucks. Pun: grill = cook on a grill / question someone intensely; fitting because the questioner is a chef.
  6. Local bakers have responded well to a difficult situation. Pun: rise to the challenge (respond well) / bread dough rises.

Accept simple past (City Hall approved…) for 1, 2 and 4. Wrong: "The chef cooked the mayor" (literal), "Library fines were cut with an axe."

Exercise 3 — Which room?
  1. b) (a) is far too formal for a friend; (c) is dated, unless used jokingly.
  2. b) Plain, specific and professional.
  3. a) Casual, current and natural for anyone.
  4. b) (a) gutted is British informal; (c) is current slang: neither belongs in a report.

Why 3c is risky (model): Borrowing another variety's slang and identity markers (knackered, innit, mate) when you don't belong to that community can sound forced or like a parody, so it draws more attention than plain English would.

Exercise 4 — Allusions
  1. a Trojan horse: something that looks like a gift but hides a threat (Greek myth, the fall of Troy).
  2. Groundhog Day: every day felt exactly the same (the 1993 film in which a man relives one day).
  3. Achilles' heel: her single weak point (the Greek hero, invulnerable except at the heel).
  4. Catch-22: a no-win situation created by contradictory rules (Joseph Heller's 1961 novel).
  5. the Midas touch: everything she does is successful or profitable (King Midas, whose touch turned things to gold).
  6. met his Waterloo: suffered a final, decisive defeat (Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo, 1815).
Exercise 5 — Hear it fast, write it full
  1. What are you going to do?
  2. Did you see the game?
  3. Let me know if you need anything.
  4. I don't know, I kind of want to stay in.
  5. You should have told me.
  6. We have got to go. / We've got to go. / We have to go.
  7. Would you mind closing the door?

Wrong: "You should of told me" (a spelling error from the weak form of have); "I kinda want" in the full form.

Dialogue — Asking about a reference

Allusions: "Houston, we have a problem" (Apollo 13, 1970, popularized by the 1995 film; the actual words were "Houston, we've had a problem") · "Groundhog Day" · "a Catch-22." Saving face: Keiko asks directly but lightly ("Sorry, is that a reference to something?") and frames the gap as a missed joke, not a failure; Marc validates it ("Oh, fair question"). Marc's last line is a playful compliment: teasing ("Now you're just showing off") after Keiko uses an allusion herself; the context (a friendly exchange, her correct use) makes it clear.

Media Decoding Lab — station answers

Station 1, Headlines: 1 COUNCIL BACKS DOWNTOWN BIKE LANES = The council has voted to support bike lanes downtown. · 2 SCHOOL BOARD EYES LATER START = The school board is considering a later start time. · 3 HEAT WAVE: PARKS DEPARTMENT KEEPS ITS COOL = pun: stays calm / keeps (the parks, the public) cool (misting stations, shade). · 4 RECORD TURNOUT FOR CHARITY RUN = A record number of people took part in the charity run. · 5 LOCAL BAND STRIKES A CHORD WITH CRITICS = pun: strike a chord = resonate / a band plays chords. · 6 CITY VOWS TO FIX POTHOLES BY JUNE = The city has promised to repair the potholes by June.

Station 2, Memes in words: M1 "This is fine" (a cartoon dog in a burning room, calmly drinking coffee): ironic denial in a crisis; shared about work overload or chaos, with self-mockery. M2 "Expectation vs reality" (9 a.m.: "I'll finish the report early" / 3 p.m.: asleep at the desk): self-deprecating humor about good intentions. M3 "Nobody: / Absolutely no one: / My cat at 4 a.m.:" (running laps of the apartment): mocks behavior that nobody asked for; affectionate absurdity. M4 "…said no one ever" ("I love a 7 a.m. meeting — said no one ever"): sarcasm marker; the tag reverses the first part. Model newcomer gloss (M1): "People share this when things are going badly and they're pretending to be calm. The joke is the gap between the fire and the dog's calm words."

Station 3, Allusion hunt (the newsletter article): the Midas touch (success with everything) · a Herculean task (enormous effort; the labors of Hercules) · opened Pandora's box (released many unforeseen problems; Greek myth) · crossed the Rubicon (passed the point of no return; Julius Caesar, 49 BCE) · sour grapes (pretending not to want what you can't have; Aesop's fox). Accept "Herculean effort" for the second.

Station 4, Slang & register: Dated: groovy · rad · da bomb · on fleek. Established: ghost someone · binge-watch · FOMO · a hot take · the vibe. Current (at the time of writing): no cap · it slaps · the ick (also accept rizz, lowkey). Accept reasonable disagreement about boundaries: the vibe and lowkey are moving from current to established. Rewrite (model): friend's text "ngl the new rota lowkey slaps, way less FOMO on weekends" → email to a manager: "I wanted to say that the new schedule is working very well for me. Having alternate weekends free has made a real difference." (rota is British for schedule.)

Self-check
Q1
As everything goes wrong, a character says flatly, "Well, this is going great." What is actually meant?
A) Things are genuinely going well.
B) The speaker has no opinion either way.
C) The opposite: things are going badly (sarcasm).
Why: The words and the situation disagree, and the delivery is flat: trust the situation and the tone.
Q2
A line gets a big laugh, but you didn't understand it. What's the best response?
A) Pause and refuse to continue until you fully understand that one joke.
B) Note that a reference probably passed, keep following the gist, and look it up later.
C) Conclude that your English isn't good enough for real media.
Why: The "why did that land?" reflex: mark it, ride the gist, look it up selectively. Even native speakers miss references; not knowing one says nothing about your English.
Q3 · new
You're emailing your manager to say the new schedule is working well. Which sentence is best?
A) "No cap, the new schedule slaps."
B) "The new schedule is working really well for the team."
C) "The new schedule is lowkey iconic."
Why: A and C use current in-group slang in a professional email; B is clear, warm and appropriate. Understand slang widely; use it sparingly, and not at work in writing.
Errors you will meet most (5.3)

Taking sarcasm literally · using dated idioms as current ("It's raining cats and dogs," "groovy") · current slang in emails and reports · writing reductions in formal text ("I'm gonna send it") · "should of" · "This slang is very actual" · "on the TV," "in the internet" · reading headlines literally ("Library fines axed" = fines cut with an axe) · glossing a slang term with mockery of the community it comes from · believing a missed reference means weak English. For range, praise it went over my head, I think I missed the reference, ride the gist, the pun turns on…, it's deadpan, it's a dated expression now.
