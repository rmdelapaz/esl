Mastery English Workbook · Module 5

Interpreting the Real World

Three lessons that take you beyond the English of textbooks. Tune your ear to the world's Englishes and talk about accents with precision and respect. Read a demanding literary text slowly enough to hear what it is doing, and defend your reading with the words on the page. Then speed back up: slang, headlines, memes, allusions, fast speech and sarcasm, with strategies for the part that sails past you.

How this module works

Each heading tells you what to do: Read, Listen, Write, Say, Pair work or Group work. Many tasks here are interpretive: more than one answer can be right, provided you can point to the evidence for it.

Two ground rules for this module. First, no one will ever be asked to perform, imitate or be judged on an accent, yours or anyone else's; we listen to varieties, we don't act them. Second, when a task draws on your own experience (your English, your media habits), you decide what to share: your real life, a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good, and you can always say "Pass."

Many Englishes — Accents, Dialects & Global Englishes

Class C14
Read and say — New words and chunks
a variety of Englishan accenta dialectrhotic / non-rhotict-flappinga glottal stopstress-timed / syllable-timeda lingua francaWorld Englishesa false friend
intelligibilityaccommodationto tune in toto decompose an accent into featuresassume variety, not erroran accent I'm less familiar withaccent biasto place a speaker

rhotic = pronouncing the r after a vowel (car, hard) · intelligibility = being understood by your listener, whatever your accent · accommodation = adjusting your speech (and your listening) to meet another speaker halfway · a false friend = a word that exists in two varieties with different meanings

Grammar box — Hearing and naming variety

Listen for features, not "an accent."

The rrhotic: car, hard with the r sounded (most of the US, Canada, Scotland, Ireland) · non-rhotic: "cah," "hahd" (southern England, Australia)
The vowel in bathbroad "ah" (southern England, Australia) · short, flat a (US, northern England)
The tflap: water ≈ "wader" (US, Canada) · glottal stop: butter ≈ "bu'er" (London, much of England) · crisp t elsewhere
The rhythmstress-timed: strong beats, squeezed syllables (US, UK) · syllable-timed: even syllables (India, Nigeria, Singapore)

Words, spelling and grammar that place a speaker or a writer.

Vocabularylift / elevator · flat / apartment · queue / line · trousers / pants · mobile / cell phone · the bill / the check · football / soccer · garden / yard
False friendsfirst floor (UK: one flight up · US: street level) · table a proposal (UK: discuss it now · US: postpone it) · quite good (UK: fairly good · US: very good) · momentarily (US: very soon · UK: briefly) · homely (UK: cozy · US: plain-looking)
Spellingcolour / color · centre / center · defence / defense · travelled / traveled · catalogue / catalog · organise or organize (both British) → one variety per document
Grammar tellsHave you got a minute? / Do you have a minute? · The committee are… / The committee is… · Have you eaten yet? / Did you eat yet? · got / gotten

World Englishes are established standards, not learner errors: prepone, do the needful, out of station (India) · go-slow, no wahala (Nigeria) · comfort room (the Philippines) · can or not? (Singapore) · arvo, heaps (Australia).

Language that locates the fault. Not: He has a heavy accent. · She speaks broken English. Instead: He has an accent I'm less familiar with. · It took me a minute to tune in.

Read and say — Dialogue: Tabled

Dana: I just got an email from the London office. They say they'll "table our proposal" on Monday. So that's it, then: they're shelving it.

Arjun: Hang on. In British English, tabling something means putting it on the agenda. They're going to discuss it on Monday.

Dana: Seriously? Here it means the opposite. We'd table something to kill it politely.

Arjun: Exactly: same word, opposite plan. And look, they also say the draft is "quite good."

Dana: Which, from them, is… lukewarm?

Arjun: Probably "decent, but it needs work." Mind you, I wouldn't bet on it either way; it depends on the writer.

Dana: So what do we do? I'd rather not guess.

Arjun: Accommodate. Reply in plain international English: "Great, we look forward to discussing it on Monday. Is there anything you'd like us to revise first?"

Dana: No idioms, no ambiguity, and a question that invites them to be specific. I like it.

Find the two false friends. Which sentence is the accommodation strategy? Why does Arjun hedge ("Probably… Mind you…")?

Reading Closely — Literature & Complex Texts

Class C15
Read and say — New words and chunks

the passage evokes…the image juxtaposes X and Ythis detail foreshadows…the tone shifts from… to…the effect is to…which suggests / reinforces…it is heavy with ironyI'd read it slightly differentlyWhere does it say that?

theme = the underlying concern a text keeps returning to (not what happens) · an unreliable narrator = a narrator whose account we come to doubt · a periodic sentence = a sentence that delays its main clause until the end

Grammar box — Reading for meaning, reading hard sentences
SubjectWhat literally happens? · a father and son spend a day fishing
ThemeWhat is it really about? A specific, arguable claim · love that cannot be spoken aloud
PurposeWhat does it want to do to the reader? · to move, persuade, unsettle, satirize, mourn
ToneWhat is the author's attitude? · affectionate, ironic, elegiac, detached

Strip → spine → restore → paraphrase.

StripBracket the extras: relative clauses, participle phrases, asides between commas or dashes.
SpineFind the subject and the main verb: who does what?
RestorePut the pieces back one by one; pin each pronoun to its referent.
ParaphraseSay the whole thing in one plain sentence.

Watch for inversion (And sore must be the storm = the storm must be sore), verbless sentences used for effect (Fog everywhere.) and archaic meanings (wanted wear = lacked wear).

The engine of interpretation: the text does X (technique) → which creates Y (effect) → which serves Z (theme). A label on its own ("there is imagery") is not yet an interpretation.

Read — Exercise 5: Two roads

These stanzas come from Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken" (1916). The speaker has stopped where a path divides in a wood.

Then took the other, as just as fair,
And having perhaps the better claim,
Because it was grassy and wanted wear;
Though as for that the passing there
Had worn them really about the same,

And both that morning equally lay
In leaves no step had trodden black.
Oh, I kept the first for another day!
Yet knowing how way leads on to way,
I doubted if I should ever come back.

I shall be telling this with a sigh
Somewhere ages and ages hence:
Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—
I took the one less traveled by,
And that has made all the difference.

1. Which lines suggest the two roads were, in fact, much the same?

2. Many readers treat the last stanza as a celebration of choosing the unconventional path. What complicates that reading?

3. Why might the speaker "sigh"? Give two possible readings.

4. Write one arguable theme sentence for the poem.

Read and say — Dialogue: A friendly disagreement

Lina: For me, the Frost poem is about courage: he takes the road less traveled, and it changes his life.

Tomás: I'd read it slightly differently. Look at the second stanza: the paths had been worn "really about the same."

Lina: Fair point. So the difference isn't in the roads.

Tomás: Right. It's in the story he'll tell later. "I shall be telling this with a sigh / Somewhere ages and ages hence." The future tense matters.

Lina: But couldn't the sigh be nostalgic rather than ironic?

Tomás: It could. The poem sustains both, which is partly why it's so often misquoted.

Lina: Then maybe the theme is how we turn chance into a story about choice.

Tomás: Now that I'd sign. And every word of it is answerable to the text.

Underline the phrases the speakers use to disagree politely and to concede. How does Lina's final theme differ from her first?

currency (of a word) = how current it is: fresh, established or dated · headlinese = the compressed grammar of headlines · deadpan = delivering something absurd with a completely straight face

Grammar box — Decoding fast, compressed and loaded English

Slang has a date and a room.

Datedgroovy (1960s–70s) · rad (1980s) · da bomb (1990s) · on fleek (2010s): now retro or ironic
Established informalto ghost someone · the vibe · to binge-watch · FOMO · a hot take · (British) gutted, knackered: widely understood, still informal
Currentno cap · it slaps · lowkey / highkey · the ick · main-character energy: understand them; use them sparingly, and never in formal writing

Headlinese.

No articles, no beCOUNCIL BACKS BIKE LANES = The council has backed the bike lanes.
Present simple = pastMUSEUM FINDS LOST PAINTING = The museum has found / found…
to + infinitive = futureMAYOR TO UNVEIL PARK PLAN = The mayor will unveil…
Past participle = passiveLIBRARY FINES AXED = Library fines have been abolished.
Short verbsaxe (cut) · back (support) · probe (investigate) · slam (criticize) · vow (promise) · bid (attempt) · eye (consider)

Fast connected speech.

Elision · assimilation · linkingnex(t) day · tem bikes · an apple → "a-napple"
Weak formsfish 'n' chips · going tuh · a cup uh tea
Reductionsgonna · wanna · gotta · kinda · lemme · dunno · whatcha (what are you) · didja (did you) · wouldja (would you) · shoulda (should have)

Tone over text. When the words and the situation disagree, trust the situation and the delivery: "Well, this is going great" as the car breaks down means the opposite.

Read and say — Dialogue: Asking about a reference

Keiko: So the new booking system goes live on Monday.

Marc: Houston, we have a problem: nobody's been trained on it.

Keiko: Sorry, is that a reference to something? Everyone laughed, and I think I missed the joke.

Marc: Oh, fair question. It's from the Apollo 13 mission, via the film. People say it when something's gone wrong, usually with a straight face.

Keiko: Got it. So it's deadpan: a calm line for a serious problem.

Marc: Exactly. Though, to be fair, "nobody's been trained" is less Apollo 13 and more Groundhog Day. We have this problem every single launch.

Keiko: That one I know: the film where the same day repeats. Right, let's schedule the training before it becomes a Catch-22.

Marc: Now you're just showing off.

Underline the three allusions. How does Keiko ask about the reference without losing face? Is Marc's last line an insult or a compliment? How do you know?
