Module 5 — Interpreting the Real World

Three workshop classes that take learners out of the textbook and into English as it is actually spoken, written and broadcast. First, varieties of English: the audible features that distinguish accents (rhoticity, the bath vowel, t-flapping, the glottal stop, rhythm), British/American vocabulary, spelling and grammar tells, and World Englishes (Indian, Nigerian, Singaporean, Philippine and more), with an honest, respectful conversation about accent attitudes. Second, literature and complex texts: a close-reading seminar on short public-domain passages (Austen, Dickens, Thoreau, Dickinson, Frost, Jerome) that moves from technique to effect to theme. Third, authentic media: slang and its currency, headlines, memes described in words, cultural allusions, fast connected speech and sarcasm. The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

3 lessons Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer (in C14, the test return) → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-5-real-world.html. Print materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html before C14. It holds the flashcards (British/American picture pairs, feature cards, literary-technique cards, slang-currency cards), the new identity cards, the Varieties Lab transcripts, info-gap email and attitude-discussion cards, the Close-Reading Seminar text cards and role cards, the Media Decoding Lab station cards, the Decode It! board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons and an audio control for voice and speed, so learners can hear the dialogues and model sentences again at home.
  • These are workshops, not lectures. Each class protects one extended performance: the Varieties Lab (C14: feature detection on transcripts, a British/American info gap and a structured discussion of accent attitudes), the Close-Reading Seminar (C15: annotation, a role-based seminar and a written interpretation) and the Media Decoding Lab (C16: four stations on headlines, memes, allusions and slang). If you are running late, cut controlled practice (set it for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. C2 learners met irony and humor in C2 2.2, figurative language in 2.3, register agility in 1.2 and rhetoric in 4.1. Don't re-teach any of it. This module applies those tools to material nobody simplified for them: an unfamiliar variety, a nineteenth-century sentence, a headline pun, a sarcastic line at speed. The C2 step is interpretive confidence: saying what something means, why, and on what evidence, while tolerating what remains uncertain.
  • Feedback on four things. In every workshop, take notes in four columns: precision (the exact word or term: rhotic, not "strong r"; theme, not "topic"; ironic, not "funny"), appropriacy (fit for the room: current slang in a report, "broken English" in a respectful discussion, a literary term dropped without an effect), naturalness (grammatical but not what an expert would say: "the poem wants to say," "the author makes the imagery of cold," "this slang is very actual") and effect (did the interpretation persuade? did the gloss help a newcomer?). Board two or three items per column, without names. At C2 the effect column is where learners grow most.
  • Near-native and fossilized at once. Many C2 learners are superb readers who have never heard Nigerian English, or fluent speakers who still say "the author wants to say" or "on the TV." Their fossilized errors are often invisible to them because nobody corrects fluent people. Correct them privately and specifically (a sticky note with the exact phrase and a better one), and praise precisely too: "juxtaposes was exactly right there" is useful; "great English!" is not.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured test: the "Evidence or assumption?" test (C14: every claim about a variety must point to a word, a structure or a described sound, never to a stereotype or "it just sounds like…"), the "Show me the words" test (C15: every interpretive claim must quote the text) and the "Gloss it for a newcomer" test (C16: could someone who doesn't share the culture understand the gloss, and is it accurate and respectful?). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote rather than summarize.
  • Dominant speakers. Seminars attract confident talkers, and literature classes attract learners who have read everything. Use the built-in roles: in C14 the fair-language monitor (listens for the loaded words: "heavy accent," "broken English," "proper English"); in C15 the evidence keeper (may only ask "Where does it say that?") or the discussion leader who speaks last; in C16 the newcomer (plays someone who doesn't share the reference and must honestly say whether a gloss worked). Say it kindly: "You read closely. I need that skill on the evidence card this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spelling (recognise, colour, humour, labelled, practise) and, in 5.3, British informal words (quid, flat, gutted, knackered). Teach American forms, accept British forms, and in C14 make the point explicitly: consistency within one document matters more than which variety you choose. The quoted literary texts keep their original spelling (neighbourhood, traveled): don't "correct" a public-domain quotation.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Hear it → read it → decode it. In C14 learners train their ears on variety: they decompose an unfamiliar English into features, recognize that every variety is complete and valid, and practice talking about accents without locating the fault in the speaker. In C15 they slow down and read a demanding text for what it does, not only what it says, and defend a reading with the words on the page. In C16 they speed back up: slang, headlines, memes, allusions and sarcasm at native speed, with strategies for the part that sails past. The skill underneath all three is the same: notice the signal, name the feature, infer the meaning, and stay comfortable with what you can't yet catch.

Accent, identity and respect: the rules for this module

Accent is personal. Some of your learners have been corrected, mocked, passed over at work or asked "Where are you really from?" because of how they sound; some speak an outer-circle variety (Indian, Nigerian, Philippine, Singaporean English) as a first or home language and have been told it isn't "real" English. So: (1) Learners are never asked to perform, demonstrate, imitate or be assessed on their own accent, or anyone else's, and no one is asked to "say it in your accent" or to be the class example of a variety. (2) You don't perform accents either. Read every transcript in your own natural voice; the features are in the words and grammar, and the sound features are described in brackets, not acted. Mock accents slide into caricature fast. For real sound, use authentic recordings of speakers talking about their own lives (for example, the International Dialects of English Archive, dialectsarchive.com), chosen and previewed by you. (3) Discuss attitudes, not people in the room. The attitude cards are general statements; personal stories are welcome but always optional, and anyone can say "Pass." (4) Pronunciation drills in this module are about intonation, stress and connected speech in the learner's own voice (reading a model sentence, a sarcastic line, a fast-speech reduction), never about acquiring a different accent.

Content choices: literature, media and slang

The C15 seminar uses six short, public-domain passages (all published before 1929 and in the US public domain) chosen to be rich but gentle: comedy, irony, fog, hope, a walk in the woods. Avoid swapping in texts about violence, war, bereavement or abuse without warning; the online lesson's own passage is about grief, so offer it as optional homework, not as the seminar text. In C16 every slang item, headline and meme is invented or generic and no slang is offensive; don't take requests to "explain the rude ones" in class (offer a quiet word or a good dictionary instead). Where a slang term comes from a specific community, most often African American English or LGBTQ+ ballroom culture, name the origin respectfully; understanding a community's words is fine, mocking or performing them is not. No headline or meme topic touches party politics, religion or immigration policy.

Before the Module

  • Mark Progress Test 1 before C14. Write a short, positive comment on every paper: one strength + one thing to refine, phrased as an upgrade, not a deficit ("Your stance markers are precise; next, vary your cohesive devices across paragraphs"). Collect 4–5 common errors from different papers for the C14 board (no names). Note which learners scored below about 60% so you can offer a quiet 1:1 chat at the break.
  • Print the flashcards (one set for the board): British/American picture pairs and the feature cards (C14); literary-technique cards (C15); slang-currency, allusion and fast-speech cards (C16). Cut them before C14.
  • For C14: one set of Varieties Lab transcripts (8 cards, 2 per group of 3–4), one Feature Detective grid per group, the info-gap email (A/B) for each pair and one set of attitude cards per group.
  • For C15: one text card per group of 3 (six texts in three pairs), a set of seminar role cards per merged group of 6, and highlighters or two colors of pen.
  • For C16: four Media Decoding stations (headlines, memes in words, allusion hunt, slang currency) set up on four tables, plus a phone timer. The Decode It! board game (1 board per group, 1 die, counters) is for early finishers in C16 and the C17 warmer.
  • Print the new identity cards and keep them on a side table where anyone can take one without asking. This module's personal prompts ("which English shaped yours?", "a show you watch") can touch identity and history; a card is always an equal option.
  • Optional but powerful: preview and queue two or three short authentic recordings of speakers from different varieties talking about ordinary topics (an archive clip, a university lecture, a cooking segment), each under a minute. Use them in C14 after the transcripts, for listening, never for imitation.
  • Check the slang. The "current" slang column in C16 was current when this kit was written. Before C16, spend five minutes checking which terms have already dated; that drift is itself the lesson, and learners love hearing "this one is already a bit cringe."

The test return must stay at 12 minutes. Cut Workbook Ex 4 and the dictation (Script 5.1-B) and set them for homework. Returning Progress Test 1 (12 min) — keep it warm

1. Celebrate first (3 min). Before any paper comes out, write "We can…" on the board and list three things the whole class did well (for example: choose the mot juste between near-synonyms · shift register on demand · read implicature and irony · signal stance with discourse markers · summarize and synthesize · build an argument with rhetorical control). Say it out loud.

Four modules of C2. You can choose the exact word, move between registers, read what's implied and not said, hold a long text together, and argue with style. That is mastery-level English. Give yourselves a hand.

2. Return privately (2 min). Hand papers back face-down or in folders. Never read scores aloud and never compare learners. Everyone gets a written comment: one strength + one upgrade to work on.

3. "Let's refine it!" (5 min). Show 4 anonymous sentences from the tests. At C2 they are rarely plain errors; mix accuracy with appropriacy and naturalness. Use your own learners' sentences if you can; typical ones at this point: The results were very unique. · In my humble opinion, the data clearly proves the hypothesis. (report) · The committee discussed about the proposal at length. · Having read the report, the conclusion seemed obvious. (Also common: This is a very actual problem. · She made a big effort for convincing them. · It's raining cats and dogs, so to speak, in the market.) Pairs refine them and say what kind of problem each one was (accuracy, register, naturalness, logic). Praise the refining, not the error-free papers.

4. One goal (2 min). Each learner writes one goal in the workbook box ("My goal after Progress Test 1"): "I want to refine ___. Every week I will ___." Give two examples: "I want to refine my collocations. Every week I'll note ten from a long-form article and use three at work." / "I want my irony to land. I'll listen to one comedy podcast a week and note where the tone does the work."

A test is a map. It tells us where we are. It doesn't tell us who we are.

Some adults carry hard memories of tests, and highly qualified professionals whose credentials were not recognized here can feel a test as a judgment on their whole career. At C2 there's an extra twist: learners who are used to being "the best English speaker" in their workplace may be shaken by any mark below perfect. Keep your voice light and move on quickly. Offer a 1:1 chat at the break to anyone who struggled (below about 60%), and plan catch-up with the Module 1–4 online lessons. The best encouragement is a successful lesson straight after it, and today's topic rewards what these learners already have: years of listening to many kinds of English.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: varieties, not errors. A variety is a complete, rule-governed form of English with its own sounds, words and grammar. Inner-circle (UK, US, Australia, Ireland), outer-circle (India, Nigeria, Singapore, the Philippines: English as an official or institutional language, often through colonial history) and expanding-circle (English as an international lingua franca) Englishes are all real English.

"The train is delayed only, no?" Is only a mistake? (No: it's an emphasis marker in Indian English.) Who is it a mistake for? (Nobody: it's standard in that variety.) · A Nigerian colleague says "There's a go-slow on the expressway." Is there a strike? (No: in Nigerian English a go-slow is a traffic jam. In British English it can also mean a work slowdown protest, so context matters.) · Is Received Pronunciation "English done properly"? (No: it's one accent among many.)

Meaning: features. A trained listener doesn't hear "an accent"; they hear two or three features.

"Car" with the r sounded: rhotic or non-rhotic? (Rhotic.) Typical of? (Most of the US, Canada, Scotland, Ireland.) · "Water" as "wader": what's that called? (T-flapping.) Where? (US, Canada.) · "Butter" with a catch in the throat? (A glottal stop: London and much of England.) · Every syllable roughly the same length: stress-timed or syllable-timed? (Syllable-timed.) Is that "flat" or "wrong"? (Neither: it's the rhythm of that variety.)

Meaning: intelligibility and accommodation. When Englishes meet, what matters is being understood by each other. Research on English as a lingua franca finds that the features causing real breakdown are few. Accommodation = both speakers adjust (slow down, rephrase, avoid local idioms). The listener's job is half the work.

"I couldn't understand his accent." Whose skill does that sentence describe? (Mine, the listener's: my exposure.) Better phrasing? ("I'm less familiar with that accent." / "It took me a few minutes to tune in.") · In an international meeting, who should adjust: the native speakers or the non-native speakers? (Both: that's accommodation.)

Pronunciation. Keep this about recognition. The learner's own production goal is intelligibility, in their own accent. Two production points are worth drilling because they help everyone: (1) the stress of the metalanguage (RHOtic, vaRIety, inTELligiBILity, acCOMmoDAtion, GLOTtal); (2) saying the model sentences with clear chunking and stress ("In Britain you take the lift to your flat | in America you take the elevator to your apartment"). If a learner asks "Should I change my accent?", answer honestly: only if they want to, for their own reasons; the course goal is to be understood and to understand.

Drill script
  1. Swap it (choral, then individual): T: "lift" → S: "elevator." T: "queue" → "line." T: "petrol" → "gas." T: "flat" → "apartment." T: "the bill, please" → "the check, please." T: "Have you got a pen?" → "Do you have a pen?" T: "colour, with a u" → "color, no u." Reverse direction for the second round.
  2. False friend or not? T: "I'll meet you on the first floor." → S: "False friend! Which first floor?" T: "Let's table it." → "False friend: now or later?" T: "The sidewalk." → "Not a false friend: just American."
  3. Name the feature: T (describing): "The r in 'hard' is sounded." → S: "Rhotic." T: "'Better' sounds like 'bedder.'" → "T-flapping." T: "'Bottle' with a catch in the throat." → "Glottal stop." T: "Every syllable about the same length." → "Syllable-timed."
  4. Reframe it (individual, then choral for the best version): T: "He has a heavy accent." → S: "He has an accent I'm not used to yet." T: "She speaks broken English." → "She speaks English as an additional language, and she gets her point across." / "…and I understood her fine."
Varieties Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4 with roles: reader-aloud (optional; otherwise you read), scribe (fills the Feature Detective grid), evidence checker (asks "Which word shows that?") and fair-language monitor (listens for loaded words and suggests a reframe). Give the monitor role to a dominant speaker.
  • Round 1, Feature Detective (10 min): each group gets two unlabeled transcripts from the eight (Scottish, Irish, Australian, Indian, Nigerian, Singaporean, Philippine, US Southern). Read each aloud once in your normal voice, including the bracketed sound note. Groups (1) underline each variety word or structure, (2) gloss it in standard American English, (3) copy the sound features from the bracketed note, (4) choose the variety from the label list on the grid and (5) write the evidence. Then swap grids with the next group for the "Evidence or assumption?" test: every placement must cite a word, a structure or a described sound. "It sounds Indian" is an assumption; "prepone and out of station are Indian English" is evidence.
  • (Optional, 2 min from Round 3) Play one authentic recording of a speaker of a variety the group worked on. "Which features from the grid can you hear?" Listening only.
  • Round 2, Transatlantic info gap (5 min): pairs. A holds a British colleague's welcome email for a visit (first floor, the lift, a jumper, "quite good," "we'll table your proposal on Monday," the chemist's). B holds an American visitor's list of questions. B asks; A explains without showing the email. Together they list the six places an American reader could misunderstand. No one reads the email in an accent: this is vocabulary.
  • Round 3, Attitudes (10 min): each group draws three of the eight attitude cards (general statements such as "A listener who says 'I can't understand your accent' is describing themselves, not the speaker"). For each: agree, disagree or "it depends," with a reason and, if possible, an example from work, media or research, not from anyone in the room. The fair-language monitor flags loaded words. Each group reports one insight in one sentence.
  • Share (in the feedback stage): the most evidence-based placement from Round 1 and the most useful reframe from Round 3 go on the board.
Facilitating the attitudes discussion

Set the frame before Round 3: "We are discussing ideas about accents, not anyone's accent. No one describes, rates or imitates anyone's accent, including their own. You can share an experience if you want to, and you can always pass." If a learner starts to share a painful experience (being mocked, losing a job), listen, thank them, and don't ask follow-up questions in front of the class; check in privately later. If a learner says something dismissive of a variety ("Indian English is just wrong English"), treat it as an idea to examine, not a person to shame: "Interesting. What would a linguist say? Is prepone less logical than postpone?" One piece of research is worth mentioning: in a well-known 1992 study by Donald Rubin, US undergraduates heard the same recorded lecture by a speaker of General American; those shown a photo of an Asian instructor rated the lecturer as more accented and understood less. The lesson: expectation shapes listening. Keep the numbers out of it and the point simple.

Some C2 speakers are far stronger orally than in writing, and some read English fluently but have little experience of reading non-standard words. L1 watch-outs
  • "My English is the correct one." Learners schooled only in RP or only in General American may treat the other (and all outer-circle varieties) as errors. Learners who grew up with Indian, Nigerian, Philippine, Singaporean or Caribbean English may have been told their own English is wrong. Frame everything as variety; never mark an established outer-circle feature as an error in this module.
  • Mixed spelling: learners educated in British-influenced systems (much of Africa, South Asia, Europe, the Middle East) often mix colour and color, organise and organize in one text, especially with a US spell-checker. The C2 standard is consistency.
  • Hearing the r: speakers of Japanese and Korean (no r/l contrast), and of Mandarin, may find rhoticity hard to perceive; speakers of Spanish, Arabic, Russian or Italian (trilled or tapped r) may hear every English r as "weak." Use minimal listening pairs (car / cah) from recordings; perception, not production.
  • Hearing the flap: learners whose L1 has a tap (Spanish pero, Japanese r) may hear American water as "waro" or "wader" and think it's an r; tell them they're hearing a real sound: it's the same tap.
  • Rhythm: speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Turkish, Cantonese, Yoruba, Tagalog) may find their own English described as "syllable-timed." Reassure: it's a description, and syllable-timed English is highly intelligible internationally.
  • "American language" (Spanish el americano, Russian американский): say American English. Likewise British English, not "English English."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: theme vs subject. The subject is what happens; the theme is the concern the text keeps returning to. A good theme statement is specific, arguable and supported.

"The subject is a fishing trip; the theme is the silence between father and son." Which one could you photograph? (The subject.) Which one could a reader argue about? (The theme.) · "The poem is about the sea." Theme or subject? (Subject.) · "The poem is about love." Arguable? (Not really: it's too broad. Everyone agrees, and nobody learns anything.) · "The poem treats memory as something that both preserves and distorts." Theme? (Yes: specific, and someone could disagree.)

Meaning: narrator vs author; unreliability; ironic voice. The voice telling the story is constructed. An unreliable narrator reveals more than they intend; an ironic narrative voice says one thing and means another.

"I was perfectly calm — anyone would have done as I did, and only a coward would say otherwise." Was he calm? (Probably not.) What gives it away? (He protests too much; he attacks imaginary critics.) Is the author a coward-hater? (We don't know: the author built the voice so we'd see through it.) · Austen: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." Is it really universal? (No.) Who actually wants the marriage? (The families with daughters.) So is the sentence sincere? (No: it's ironic.)

Meaning: technique → effect → theme. Naming a device is not interpretation. The interpretation is what it does, and why that matters.

"There is imagery of cold." Interpretation? (Not yet: it's only a label.) What's missing? (The effect, and the theme it serves.) · "The cold imagery makes the reunion feel like a funeral, which foreshadows the estrangement." How many steps? (Three: technique, effect, theme.)

Form.

Strip → spine → restoreBracket the extras: [relative clauses] [participle phrases] [asides between dashes or commas] → find subject + verb → put the pieces back one by one → paraphrase in one plain sentenceI went to the woods [because I wished to live deliberately, …] → I went to the woods + why

Pronunciation. Reading literature aloud is a skill in itself; this is about pausing and stress, never accent. For verse, pause at punctuation and dashes, not at every line end (Dickinson's commas in "Yet, never, in extremity" are breaths). For long prose sentences, chunk at the clause boundaries you found when you stripped the sentence, and let the voice rise through the suspended part and fall on the landing. Word stress in the metalanguage: ALlusion → alLUsion (second syllable), Irony, SYMbolism, juxTApoSItion, foreSHAdowing, inTERpreTAtion, MOtif → moTIF (both are heard; American usually stresses the second).

Close-Reading Seminar — how to run it
  • The texts (all public domain; see materials Set D): A1 Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813), opening · A2 Jerome K. Jerome, Three Men in a Boat (1889), the British Museum · B1 Dickens, Bleak House (1853), the fog · B2 Thoreau, Walden (1854), "I went to the woods…" · C1 Dickinson, "Hope is the thing with feathers" (published 1891) · C2 Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916), stanzas 2–4. Six groups of 3. With fewer learners, use pair A or C only.
  • Round 1, Annotate (10 min): read the text three times (surface; slowly for word choice; aloud, quietly, for rhythm). Annotation key: underline images · circle key words and repeated words · [bracket] interruptions in long sentences · → arrows from pronouns to referents · ? in the margin for anything uncertain. Then answer the four prompts on the card and write, on the worksheet, one arguable theme sentence and one technique → effect → theme chain with a quotation.
  • Round 2, Seminar (14 min): merge paired groups (A1 + A2, etc.). Hand out role cards: discussion leader (opens with a question, keeps turns fair, speaks last with a summary), evidence keeper (may only ask "Where does it say that?" and "Which word?"), alternative reader (must offer one different, defensible reading), connector (links the two texts: "Both narrators…"). Remaining learners are contributors. Group 1's text: 1 min read aloud + 6 min discussion; then Group 2's.
  • Round 3, "Show me the words" peer review (5 min): each group gives its theme sentence and chain to the other group in the merge. Reviewers check: Is the theme arguable (could someone disagree)? Does every claim quote specific words? Is there an effect, not just a label? One strength, one suggestion, quoting the text.
  • Model answers for every text are in the materials (for you, folded back) and in the answer key. Accept any reading the words support. "Two well-supported readings can coexist" is a core message of this class.
ICQs before the seminar
In Round 1, how many times do you read? (Three.) What do you write at the end? (One arguable theme and one chain.) What must the chain include? (A quotation, an effect and the theme.) · In Round 2, can the evidence keeper give opinions? (No: only "Where does it say that?") Who speaks last? (The discussion leader.) What does the alternative reader do? (Offers a different reading, with evidence.) · Is there one right answer? (No, but every answer needs the words.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give learners who read more slowly the Dickinson text (short lines, everyday words) or the Frost text, with the three glossed words (abash, extremity, wanted wear) in the margin. Offer a sentence-frame strip: "The ___ evokes ___, which suggests ___." In the seminar, the evidence-keeper role suits them well: asking "Where does it say that?" is powerful and needs no long turn. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 3 items 1–2. Target: one chain, spoken aloud with a quotation.

Stretch (C2+)

Free indirect discourse: in Austen, whose voice is the "truth universally acknowledged"? The narrator's, society's or Mrs. Bennet's? Ask strong learners to find the exact word where irony enters (property). With Frost, ask them to argue both readings (the celebratory popular one and the ironic one) and decide which the text supports better, citing "really about the same" and "with a sigh." Or: write a 100-word pastiche of the Dickens fog paragraph about their own city's weather, using verbless sentences and anaphora, then explain one effect.

L1 watch-outs
  • "The correct interpretation": learners schooled in traditions where the teacher or the textbook provides the meaning (common in many exam systems) may wait for your answer. Hold back: "What do the words let you say?" Praise defended disagreement.
  • Narrator = author: common everywhere, but reinforced where literature is taught mainly through the author's biography. Keep asking, "Does the text endorse this voice, or invite us to judge it?"
  • "Irony" as coincidence or bad luck: from everyday English and many L1 calques. Keep the definition tight: a gap between what is said and what is meant (or expected).
  • "The author wants to say" (Spanish quiere decir, Russian хочет сказать, German will sagen): upgrade to suggests, implies, invites us to see. Also "the poem speaks about" (Spanish habla de, Portuguese fala de): the poem explores / is concerned with.
  • Periodic sentences: speakers of verb-final languages (Japanese, Korean, Turkish, German subordinate clauses) are often comfortable waiting for the verb; learners from SVO languages may panic. Strip → spine helps both.
  • False friends in literary terms: Spanish argumento / French argument / Italian argomento (= plot or topic, not argument); French caractère (≠ character in a story, which is personnage); Spanish/Portuguese novela (can mean a TV soap, but also a novel). Check the word, not the look.

  2. Headline it: T: "The city council has approved the new bike lanes." → S: "COUNCIL BACKS BIKE LANES." T: "The museum will open a new wing." → "MUSEUM TO OPEN NEW WING." T: "Scientists are investigating a strange signal." → "SCIENTISTS PROBE MYSTERY SIGNAL."
  3. Name the allusion: T: "Math was always her ___ heel." → S: "Achilles'." T: "Opening that file was like opening ___ box." → "Pandora's." T: "He can't get the job without experience, and can't get experience without the job: it's a ___." → "Catch-22."
  4. Sincere or sarcastic? T reads "Oh, perfect." / "Thanks a lot." / "I just love waiting." with either intonation → S: "Sincere!" / "Sarcastic!" and name the cue.
Media Decoding Lab — how to run it
  • Set up four stations before class (materials Set E). Each station has a card with its task and a station sheet where every group writes one "gloss for a newcomer": a one- or two-sentence explanation that someone new to the culture could understand.
  • Groups of 3–4 with roles that rotate at every station: reader, scribe, time-keeper and newcomer (plays a person who has never met the reference and says honestly whether the gloss makes sense). The newcomer role is ideal for a dominant speaker: they may only ask questions.
  • Station 1, Headlines: six invented headlines. Rewrite three as full sentences; identify the pun or double meaning in two.
  • Station 2, Memes in words: four memes described in text. For each: what's the joke, what's the attitude (irony? self-mockery? frustration?), and when would someone share it?
  • Station 3, Allusion hunt: a short invented newsletter article with five allusions. Find, gloss and give the source of each.
  • Station 4, Slang & register: sort twelve cards into dated / established / current, then rewrite a slang-heavy text to a friend as a two-sentence email to a manager.
  • Rotation: 4 minutes per station. Ring a bell at 3:30. Groups may not finish every item; that's fine (ride the gist!).
  • "Gloss it for a newcomer" test (6 min): at its final station, each group reads the glosses the other groups left. Check: accurate? understandable without the culture? respectful (no mockery of the community a word comes from)? One strength, one suggestion.
ICQs before the Media Decoding Lab
How long at each station? (Four minutes.) Must you finish everything? (No.) What do you leave at every station? (One gloss for a newcomer.) What does the newcomer do? (Asks questions, says honestly if the gloss works.) At Station 4, who is the email for? (A manager: no slang.) At the end, what three things do you check in a gloss? (Accurate, understandable, respectful.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Headlines and memes are short, which helps. Give learners who read slowly the headline-rules strip (no articles · present = past · to = future · short verbs) and let them focus on Stations 1 and 4. The allusion article is the densest text: pre-teach two allusions from it (Achilles' heel, Trojan horse) during the reference stage. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 5 items 1–5. Target: two headlines rewritten as sentences and one clear newcomer gloss.

Stretch (C2+)

Why slang dates: ask strong learners to explain the life cycle (in-group → spread → mainstream → adopted by brands and parents → "cringe" → retro) with one example. Or: write three headlines of their own for real, light news from the week (workplace, science, sport, arts) with at least one pun, and explain each pun. Or: find a reference in a show they watch and write a 60-word gloss for a classmate from a different country, then test it on them. Link to C2 2.2: which comic mode (deadpan, understatement, satire, wordplay) does each meme use?

L1 watch-outs
  • Dated textbook idioms as "cool English": many learners were taught "It's raining cats and dogs," "the bee's knees," "groovy" as living idioms. Place them honestly on the timeline; praise the curiosity, not the usage.
  • Sarcasm read literally: learners from communication cultures where sarcasm is rare in public or signaled differently (for example, some East and Southeast Asian contexts), and speakers of tonal languages (Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese, Thai), where pitch carries word meaning, may not attach attitude to pitch in English. Contrast the two readings explicitly.
  • British understatement ("not bad at all," "a bit of a problem") heard as literal: link to C2 2.2 and 3.3 in C1. In media, a flat "Brilliant." is often the harshest line in the scene.
  • Reductions in writing: learners who pick up gonna, wanna, kinda from media sometimes write them in emails. Clear rule: recognize everywhere, write only in texts to friends, never in formal writing.
  • "Actual" = current (Spanish actual, French actuel, German aktuell, Russian актуальный): "This slang is very actual" → current, up to date, topical. Also "on the TV" → on TV, and "in the internet" → on the internet.
  • Allusion sources: allusions to Greek myth and Shakespeare may be familiar to learners from European schooling and unfamiliar to others; allusions to American film and TV depend on exposure, not education. Make it clear that not knowing a reference is never a sign of low English.

Final assessment (C26) covers all eight modules. From this module, expect: naming and glossing variety features from a transcript (without judging them), British/American false friends and spelling consistency, a short close reading (theme, one technique → effect → theme chain, with quotation), decoding a headline and an allusion, placing slang by register, writing the full form of fast-speech reductions, and reading sarcasm in context.
