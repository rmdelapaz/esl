Module 4 — The Complete Communicator: Answers

Answers to every Module 4 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the persuasive speech spine, one event in three voices, the written re-pitch), and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 4.1–4.3Classes C10–C129 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect, not accuracy alone. Almost every item in this module is a rewrite, an analysis or a creative task, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps the meaning and every fact, (2) is precise and natural (a figure whose parts are genuinely parallel; a fallacy named for the right reason; a cut that keeps the nuance), and (3) achieves the intended effect for the intended reader or listener. In analysis tasks, accept a different label when the learner can justify it: figures and appeals overlap, and "judge by the dominant effect" is the online lesson's own rule. When a learner's version is correct but a little flat, accept it and push for effect: "Good. Now make the last word the strongest one." American English is the model; accept British spellings (recognise, colour, humour, practise as a verb) and British usage (a pound, a quid).

4.1 · Rhetoric & Persuasion (C10)

Exercise 1 — "Lend It, Don't Buy It" (Script 4.1-A)

a) A tool library in the community-center basement, run by volunteers, free to every household. We should trust her because she was a carpenter for thirty-one years (ethos). She anticipates that tools will come back broken, or not at all.

b)Missing wordsFigure / appeal
1sawethos (credential)
2use · storetricolon (we buy it, we use it once, and we store it), with a wry, ironic edge
3asset · costantithesis (plus a metaphor: a cost with a handle)
4I'll tell you whyhypophora (accept "rhetorical question")
5more · lessantithesis
6share the know-howanaphora + tricolon
7neighborhoodrhetorical question (pathos: shared values)

c) 1 her credentials · 2 the proposal · 3 the survey figures · 4 why the figures matter (the warrant) · 5 a story about a neighbor (pathos) · 6 the objection granted · 7 the objection answered · 8 the proposal restated. (The counterfeit line sits between 7 and 8.)

d) "Besides, everyone I've spoken to on this street already agrees with me, so the matter is really settled." Bandwagon (appeal to popularity); accept hasty generalization too (her conversations are not a representative sample). Why it matters: it's the one line that asks the audience to stop thinking, and in an otherwise honest speech it risks the credibility she built. A good revision: "Several neighbors have already offered to volunteer."

Exercise 2 — Name the appeal and the figure
  1. Ethos (forty years' experience), with pathos (the hands). Figures: anaphora (I have… I have…) and an antithesis (held vs only read about).
  2. Logos in form but really pathos by rhythm (accept logos with a reason). Figures: anaphora (We cannot…) + tricolon; the repeated our way to is parallel structure.
  3. Logos: data, replication, mechanism. No figure; that's the point.
  4. Pathos framed as reasoning. Rhetorical question built on antithesis (strongest / weakest).
  5. Ethos: admitting a past error signals honesty and fairness. No figure (accept "a concession to the other side").
  6. Pathos (accept ethos: shared values). Chiasmus (pass the time / time does not pass us by) with antithesis.

Judge by the dominant effect; overlaps are expected. Push strong learners to say why an extract that looks like logos (item 2) is really working on feeling.

Exercise 3 — Recast a flat claim (models)
  1. Learning a language as an adult sharpens the memory, widens the circle, and humbles the ego in the best possible way.
  2. We should not wait for the problem to arrive and then fight it; we should build so that it never arrives. / Our task is not to cure the problem, but to prevent it.
  3. This library deserves hours that fit working lives. This library deserves seats for everyone who comes. This library deserves a connection that doesn't give up at the first download.
  4. How many more months can we afford to spend deciding whether to decide? / If not now, when?

Reward parallel grammar (three -s verbs in 1; This library deserves + noun + relative clause in 3), a clean landing on the strongest word, and a falling tone on 4 when said aloud. Wrong: a "tricolon" of four items or three items with different grammar (sharpens the mind, for friends, and being humble); an "antithesis" with no real opposition.

Exercise 4 — Name the fallacy and counter it (models)
  1. False dilemma. "Those aren't the only options: a cheaper tool, a shared license or a trial would all sit in between."
  2. Ad hominem (attacking her credentials rather than the advice). "Her restaurant history isn't the question; the advice is. Does the recipe work?" (Some learners call it the reverse of an appeal to authority: accept that as a thoughtful answer.)
  3. Slippery slope. "Each step would need its own justification. What mechanism makes flexible hours lead to nobody turning up?"
  4. Bandwagon. "Popularity isn't proof. What does the template actually do better than the alternatives?"
  5. Hasty generalization. "Two engineers aren't a pattern. What does a wider sample show?"
  6. Straw man. "That's not what I said. I suggested fewer meetings, not none."

Reward a counter that is calm and redirects to the actual claim. A counter that insults back ("Well, you can't even run a meeting") is itself an ad hominem: point that out with a smile.

Exercise 5 — Concede narrowly, refute decisively (models)
  1. I grant that some genuinely urgent problems will arise on a Wednesday afternoon. But an emergency channel handles those, and protecting four hours of focus each week doesn't stop anyone answering a real emergency.
  2. There is force in the objection that museums would lose some ticket income on those evenings. That may be so, yet it does not follow that they would lose money overall: first-time visitors buy memberships, coffee and gifts, and some come back and pay.
  3. I readily accept that most home cooks don't own a kitchen scale today. But a scale costs less than one ruined birthday cake, and a recipe can print both weights and cups while cooks make the switch.

Check the narrow concession (some, a few, today) and the decisive refutation (a reason, not just a repeated claim). Too wide: "I agree urgent issues can't wait, so maybe we should keep meetings" (the claim disappears). No concession at all: "Urgent issues are not important" (partisan; the audience discounts it).

Dialogue — the meeting-free afternoon

Concessions: I'll grant you the calendars (Marco) · Admittedly, some decisions can't wait (Leila). Refutations: But meetings are where decisions get made… · Yet it doesn't follow that the other nine were useless. Figures: metaphor (a mosaic with no picture), simile (looks like a crossword), and Leila's antithesis (whether we meet because we must, or because we always have). Logos: two out of eleven. The disagreement narrows when Leila reframes the question (not meetings vs no meetings, but why we meet) and Marco proposes a trial: a model of how honest rhetoric can move both sides.

Persuasion Lab — a model spine (Topic 5, FOR)
Opening (ethos)"I've spent twenty years in kitchens where one spoonful too many could ruin a hundred pastries, so forgive me if I care about measurement."
ClaimRecipes should give weights, not cups.
Grounds (logos)A "cup" of flour can vary by about a quarter depending on how it's scooped; professional kitchens, and most of the world, already weigh.
WarrantA recipe is a promise: follow it and it will work. A measurement that varies by a quarter breaks the promise.
Concession"I grant that most home cooks don't own a scale today."
Refutation"But a kitchen scale costs less than one ruined birthday cake, and a recipe can print both while we make the switch."
Close"Cups measure how you scooped; grams measure what you meant. Weigh it once, and it works every time. Isn't that what a recipe is for?" (antithesis · rhetorical question · restated claim)

The model has three figures, all at the hinges. Its proudest line (Cups measure how you scooped; grams measure what you meant) passes the flat-reading test: read flatly, it still makes a claim.

Self-check
Q1
"Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country." Which pair of figures is at work?
A) Anaphora and climax
B) Tricolon and epistrophe
C) Antithesis and chiasmus
Why: the two halves oppose each other (antithesis) and mirror the order country → you / you → country (chiasmus, A-B-B-A). There are two parts, not three (not a tricolon), and no escalating sequence (not a climax).
Q2
"You can't trust his report on energy use; he drives a huge car." Which fallacy is this?
A) False dilemma
B) Ad hominem
C) Slippery slope
Why: it attacks the person's behavior instead of the report's evidence. Even if he is inconsistent, the report could still be right. Counter: "His car isn't the question; the data are."
Q3 · new
Tools will come back broken. Which reply concedes narrowly and refutes decisively?
A) "You're completely wrong…"
B) "I grant that some tools may come back damaged; but a small repair fund costs far less than every household buying its own."
C) "You're right… so perhaps we shouldn't bother."
Why: B grants a small, true point (some tools) and answers it with a cost comparison. A concedes nothing and sounds partisan; C concedes so much that the claim collapses.
Errors you will meet most (4.1)

Precision: "tricolon" for lists of four or two · anaphora and epistrophe confused · any repetition called "anaphora" · "rhetorical question" for a genuine question · "ad hominem" for any rude remark (it must replace the argument). Appropriacy: pathos that outruns the evidence; three rhetorical questions in a row; soaring figures in what should be a plain report; a "concession" that gives away the claim. Naturalness: "I will explain you why," "Let's discuss about," "as a conclusion," "actually" for currently, "eventually" for possibly, "according to me." Delivery: rising intonation on rhetorical questions; a tricolon with no pause before the last item. For range, praise a well-placed hypophora at the pivot, a chiasmus that sounds effortless, and a concession phrased generously (There is real force in that objection).

4.2 · Style, Voice & Creative Command (C11)

Exercise 1 — One fire drill, three voices (Script 4.2-A)
FeatureABC
exact times and numbers✔(✔ twenty-six minutes)✔

Names: A the Reporter · B the Lyric Essayist · C the Deadpan Wit. Accept any name that captures the voice (the journalist, the poet, the comedian). Accept a tick for B's time phrase if learners hear it; the Essayist uses numbers as texture, not as data. A's last sentence (described the evacuation as orderly) is reported speech, not opinion: the Reporter stays neutral.

Exercise 2 — Name the shape
  1. loose (point first, then elaboration)
  2. simple (short, one clause)
  3. complex (subordinate + main clause)
  4. periodic (the main clause the train arrived held back to the end)
  5. compound (two main clauses joined by and)

After a very long sentence: 2, "Nobody came." The contrast in length makes the short sentence land like a drumbeat. Accept 4 as "periodic" or "complex": periodic is the more precise answer.

Exercise 3 — Cut to the lean, graceful form (models)
  1. If the weather turns bad, we'll cancel. (Formal: "If the weather turns bad, the event will be canceled.")
  2. Her long experience let her help the newer team members. / Because she was so experienced, she could help the newer members of the team.
  3. Every applicant must now submit a complete application.
  4. The report probably contains factual errors. (One hedge survives: probably.)
  5. The committee will review the proposal and recommend what to do with it. / …and recommend its future.

Many defensible trims exist. If a learner keeps a nuance the model dropped (now in 3, a softer may in 4), accept it: economy serves meaning, not the word count. Too far: "Weather bad: cancel." (telegram prose) · "Experienced; helped newcomers." Not far enough: keeping each and every, full and complete, it seems that perhaps.

Exercise 4 — Give a monotone passage its music (model)

The market opened at seven. By half past, the stalls were heaped with fruit, the sellers were shouting their prices over one another, and the air was thick with bread and coffee and the promise of a slow Saturday. A small boy, one hand firmly in his father's, pointed at a pyramid of oranges. It fell. Everyone laughed.

Machinery: a short opener, a long accumulating middle sentence (three clauses and a tricolon of smells), a loose sentence for the boy, then two very short sentences. Check that every fact survives (seven o'clock, fruit, prices, bread and coffee, the boy and his father, the oranges, the fall, the laughter). Judge by ear: read both aloud. Common problem: every sentence made long, which is a new monotone.

Exercise 5 — Voice fingerprints
  1. The Reporter: exact times (8:15, 7:50, noon), a number (forty passengers), passive and neutral verbs, no opinion.
  2. The Deadpan Wit: understatement (There was, I should say, some wind), a stance hedge (I should say), the twist (recovered our hats).
  3. The Lyric Essayist: a periodic opening (Out on the gray water…, the wind was writing…), a sustained metaphor (the wind writing on the waves, trying to read it), a short closing line (We never did.).

Accept grey (British) as well as gray in learners' own sentences. The final open sentence: accept any voice that is consistent across the whole sentence.

Dialogue — killing a darling

Karim praises first ("It's lovely."), asks permission ("Would you like a suggestion?"), gives a reason rooted in voice (the rest of the piece is warm and plain; the line "sounds like a different writer") and offers a specific fix. The darling: that patient cathedral of the unhurried mind. The replacement (The chairs are new. The quiet is not.) works because it is concrete, short, witty (an antithesis with a small twist) and consistent with the plain voice of a newsletter. Ines thanks him instead of defending: the Critique Circle's last rule.

Style & Voice Workshop — one event, three voices (model, Event 4: the bus)

The Reporter. The 7:42 bus to the city center arrived at 7:40 on Monday and departed immediately. Twelve passengers walking toward the stop were left behind. The next service arrived at 8:05. Two of the twelve, who had not met before, said they had become friends while they waited.

The Lyric Essayist. Two minutes before its promise, lit and indifferent, humming with the warmth of people luckier than us, the 7:42 slid away from the curb. Twelve of us stood in the cold and watched it go. Then the waiting. Twenty-three minutes is a long time to share a bench with a stranger; long enough, it turns out, to stop being strangers.

The Deadpan Wit. The 7:42 bus, keen as ever, arrived at 7:40 and left without us. Twelve of us admired its punctuality from a short distance. The 8:05 was less ambitious. I would like to thank the transit authority for the twenty-three minutes it gave me to make a new friend, a service not listed on the timetable.

All four facts appear in every version. What to praise: the Reporter's discipline (no unfortunately, no sadly), the Essayist's periodic opening and quiet last line, the Wit's straight face and last-word twist. What to question: a Reporter who editorializes, an Essayist who is merely formal, a Wit who explains the joke or aims the irony at a person.

Self-check
Q1
Most economical yet graceful revision of "Due to the fact that we had reached a decision, we made an announcement to the public"?
A) Having decided, we announced it publicly.
B) Decision. Announcement.
C) Because we had reached a decision, we made a public announcement.
Why: A frees both smothered verbs (reached a decision → decided, made an announcement → announced) and cuts the deadweight due to the fact that, and it still reads gracefully. B is telegram prose; C keeps both weak verb + noun pairs.
Q2
Five medium-length sentences in a row and the paragraph plods. The classic rhythm fix?
A) Replace plain words with longer synonyms.
B) Add adjectives to every sentence.
C) Fuse a couple into one longer sentence and drop in a short one for impact.
Why: the problem is uniform length and shape, so the cure is variety. A and B change the vocabulary, not the rhythm, and push the prose toward purple.
Q3 · new
"At this point in time, it is really important to note that each and every volunteer should, if at all possible, arrive early." Which revision cuts the deadwood without going terse?
A) Volunteers: early.
B) Every volunteer should arrive early if possible.
C) Now, importantly, every single volunteer should really try to arrive early.
Why: B removes the deadweight phrase, the throat-clearing, the redundant pair and the padded hedge while keeping the meaning and the courtesy (if possible). A is telegram prose; C swaps old deadwood for new (importantly, every single, really try).
Errors you will meet most (4.2)

Precision: "periodic" used for any long sentence (the main clause must be withheld) · "very unique," "more perfect," "return back," "repeat again" · a "fragment" that is really an accidental incomplete sentence. Appropriacy: a Reporter who editorializes; purple prose in the Essayist (three adjectives per noun); fragments carried into formal writing; sarcasm aimed at people in the Wit. Naturalness: "It was raining strongly" (heavily), "The bus made a delay" (was late), "make a photo" (take), "the most part of" (most of), comma splices from long L1 periods. Effect: cutting so hard that grace and nuance go (telegram prose), or keeping a darling because it was hard to write. For range, praise a periodic sentence that genuinely builds suspense, a short sentence placed exactly after a long one, a freed verb that is more precise than the original noun, and critique language that quotes and asks permission.

4.3 · Review Project — Mastery in Action (C12)

Exercise 1 — A decision log for every team (Script 4.3-A)
a) Hook"Raise your hand if you have ever left a meeting certain that something had been decided, and arrived at the next one to discover that nobody agrees what." (a question to the audience, then "I thought so.")
Turn"Admittedly, nobody went into healthcare to fill in forms… That said, a line in the log takes about ninety seconds."
Landing"It won't make us wiser. But it will make us honest: about what we chose, why we chose it, and when we'll look again." (antithesis + tricolon)

b) mot juste: not a transcript… but a ledger · register shift: In plain English: we stop arguing about what we argued about. (also the most satisfying spreadsheet of my career) · fixed expression: by and large, part and parcel · understatement: was, shall we say, memorable. For some of the wrong reasons. · metaphor: memory is a generous storyteller and a poor accountant (also an antithesis) · stance markers: Frankly, Admittedly, To my mind · ethos: eleven years in hospital pharmacies · logos: the three-month ward trial, fell by roughly half · tricolon: the landing (also what we decided, who decided it, why). Also accept hypophora (Why does this matter? Because…).

c) He paraphrases before answering: "If I read your question rightly, the worry is that the log becomes the work instead of recording it," then concedes ("That's a fair challenge") and counters (one line, ninety seconds; if it takes longer, fix the design). The question beneath Tom's question is "Will this be used to blame people?"; Daniel names it and answers it (decisions, not culprits; the log protects people).

Exercise 2 — Name the mastery move
  1. Irony / understatement, with a stance hedge (shall we say); optimistic damns by understatement.
  2. Concession + cohesion: a stance-marked concessive turn (For all its flaws…) that binds and pivots; the parenthetical and there are many adds irony.
  3. Antithesis (not… the loudest voice, but… the last one still standing) carrying a metaphor (still standing); logos in shape.
  4. Implicature: damning with faint praise (energy is the only thing conceded, so everything else is implied to be lacking).
  5. Synthesis (across three reports) with an antithesis (incentives, not intentions).

Defensible variants exist: judge by which device does the heavy lifting.

Exercise 3 — Layer up a flat line (models)
  1. "Frankly, constant notifications don't merely interrupt our concentration; they auction it off, a few seconds at a time." Layers: stance marker (Frankly) · antithesis (don't merely…; they…) · metaphor (auction it off) · rhythm (the weighted final phrase).
  2. "To my mind, good teachers are the quiet architects of every profession, and we remember them far better than we reward them." Layers: stance marker · metaphor (quiet architects) · antithesis (remember / reward) · the mot juste (quiet).

Accept any version with three or more layers that work together. Flag over-orchestration (a metaphor, an idiom, a tricolon and a register drop fighting in one sentence) and mixed metaphors.

Exercise 4 — One idea, two rooms (models)
  1. Expert: "The evidence suggests that scheduled short breaks may substantially reduce error rates in data-entry tasks." · Lay: "Take a few minutes off every hour or so and you'll make fewer mistakes. It really works."
  2. Expert: "Shared reading in early childhood is strongly associated with gains in expressive vocabulary." · Lay: "Read to your kids out loud. It's one of the best ways to give them more words, and they'll love it."

Check that register, density, idiom and rhetoric shift together: hedged, nominal, impersonal and Latinate for the expert room; direct address, short sentences, contractions and warmth for the lay room. The substance must not change: the lay version may not promise more than the evidence (guarantees, always).

Exercise 5 — Judge the adaptation
  1. B: warm, personal, humble, with a light touch of wit; A is clinical and wrong for a celebration.
  2. B: logos-forward, precise and hedged for scientists; A is pathos-heavy anaphora that a funding panel will distrust.
  3. B: warm, grateful and concrete (the good gloves); A is legalistic (pursuant to, mandatory) and cold for volunteers.

The point of all three: the rejected openings are grammatically flawless. At C2, the flawless sentence in the wrong room is the real failure.

Dialogue — the question beneath the question

Paraphrase: If I read your question rightly, the worry is about seriousness, not breakage. Concessions: That's a fair challenge · Granted, and yet… · I take your point, and I'd go further. Counters: the scholars who learned by handling things, and the antithesis touch doesn't make the encounter less serious; it makes it more memorable. The question beneath Amara's second question is really about supervision and resources; Joel answers it with it needs staff, not just a table of replicas and the fixed expression part and parcel.

The written re-pitch — a model (Audience 5: a skeptical manager · about 170 words)

Subject: A one-line decision log: a four-week trial?
Dear Ms. Reyes,
I would like to propose a small experiment for our team: a shared decision log, with one line per decision recording what we decided, who decided, why, and when we will review it. On our ward, a three-month trial roughly halved the number of emails disputing earlier decisions.
One colleague questioned whether the log would simply add bureaucracy. It is a fair concern. However, each entry takes about ninety seconds, whereas reopening a settled decision typically costs us an hour of meeting time. If an entry ever takes longer, the format should be simplified, not the idea abandoned.
Another raised the concern that names in the log might be used to assign blame. The log would record decisions, not culprits; if anything, it would show that each decision was reasonable given the information available at the time.
Could we trial it for four weeks and review the results at our next monthly meeting?
Best regards,
Daniel

Why it works for this reader: evidence first, neutral register, both challenges reported fairly with precise reporting verbs (questioned whether, raised the concern that) and answered, a small, low-risk request. The speech's flourishes (Raise your hand…, a generous storyteller and a poor accountant) are gone because this reader wants a decision, not a performance. For a newspaper op-ed, the metaphor would come back.

Mastery rubric and speech planner

No single answer. When you read learners' self-assessments, look for specific evidence ("my rhetorical question rose instead of falling, so two people tried to answer it") rather than labels ("fluency 4"), and for honest calibration: C2 learners often under-score delivery and over-score idiomatic naturalness. Compare with your checklist (materials Set F) in C13 and discuss one dimension where your scores differ.

Self-check
Q1
Same technical finding, a specialist panel and then a general audience. The masterful adaptation?
A) The identical script both times.
B) Keep the substance, but re-pitch register, density, idiom and rhetoric for each room.
C) Simplify crudely for the public; pile on jargon for the experts.
Why: mastery keeps the substance constant and changes the delivery. A ignores the room; C distorts the substance (crude for one, obscure for the other).
Q2
"Their generosity, if we can call it that, extended to a single, grudging cup of tea." Which move is doing the most work?
A) Irony and understatement (with a stance hedge)
B) Extended metaphor
C) Summary and synthesis
Why: generosity is ironic, if we can call it that is a stance hedge that signals the irony, and a single, grudging cup of tea understates the meanness. There is no sustained image (B) and nothing is summarized (C).
Q3 · new
"Honestly, it's a no-brainer" → a briefing note for a hospital board. Best re-pitch?
A) Honestly, it's a no-brainer.
B) The benefits would appear to outweigh the modest costs involved.
C) It's a total no-brainer, to be honest with you.
Why: B is formal, hedged and specific about the reasoning (benefits vs costs), which a board can act on. A and C keep the informal idiom and the casual stance marker; C adds even more informality.
Errors you will meet most (4.3)

Architecture: no genuine turn (a speech that never concedes) · a landing that simply stops ("So, yeah, that's it"). Adaptation: the written piece is a summary of the speech rather than a re-pitch · idioms and contractions carried into a formal re-pitch · condescension in the teenagers' version · the challenge omitted or reported unfairly ("Some people wrongly think…"). Q&A: answering before understanding · countering without conceding, or conceding until the position disappears. Over-orchestration: layers that fight; mixed metaphors under pressure. Fossils under pressure: "discuss about," "since three years," "informations," "I will explain you," "according to me." For range, praise a layer that was felt rather than spotted, an answer to the question beneath the question, a reporting verb chosen with care (conceded that, objected that), and a register shift made on purpose, then explained.
