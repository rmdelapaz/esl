Module 4 — The Complete Communicator: Style & Rhetoric

Three classes that turn command into effect. First, a rhetoric and persuasion lab: the three classical appeals (ethos, pathos, logos), the named figures that make a line memorable (tricolon, anaphora, antithesis, the rhetorical question), the architecture of a case that survives attack (claim → grounds → warrant → concession → refutation) and the counterfeit moves a master refuses and names (straw man, false dilemma, ad hominem). Then a style and voice workshop: rhythm and sentence shape, economy (omit needless words), strong verbs, and voice, practiced by writing one small event in three distinct voices and critiquing each other's drafts. Last, Mastery in Action, the halfway review: a short speech, a live Q&A and a re-pitched written piece that orchestrate everything from Modules 1–4, scored on a checklist that feeds Progress Test 1 in C13.

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop (a speech, a set of stylistic rewrites, a performance) → feedback with delayed correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-rhetoric.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C10. It holds the flashcards (appeals, figures, fallacies, sentence shapes, deadwood, editing passes and six picture event cards), the new identity cards, the Persuasion Lab topic and side cards with the speech spine sheet and device-hunt grid, the Style & Voice Workshop event cards, voice cards and Critique Circle card, the Mastery in Action topic, audience and challenge cards, the C12 teacher checklist (feeds C13), the Layer It! review game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and shadow their rhythm, which matters more in this module than in any other.
  • Run these as workshops, and protect the workshop. Roughly a quarter to a third of every class is one extended product: a two-minute persuasive speech (C10), one event in three voices plus a critique circle (C11), a speech, Q&A and written re-pitch (C12). If you're running late, cut the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. At C1 (Lesson 4.3) learners met the rule of three, contrast, the rhetorical question and signposting for a three-minute talk, and in C1 Module 3 they learned plain English and the "Who does what?" test. Don't re-teach any of it. The C2 step is deliberate effect with restraint: naming a figure and choosing it for a reason, balancing the three appeals, conceding on purpose, spotting manipulation, and cutting the line you love when it serves only itself.
  • Feedback on four things, not one. C2 learners rarely make errors that block meaning, so "accuracy" alone gives them almost nothing. Take notes in four columns: precision (the nearly-right word or figure: a tricolon of four items, anaphora used for what is really epistrophe, very unique), appropriacy (right device, wrong room: soaring anaphora in a status update, a joke at a moment that needed gravity), naturalness (grammatical but no English speaker would say it: I will explain you why, as a conclusion) and effect (did it land? where did the room lean in, and where did it drift?). The effect column is the most useful at this level, and the one learners almost never receive. In the feedback stage, board one item from each column, without names.
  • Near-native in some areas, fossilized in others. Your strongest learners may argue brilliantly and still say discuss about, informations or since three years, errors they have made for twenty years without anyone telling them. Keep a private fossil list per learner (one line each). Once a week, give each learner one fossil, privately, with the correct form and one sentence of their own rewritten: "You said We need to discuss about the budget. At C2 it's discuss the budget. That one's worth fixing for good." One fossil at a time is fixable; a list of twelve is discouraging.
  • Peer critique with a protocol, every class. Unstructured peer feedback at C2 collapses into politeness ("Very good!") or into a showcase for the most confident critic. Each workshop has its own protocol: the Device Hunt + flat-reading test (C10: listeners quote the devices they heard, then the speaker reads their best line flatly: if it only works with the performance, question it), the Critique Circle (C11: the author stays silent; critics say what stayed with them, the author asks one question, critics ask neutral questions, and only then offer one darling to kill and one fix, with permission) and the re-pitch check (C12: could the new reader act on this?). Critics always quote the text instead of summarizing it.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes attract accomplished people used to holding a room: lawyers, academics, managers. Give them the jobs that need listening, not talking: the fallacy watcher and timekeeper in C10, the last critic in C11 (they may speak only after everyone else, and may not repeat a point), the challenger who must paraphrase before asking in C12. Say it kindly: "Your ear for this is excellent. I want you on the fallacy card this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and a few British words (recognise, generalisation, defence, honour, practised, per cent, summarising, humour, colour, sceptical, theatres, pound). Teach recognize, generalization, defense, honor, practiced, percent, summarizing, humor, color, skeptical, theaters, dollar. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Persuade → sound like yourself → orchestrate. In C10 learners aim their language at an audience: an appeal chosen on purpose, a figure placed at a hinge, a concession that buys trust, and the critical armor to feel someone else's rhetoric working on them and still refuse it. In C11 they turn the same attention inward, to the sound of their own prose: rhythm, economy, strong verbs, and a voice consistent enough to be recognized. In C12 they do everything at once, for a real audience, and then re-pitch it for a different one. Every class ends with something another learner hears or reads: a two-minute speech, three short pieces in three voices, a speech with Q&A and a written re-pitch.

Trauma-informed: neutral topics, assigned sides, invented pathos

Rhetoric invites strong feelings. Every persuasion topic in this module stays in workplace, education, science, arts and culture, technology, language and community territory (a tool library, a meeting-free afternoon, handwriting, quiet carriages, museum opening hours). There are no topics about party politics, religion, immigration policy, war or national identity, and no learner is asked to argue about their own country or community. Sides are assigned on the card: learners argue the card, not their convictions, and may say so ("I've been assigned the case against"). Pathos is the risky appeal: model invented, low-stakes anecdotes (a neighbor waiting three months for a fence repair) and say explicitly that nobody should use a personal loss or hardship as their story. The C11 events are small everyday mishaps (a collapsed cake, a bus that left early), never accidents, illness or grief. The online 4.3 journal asks learners to write to "the person you were at the start"; for some newcomers that period was painful, so always offer "or a letter to a learner who is just starting out." Anyone can say "Pass," take a new identity card, or present only to you.

Rhetoric & Persuasion — The Art of Argument

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • identify the dominant appeal in a persuasive extract (ethos: credibility; pathos: emotion; logos: reason and evidence) and explain why a strong case braids all three
  • recognize and produce the core figures, tricolon, anaphora, antithesis and the rhetorical question, with parallel grammar and the right rhythm, and place them at the hinges of an argument (opening, pivot, close)

  • name common fallacies (straw man, ad hominem, false dilemma, slippery slope, bandwagon, hasty generalization) and counter them calmly by redirecting to the actual claim
  • judge when high rhetoric fits the occasion (a speech, an op-ed) and when it grates (a report, a status update)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the appeals. Every persuasive utterance draws on credibility, emotion or reason, usually more than one. The question is never "which appeal?" but "which one dominates, and is the balance right for this audience?"

"As a former hospital administrator, I know what a stretched budget feels like." Which appeal? (Ethos: experience, shared understanding.) Is it evidence? (No: it's a reason to trust the speaker.) · "Behind every statistic is a family waiting for a fence to be fixed." Which appeal? (Pathos.) Is that dishonest? (Not if the statistic is real; it becomes dishonest if the feeling replaces the evidence.) · A speech that is only numbers: will people act on it? (Rarely: logos alone leaves the will unmoved.)

Meaning: the figures. A figure is a patterned arrangement of words that pleases the ear and stresses the sense. It should clarify the argument, not decorate it.

"We buy it, we use it once, and we store it for a decade." How many parts? (Three.) Same grammar in each? (Yes: we + verb + object.) What's the effect? (Complete, memorable, a little ironic.) · "I'm not asking you to spend more; I'm asking you to own less." What's opposed? (Spend more / own less.) Name? (Antithesis.) · "Isn't that what a neighborhood is for?" Does she want an answer? (No: the listener supplies "Yes.") · Five figures in one paragraph: more persuasive? (No: nothing stands out when everything is figured.)

Meaning: the strategic concession. Granting the opponent's strongest fair point makes you look reasonable, disarms the objection before it's raised against you, and makes your refutation land.

"I grant that some tools may come back damaged. But a small repair fund costs far less than forty-two households each buying a drill." Does she agree with the objection? (Partly: the fact is true.) Does she give up her claim? (No.) Which part is narrow? (The concession: some tools.) Which part is decisive? (The refutation: the cost comparison.) · A speaker who never concedes anything: how do we see them? (As partisan: we discount them.)

Meaning: fallacies. A fallacy looks like an argument but proves nothing. The master refuses them and names them in others, calmly.

"You can't trust his report on the parking lot. He can't even park straight." Is that about the report? (No: it attacks the person.) Name? (Ad hominem.) How do you answer? ("His parking isn't the question; the report is. What's wrong with its figures?") · "Either we cancel the party or we go over budget." Only two options? (No: a cheaper party, a potluck, a later date.) Name? (False dilemma.)

Form.

Tricolonthree parallel elements, often rising in length or weightReading sharpens the mind, softens the heart, and widens the world.
Anaphorathe same words open successive clausesThis city deserves schools that… This city deserves transport that…
Epistrophethe same words end successive clauses…of the people, by the people, for the people.
Antithesiscontrary ideas in balanced, parallel grammar: not X but Y · X, not Y · It is not that…; rather…This is not a setback; it is an invitation to begin again.
Chiasmusmirror order, A-B-B-AWe do not work to live well; we live well to work well.
Rhetorical question · hypophoraa question for effect · a question the speaker answers at onceCan we really afford to wait? · Why does that matter? I'll tell you why.
Concede → refuteI grant that… / There is force in the objection that… / To be fair to the other side,… → But this overlooks… / That may be so, yet it does not follow that…Admittedly, culture is harder to measure. Yet is it the office that builds it, or the people?

Pronunciation. Figures live in the voice. Tricolon: rise, rise, fall, with a small pause before the last item: "It's cheaper ↗ | it's cleaner ↗ | and it's already here ↘." Antithesis: contrastive stress on the opposed words, and a pause at the hinge: "not to spend MORE | but to own LESS." Anaphora: keep the repeated opening at the same pitch and let the variable part carry the stress, so the ear hears the pattern. Rhetorical questions normally fall at the end ("Isn't that what a neighborhood is ↘ for?"); a rising tone sounds like a real question and invites an answer. Hypophora: a real pause after the question, then a lower, slower answer.

Drill script
  1. Three beats (choral, then individual; clap the three beats if the class enjoys it): T: "We cannot wait, we must not wait, and we will not wait." → S repeats with rise-rise-fall. T: "Share the drill, share the ladder, share the know-how." → S. Then learners complete a tricolon from a stem: T: "Good design is…" → S: "…simple, honest, and built to last."
  2. Hinge it (antithesis, individual): T gives a flat pair; S makes it balanced and stresses the opposed words. T: "Prevention is better than reaction." → S: "We should not wait for the fire to fight it; we should build so it never starts." T: "Owning less, sharing more." → S: "I'm not asking you to spend more; I'm asking you to own less."
  3. Fall, don't rise: T reads a rhetorical question with a rising tone; S corrects it with a fall. "Can we really afford to wait?" · "Isn't that what a neighborhood is for?" · "If we do nothing today, what do we tell our children tomorrow?"
  4. Concede → refute (open pairs, fast): T reads an objection ("Won't the tools get stolen?"); S1 concedes narrowly ("I grant that a few may go missing"), S2 refutes decisively ("but the evidence from other libraries is that borrowers bring things back, because their neighbors are waiting").
Persuasion Lab — how to run it
  • Topics and sides. Ten neutral topics on Set C, each with a for and against side card: a meeting-free afternoon every week, handwriting in adult education, free museum evenings, a tool library, recipes in weights rather than cups, quiet carriages on commuter trains, a four-day school timetable for adult classes, repair cafés, rotating who chairs meetings, and learning a musical instrument as an adult. Learners draw a topic and a side; they argue the card, not their convictions. A learner may swap once, no reason needed.
  • Two minutes, not four. The online project asks for 3–4 minutes; in class, two minutes per speaker lets three speakers perform and be critiqued inside 23 minutes, and forces economy: roughly 250–280 words. The full version is homework.
  • Notes, not a script. The spine sheet has one line per move. Only three lines are scripted in full: the opening, the pivot (concession → refutation) and the close, because that is where figures belong. Speakers mark their figures in the margin (T for tricolon, A for anaphora, X for antithesis, RQ for rhetorical question).
  • Device Hunt. Each listener has a device-hunt grid (Set C): three appeals and seven figures, plus "concession" and "counterfeit move." They tick what they hear and quote a few words so the speaker knows where. After each speech, one minute: listeners report ("I heard antithesis in the close: not louder, but clearer"). The speaker then reveals their marked script: devices that were present but not heard are interesting data, not failures.
  • The flat-reading test. Each speaker reads their proudest line in a completely flat voice. If the line still makes a claim that holds, it's rhetoric serving an argument. If it only worked with the performance, question it. This is the online lesson's "If a line survives being read flatly, keep it" made into a routine.
  • Roles. In each group of 3: speaker, timekeeper (also listens), fallacy watcher (also listens; flags any counterfeit move, kindly). Rotate after each speech. Give the fallacy watcher and timekeeper roles to dominant speakers first.
  • If a learner freezes, the timekeeper says "Thirty seconds: your close." A strong close after a shaky middle still earns applause.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 speakers argue far better aloud than on paper (often learners who have lived and worked in English for years with little formal schooling in it). Give them a spine strip with the six moves pre-printed and one frame for each ("I want to argue that… · Consider this: … · This matters because… · I grant that… · But… · So I come back to…") and accept one figure placed well (an antithesis in the close is enough). They can deliver from the strip without scripting anything. In the Device Hunt, let them tick without quoting. Fewer items: Workbook Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 4 items 1–3. Their ear is often the sharpest in the room for a line that "sounds fake": invite that judgment in the flat-reading test.

Stretch

Ask strong learners to stack figures in one line (antithesis + chiasmus: We do not share tools to save money; we save money because we share), to build a close with epistrophe, and to use hypophora at the pivot. Then the harder skill: restraint. Their two-minute speech may contain no more than four figures; they must say afterward which figure they cut and why. For the fallacy work, ask them to write a persuasive but fallacious paragraph (one hidden fallacy, dressed in an honest-looking tricolon) and challenge the group to find it. Discuss: the craft is neutral; the ethics are not.

L1 watch-outs
  • Where the claim goes: persuasive writing in Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Thai traditions often builds context first and states the position late, or leaves it implicit. English persuasion expects the claim early and restated at the end. Present it as a genre difference ("in this room, the claim comes first"), not as a flaw in their thinking.
  • Repetition and ornament: Arabic, Persian and Urdu rhetoric values parallelism, repetition and elaborate praise, and learners may produce beautiful anaphora three times in a row. Praise the control, then teach spacing: one anaphora per speech lands harder than three.
  • Syllable-timed rhythm: speakers of Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Tagalog, Turkish and Cantonese may give every syllable similar weight, which flattens a tricolon and hides contrastive stress. Drill the stressed words and the pause before the last item.
  • Rhetorical questions with a rise: many learners (and some world Englishes) rise at the end of every question, so the audience starts to answer. Teach the fall.
  • False friends in argument: Spanish argumento and Italian argomento (plot or topic) → "the argument of the film"; French discuter de and Spanish discutir sobre → "discuss about"; actualmente / actuellement / aktuell → "actually" meaning "currently"; eventualmente / éventuellement / eventuell → "eventually" meaning "possibly"; "as a conclusion" (como conclusión, en conclusion) → In conclusion / In short.
  • "I will explain you why" (Spanish te explico por qué, Italian ti spiego, Russian объясню вам): explain takes to + person, or none: Let me explain why. A classic fossil at this level.

4.2 · C11

Style, Voice & Creative Command

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • explain style as the sum of consistent choices (word, register, figure, rhythm, stance) and voice as the personality those choices project
  • vary sentence length and shape on purpose (simple, compound, complex, periodic, loose), place a short sentence or a fragment for impact, and use the read-aloud test
  • cut deadwood (redundant pairs, empty intensifiers, throat-clearing, deadweight phrases, hedging overload, clichés) without sliding into terse "telegram prose"
  • free smothered verbs (make a decision → decide) and prefer the concrete, active and specific by default, breaking the default knowingly
  • write the same event in three distinct, sustained voices, and give and receive precise, quoted critique on style and voice

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: style and voice. Style is the pattern that many small, consistent choices make; voice is the personality a reader hears in that pattern. Neither is more "correct"; both are judged by fitness and consistency.

Two writers report the same fire drill; one uses exact times and no adjectives, the other uses images and long sentences. Is one of them wrong? (No: different choices.) What makes each one a voice? (Consistency: every sentence leans the same way.) If the Reporter suddenly writes "like people released from a sentence," what happens? (The voice breaks: we stop trusting the persona.)

Meaning: periodic and loose sentences. A periodic sentence withholds the main clause until the end and builds suspense; a loose sentence delivers the point first and elaborates, which feels relaxed.

"When she rose, when she drew breath, when at last she spoke, the room fell silent." When do we get the main point? (At the end.) Effect? (Suspense, weight.) · "The room fell silent, all argument spent, every eye turned toward her." Main point where? (First.) Which one feels more conversational? (The loose one.) · Five periodic sentences in a row? (Exhausting: vary.)

Meaning: economy. Every word earns its place. The goal is not the fewest words but no dead ones.

"Every applicant must submit a complete application." What did we cut from "each and every one of the applicants is required to submit a full and complete application"? (Redundant pairs, padding.) Did we lose meaning? (No.) · "Her long experience let her help the newer team members." Could we cut to "Experienced; helped newcomers"? (Shorter, but telegram prose: it lost grace and rhythm.) · "The samples were contaminated." Passive: wrong? (No: the doer is unknown or irrelevant. Break the default knowingly.)

Meaning: kill your darlings. The line you're proudest of is the one to interrogate hardest.

A writer loves the line "the moon, that pale accountant of the night." Does it serve the piece about a delayed bus? (Probably not: it's ornament.) What do you do with it? (Cut it; save it for another piece.) Is that a loss? (A small one, and the piece gets stronger.)

Pronunciation. The read-aloud test is the pronunciation focus. Read long sentences in chunks, with a slight fall at each comma and breath at the dashes; let the voice drop and slow on the short sentence that follows ("Then everything ↘ changed."). A fragment needs a full stop in the voice: a clear pause before and after. English prose rhythm rewards sentences that end on a strong, stressed, concrete word ("…and we did not sleep"), not a weak preposition or abstraction. Read the Deadpan Wit version flatly: deadpan is a delivery as much as a style, and a smile in the voice gives the joke away.

Drill script
  1. Long, then short (choral, then individual): T reads a long accumulating sentence and pauses; learners say the short one in chorus. T: "The negotiations dragged on for months, through draft after weary draft, concession traded for concession until no one could recall who had wanted what." → Ss: "Then everything changed." T: "The rain fell." → Ss: "It fell hard." → T: "It fell all night," → Ss: "and we did not sleep."
  2. Cut it (individual, fast): T: "each and every" → S: "every." "first and foremost" → "first." "due to the fact that" → "because." "in the event that" → "if." "make an improvement to" → "improve." "at this point in time" → "now." "very unique" → "unique."
  3. Free the verb: T: "The committee will conduct an investigation into the matter and then reach a decision." → S: "The committee will investigate and decide." T: "We need to provide assistance to new members." → S: "We need to help new members."
  4. Three voices, one line (open pairs): T gives a fact ("The bus left two minutes early."); S1 says it as the Reporter, S2 as the Lyric Essayist, S3 as the Deadpan Wit. E.g.: "The 7:42 bus departed at 7:40." · "It slid away from us, lit and indifferent, two minutes before its promise." · "The bus, keen as ever, left without us."
Style & Voice Workshop — how to run it
  • The events (Set D, picture cards): a suitcase that went to the wrong city; an umbrella that turned inside out at the worst moment; a birthday cake that collapsed at an office party; a bus that came early and left without us; the last loaf at a Saturday market; a smoke alarm during a dinner for friends. Each card lists four fixed facts (time, place, what happened, how it ended). All three versions must keep the facts; voices change the telling, not the event.
  • The three core voices (voice cards): the Reporter (short declaratives, exact numbers and times, no opinion adjectives, third person or impersonal, past simple), the Lyric Essayist (first person, at least one periodic sentence and one image, reflective stance, a quiet closing line), the Deadpan Wit (understatement and irony from Module 2, flat delivery, the twist in the last few words, never explains the joke). 50–70 words each. The workbook frame has three boxes with a checklist under each.
  • Pastiche (stretch): six genre voice cards (the noir detective, the nature-documentary narrator, the sports commentator, the formal nineteenth-century letter-writer, the breathless friend on the phone, the minimalist). Early finishers write a fourth version in one of them. Pastiche imitates a manner, not anyone's actual sentences.
  • The Critique Circle (groups of 4, about 3 minutes per writer; the protocol card is in Set D): 0. Blind reading. Someone else reads the writer's three pieces aloud, in random order and unlabeled; the group names each voice and two features that gave it away. 1. What stayed with me. Each critic quotes one phrase that stayed with them and says why (no suggestions yet). 2. The writer's question. The writer, silent until now, asks one question about their own draft ("Did the Wit version land, or did I explain it?"). 3. Neutral questions. Critics may ask questions without opinions hidden inside ("Why did you choose slid?", not "Don't you think slid is too much?"). 4. One darling, one fix, with permission. "Would you like a suggestion?" If yes: one line that may be a darling (quote it) and one specific fix. The writer says "Thank you," not a defense.
  • Roles against dominance. The last critic speaks only after the others and may not repeat a point already made. The reader in step 0 is never the writer. Put the most dominant critic in the last-critic role.
  • What you're listening for as you circulate: voices that are really registers only (the Essayist that is merely formal), a Wit who uses sarcasm aimed at people rather than situations (redirect: irony at the situation, never at a person or a group), and precise, quoted critique. Note two examples of excellent critique language to praise in feedback.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a two-voice version of the task: the Reporter and the Deadpan Wit only, 30–40 words each, with sentence starters on a strip (Reporter: "At 3:15 on Friday, … · … minutes later, …" · Wit: "It was, I'm told, … · I am pleased to report that …"). The picture event card is the prompt; the facts can be said aloud and written with a partner as scribe. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–3, Ex 3 items 1–2. The read-aloud test suits these learners perfectly: often their ear for rhythm is better than their eye.

Stretch

Ask strong learners to (1) write a fourth version in a genre voice (pastiche) and then a fifth that diverges from it: keep one habit of the imitated manner and replace the rest with their own; (2) write one paragraph that shifts voice on purpose once, and justify the shift; (3) run the six editing passes on one of their pieces and keep all drafts, noting which pass changed the most. Discuss with them why a voice is "easier for a reader to hear than for a writer to sense."

L1 watch-outs
  • Long sentences joined by commas: Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Greek and Russian prose tolerates long chains of clauses joined by commas. In English this becomes the comma splice ("The bus left early, we missed it, it was raining"). The fix is a stylistic choice: a full stop, a semicolon, or a subordinator, and each one changes the rhythm.
  • Fragments as "mistakes": learners trained in exam writing (and some teachers) treat every fragment as an error. Frame the deliberate fragment as a tool for effect in narrative, essay and speech, and still out of place in a report or an exam answer that asks for formal prose.
  • Intensifiers and redundant pairs: very unique, more perfect, return back, repeat again, free gift are common across many L1s (Spanish volver atrás, French répéter encore). Treat them as deadwood, not grammar.
  • Hedging overload: learners from high-context cultures (Japanese, Korean, Thai, Indonesian) may stack hedges for politeness ("it seems that perhaps it might possibly be…"). Keep one hedge, and keep the politeness in the relationship, not the claim.
  • Nominal style as "good style": German, Russian and Romance academic traditions reward long noun chains. Remind them of the C1 "Who does what?" test: free the verb.
  • Deadpan and irony across cultures: understatement reads as humor to some listeners and as sincerity to others. If a learner doesn't find the Wit funny, that's not a failure of English; ask what signals would make the irony visible in their language.

4.3 · C12

Review Project — Mastery in Action

90 min
A task-based review, not a new-language class

There is no new language today. The class is built around one combined task in three parts: a three-minute persuasive or analytical speech to a small group, two minutes of unscripted questions, and a 150–180-word written re-pitch of the same argument for a different reader (an audience card), which must fairly report the strongest challenge raised in the Q&A. The focus is orchestration (several layers from Modules 1–4 working together without showing off) and adaptation (the same substance, re-pitched for a new room). You listen and read with the teacher checklist (Set F). Language work comes from the tasks: a short focus-on-form clinic between them. The checklist results feed C13 (Progress Test 1): they are your spoken and written evidence for Modules 1–4 and tell you who goes to which catch-up station.

Objectives — learners can…
  • structure and deliver a three-minute speech with a clear arc (hook, turn, landing) and the spine from C10 (claim, grounds, concession, refutation)
  • orchestrate at least six deliberate layers: a mot juste, a register or tone shift, a fixed expression or strong collocation, a moment of implicature or understatement, a figure (metaphor or rhetorical figure), a stance marker, and an appeal, and keep them invisible in delivery
  • handle unscripted questions gracefully: paraphrase the question, answer "the question beneath the question," concede before countering, and adapt register to the questioner
  • re-pitch the argument in writing for a different audience and mode (register, density, idiom, rhetoric), reporting the strongest challenge fairly with a precise reporting verb
  • self-assess against the eight-dimension mastery rubric with specific evidence: two strengths and one refinement

T: "It happens every month. Fixed expression." → S: "It's part and parcel of every month-end."
  2. Two rooms (open pairs): T gives an idea; S1 says it for experts, S2 for a community meeting. T: "Screens may affect children's sleep." → S1: "There is growing evidence that evening screen use may delay sleep onset in children." → S2: "Put the tablet away an hour before bed and they'll sleep better, by and large."
  3. Paraphrase, then answer: T asks a challenge ("Won't people just ignore the log?"); S paraphrases before answering: "If I read your question rightly, the concern is that it won't be kept up. That's fair. What I'd say is…"
  4. Report it fairly: T gives a challenge in direct speech ("This will just create more paperwork!"); S reports it for a written piece: "One colleague objected that the log would add to the paperwork." / "It was questioned whether the benefits would outweigh the administrative burden."
Mastery in Action — how to run it
  • Three minutes, not six. The online capstone asks for 5–6 minutes; in class, three minutes plus two minutes of questions per speaker keeps four speakers inside 22 minutes and still shows arc, layers and adaptation. The full version is homework (a phone recording).
  • Topics (Set E): every team should keep a decision log; handwriting still deserves a place in adult education; cities should protect "third places" (libraries, cafés, parks) as essential infrastructure; translation apps will never replace learning a language; museums should let visitors handle more of the collection (replicas); every professional should explain their work to a twelve-year-old once a year; silence (quiet carriages, quiet floors) is a public good worth protecting; online reviews do small businesses more harm than good; science should be taught through its failures as well as its successes; mentoring should count as real work in performance reviews. A learner's own topic is welcome if it stays in this territory. Side: for, against, or "yes, but" (a nuanced position), on the card or chosen.
  • The six layers. The planner's layer strip lists: a mot juste moment, a register or tone shift, a fixed expression or strong collocation, implicature or understatement, a figure (metaphor or rhetorical figure), a stance marker, an appeal. Six of the seven, planned; one in reserve. Learners mark them in the plan and aim to make them invisible in delivery.
  • Q&A. Each listener asks one question or challenge with a starter (Set E: "Isn't there a risk that…?" · "How would that work for…?" · "You said…, but surely…?" · "What would you say to someone who…?" · "Where does the evidence for… come from?"). The challenger paraphrases before asking ("So your claim is that… Isn't there a risk that…?"). The speaker concedes before countering at least once. The timekeeper stops the Q&A after two minutes, kindly. "That's beyond the scope of this proposal, but…" is allowed once.
  • Challenge notes feed the re-pitch. After each speech, listeners write the speaker's claim in one sentence and the strongest challenge they heard on the back of their card and hand it over. The speaker now has an outsider's version of the argument and the objection to report.
  • The written re-pitch is an adaptation, not a summary: the same argument for a different reader (the audience card), 150–180 words, with register, density, idiom and rhetoric chosen for that reader. It must report the strongest challenge fairly and answer it. For formal readers: no contractions, no idioms, hedged claims. For lay or young readers: plain, warm, concrete, still accurate.
  • If a Q&A gets heated, the timekeeper (or you) says: "Great challenge. One sentence to answer, then we move on." Challenge the idea, never the person.
The teacher checklist — how to score it (feeds C13)

Speaking: five criteria, 0–3 points each, /15. Writing: four criteria, 0–3 points each, /12. 0 = no response · 1 = not yet C2 (a strong C1 performance) · 2 = C2 · 3 = strong C2 (an educated, articulate speaker at their best). Score the whole spoken performance across the speech and the Q&A, and the written re-pitch separately. Tick the module boxes (M1–M4) when you hear or read a move from that module used well (precise, appropriate, natural). The full descriptors, the class grid and the score slips are in the classroom materials (Set F).

Speeches: How long do you speak? (Up to three minutes.) · Can you read your notes? (Only a glance.) · How many layers do you plan? (Six, one in reserve.) · What must a challenger do before asking? (Paraphrase the claim.) · What do listeners write on the card? (The claim in one sentence and the strongest challenge.) · Do you have to speak to the group? (No: you can speak only to the teacher.)

(No: the same argument, adapted for the new reader.) · What must you include? (The strongest challenge, reported fairly, and my answer.) · What do you do in the last four minutes? Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a speech strip ("Have you ever…? · My proposal is simple: … · Consider this: … · Admittedly, … That said, … · So let me leave you with this: …") and a reply strip ("If I understand you, your worry is… · That's fair. What I'd say is…"). A two-minute speech with a hook, a concession and a landing, and three layers placed well, is a complete task. For the re-pitch, give a frame for the audience card ("I'm writing to propose… · The main reason is… · Some colleagues have asked whether… · My answer is… · I'd suggest we…") and accept 100–120 words. Score on the same checklist: a clear, well-judged short performance can earn full marks on S1, S4 and S5.

Stretch

Ask strong learners to place a layer from each module at a hinge (hook, turn, landing) and to keep two layers in reserve, then explain afterward which ones they withheld and why. In the Q&A, they answer at least one question in a deliberately different register from the speech, matched to the questioner. In the re-pitch, choose the audience card furthest from the original room (a speech for a board → a piece for teenagers), use one sustained metaphor that survives the change of reader, and add one concessive structure (For all that…, …). Homework: the 5–6 minute version with no notes.

L1 watch-outs
  • Over-formal by default: speakers of Romance languages, Greek and Russian may deliver the whole speech in the formal register and read the room as "always formal." Praise the control, then ask for one deliberate drop into warmth. At C2, a register shift on purpose scores; an unbroken formal wall does not.
  • Idioms in the written re-pitch: learners who have worked hard on fixed expressions may carry them into a formal piece. "Spoken: yes. Board report: re-pitch it."
  • "According to me…" (French selon moi, Spanish según yo, some Indian English): according to is for other sources. Offer To my mind…, In my view…, I'd argue that….
  • Irony that doesn't travel: understatement can be heard as sincerity by listeners from more literal traditions. In mixed groups, the speaker needs a tiny signal (a pause, a flat tone, "shall we say"). Discuss it as a C2 skill: making irony legible to a mixed audience.
  • Directness in the Q&A differs. Some learners counter without conceding ("No, this is not correct."); others concede so fully that their position disappears. Teach the middle path, concede, then counter, as a technique, not a personality change.
  • Fossils under pressure: in extended speech, the old errors return (discuss about, since three years, informations, I will explain you). Note them for the fossil list, not for the public clinic, unless they're common to the whole class.
