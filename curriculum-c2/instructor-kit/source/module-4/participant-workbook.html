The Complete Communicator: Style & Rhetoric

Three lessons in which command becomes effect. You will learn the named techniques behind persuasion, and the critical armor to resist them when they are used on you; you will listen to the music of your own prose and make every word earn its place; and, at the halfway point of C2, you will orchestrate everything you have refined so far in a single performance, then re-pitch it for a different audience.

How this module works

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Read, Listen, Write, Say or Pair work. Almost every task here has many good answers; judge your work by its effect on a reader or listener, not only by its correctness.

When you argue a case, you argue the card, not necessarily your own conviction. When a task draws on your own experience, you decide what to share: your real life, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally valid, and you can always say "Pass."

Grammar box — the shapes of persuasion
Tricolonthree grammatically parallel elementsReading sharpens the mind, softens the heart, and widens the world.
Anaphorathe same words open successive clausesThis city deserves schools that lift every child. This city deserves transport that reaches every corner.
Antithesiscontrary ideas in balanced grammar: not X but Y · It is not that…; rather…This is not a setback; it is an invitation to begin again, wiser.
Rhetorical questiona question asked for effect; the listener supplies the answerIf we do nothing today, what do we tell our children tomorrow?
Chiasmusthe second half mirrors the first (A-B-B-A)Let us never negotiate out of fear, but let us never fear to negotiate.

The spine of a case. claim → grounds (evidence) → warrant (why it counts) → concession (the fair objection, granted narrowly) → refutation (why it does not overturn the claim) → restated claim.

Parallelism is the engine. A figure fails when its parts are not grammatically parallel. Weak: We came to listen, for learning, and so that we could change. Stronger: We came to listen, to learn, and to change.

Read and say — Dialogue: the meeting-free afternoon

Leila: I want to argue that we protect Wednesday afternoons: no meetings, no exceptions. Our calendars have become a mosaic with no picture.

Marco: I'll grant you the calendars; mine looks like a crossword. But meetings are where decisions get made. Cancel them and the decisions don't vanish; they just wait.

Leila: Admittedly, some decisions can't wait. That said, how many of Wednesday's meetings produced a decision last month? I counted: two out of eleven.

Marco: That's a striking figure. Yet it doesn't follow that the other nine were useless. Some of them were the only time the teams actually spoke.

Leila: Then perhaps the question isn't meetings or no meetings. It's whether we meet because we must, or because we always have.

Marco: Now that's an antithesis I can live with. Suppose we trial it for a month and let the numbers decide?

Find one concession, one refutation, one figure and one piece of logos. Where does the disagreement narrow?

Economy is not terseness. Every word should earn its place; that does not mean using as few as possible. A long sentence can be perfectly economical, and a short one can be flabby.

Read and say — Dialogue: killing a darling

Ines: Would you read my opening? It's for the library newsletter. I'm rather proud of the second sentence.

Karim: "The library, that patient cathedral of the unhurried mind, reopened on Monday." Hmm. It's lovely.

Ines: But?

Karim: Would you like a suggestion? The rest of the piece is warm and plain. Then there's this cathedral. It sounds like a different writer.

Ines: So it's a darling.

Karim: I think so. What if the second sentence just said what people found? "The chairs are new. The quiet is not."

Ines: Oh, that's better. Shorter, and it sounds like me. Thank you. I'll save the cathedral for something grander.

How does Karim offer the critique? Which line is the darling, and why does the replacement work better for this reader?

Read and say — Dialogue: the question beneath the question

Amara: So your claim is that museums should let visitors handle replicas. Isn't there a risk that it turns a museum into a playground?

Joel: Granted, and yet the most serious scholars I know learned by handling things. What I'd say is this: touch doesn't make the encounter less serious; it makes it more memorable.
