Module 2 — Reading Between the Lines: Pragmatics

Three classes about the gap between what is said and what is meant. First, implicature and inference: how a cooperative speaker means more than the words ("He's very punctual" in a reference), how a sentence smuggles in a premise ("Why is your team still behind?"), and how writers imply a verdict through diction, omission and distancing words (reportedly, insisted, so-called). Then irony, humor and wordplay: the same gap used for comic effect, from dry understatement (a bit of a problem) and sarcasm detection to puns in headlines. Last, figurative language: the conceptual metaphors that quietly organize English (ARGUMENT IS WAR, TIME IS MONEY) and the craft of building a descriptive passage on one controlled image.

3 lessons Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C4–C6 Weeks 2–3 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding workshop → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-pragmatics.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C4. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Implicature Lab source texts and encode cards, the Wit Workshop headline-desk stories and mock-review prompts, the Figurative Language Studio family-sort cards, imagery passages and subject cards, the A/B pair cards for each lesson, the Between the Lines board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences, and the intonation that carries the implied meaning, again at home.
  • Run every class as a workshop. About a quarter of each class goes on one extended product: a decoded text plus a message encoded entirely by implication (C4), a set of pun headlines and a short comic piece in writing (C5), a descriptive passage built on one controlling metaphor (C6). Protect that time. If you're running late, cut controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. At C1 learners met hedging, diplomatic softening, litotes and understatement (C1 3.3), and connotation and idiom (C1 Module 4). Don't re-teach any of it. The C2 step is deliberate control of the unsaid: hearing the implied verdict at conversational speed, naming the mechanism that produced it, and producing it on purpose, at exactly the strength intended.
  • Feedback on four things. C2 learners are often near-native in some areas and fossilized in others (a flawless reader who takes every spoken remark literally; a witty speaker who still says "I have a doubt"). In every workshop, take notes in four columns: accuracy (errors to fix, including the fossilized ones: "the regime claims" used neutrally, "only she got a B" for "she only got a B"), precision (the exact word or trigger: insisted vs said, even vs also), appropriacy (grammatical but wrong for the room: sarcasm to a manager, a pun in a condolence note, an implication so buried that nobody gets it) and effect (did it land? would a native listener infer what you meant, at the strength you meant?). The effect column is the most useful at C2. Board 2–3 items per column in the feedback stage, without names.
  • Give feedback that a C2 learner can use. "Good!" teaches them nothing. Quote the exact words, name the effect, and offer one precise alternative: "'He is punctual' implies faint praise. Did you mean that? If not, add a second quality." When a learner is right but unidiomatic, say so plainly: "Grammatical and clear, but no one would say it; we say damn with faint praise." Target fossilized errors one at a time, privately, with a written note: a learner who has said "explain me" for fifteen years needs a strategy (a sticky note on the laptop), not a correction in front of the class.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured critique, and each has its own test: the "What's the real message?" test (C4: the reviewers write, in one line, the message they inferred; the writers reveal their card), the "Gap and Target" test (C5: can the reviewer point to the words where the gap lies, and who pays for the joke?) and the "Name the frame" test (C6: the reviewer names the controlling metaphor without help and flags any image that breaks the frame). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote the text instead of summarizing it ("In line 2, ambitious does all the work. Nice.").
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes often include senior professionals, academics and teachers who are used to having the last word. Give them the roles that need listening: in C4 the decoder who may only speak after the group has written its reading; in C5 the editor who cuts words from other people's drafts but writes none; in C6 the cold reader who reads a passage aloud and names its frame without discussion. Say it warmly: "Your ear is the best in the room. I need it on the checklist this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first," and a 30-second silent writing pause before any open discussion, so faster speakers don't set the answer for everyone.
  • Stretch for the very strong. Each lesson has a stretch box. The direction for this module is calibration: producing the same implied message at three strengths (C4), writing irony that survives in print without any signal of tone (C5), and reframing one argument through two different conceptual metaphors to show how the metaphor steers the thinking (C6).
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and words (humour, realise, programme, behaviour, analyse, queue, favourite, colour, counsellor) and several British cultural references (Downing Street, Fleet Street, Monty Python). Teach humor, realize, program, behavior, analyze, line, favorite, color, counselor, and gloss the references quickly. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them. British English is famous for understatement: point that out as a cultural pattern, not as a standard learners must copy.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Infer it → play with it → picture it. Every lesson in this module lives in the same gap: the distance between the words and the meaning. In C4 learners decode that gap in workplace emails, references, reviews and minutes, and then encode a hard message into it so skillfully that it lands but cannot be quoted back. In C5 the gap becomes comic: irony says the opposite, understatement says less, hyperbole says more, and a pun says two things at once. In C6 the gap becomes an image: an argument becomes a battle, time becomes money, a library becomes a sleeping animal. Each class produces something another learner must decode: an implied message, a headline or mock review, a descriptive passage.

Handle humor and implied criticism with care

No humor at anyone's expense. Every joke, headline and example in this kit is about objects, situations, weather, bureaucracy or the speaker's own small misfortunes. Never use or allow humor about nationalities, ethnic groups, religions, accents, disabilities, bodies, age or gender, and never ask a learner to explain "how people from your country joke." If a learner offers such a joke, redirect calmly and without shaming: "Let's keep our targets to situations and ourselves today," and move on. Nobody has to perform a joke. In C5 learners analyze humor and write short comic pieces in pairs; reading aloud is always optional (the writer, a volunteer partner or you may read, or the piece can be displayed silently), and "Pass" is always accepted. Nobody is asked to perform their own accent or to be "the example" of a culture. Keep criticism invented. C4's implied messages (a weak reference, a rejected proposal, a bad hotel stay) are all on cards: learners encode the card's message, not their real opinion of a real colleague. The online lesson 2.1 uses the old courtroom example "Have you stopped beating your wife?": this kit replaces it with workplace loaded questions, and you should do the same in class. Some learners come from workplaces or countries where an innocent-sounding question could be a trap with real consequences; loaded questions are practiced as a language pattern with low-stakes office examples only.

Real examples convince C2 learners that this is how English actually works.

2.1 · C4

Implicature, Inference & Reading Between the Lines

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • distinguish sentence meaning (what the words say) from speaker meaning (what this speaker, here, intends), and infer the implicature of a reply that is too thin, too long, off-topic or obviously untrue
  • name the maxim that a speaker visibly flouts (quantity, quality, relation, manner) and explain why the implied meaning is deniable
  • spot presupposition triggers (still, again, stop, even, only, the, realize, regret, manage to), state what a sentence takes for granted, use the negation test, and refuse the premise of a loaded question before answering it
  • read a short text for its slant (diction, omission, juxtaposition, distancing markers such as reportedly, insisted, so-called) and state the verdict it never states
  • encode a face-threatening message by implication (hinting, understatement, strategic vagueness), so that a listener infers it at the intended strength

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: implicature. We assume a speaker is cooperative. When a reply seems to fall short (too little, too much, off-topic, obviously untrue, oddly worded) we don't decide the speaker has malfunctioned: we infer an extra meaning. That inferred meaning is the implicature. It is deniable: the speaker can retreat to the literal words.

"Is she good at her job?" "She's very punctual." Did the speaker say anything negative? (No.) What do you understand? (She isn't good at the job.) Why? (That's the only good thing mentioned: if there were more, they'd say it.) Could the speaker deny it? (Yes: "I only said she was punctual.") Which maxim was flouted? (Quantity: too little information.)
"What did you think of my presentation?" "Shall we get coffee?" Did the speaker answer? (No.) Is that an accident? (No: it's deliberate.) What does it mean? (I'd rather not say, so probably not good.) Which maxim? (Relation: the reply is off-topic on purpose.)

Meaning: presupposition. Not implied by the speaker but assumed by the sentence, carried by a trigger word. It survives negation, which is why it is hard to challenge.

"Have you stopped cutting corners?" If I say yes, was I cutting corners before? (Yes.) If I say no? (Yes, and I still am.) So what's the problem with answering? (Any answer accepts the premise.) What should I do first? (Reject the premise: "That assumes I was cutting corners.") · "Even Tom passed." What do we learn about Tom? (He was the least likely to pass.) Is that stated? (No, even assumes it.)

Meaning: slant in text. Writers imply a verdict without a false sentence: insisted (against the evidence), reportedly (I'm not vouching for this, but I'm telling you), a fact placed next to another fact (juxtaposition), a question not asked (omission).

"The CEO insisted the launch was a success." Is insisted neutral? (No.) What does it suggest? (Others disagree; the evidence says otherwise.) What's the neutral word? (said.) · "The app reportedly crashed twice." Does the writer say it crashed? (Not directly.) Why say it at all? (To tell us, while keeping a distance.)

Pronunciation. Implied meaning is carried by stress and pause as much as by words. Faint praise has a tell-tale fall-rise and a pause before the praise: "Well… she's very PUNCtual." The implying adjective comes after a hesitation: "It's certainly… BOLD." Presupposition triggers can be stressed to make the attack obvious ("Are you late aGAIN?") or left unstressed to slip them past the listener: point out that the unstressed version is the more dangerous one. In the refusal formula, stress the word you are rejecting: "That assumes we're STILL behind."

Drill script
  1. What does it take for granted? (fast, individual): T: "Did you manage to find the file?" → S: "Finding it was difficult." T: "Are you late again?" → "You've been late before." T: "Even the intern understood." → "The intern was the least likely to understand." T: "She only got a B." → "A B was less than expected." T: "When did you realize the budget was wrong?" → "The budget was wrong."
  2. Refuse the premise (choral formula, then individual): T: "Why is your team still behind schedule?" → S: "That assumes we're still behind. In fact, we're on track for the new date." T: "When will you stop missing deadlines?" → "I don't accept that I miss deadlines. What I can tell you is…" T: "Why did your project fail?" → "I wouldn't say it failed. What happened was…"
  3. Say it with the pause (choral, then individual): "Well… she's very punctual." · "It's certainly… bold." · "That's one way of doing it." · "I'm sure you had your reasons." Then ask one learner to say "It's certainly bold" sincerely, and another with the pause: the class says which one is a compliment.
Implicature Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 with three roles: reader (reads the text aloud with the implied tone), scribe (fills the worksheet) and decoder (in Round 1 may speak only after the group has written its reading, then challenges it: "Is that really implied, or are we over-reading?"). Give the decoder role to a dominant speaker. Rotate roles between rounds.
  • Round 1, Decode (10 min): each group gets one source text: C1 a director's email rejecting a proposal, C2 a reference letter that says nothing about the work, C3 a polite hotel review that is a demolition, C4 meeting minutes that record a row without saying so. For every loaded phrase: Said · Meant · Mechanism. Then one line: "The real message is…". The teacher's model reading is printed small at the foot of each card: fold it under before handing out.
  • Round 2, Encode (8 min): each group draws an encode card (for example "Tell a colleague that their slides have far too much text" or "Tell a neighbor that their music is too loud after 11 p.m."). They write a 4–6 line email or exchange that conveys the message entirely by implication: at least one flouted maxim, one presupposition trigger and one indirect phrase from the bank, and no sentence that states the message. They keep the card hidden.
  • Round 3, Peer test (7 min): swap texts with the next group. Run the "What's the real message?" test: the reviewers write, in one line, the message they inferred and how strong it is (mild / clear / severe). Then the writers reveal their card. Pass = the message matches and every line could be defended literally. Too buried = the reviewers inferred nothing or the opposite. Too bald = one line says it outright. Reviewers give one strength and one suggestion, quoting the text.
  • Share: two groups read their encoded texts aloud (the reader role, or you read them). The class guesses the message before the card is revealed.
  • Keep the ethics visible: tact communicates a true message gently; manipulation uses inference to mislead. If a group's text implies something false (for example a reference that invents a problem), point it out as the line not to cross.
ICQs before the Implicature Lab
In Round 1, how many columns? (Three: said, meant, mechanism.) When can the decoder speak? (After the group has written its reading.) · In Round 2, can you say the message directly? (No, never.) What must you include? (A flouted maxim, a trigger, an indirect phrase.) Do you show your card? (No, not until the test.) · In Round 3, what do the reviewers write? (The real message, in one line, and how strong it is.) What does "too buried" mean? (Nobody could infer it.) Whose opinion is on the card, yours or the card's? (The card's.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 speakers are much stronger orally than in writing, and they are often superb at hearing implied meaning. Give them the reference letter (C2), the shortest text, with the loaded phrases already underlined, and a strip with the four maxim names and a one-word gloss (enough · true · relevant · clear). Let them be the reader in Round 1 and the voice in the share. In Round 2 they may dictate the encoded message while the scribe writes. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 3 items 1–4. Target: decode three phrases orally and produce one deniable line.

Stretch

Calibration. Ask strong learners to encode the same message at three strengths (a mild doubt, a clear objection, a severe verdict) using only implication, and let the group rank them blind. Then give them the online lesson's ironic manager dialogue and ask: which line would be hardest to deny in a formal complaint, and why? For reading: rewrite the slanted news item (Ex 4) twice, once neutral and once slanted the opposite way, without adding any new facts.

L1 watch-outs
  • Literal reading as a sign of respect: learners from low-context communication cultures (for example, German, Dutch, Scandinavian, Israeli and many Eastern European workplaces) may read faint praise as praise ("The reference says he is punctual, so it is positive"). Present implicature as information that native readers will act on, whether or not the learner likes the convention.
  • Over-reading: learners from high-context cultures (for example, Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai and Indonesian workplaces) are often expert decoders but may find implied criticism in every neutral sentence. The decoder's question "Is that really implied?" is for them.
  • only and even position: in English their position changes the presupposition (She only got a B · Only she got a B · Even Tom passed · Tom even passed). Speakers of Russian, Polish, Chinese, Spanish and Arabic often place only by L1 habit. Drill two or three contrasts.
  • Definite article as a trigger: learners whose languages have no articles (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Hindi-Urdu) may not hear that the mistake in your figures presupposes a mistake while a mistake doesn't. Make the contrast explicit; it is also a common source of accidental accusations in their emails.
  • False friends in slant words: Spanish pretender (= to try to), French prétendre (= to claim), Italian pretendere (= to demand), and Portuguese pretender (= to intend) lead to "He pretended the scheme was a success." In English, pretend means to fake. Also eventually (≠ Spanish eventualmente, French éventuellement = possibly), which changes a sentence's implication.
  • "I have a doubt" (Spanish tengo una duda, Portuguese tenho uma dúvida, Italian ho un dubbio, Hindi-Urdu English): in English "I have a doubt about your figures" implies suspicion, not a question. Offer I have a question about…

2.2 · C5

Irony, Humor & Wordplay

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • tell verbal irony (saying the opposite, often gently) from sarcasm (irony with a target and an edge), and detect both from the three signals: stress, tone and context clash
  • use understatement and litotes (a bit of a problem, not bad at all, not exactly ideal) for dry humor and tact, and hyperbole sparingly for comic effect
  • recognize situational and dramatic irony in stories, news and film
  • explain exactly how a pun (two meanings, or two words that sound alike), a malapropism and a spoonerism work, and decode and write pun headlines
  • judge the register of humor (who is the target, who is listening, is this the moment?) and write a short comic piece that targets a situation or themselves
Materials
  • Workbook 2.2 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, Wit Workshop frame, self-check)
  • Slides 9–13 · Irony-situation picture cards (umbrella, flight, coffee, mobile phone) · Device cards · Headline Desk story cards (8) · Mock review / wry anecdote prompt cards · Gap and Target checklists · C5 A/B cards · New identity cards
  • Listening scripts 2.2-A (sincere, ironic or understated?) and 2.2-B (dictation)

Left: the gap. Irony = the opposite ("Lovely weather for it." in a storm) · sarcasm = irony + target + edge ("Oh, nice parking.") · signals: STRESS · tone (flat / drawled) · context clash. Understatement: a bit of a problem · not bad at all · I've had better days. Hyperbole: a mountain of paperwork. Keep it rare.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: irony vs sarcasm. Both say the opposite of what is meant. Irony can be gentle, affectionate or aimed at yourself; sarcasm has a target and an edge and is meant to sting.

It's pouring on the company picnic. "Perfect weather for a picnic." Is the weather perfect? (No.) Is the speaker lying? (No: everyone knows the truth.) Is anyone being attacked? (No.) So: irony or sarcasm? (Irony.) · A colleague parks across two spaces. "Oh, nice parking." Is there a target? (Yes, the driver.) An edge? (Yes.) So? (Sarcasm.) Would you say it to your manager? (Probably not.)
How do we know it's ironic? (The situation clashes with the words; heavy stress; a flat or drawled voice.) Which signal disappears in an email? (Tone and stress.) So what's the risk in writing? (The reader takes it literally.)

Meaning: understatement and hyperbole. Understatement says far less than the situation deserves; the listener supplies the missing emotion. Hyperbole says far more. Both are signals, not lies.

"The client had a few reservations." Were they mildly unsure? (No, they were furious.) Why say it that way? (It's dry and calm, and it's funny because of the gap.) Is it also polite? (Yes.) · "I've told you a million times." A million? (No.) So what does it mean? (Many times, and I'm annoyed.) What happens if everything is "the worst ever"? (It stops meaning anything.)

Meaning: puns and headlines. A pun makes one word or phrase carry two meanings at once (interest: money / enthusiasm), or uses two words that sound alike. Headlines use puns because they are short and memorable.

"BAKERY RISES TO THE OCCASION." What does bread do in an oven? (It rises.) What does "rise to the occasion" mean? (Perform well when it matters.) How many meanings are active? (Two.) Would this headline work in your language? (Almost certainly not: puns rarely translate.)

Pronunciation. Irony lives in stress, pitch range and pace. Sincere praise uses a lively, rising-falling pitch; ironic praise is either exaggerated ("Oh, FANtastic" with a slow, wide fall) or completely flat. Deadpan understatement is spoken with a narrow pitch range, level volume and no smile: that is the joke. Stress can create the irony on its own: "Well, THIS is going brilliantly." In puns, the pivot word is usually said neutrally: stressing it too much "explains" the joke. Learners from tone languages or syllable-timed languages often use a narrower pitch range overall, so their sincere praise may sound ironic, and their irony may not register at all. Practice the contrast in the drill.

Wit Workshop — how to run it
  • Pairs (or groups of 3 with an editor, who cuts words from the draft but writes none: give this role to a dominant speaker). The workshop is about writing and analyzing humor. Nobody is required to read aloud, act or "be funny" in front of the class.
  • Round 1, Headline Desk (7 min): each pair gets two story cards (all invented, harmless local news: a bakery wins a national prize, a town pool reopens, a library forgives all late fees, a dentist opens a free children's clinic…). Write a pun headline for each (max. 7 words, headline style: present tense, no articles) and one line naming the pivot word and its two meanings. Model headlines are on the back of each card for when a pair is stuck.
  • Round 2, Comic piece (10 min): choose one prompt card: a mock review (a mundane object reviewed as if it were a luxury product, or damned as if it were a disaster: the office stapler, the break-room microwave, a self-checkout machine, an umbrella that turns inside out) or a wry anecdote (a small disaster told dry: locked out of your own apartment, a flat-pack bookshelf with one extra screw, a video call with the camera on). 80–120 words. Include irony or sarcasm aimed at the object or situation, understatement or hyperbole, and one piece of wordplay. The punchline comes last.
  • Round 3, Gap and Target review (8 min): swap with another pair and fill the checklist: 1. The gap: underline the words where what is said and what is meant come apart. 2. The signals: in writing, is the irony clear without a tone of voice? Mark any line a reader could take literally. 3. The target: who pays for the joke? It must be the writer, an object, a situation or a system. 4. The cut: one word or phrase that delays the punchline. Then one strength and one suggestion, quoting.
  • Share (optional): up to three pieces. The writers decide how: read it themselves, ask a volunteer or you to read it, or show it on the document camera. "Pass" is fine. Ask the class to point at the pivot word, not to rate how funny it is.

Stretch

Irony that survives in print. Ask strong learners to write a mock review in which the irony is clear with no exaggeration words (so, very, amazing, literally) and no exclamation marks: only the gap between calm words and absurd facts. Then give them a sincere product review and ask them to change three words to make it ironic, and to explain which change did the most work. For wordplay: write a headline with a pun that works on two levels of the story (the literal event and its consequence).

L1 watch-outs
  • Irony across cultures: in some communication cultures heavy irony toward other people sounds cold or dishonest, and sarcasm toward a superior is unthinkable; learners from these backgrounds may detect irony perfectly but reasonably prefer not to produce it. That's a legitimate choice: the production target is understatement and situational irony, which travel well.
  • Pitch range: speakers of Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese and Thai use pitch for word meaning and often narrow their pitch range in English, so ironic "Oh, FANtastic" may sound sincere and sincere praise may sound flat. Speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Turkish) may not produce the stressed THIS that flags irony. Practice the contrast.
  • Homophone puns: learners who don't separate English /ɪ/ and /iː/ (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Russian, many others) or /l/ and /r/ (Japanese, Korean) may miss or mishear some homophone puns. That's not a comprehension failure: explain the sound, and choose polysemy puns (interest, put down) for the drill.
  • ironic for coincidental: many speakers of all backgrounds (native speakers too) use ironic for any coincidence. Situational irony needs an outcome contrary to expectation (the fire station burns down), not just a surprise.
  • Literal translation of L1 humor: "It's not my circus, not my monkeys" (Polish) travels; most L1 puns don't. Learners may enjoy sharing an L1 saying, but never require it, and never ask anyone to explain "how your culture jokes."
  • False friend: French humour ≈ English humor, but Spanish humor also means mood (estar de buen humor): "I'm in bad humor" → I'm in a bad mood.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: metaphor vs simile. A simile keeps the two things apart and invites comparison (like, as); a metaphor fuses them and asks the reader to live inside the image. Metaphors can be sustained; similes usually can't.

"Her argument was a fortress." Was it a building? (No.) What do we understand? (It was very hard to attack.) "Her argument was like a fortress." Which is bolder? (The metaphor.) Which one could continue for a paragraph? (The metaphor: walls, siege, gates…)

Meaning: metonymy vs synecdoche. Metonymy names something by an associated thing (the front desk = the receptionists; Hollywood = the film industry). Synecdoche names it by a part (hands = workers; wheels = a car).

"The front desk called about your booking." Did a desk make a call? (No.) Is the desk part of the person? (No, it's associated with them.) So? (Metonymy.) · "We need more hands on the night shift." Are hands part of a worker? (Yes.) So? (Synecdoche.) Why say "hands"? (It focuses on the physical work.)

Meaning: conceptual metaphor. Whole families of ordinary words map one concept onto another. Knowing the family lets you coin new expressions that sound natural.

"She shot down my idea." "He defended his claim." "I won that argument." What is an argument in these sentences? (A battle, a war.) Is that decoration or the normal way to talk? (The normal way.) If ARGUMENT IS WAR, can I say "Her question ambushed him"? (Yes: it's on the map.) Can I say "Her question watered him"? (No: that's a different map.)
Dead, clichéd or live? "The foot of the hill." (Dead: nobody sees a foot.) "Think outside the box." (Cliché: we notice it, and we're tired of it.) "Hope is the thing with feathers." (Live: we see a bird.) Which one should you refresh? (The cliché.)

Figurative Language Studio — how to run it
  • Round 1, Analyze (6 min): groups of 3, one imagery passage each (from the materials: F1 the morning commute as a river, F2 a deadline as a siege, F3 a restaurant kitchen as an orchestra). Each has one planted image from the wrong domain and one cliché. Tasks: name the controlling metaphor (THE COMMUTE IS A RIVER), underline every supporting image, circle the intruder and the cliché, and propose a replacement for each. Groups that finish early do Workbook Ex 5.
  • Round 2, Write (10 min): each learner (or pair) takes a subject card (a place, a mood, an experience or an argument: a hospital waiting room at night · a job interview · learning to drive · the first day in a new city) and a source-domain card (weather, the sea, a theater, a garden, a machine, a chess game, a kitchen, a river), or chooses their own pairing. Name the controlling metaphor in one line, then write 120–150 words: at least four sentences in the same domain and two or three supporting images. New identity cards include a "place I know well" for anyone who prefers not to describe their own life.
  • Round 3, Name the frame (7 min): swap. The reviewer is a cold reader: they read silently and write on the slip (1) the controlling metaphor and source domain, (2) the strongest image, quoted, (3) any image that breaks the frame, (4) one cliché to refresh. Only then do they talk to the writer. If the reviewer can't name the frame, the metaphor isn't controlling enough: that's useful information, not failure. Writers revise one sentence on the spot.
  • Share: two or three volunteers read (or you read them). The class names the frame before the writer says it.
  • Personal content stays optional: a passage about "a mood" can be invented, and a hospital or a new city can be anyone's. Nobody is asked to explain what a passage says about their life.

Stretch

Metaphors steer thinking. Give strong learners a short paragraph about a workplace disagreement written entirely in ARGUMENT IS WAR (attacked, defended, retreated, won) and ask them to rewrite it as ARGUMENT IS A DANCE or ARGUMENT IS BUILDING (laid the foundations, added a floor, reinforced). L1 watch-outs
  • Different maps: conceptual metaphors are partly universal (UP IS GOOD is very widespread) and partly language-specific. Speakers of Chinese may reach for TIME IS A RIVER, and some languages place the future behind the speaker. Invite comparison if learners want to share ("Is time money in your language?"), but don't make it a required cultural display.
  • Idiom blending: C2 learners often cross an L1 idiom with an English one ("to kill two flies with one stone," Spanish/German/Russian; "to put the cart before the oxen," Spanish/Portuguese; "the drop that made the glass overflow," from many European languages). These are mixed idioms, not mixed metaphors: correct them gently and note that the L1 image is perfectly vivid; it just isn't the English fixed form.
  • like vs as: "as brave like a lion," "like I said you" and "as a ghost" (for like a ghost) from Spanish como, French comme, Portuguese como, Italian come. Board: as + adj + as · like + noun · as + role (she worked as a nurse).
  • Metonymy with institutions: learners used to capital-for-government metonyms in their own press will understand Washington or Brussels, but may not know workplace metonyms (the front desk, the top floor, HR says). These are the more useful ones to teach.
  • Over-decoration: academic and literary traditions in some languages (for example Arabic, Persian, Spanish, Russian) prize rich figurative prose; in English workplace and academic writing, one precise figure beats five. Frame it as a genre difference, not a flaw.
