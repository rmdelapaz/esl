Module 2 — Reading Between the Lines: Answers

Answers to every Module 2 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the encoded messages, the headlines and comic pieces, the one-metaphor passages), and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 2.1–2.3Classes C4–C69 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark accuracy, precision, appropriacy and effect. Most items in this module are interpretations or rewrites with many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. For an interpretation, accept any reading that (1) identifies the withheld or implied verdict at roughly the right strength and (2) points to the words that produce it. Don't reward over-reading: an implication the text doesn't support is wrong, however clever. For a rewrite, accept any version that (1) keeps the message, (2) would be inferred by a proficient reader, and (3) fits the register: deniable for the implication tasks, aimed at a situation or the speaker for humor, coherent in one frame for metaphor. American English is the model; accept British spellings (humour, realise, programme, analyse).

2.1 · Implicature, Inference & Reading Between the Lines (C4)

Exercise 1 — The reference call (Script 2.1-A)
123456
punctualenthusiasmcolorfulsocial eventsrestructuredeventually

Implications: 1 attendance is his only merit worth citing (quantity: faint praise) · 2 enthusiasm, not results (quantity) · 3 style over substance; possibly colorful = not serious or not accurate (quantity, and a loaded word) · 4 liked socially, which says nothing about leadership, the question asked (relation) · 5 an evasion: the honest answer would be no (relation / strategic vagueness) · 6 he missed deadlines (quantity; eventually is the lever). Dana's presupposition: "Did he manage to meet his deadlines in the end?" (manage to + in the end presuppose that meeting deadlines was a struggle). Her closing "that's very helpful" is sincere: she understood exactly. Accept colourful.

Exercise 2 — State the implicature (models)
  1. The content is weak (or the formatting is chaotic); the only remark is about fonts. Quantity (faint praise) / relation. Accept "the report looks unprofessional."
  2. She isn't handling the clients well: politeness on the phone is the only positive. Quantity.
  3. The coffee is bad. Quantity (the obvious thing a café should be praised for is missing), plus a hint.
  4. I don't want to discuss the budget (or: now is not the time). Relation.
  5. It was far too long. Understatement / faint praise (quantity); certainly + thorough is a classic polite complaint.
  6. There was a heated argument. Understatement / euphemism in the minutes (manner and quantity); lively is the lever.

Accept a different maxim label if the learner's reasoning is sound: items often flout two at once. Not yet: literal paraphrases ("She is polite on the phone") that miss the verdict.

Exercise 3 — What is taken for granted?
  1. the error · yet → there is an error in the invoice, and it should have been fixed by now
  2. Even → the new intern was the least likely person to spot it (so others should have)
  3. again → you have worked from home before (possibly: too often)
  4. regret (factive) → you signed the contract (and, as implicature, it may have been a mistake)
  5. only → second was less than expected of her
  6. manage to → speaking to the landlord was difficult
  7. Model: "That question assumes we're disorganized, which I don't accept. What I can tell you is that we've cut response times by a third this quarter."
  8. Model: "I don't accept that the team stopped following it. We had one missed step in March, and we've added a second sign-off since then."

For 7–8, accept any answer that names and rejects the premise before adding information. Not yet: "We're not so disorganized" (accepts the premise) or a defensive "Why do you say that?" alone.

Exercise 4 — The verdict between the lines

Levers (any five): much-anticipated (diction: sets up a fall) · reportedly (distancing: airs the crashes without vouching for them) · the juxtaposition of the crashes with "a game-changer" · the scare quotes around "a game-changer" and "well over two hundred" (distancing) · declined to take questions (omission: implies evasion) · A modest crowd of some forty people (diction) juxtaposed with the press release's figure. The verdict: the launch was a flop, and the company is exaggerating (or not being honest) about it. Neutral model: "The company launched its new app at an event attended by about forty people, according to reporters; its press release gave the figure as over two hundred. The chief executive described the app as a game-changer and did not take questions. Some attendees reported that the app crashed twice." (Every fact remains; did not take questions is more neutral than declined.)

Exercise 5 — Imply it, don't state it (models; judge by tact and deniability)
  1. "There's a lot of great material here. I wonder whether the audience will manage to read it all before you move on?" (hint + manage to)
  2. "It's certainly ambitious. Have we had a chance to stress-test the timeline?" (faint praise + presupposing question)
  3. "Ah, things have been a bit complicated with the car lately. I've had to become rather strict with myself about lending it." (strategic vagueness)
  4. "The location is excellent, and the staff clearly do their best. I'd suggest bringing your own cleaning wipes." (faint praise + a hint)

Accept any version a listener would decode but the writer could plausibly disown. Too bald: any sentence that states the message ("It's too long"). Too buried: praise with no hint at all.

Dialogue — decoding the director

Implying phrases: It's clear a great deal of work went into it · certainly comprehensive · a slightly different direction · for now · yet · Let's touch base next quarter. Three messages: (1) the proposal is rejected, at least for now; (2) it is too long; (3) she doubts the budget figures. Hardest to deny: the doubt about the budget, because yet presupposes that the check should already have happened (presuppositions are harder to disown than implicatures). Accept "the rejection" with good reasoning.

Speaking — Implicature Lab

Round 1: model readings for all four source texts are printed small at the foot of each card (classroom materials, set C). A good worksheet quotes the exact words in the Said column, keeps Meant to one short clause, and names a mechanism for every line. Round 2, model (encode card 1, "your slides have far too much text"): "Hi Tom, thanks for sharing the deck. There's clearly a lot of expertise in it. I couldn't help wondering whether the back row will manage to read slide 4 before you move on. You might want to keep the detail for the handout? Happy to look at a second version." Result: pass (message clear, every line defensible). A version that says "Slide 4 is unreadable" is too bald; one that only praises is too buried.

Self-check
Q1
"Did you enjoy the concert?" "The program notes were beautifully printed."
A) The speaker only cared about the printing.
B) It presupposes the concert was canceled.
C) The concert was disappointing, implied by praising only a trivial detail.
Why: the reply withholds everything relevant (quantity) and changes the subject (relation). A takes it literally; B confuses implicature with presupposition: nothing in the sentence takes a cancellation for granted.
Q2
Which question takes a damaging premise for granted?
A) "What did the committee decide about the data?"
B) "When will you admit the data was falsified?"
C) "Do you think the data might be reviewed?"
Why: admit is a factive verb: it presupposes that the data was falsified, and when assumes you will eventually confess. A and C are open questions.
Q3 · new
"Why is your team still behind schedule?" Best first move?
A) "We're not that far behind, honestly."
B) "That question assumes we're behind schedule. In fact, we're on track for the revised date. Let me show you."
C) "Sorry about that. We'll catch up soon."
Why: B names and rejects the premise before giving information. A and C both accept the premise carried by still (A partly, C completely).
Errors you will meet most (2.1)

Literal readings of faint praise ("The reference is positive: he is punctual") · over-reading (finding sarcasm in a neutral sentence) · "Only she got a B" for "She only got a B" · a mistake vs the mistake confused (accidental accusations) · "He pretended the scheme was a success" (for claimed) · "I have a doubt about the figures" (for a question) · answering a loaded question with yes/no first · encoded messages that are either bald or so buried the partner infers the opposite. For precision, praise insisted, reportedly, for now, certainly + adjective, and a well-placed yet.

2.2 · Irony, Humor & Wordplay (C5)

Exercise 1 — Sincere, ironic or understated? (Script 2.2-A)
12345678
SIISI (sarcasm)UIS

Signals: 1 warm, lively pitch; context (the printer works) · 2 same words, flat tone; context clash (it's jammed again) · 3 context clash (pouring rain) · 4 no clash (sunny) · 5 exaggerated politeness + clash (she's 50 minutes late); edge and a target, so sarcasm · 6 litotes (not bad for an award); level, understated delivery · 7 clash (a "quick" meeting that lasted three hours) · 8 sincere: warm tone, no clash. Items 1–2 and 3–4 prove that the situation, not the words, carries the irony. Accept "I" for 6 if the learner explains the understatement well.

Exercise 2 — What's really meant?
  1. It's a disaster. Understatement (also accept irony).
  2. I've been on hold far too long, and I'm annoyed. Hyperbole (a lifetime) with irony in only.
  3. The printer is badly made / keeps failing. Litotes / understatement.
  4. You left me no gas at all. Sarcasm (a target and an edge).
  5. He is often late. Litotes / understatement.
  6. This is a huge amount of reading and my weekend is ruined. Irony (little light) plus understatement.
Exercise 3 — The headline desk
  1. see eye to eye: agree / opticians work with eyes
  2. winds down: gradually closes / you wind a (mechanical) clock
  3. buzz: excitement, attention / the sound bees make
  4. spans: lasts (two decades) / a bridge spans a river
  5. dig in: refuse to give way / gardeners dig soil
  6. Models: YOGA STUDIO STRETCHES TO SECOND SITE · YOGA STUDIO BENDS OVER BACKWARD FOR NEW BRANCH · YOGA STUDIO STRIKES A NEW POSE. Accept any headline of 7 words or fewer with a genuine pivot word and headline style (present tense, no articles).
Exercise 4 — Name it and fix it
  1. Malapropism → scapegoat
  2. Malapropism (an "eggcorn") → for all intents and purposes
  3. Malapropism → a dog-eat-dog world
  4. Spoonerism → Is the dean busy? (the /d/ and /b/ swap)
  5. Situational irony (a time-management product that is itself late)
  6. Dramatic irony (the audience knows what the guests don't)

Accept "eggcorn" for 2 and 3 from learners who know the term. For 5, not "coincidence": the point is the outcome contrary to expectation.

Exercise 5 — Blunt into dry (models)
  1. "I'm not sure this one needed to be a meeting." · "Well, that's an hour I'll treasure."
  2. "The traffic was a touch sluggish this morning." · "I've had faster commutes. On foot."
  3. "Let's just say it won't be making my highlights reel." · "Not my finest twenty minutes."
  4. "The instructions are… open to interpretation." · "I now have a bookshelf and four spare screws, so I'm calling it a draw."

Judge by the gap (is it understated or ironic?), the target (the situation or the speaker, never a named person) and register. Not yet: versions that only soften without any gap ("The meeting was not very useful").

Dialogue — the morning after the presentation

Maya 1: sincere question · Dev "it went swimmingly": irony · Maya "Are they still with us?": gentle irony / understatement · Dev "a few reservations": understatement · Maya "not our finest hour": understatement · Dev "achieve self-awareness … small mercies": hyperbole + irony · Maya "Wonderful": irony · Dev "You're a saint. Truly.": deadpan overstatement (affectionate, half sincere) · "paper has the decency to burn quietly": personification + hyperbole, aimed at the equipment. Target: the situation, the projector and themselves, which is why it is safe: nobody present or absent is humiliated.

Speaking — Wit Workshop

Round 1: model headlines are printed on the back of each story card (classroom materials, set E). Round 2, model mock review (the office stapler, audience: colleagues): "Five stars. Our stapler is a minimalist: it believes in one staple per week, applied with great ceremony and no precision. It has jammed so often that IT now lists it as a staff member. Last Tuesday it bit Marcus, which he took personally; the stapler, I think, did not. Still, in an age of constant change, there is something reassuring about a machine that refuses to evolve. Would it hold my documents together? Hardly. But it holds the office together, and that, surely, is the point." (Irony: five stars; hyperbole: "a staff member"; personification; pun on holds together; the punchline comes last. Target: the object.) Gap and Target: a pass needs a clear gap in writing, a harmless target and no word that delays the final line.

Self-check
Q1
"Well, this is going brilliantly." (flight canceled)
A) Using irony: she means it's going terribly.
B) Using hyperbole to exaggerate her delight.
C) Making a pun on "brilliantly."
Why: the words clash with the situation, so they mean the opposite. There's no real delight to exaggerate (B), and brilliantly has only one active meaning here (C).
Q2
"I used to be a banker, but I lost interest."
A) First sounds swapped.
B) A wrong word used by accident.
C) "Interest" carries two meanings at once.
Why: interest = money a bank pays and enthusiasm, both active. A describes a spoonerism; B a malapropism.
Q3 · new
The safest humor for a welcome speech to new colleagues
A) A sarcastic remark about the manager's tie
B) A gently self-deprecating line about your own first-day sense of direction
C) A double entendre about the office party
Why: self-deprecation about something trivial risks nobody's dignity but your own. A targets a person, with an edge, in front of strangers; C is a register risk in a professional setting.
Errors you will meet most (2.2)

"It was a bit of problem" (missing a) · "I nearly died of laugh" (→ laughing) · "ironic" for any coincidence · sarcasm in writing with no clear context clash (read literally) · understatement without the gap ("It was not very good") · hyperbole in every sentence · headlines with articles and past tense ("The bakery rose to the occasion") · explaining the pun inside the punchline · humor aimed at a named colleague. For range, praise precise litotes (not exactly ideal, no small feat), deadpan that works in print, and pivot words with two genuinely active meanings.

2.3 · Figurative Language — Metaphor & Imagery (C6)

Exercise 1 — The library at closing time (Script 2.3-A)
123456
blinkeyelidsexhalesbellyfedcurled

Controlling metaphor: THE LIBRARY IS A (SLEEPING) ANIMAL / CREATURE (accept "a sleeping giant" or "a living body"). Simile: like heavy eyelids. Personification that fits the frame: the street lamps keep watch (accept dreaming or the heating rumbles with good reasons). Other frame images: fall asleep, breathing softly through the vents, swallowed whole, great creature.

Exercise 2 — Name the figure and its effect
  1. Metonymy (the desk = the reception staff): compact, impersonal; no individual is named
  2. Metaphor (inbox = swamp): makes the email load feel heavy, slow and draining
  3. Personification (the elevator complains): comic; suggests age, noise and reluctance
  4. Simile (like a spilled drink): the news spreads fast, uncontrollably and messily
  5. Synecdoche (hands = workers): focuses on physical labor
  6. Metonymy (Hollywood = the film industry): compresses a whole industry into a place name

Accept reasonable alternative effects. Accept "synecdoche" for 1 only with a thoughtful argument; the standard analysis is metonymy (a desk is not part of a person).

Exercise 3 — Which family?
W (ARGUMENT IS WAR)a · e · i
T (TIME IS MONEY)b · f · j
F (IDEAS ARE FOOD)c · g · k
J (LIFE IS A JOURNEY)d · h · l

Extend the map (models): W: Her first question outflanked the whole proposal. · He dug into a trench of statistics. · My counterargument never made it past his defenses. F: The report was all garnish and no main course. · I'm still chewing on your second point. · That theory has gone stale. Accept any coinage a proficient reader would understand immediately because of the map. Note that j (running out of time) can also be read as TIME IS A RESOURCE, the wider family: accept it.

Exercise 4 — Fix the mix, refresh the cliché (models)
  1. Mixed (bullfight + swimming pool). "We'll have to take the bull by the horns." or "We'll have to dive in at the deep end." or plain: "We'll have to start immediately, without much preparation."
  2. Mixed (air + tree + seeds); breath of fresh air is also a cliché. "The new manager will prune what isn't growing and plant something new." (garden frame throughout)
  3. Two clichés. Plain: "Ultimately, we need a solution nobody has tried yet." Re-coined: "We need to stop rearranging the furniture and knock down a wall."
  4. Mixed (fairground + race + sailing). "The project has been a long race, but we're on the home stretch now." or "…a rough voyage, but the harbor is in sight."
  5. Mixed and wrong in meaning: move the goalposts = change the rules unfairly. "Our competitors have raised the bar, so we'll have to jump higher." or "…so we need to raise our game."

Tests: one coherent picture, or no picture at all; a phrase the reader can see, not merely recognize.

Exercise 5 — Analyze the imagery
  1. a. THE MARKET IS A THEATER / A PLAY; source domain: theater
  2. b. Any six: a stage · its cast · stagehands · scenery · the lights come up · takes a bow · clears his throat and delivers his opening lines · an audience · the whole company · in full voice · every stall a scene · a small drama · the curtain falls · a sold-out performance. The simile: arrive like stagehands.
  3. c. runs out of gas (a car or engine image). Replacement models: the house lights come up · the last act ends · the cast takes its final bow.
  4. d. Personal answer; strong candidates: the fruit takes a bow (the sun catching oranges) and every bargain a small drama. Credit a reason tied to what the reader sees.
Dialogue — the writing group

Frame-breakers: a bottleneck (a bottle) and running on fumes (a car; also a cliché). Controlling metaphor: THE CITY AT RUSH HOUR IS AN OCEAN / THE SEA. "Stop before you get to the sharks" = don't over-extend the metaphor; stop while it still illuminates (proportion), before it becomes strained or comic.

Speaking — Figurative Language Studio

Round 1: the intruder and cliché for each imagery passage are printed small at the foot of the card (classroom materials, set F). Round 2, model (subject: a job interview; source domain: the sea): "The interview was a crossing in open water. At the reception desk the sea was calm, and I rehearsed my answers like a sailor checking knots. Then the door opened, and three faces rose on the horizon. The first question arrived as a slow swell; I rode it. The second broke over the bow before I saw it coming, and for a long moment I was bailing out words. But the wind turned. Somewhere around my third story, I felt the current begin to carry me, and by the time they asked whether I had any questions, I could see the harbor lights. Whether they'll let me dock, I won't know until Friday." (One domain throughout; simile, the wind turned, a final understatement.) Name the frame: a pass = the reviewer names the frame unprompted and finds no intruder.

Self-check
Q1
"All hands on deck"
A) Synecdoche
B) Personification
C) Simile
Why: the hands (a part) stand for the whole sailors. There's no human quality given to a thing (B) and no like / as (C).
Q2
Which sentence is a mixed metaphor?
A) The firm was adrift…
B) "We'll burn that bridge when we come to it."
C) Grief settled on her like snow…
Why: B collides burn your bridges with cross that bridge when we come to it. A sustains one image (a ship); C is a coherent simile.
Q3 · new
"…nip this problem in the bud before it snowballs."
A) "…before it spreads through the whole garden."
B) "…before the bud snowballs."
C) "We need to nip this snowball in the bud."
Why: A keeps one frame (plants and gardens). B and C make the collision worse by welding the two images together.
Errors you will meet most (2.3)

"as brave like a lion" · "like I told you" in formal writing (accept in speech) · L1-English idiom blends ("kill two flies with one stone," "the drop that overflowed the glass") · metonymy and synecdoche labels swapped (accept either when the learner's reasoning is sound; insist only on the clear cases) · move the goalposts used to mean "set higher targets" · sustained metaphors that drift into a second domain halfway · over-extension ("the sharks") · clichés kept in the "fresh" draft (at the end of the day, a rollercoaster of emotions, a breath of fresh air). For range, praise coinages that obey a conceptual map and images tied to precise, concrete detail.
