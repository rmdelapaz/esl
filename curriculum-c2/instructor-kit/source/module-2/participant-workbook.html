Reading Between the Lines: Pragmatics

Three lessons about the distance between what is said and what is meant. Learn to hear the verdict folded inside a polite remark and to deliver one yourself, deniably; to enjoy, analyze and craft irony, understatement and the well-placed pun; and to see the hidden metaphors that organize everyday English, then build a passage on one image of your own.

How this module works

Each heading tells you the kind of task: Read, Listen, Write, Say or Pair work. Almost every task here has more than one good answer. What matters is precision (the exact word that carries the meaning), appropriacy (the right strength for the reader and the room) and effect (would a proficient listener infer what you intended?).

When a task draws on your own experience, you decide what to share: your real life, a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good. The criticism and difficult messages in this module are written on cards; you encode the card's message, not your own opinion of anyone. In the humor lesson you will analyze and write, but you never have to perform a joke. You can always say "Pass."

What They Said, What They Meant — Implicature & Inference

Indirect moves (each one lets the listener draw the conclusion):

I couldn't help noticing…You might want to…It's not really my place, but…That's one way of doing it.I'm sure you had your reasons.It's certainly… bold.Have you had a chance to…?Let's just say it wasn't a triumph.Let's circle back on that.With the greatest respect…

implicature = what a speaker means beyond the literal words, inferred from context · presupposition = what a sentence takes for granted as already true · to flout = to break a rule openly, so that everyone notices and draws a conclusion

Grammar box — the machinery of the unsaid

Four expectations we have of a cooperative speaker, and what we infer when one is visibly broken:

Quantity: say enough"Is she good at her job?" "She's very punctual." → She has no better quality worth mentioning.
Quality: say what's trueIn a downpour: "Well, that was a lovely day out." → It was a disaster (irony).
Relation: be relevant"What did you think of my presentation?" "Shall we get coffee?" → I'd rather not say.
Manner: be clear"Did they sing?" "They produced a series of sounds corresponding to the score." → It was barely singing.

Presupposition triggers. A presupposition survives negation: "Your team is still behind" and "Your team isn't still behind" both assume it was behind.

stop · start · keepHave you stopped cutting corners? → you were cutting corners
again · still · yetAre you late again? → you've been late before
even · onlyEven Tom passed. → Tom was the least likely to pass
the · possessivesthe mistake in your figures → there is a mistake
realize · regret · admitWhen did you realize the plan was flawed? → it was flawed
manage toDid you manage to finish? → finishing was difficult

Refusing the premise. Never answer a loaded question with yes or no. Name the assumption, reject it, reframe: That question assumes X, which isn't the case. What I can tell you is…

Slant in writing comes from four levers: diction (insisted rather than said), omission (the question not asked), juxtaposition (two facts placed side by side) and distancing markers (reportedly, allegedly, apparently, so-called, scare quotes).

Read and say — Dialogue: decoding the director

Sam: Helen finally replied about my proposal. "It's clear a great deal of work went into it. It's certainly comprehensive."

Priya: Comprehensive. Hmm.

Sam: What's wrong with comprehensive?

Priya: Nothing, on its own. But it's the only adjective in the email. What else does she say?

Sam: "We've decided to go in a slightly different direction for now."

Priya: "For now" is doing a lot of work there. I'd read that as a polite no.

Sam: Then: "Has the budget section been checked by finance yet?"

Priya: That "yet" assumes it should have been. She has doubts about your numbers.

Sam: So "comprehensive" actually means…

Priya: Too long, probably. Deniably, of course. Ask her and she'll say it was a compliment.

Sam: And she signs off, "Let's touch base next quarter."

Priya: Which is three months away. I'd take the hint: fix the budget, cut it to two pages, and send it back before she asks.

Underline every phrase that implies more than it says. What three messages should Sam take from Helen's email? Which one would be hardest for Helen to deny?

Saying the Opposite — Irony, Humor & Wordplay

Class C5
Read and say — New words and chunks
verbal ironysarcasmdeadpanunderstatementlitoteshyperbolesituational ironydramatic ironya puna pivot worda homophonea malapropisma spoonerisma double entendrethe setupincongruitythe punchlineself-deprecationto landto fall flat

Ironic set phrases (praise that means the opposite):

Oh, fantastic.Just what I needed.Well, that went well.Couldn't be better.Lovely weather for it.Nice one.

Understatement (the listener supplies the missing emotion):

a bit of a problemnot exactly idealI've had better daysnot our finest hourslightly overdueno small featnot bad at alla few reservations
Grammar box — how the gap is made
Verbal ironysaying the opposite, trusting the listener to hear it; can be gentle or self-directed(stuck in traffic) "Well, this is going brilliantly."
Sarcasmirony with a target and an edge; meant to sting(to someone parked across two spaces) "Oh, nice parking."
Understatementsaying far less than the situation deserves(the projector caught fire) "It wasn't our finest hour."
Hyperboleobvious exaggeration, for emphasis or comedy"I've been on hold for a geological age."
Situational ironyan outcome contrary to expectationThe fire station burns down.
Dramatic ironythe audience knows what the character doesn'tWe know the letter is in her pocket; she searches the house.

How we hear irony: heavy stress ("Well, THIS is going brilliantly"), an exaggerated or completely flat tone, and above all a clash between the words and the situation. In writing, only the clash survives, so irony in print must be unmistakable or not attempted.

Punone word, two active meanings, or two words that sound alikeI used to be a banker, but I lost interest.
Malapropisma wrong word that sounds like the right onea real escape goat (scapegoat)
Spoonerismthe first sounds of two words swappeda well-boiled icicle (a well-oiled bicycle)

The architecture of a joke: setup (an expectation) → incongruity (something that doesn't fit) → punchline (a second frame snaps into place, and it comes last). Who pays? Safe targets are situations, objects, systems and yourself. Humor about people's nationality, religion, accent, body or disability is never on the menu.

Seeing One Thing as Another — Metaphor & Imagery

Class C6
Read and say — New words and chunks
figurative / literala metaphora similemetonymysynecdochepersonificationa conceptual metaphora source domaina target domaina dead metaphora clichéa live metaphora sustained (extended) metaphora controlling metaphora mixed metaphorimageryto refreshto re-coin

source domain = the concrete world an image is borrowed from (war, money, food, a journey) · target domain = the abstract idea it describes (argument, time, ideas, life)

Grammar box — the figures and the maps
MetaphorX is Y: fuses two things; can be sustainedHer argument was a fortress.
SimileX is like Y / as … as: invites comparisonFear rose in him like cold water in a cellar.
Metonymynaming by associationThe front desk called. · Hollywood loved it.
Synecdochenaming by part (or whole)All hands on deck. · nice wheels
Personificationhuman qualities for non-human thingsThe old house groaned in the wind.

Conceptual metaphors: whole families of everyday expressions share one map.

ARGUMENT IS WARdefend a claim · attack a position · shoot down an idea · win the point · indefensible
TIME IS MONEYspend · save · waste · budget your day · that cost me a week · borrowed time
IDEAS ARE FOODfood for thought · half-baked · hard to swallow · digest · chew it over · raw data
LIFE IS A JOURNEYat a crossroads · a dead-end job · moving on · we've come a long way
UP IS GOODspirits rose · feeling down · things are looking up · morale sank
UNDERSTANDING IS SEEINGI see · a clear argument · a murky explanation · shed light on it

Dead metaphors are invisible (the leg of the table); clichés are visible and tired (think outside the box): refresh or re-coin them; live metaphors still make the reader see (hope is the thing with feathers). A sustained metaphor draws every image from one source domain. A mixed metaphor puts two incompatible pictures in one frame: We'll burn that bridge when we come to it.

Read and say — Dialogue: the writing group

Ana: Here's my opening: "At rush hour the city is an ocean. Commuters surge through the station in waves, and the escalators pull them under."

Joon: I like it. I can see the tide. Is the whole paragraph the sea?

Ana: Mostly. Then: "The ticket barriers are a bottleneck, and everyone is running on fumes."

Joon: That's where I fell out of the water. A bottle and a car engine, in the middle of an ocean?

Ana: Hm. Fair. So the barriers could be… rocks? "The barriers are a reef the crowd breaks against."

Joon: Much better. And "running on fumes" is a bit of a cliché anyway.

Ana: "Everyone is swimming against their own exhaustion"?

Joon: Now you're sustaining it. One sea, start to finish. I'd just stop before you get to the sharks.

Which two images broke Ana's frame? What was the controlling metaphor? What does Joon mean by "stop before you get to the sharks"?
