Module 8 — The Summit: Exams, Performance & Lifelong Command: Answers

Answers to every Module 8 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (a model essay summary, a marked-up speech passage, poised answers under challenge, precise peer responses), and the errors you can expect most.

Marking principle

At C2, mark precision, appropriacy, naturalness and effect, not just accuracy. The Use of English items in 8.1 are closed: they have one answer (or a short list of alternatives given below), and they are marked the way the task type is marked, against the four checks (key word unchanged, word limit, same meaning, grammatical fit). Everything else in this module is open. An answer is correct when it works for the reader or listener: precise, idiomatic, right for the register, and doing what the task asked. Accept any version that does this, even if it differs from the key, and use the key's version to show one more option ("Good. Another way is…"). American English is the model; British spellings are acceptable when used consistently.

Exercise 4 — The essay summary

Key points (model paraphrases; accept any accurate paraphrase that doesn't lift phrases):

Text 1 · aWe can only think with knowledge we already carry; recognizing patterns requires it, so looking everything up is not a substitute.
Text 1 · bThe problem is not memorizing as such but the tedious ways it has been taught.
Text 2 · aClass time spent on facts that can be retrieved instantly would be better used on judging and connecting information.
Text 2 · bWhen information is plentiful, the valuable skill is evaluating it, not remembering it; curricula that reward recall are out of date.

Where they agree: both accept that memory still has some value (Text 2: "Memory has not become worthless"); they disagree about how much school time it deserves and about whether it competes with understanding.

Model essay (about 270 words).

Few educational questions are as quietly contested as the place of memorization. The two texts agree that memory retains some value, yet they draw opposite conclusions about how much classroom time it deserves.

The first writer argues that dismissing memorization misreads how thinking operates. Reasoning, on this view, depends on knowledge already held in mind: expertise consists largely in recognizing patterns, which is impossible if every fact must be looked up. The writer's most persuasive move is to separate memorization itself from the tedious drills that have given it a bad name. Less convincing is the implied assumption that the knowledge worth storing is obvious; the text never says who decides.

The second writer contends that schools squander time on facts that are instantly retrievable, and that the capacity now in short supply is judging information rather than recalling it. The point is well made, particularly given how much unreliable material circulates. However, the argument risks a false opposition: one can hardly evaluate a source on a subject one knows nothing about.

In my view, the two positions are less incompatible than they first appear. Evaluation is not an alternative to a well-stocked memory but one of its fruits, since we detect a dubious claim precisely because it clashes with what we already know. Schools should therefore teach less by rote, but not ask students to remember less: they should choose a core of knowledge that makes judgment possible, and then train students to question it.

Check against the task: both texts summarized ✓ · both evaluated (one strength, one weakness each) ✓ · own view, weighed ✓ · no lifted phrases ✓ · formal, essay register ✓. Common problems: summarizing without evaluating; evaluating only one text; copying "the scarce skill is not recall but evaluation" word for word; an opening that says nothing ("Nowadays, with the development of technology…"); running far over the word limit.

Dialogue — the collaborative task

A1: "Shall we start with…?" organizes the discussion (accept I or C: it proposes a starting point) · B1: "That's a fair point, though I'd add…" B; "What's your take on bike lanes?" I · A2: "I see it slightly differently, in that…" D; "So, are we agreed that…?" C · B2: "Agreed. Let's recommend those two." C. The conclusion comes last because the task demands a decision: a discussion that never decides has not completed the task. Turns are short because the task assesses interaction; a long turn here would crowd the partner out.

Self-check
Q1
On the collaborative speaking task, what do examiners most reward?
A) One candidate delivering a fluent, uninterrupted speech
B) Genuine interaction: inviting, building, negotiating toward a decision
C) Reaching agreement as fast as possible with no discussion
Why: the collaborative task exists to assess interaction. A polished monologue misses the point; instant agreement skips the negotiation the task is designed to elicit.
Q2
A reading option seems true, but you can't find supporting words in the passage. You should:
A) choose it: it matches what you already know is true
B) leave it blank to be safe
C) reject it: if you can't underline the evidence, it isn't supported
Why: the "true but not there" option is the examiner's favorite distractor. World knowledge is not evidence. Leaving a blank never scores.
Q3 · new
Key word BRINK, 3–6 words. "The company very nearly collapsed." → "The company ________ collapse." Which answer scores?
A) was on the brink of
B) was on brink of
C) found itself teetering right on the brink of
Why: A passes all four checks (5 words). B fails "fit": the fixed phrase needs the article. C is idiomatic but 8 words: over the limit, so it scores nothing.
Errors you will see and hear most (8.1)

Use of English: changing the key word (soon for SOONER) · no inversion after a negative adverbial · dropped articles in fixed phrases (on brink of, in wake of) · counting a contraction as one word · the right root in the wrong form (reluctancy) · the wrong negative prefix (unconclusive, inforeseen) · choosing by meaning, not collocation (heavy possibility). Reading and listening: matching repeated words instead of paraphrase · the first-mentioned option in listening · "true but not there." Writing: the under-answered prompt · formulaic openings · lifted phrases. Speaking: a long turn with no close (the speaker runs out of time mid-point) · a collaborative task run as two monologues · "discuss about" · I am agree under pressure. For range, praise precise collocations (a double-edged sword, cast doubt on), invitations that genuinely hand over the floor, and closes that pull the long turn together.

Exercise 3 — Open, close, answer

1 (b): a hook that opens a curiosity gap; (a) and (c) waste the first fifteen seconds and lower expectations. · 2 (c): a resonant, decisive close on a full stop; (a) and (b) fizzle at the moment of highest attention. · 3 (b): concede the valid part, then narrow to the real claim; (a) is defensive and (c) evasive, and both cost credibility.

Exercise 4 — Mark it up for delivery (model)

Every expert you admire || was once catastrophically bad | at the thing they're now famous for. ||

Every single one. ||

We tell ourselves a comforting lie: | that talent is something you're handed | at birth.

It lets us off the hook. ||

The gap between you and the person you admire | is not talent. || It is time.

Accept other defensible choices (for example stress on comforting rather than lie). The longest pause belongs before "It is time": it primes the answer to the question the speech has been building, and the contrast talent / time needs the silence to land. Weak mark-ups: a pause after every two or three words (robotic), stress on function words (the, is, at), or no pause before the key line.

Exercise 5 — From defensive to poised (models)
  1. "That's a fair challenge: it's true that many jobs, in care or in retail for example, can't simply drop a day. What I'm arguing is narrower: that where the work is measured by output rather than hours, a four-day week is worth trialing."
  2. "I'm glad you asked. I don't have the source on hand, so let me not lean on that exact figure; I'll send it to you. The broader point doesn't depend on it, though: the trend in the studies I read was consistently upward."
  3. "That's a good question. You're right that most of my examples are large cities. I'd put it more cautiously, then: it works best where public transportation is already strong, and smaller towns would need a different first step."

Look for three moves: acknowledge, concede what is fair, narrow or bridge back. Reject rewrites that simply become polite without conceding anything ("Thank you for your question, but I think you are wrong"), and rewrites that collapse ("You're right, my speech was wrong").

Dialogue — the speech and its Q&A

1 The lie is that talent is innate, "handed at birth"; it lets us off the hook because it excuses us from trying ("I'm just not gifted"). · 2 Antithesis: "The gap… is not talent. It is time" (also: "Don't wait to be good before you begin. Begin."). The pauses sit around the highest-stakes lines: after the opening claim (to let it land), after "Think about that" (to give the audience thinking time), and before "Begin" (to prime the call to action). · 3 The speaker concedes the valid part ("the starting lines are not equal"), narrows the claim to one that survives the objection ("quitting early guarantees you stay behind it"; persistence is the controllable part), and checks that the answer landed ("Does that distinction land for you?"). It persuades because it takes the questioner seriously and leaves a stronger, smaller claim standing.

Speech plan and feedback record

Open tasks: no key. Check that the open and close are written in full and that the close ends on a full stop. The most common planning problem is a spine with five or six points: a three-minute speech holds two or three.

Self-check
Q1
Why is a deliberate pause before a key sentence so effective?
A) It primes the audience's attention and lets the line land
B) It simply slows the talk down to fill time
C) It hides the fact that the speaker is unprepared
Why: the pause before says "listen, this matters"; the pause after says "let that sink in." It is a tool of emphasis, not filler, and it reads as confidence.
Q2
A questioner challenges your talk. The most poised response is to:
A) concede the valid part, then narrow to your actual point
B) insist firmly that they're mistaken
C) decline to engage with the question
Why: conceding what is fair keeps credibility; narrowing keeps the argument. B is defensive and C evasive: poise is the persuasion.
Q3 · new
You are preparing a three-minute speech. The best way to prepare the words is to:
A) memorize every word so that nothing can go wrong
B) memorize the open and the close, and speak the middle from a skeleton of key points
C) improvise all of it, so that it sounds natural
Why: automatic bookends are the best insurance against nerves and the fizzled ending; a skeleton keeps the middle alive. Full memorization sounds recited and collapses if you lose your place; pure improvisation rambles.
Errors you will hear most (8.2)

The apologetic open ("Sorry, I'm not a good speaker…") · the fizzled close ("So… yeah, that's it") · too many points for three minutes · stress on every word, or on function words · a rhetorical question with no pause after it · pauses that fall mid-phrase (breathing where the lungs run out, not where the idea ends) · speeding up under nerves · defensive or evasive answers in Q&A · discuss about, explain me, return back under pressure. For range, praise closes that echo the open, triads whose third beat falls, genuine concessions in Q&A, and feedback that quotes a line before judging it.

8.3 · Your Masterwork & Lifelong Command (C25)

Exercise 1 — Name the thread

1 mot juste · 2 register · 3 cohesion · 4 rhetoric · 5 personal voice. (From the online lesson.) A masterwork weaves all five together until the seams disappear.

Exercise 2 — Principles of lifelong command

1 reading · 2 teach (accept explain) · 3 garden · 4 door (accept graduation). (From the online lesson.)

Exercise 3 — Judge it against the C2 standard
  1. Below the bar. Grammatically flawless but flat: short, undifferentiated clauses that state without shading.
  2. Meets the bar. Precise diction ("ambitious to the point of recklessness"), a nuanced concession ("in spite of itself"), and a differentiated observation about the audience.
  3. Below the bar. A register mismatch and vague fillers ("like," "and stuff") that blur rather than sharpen the meaning.
  4. (new) Meets the bar. A negative inversion used for genuine emphasis, a precise and evaluative collocation ("quiet evasions"), and a hedged, ironic stance ("I suspect, precisely what its authors intended") that differentiates a fine shade of meaning: the evasions were deliberate.

The point to draw out: the C2 standard is met by precision and differentiated nuance, not by long words. Item 4 would fail if the inversion were decoration; here it enacts the delay it describes.

Exercise 4 — Concede, then narrow (models)
  1. "That's a fair challenge, and I'd hate to sound nostalgic; phones have given us a great deal. Let me draw the distinction I actually mean: I'm not saying screens are the problem; I'm saying constant interruption is, and the evidence on that is hard to ignore."
  2. "You're right to press that: the three studies are all from one country, and I should say so more clearly. So I'd narrow the claim: the findings are strong for that context, and they give us a reason to test the idea elsewhere, not a reason to assume it works everywhere."
  3. "Granted, real life is rarely that tidy, and I did consider a more open ending. What I was after is narrower than realism: the neatness is the character's wish, not the world's, and the last line is meant to make you doubt it. If that doesn't come through, that's something I'd want to fix."

Accept any answer that concedes something specific and restates the claim more precisely. Reject answers that concede everything ("You're right, it's just nostalgia") or nothing ("It's fiction").

Exercise 5 — From vague to precise feedback (models)
  1. "What landed for me was the line 'the choices we don't notice are the ones shaping us': it turned the whole talk back on the audience."
  2. "Your ending summarizes points we'd just heard. I wonder whether you could end on the image of the empty library instead; if this were mine, I might cut the last two sentences."
  3. "The door metaphor carries the piece for me. I wonder whether the river image in paragraph three competes with it: two journeys at once. If this were mine, I might keep only the door."
  4. "Someone might push back on the claim that all schools can afford this: how would you answer a principal with a fixed budget?"

Look for a quotation or precise reference, an effect, and either a question or one offered change. Disagreement (item 4) becomes useful when it turns into a challenge the writer can answer.

Dialogue — the masterwork and its discussion

1 The door and the room: fluency is only the entrance; the value lies in what fluency makes possible (catching the humor, being moved, meeting people in their own words). "Mistaking the door for the room" means treating basic fluency as the goal rather than the threshold. · 2 Something more precise: she concedes the risk ("you're right to press it; I'd hate for this to sound like a hierarchy of worth"), then narrows the claim (mastery opens particular rooms; it doesn't make anyone's life richer or their worth greater). · 3 Precise diction: moved set against understood, hierarchy of worth; rhetorical device: the rhetorical question "Good enough for what?", the door/room antithesis; personal voice: the candid "I spent years, frankly, mistaking the door for the room." · 4 The summit is "not an ending but a door of its own": mastery is a graduation into lifelong use, the lesson's central theme.

Planning frame, checklist, lifelong plan, rubric

Open tasks: no key. When you read planning frames (C24) and pieces (after C25), use the format rubrics (materials set J). The two most common planning problems: a subject without a controlling idea ("My piece is about libraries") and a range plan that is a checklist (every device in the course, planted). Ask one question each rather than rewriting: "What is the one thing you want the audience to think afterward?" · "Which of these does the thought actually need?" In the lifelong plan, the most common problem is vagueness ("read more"); ask "What, how often, starting when?"

Self-check
Q1
What is the highest skill the masterwork tests?
A) Learning a final new technique
B) Integrating the whole range into one seamless, effortless piece
C) Speaking for as long as possible
Why: nothing in the masterwork is new; the skill is orchestration. Length is not the point, and there is no final trick left to learn.
Q2
Now that you have reached C2, what is the path forward?
A) Move up to the next CEFR level above C2
B) Consider the language finished and stop practicing
C) Keep growing through lifelong reading, real-world use, teaching, and living in it
Why: C2 is the top of the CEFR; there is no higher level. A mastered language left untended fades; tended, it deepens.
Q3 · new
Which response to a peer's masterwork is most useful to a C2 writer?
A) "Great job, very fluent! I loved it."
B) "The garden metaphor landed for me: it made the idea of practice feel hopeful. I wonder whether the second metaphor in paragraph three competes with it. If this were mine, I might cut it."
C) A list of ten corrections, from commas to word choice
Why: B follows the protocol: it quotes, describes an effect, wonders before suggesting, and offers one change the writer can accept or refuse. A is kind but gives the writer nothing to use; C buries the one change that matters.
Errors you will see and hear most (8.3)

In pieces: the highlight reel (every device in one paragraph) · a subject without a controlling idea · mixed metaphors · a register slip in the final paragraph (the formal op-ed that ends "and that's pretty cool") · overclaiming from sources in research summaries ("studies prove") · uncited figures · fossilized items: the society (generic), researches, advices, informations, present perfect with a finished time, depend of, consist in (for consist of), discuss about · mixed American and British spelling. In the discussion: reading the piece aloud instead of presenting · answering the prepared question rather than the one asked · collapsing or stonewalling · responses that praise without quoting, or correct without asking. For range, praise precise concessions, narrowed claims, responses that name an exact phrase and its effect, and challenges aimed at craft choices, not only at arguments.
