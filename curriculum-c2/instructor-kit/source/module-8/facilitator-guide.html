Module 8 — The Summit: Exams, Performance & Lifelong Command

The last three classes of the whole A1–C2 ladder are about performance, in three different arenas. First, the exam room: a C2 exam-skills clinic in which learners treat proficiency-level tasks (key word transformations, gapped text, the essay summary, the speaking long turn and collaborative task) as solvable puzzles with their own logic. Then the stage: a public-speaking lab on structure for the ear, rhetoric performed rather than written, the working voice, nerves and Q&A, ending in three-minute speeches with peer feedback. Finally, the review project, Your Masterwork & Lifelong Command: each learner presents a capstone piece of their own choosing (a speech, an op-ed, a creative piece or a research summary), fields a searching discussion, and shares a personal plan for keeping their English growing when there is no next level.

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The shape is the usual one: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice → feedback with delayed error correction → homework. At C2, 8.1 and 8.2 are workshops: the "language" is a set of task logics, delivery techniques and discussion moves, and most of the time goes to doing them under realistic pressure. C25 is a project class: short input, then the Masterwork Salon.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-8-masterwork.html. Print materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html before C23. It holds the phrase cards, the collaborative-task picture cards, new identity cards, the transformation relay cards, the cut-up gapped text, the essay summary task sheet, long-turn prompt cards and the examiner's descriptor sheet, the speech cards and speech feedback slips, the Summit Climb board game, the masterwork kit (format cards, rotation sheet, chair's script, response slips), the four format rubrics and the discussion rubric, the lifelong-command exchange card, the teacher observation checklist and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and choose a US or UK voice. For this module the online lessons are also where learners practice alone under time (8.1), record a talk (8.2) and record their masterwork (8.3).
  • The project starts in C23, not C25. A polished capstone cannot be produced in class. Learners choose a format and a subject at the end of C23, fill in the masterwork planning frame (Workbook 8.3) before C24, draft and polish after C24, and send written pieces to their salon group before C25. The timeline is in the box below; board it in C23 and leave it up.
  • Refinement toward mastery, not re-teaching. Every skill this module asks for was built earlier: precision and collocation (1.1, 1.3), register (1.2), implicature (2.1), figurative language (2.3), stance and cohesion (3.1–3.2), summary and synthesis (3.3), rhetoric (4.1), style and voice (4.2), writing for readers and at length (6.1–6.2), diplomacy (7.2). Point back whenever a learner reaches for a tool: "That's your 4.1 antithesis, now performed." The new element is performance under constraint: a mark scheme, a clock, an audience, a challenger.
  • Feedback that is genuinely useful at C2. Your learners are near-native in some areas and fossilized in others, and they know it. Generic praise ("very fluent!") is useless to them, and so is a list of ten corrections. Take notes in three columns: Fix (errors, especially the persistent ones: an article with an abstract noun, discuss about, a present perfect with a past time phrase), Refine (correct but imprecise, unidiomatic, off-register, or lacking effect: "correct, but a native editor would say cast doubt on, not put doubt on") and Wow (a choice worth spreading). Board 2–3 of each without names. Keep a private fossilization log: two or three persistent items per learner, which you hand back on a slip at C26. At this level, one precise, named observation is worth more than any score.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes often contain one or two brilliant, fast talkers who fill every silence. In C23 give them the examiner role (they must listen, rate against the descriptors and report in 20 seconds). In the collaborative task, the task itself rewards inviting a partner in: tell them so ("Monologue loses marks here"). In C24 they are timekeepers with a strict three-minute limit, and in C25 the chair, whose job is to bring others in and keep the challenge fair. Say it early and kindly: "Great point, Daniel. Let's hear Mei's take first."
  • Quiet or anxious learners. Speaking in front of peers is the most exposing task in the course, even for a learner who writes beautifully. Allow a seated speech in C24, notes on the table, and a shorter slot (two minutes) if needed. In C25 the four formats exist partly for them: a writer may choose the op-ed or research summary, present a short spoken frame and read a passage. Nobody presents to the whole class: all groups run in parallel.
  • Stretching the strongest. Each lesson has a stretch box. The direction for this module is invisibility of craft: the exam answer that looks effortless, the rhetorical device that sounds like thought rather than technique, the response to a challenge that reframes the question rather than defending against it. Ask strong learners to explain why a choice works, and give them the hardest cards.
  • Peer review, the C2 way. All three classes use the same protocol, which learners know from earlier modules: (1) quote before you judge (name the exact phrase or moment); (2) describe the effect it had on you; (3) ask a genuine question before you suggest a change; (4) offer one change, framed as "If this were mine, I might…"; (5) the writer or speaker decides. Post it on the wall. Comments are about the work, never the person, and never about accent.
  • Exam material: original only. Everything in this module is written for teaching and imitates the style of proficiency-level task types. Nothing reproduces a real or past exam paper. Cambridge English C2 Proficiency (often called CPE) and IELTS are the trademarks of their owners; the kit is independent and unofficial. Formats, timings and word limits change: tell learners to check the current official specification for any exam they plan to take, and describe tasks in class as "typical" rather than "exact."
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and vocabulary too (recognize, organized, practice as a verb, analyze, judgment, defense, period, public transit, toward). Accept British forms (recognise, practise, judgement, full stop, public transport, towards) when learners use them, but ask for one system per piece. Both exams accept either spelling system used consistently; Cambridge's own terms (marks, mark scheme) appear in 8.1 because they are the exam's vocabulary.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Perform for a mark scheme → perform for a room → perform as yourself. In C23 the audience is an examiner with descriptors, and the skill is converting existing mastery into points: read the task, bank the sure marks, prove every answer. In C24 the audience is a room of people, and the skill is making meaning easy to receive: a hook, a spine, a close; the pause; the stressed word; poise under a hard question. In C25 there is no mark scheme and no set topic: learners choose what to make and whom to make it for, and then say how they will keep growing. The module ends the whole ladder on the learner's own terms: command is not something the course gives them; it is something they now keep.

Trauma-informed: topics, exams, audiences and "pass"

Exams carry real weight for many adults at this level: a visa, university admission, professional registration, a job that depends on a band score. Some learners have failed a high-stakes test before, sometimes more than once. Never ask anyone to share a past score or a reason for taking an exam. Frame 8.1 as "technique for anyone who performs English under pressure," not "exam prep for exam candidates." All topics stay in workplace, education, science, arts/culture, technology, language and community territory: the kit's cards contain no party politics, religion, immigration or border policy, policing, war or national identity; don't add any, and steer a masterwork away from them privately if a learner proposes one ("Let's find an angle on this that the group can discuss as ideas"). Long-turn prompts that invite a personal story can always be answered as a new identity or invented, and the lifelong-learning plan may be written for a persona. Any learner may say "Pass, next card." In C25 the discussion challenges the work; the chair stops any question that becomes personal, and nobody is asked where they are from, what they believe, or to speak "for" a culture.

Why these four formats, when the online lesson names a talk, an essay-into-speech or an article you then present? They are the same idea, widened so that every kind of C2 user can show their best: the speech is the online "talk"; the op-ed is the "article you then present" (the C2 6.1 genre); the research summary is the "essay-into-speech" for learners whose English lives in academic or professional reports (3.3 and 6.2); the creative piece (a short story, a personal essay, a sequence of prose poems, a monologue) serves learners whose voice is literary (2.3, 4.2, 5.2). All four are presented aloud and discussed, so all four test the same integration. If a learner's week is too full, 400 well-polished words or a three-minute speech is fine: tell them privately.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the strategist's mindset. A proficiency exam is not a fair sample of a learner's English; it is a stylized, time-pressured performance. At C2, lost marks are almost always leaks in technique, not gaps in knowledge.

"My English is excellent but my score was lower than I expected." Is the problem probably vocabulary? (No, usually technique.) · A reading option is true in real life, but you can't find it in the passage. Right or wrong? (Wrong: "true but not there.") · You wrote a brilliant answer to half of a two-part writing task. Can it reach the top band? (No: the prompt is under-answered.) · In the collaborative task, is a fluent two-minute monologue a good answer? (No: the task rewards interaction.)

Meaning and form: key word transformations.

"I couldn't understand the lecture at all." → "I could make no sense of the lecture." Did the meaning change? (No.) Is NO exactly as given? (Yes.) How many words in the gap? (Three: no sense of; "the lecture" is already printed.) What fixed phrase is being tested? (make sense of.) · "No sooner had she arrived than the argument started." Which word order after No sooner? (Auxiliary before subject: inversion.) Could you write "No sooner she had arrived"? (No: that loses the mark.) · If the limit is 3–6 words and "I'll" is in your answer, how many words is it? (Two.) · You write a perfect sentence but change SOONER to SOON. Mark? (Zero.)

Meaning: word formation and collocation.

"The evidence was, regrettably, ___ (CONCLUDE)." Which part of speech? (Adjective.) Does "regrettably" suggest the evidence settled the question? (No.) So? (inconclusive: the prefix carries the meaning.) · "a strong / heavy / thick / hard possibility." Do all four mean something like "big"? (Roughly.) Which one keeps company with possibility? (strong.) So what are you testing: meaning or partnership? (Partnership: collocation.)

Meaning: gapped text and the essay summary.

The sentence after the gap begins "This compression…". What must the missing sentence mention? (Something being compressed.) · A distractor sentence is about the right topic but has no link to either neighbor. Keep or reject? (Reject: it must fit what comes before and after.) · The essay summary asks you to "summarize and evaluate." If you only summarize, what's missing? (The evaluation and your own view: part of the task.) · Can you copy a key sentence from the text? (No: paraphrase. Lifted phrases don't show your range.)

Pronunciation: the speaking criteria. Proficiency speaking assesses pronunciation as intelligibility and prosody, never as closeness to a native accent. Say this explicitly, because many C2 learners believe their accent costs them marks. What earns credit is chunking (pausing at the boundaries of ideas, not mid-phrase), sentence stress on the new or contrasted information, and intonation that signals structure: a fall at the end of a completed point, a rise or level tone on a list that continues. In the long turn, a signpost followed by a short pause ("My second point | is cost.") sounds controlled. In the collaborative task, questions that invite should rise at the end ("What's your take on it? ↗"); a proposal to close should fall ("So, are we agreed that these two are strongest? ↘").

The Speaking Circuit — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 (candidate · interlocutor · examiner). Each group has the long-turn prompt cards face down, the six picture cards, one examiner's descriptor sheet and a phone timer.
  • Round 1: the long turn (three rotations of about 3½ minutes). The interlocutor offers two cards; the candidate chooses one, has 1 minute to prepare (three key words only), then speaks for 2 minutes. The interlocutor calls "30 seconds" and "Thank you," then asks one follow-up question from the card (30 seconds). The examiner reports in 20 seconds, using the sheet's four criteria: "Strong frame and close; two signposted points; one great collocation, a double-edged sword; pace rushed in the middle."
  • Round 2: the collaborative task (about 5 minutes). Two candidates discuss the six picture cards for 3 minutes: first how effective each idea might be, then which two to recommend. The third learner examines interaction only (tallies invites, builds, soft disagreements and the move to a decision). If time is short, one pair demonstrates and everyone else examines.
  • Descriptor sheet (materials set F): four criteria (fluency & coherence · lexical range · grammatical range & accuracy · pronunciation) plus interaction for the collaborative task, each at three levels written in plain language. The descriptors are original and simplified; they are a practice tool, not an official band scale. Say so.
  • Personal prompts ("Describe a skill you learned later than most people…") can be answered as a new identity or invented. Any candidate may say "Pass, next card."
  • You circulate with Fix / Refine / Wow columns. Sit through one full long turn per group. Listen especially for the ending: most strong candidates run out of time rather than closing.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Some C2 learners speak brilliantly but read slowly under time, or learned to read English late. Give them the four checks as a card to hold, and let them do Ex 1 items 1–4 and Ex 2 part A only. For the gapped text, give the cut-up strips and ask them to find the breadcrumb for one gap first, then the others. In the long turn, allow a 90-second turn and let them choose from the more concrete cards. In the essay summary, a partner may read the two texts aloud once. Target: one transformation checked aloud against all four checks, and one long turn with a clear frame and close.

Stretch

Write two original transformation items for the class (sentence, key word, limit, answer) that test different structures, and justify why each has only one correct answer. In the gapped text, explain why the distractor fails in both positions it seems to fit. In the long turn, take the hardest card and add a concession ("Admittedly…") before the close. As examiner, give feedback that names a specific phrase for each criterion. Homework: the full essay summary under 40 minutes, self-marked against the descriptors.

L1 watch-outs
  • Articles inside fixed phrases. Learners whose languages have no articles (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, and others) may write on brink of or in wake of. In a transformation, a missing article loses the whole mark. Train the eye: the brink, the wake.
  • Negative prefixes by analogy. Romance-language speakers transfer in- where English takes un- (inforeseen, Spanish imprevisto) or the reverse (unconclusive). Keep a class list of the tricky ones: inconclusive, unforeseen, irrelevant, illegible, dissatisfied, misleading.
  • Light-verb collocations transfer from L1: Spanish/Portuguese/Italian make a question (hacer una pregunta), German/Dutch make a photo, Arabic and Persian do a mistake, French take a decision (British, but check the context). These are exactly what the collocation task targets.
  • Memorized essay formulas. Learners from exam systems that reward set phrases (common in Chinese, Arabic, Persian, Turkish and South Asian test-preparation cultures) may open with Nowadays, with the development of society… or Every coin has two sides. Examiners at this level read these as formulaic: they lower the range score. Offer an alternative that engages the actual texts.
  • "True but not there." Learners from academic traditions that value bringing in outside knowledge (a strength in a seminar) are especially prone to choosing the option that is true in the world. Frame the exam as a different game with different rules.
  • Monologue as politeness. Some learners avoid interrupting or inviting a partner because it feels rude or presumptuous (often Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai and Vietnamese speakers); others hold the floor because turn-taking in their L1 conversation is more competitive (sometimes Spanish, Italian, Arabic, Hebrew or Greek speakers). The task rewards the middle; teach the invitation phrases as the polite move.

Meaning: content vs delivery; structure for the ear.

Pronunciation: the working voice. English is stress-timed: stressed syllables fall at roughly regular intervals and unstressed ones are squeezed between them. That is why one strongly stressed word in a thought group carries so much meaning, and why stressing every word ("I-NE-VER-SAID-SHE…") sounds robotic. Teach the notation: | short pause, || long pause, CAPITALS for the stressed word, ↘ for a final fall. A tricolon rises on items one and two and falls on three ("quick ↗ | clean ↗ | completely WRONG ↘"). Antithesis stresses both poles ("known MORE | and understood LESS"). A rhetorical question falls (it is not a real question) and is followed by a real silence. For intimacy, drop the volume, not the clarity; for a call to action, lift pitch and slow down. Accent is never the target. A learner's accent is part of their voice; the goal is intelligible chunking, deliberate stress and controlled pace.

The Speech Lab — how to run it
  • Groups of 4, chairs in a small arc so the speaker can stand (or sit) facing three listeners. All groups run at once: nobody speaks to the whole class. Put a big timer on the screen and call "Change" every 6 minutes so groups stay together.
  • One turn ≈ 6 minutes: speech (3 minutes; the timekeeper shows "1 min" and "30 s" cards and says "Thank you" at 3:15) → the questioner's one question and the speaker's answer (1 minute) → feedback (1½ minutes): the feedback lead reads the slip, and each other listener adds one line they remember and one change.
  • Topics: learners' own (prepared at home), a speech card (materials set G), or, for speech-format masterwork learners, the first three minutes of their masterwork (a real rehearsal). Speeches on a personal topic may use a new identity.
  • The feedback slip has five criteria (open · spine · close · voice · presence & Q&A) at three levels (Mastery · Secure · Developing), plus two lines: "The line I'll remember:" and "If this were mine, I might…". The speaker keeps all three slips.
  • Rules on the slide: no apology to open; no "that's about it" to close; a question is answered, not argued with; feedback quotes before it judges, and never mentions accent.
  • You circulate with Fix / Refine / Wow. Hear at least one full speech per group. The most common C2 problem is not language but time: rich speakers overrun. The timekeeper's "Thank you" at 3:15 is final.

L1 watch-outs
  • Syllable-timed rhythm. Speakers of syllable-timed languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese in part, Turkish, Tagalog, and many others) and mora-timed Japanese give syllables more equal weight, so contrastive stress and the squeezed unstressed syllable feel unnatural. The stress drill helps more than explanation. Praise audible contrast, not accent change.
  • Tone languages and pitch. For Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese and Thai speakers, pitch carries word meaning in L1, so using pitch for emphasis or attitude can feel risky, and some speakers flatten it. Show that English uses length and loudness as well as pitch for emphasis.
  • The opening apology. In several speech cultures (Japanese is the best-known example, and it occurs in Korean, Chinese and many others) a modest opening ("I am not a skilled speaker, but…") is a courtesy. In an English speech it reads as a lack of confidence. Present it as a genre convention, not a moral one, and offer a warm alternative: "Thank you for having me. I want to begin with a question."
  • Eye contact norms. Sustained eye contact with individuals can feel intrusive or disrespectful to some learners (in parts of East Asia, among some West African and Indigenous communities, and for many neurodivergent people regardless of background). Offer "scan the room and rest on friendly faces" or "look at the space between two people" as full alternatives; never mark eye contact down on its own.
  • Bold claims and modesty. Learners from traditions that prize understatement (Japanese, Korean, Scandinavian, Dutch and others, for different reasons) may resist a "bold claim" open. Offer the question or story open instead. Learners from more oratorical traditions (Arabic, Persian, Greek, Italian, many West African) may over-ornament: remind them that at C2 the device should be invisible.
  • "Discuss about," "explain me," "return back." These fossilized items come back under pressure, especially in the Q&A. Note them for the private fossilization log, not the board.

Pronunciation for presenting and discussing. Bring 8.2 into the salon. The opening line is said slowly with a pause after it. A read-aloud passage (op-ed, creative piece) is performed, not read: look up at the end of each sentence, pause at paragraph breaks, and slow the last line. In the discussion, the concession falls and is sincere ("You're right to press it ↘"); the narrowing restarts with a slightly higher pitch and stresses the contrast ("I'm not saying RICHer; I'm saying it opens ROOMS"). A respondent's quotation is said in a slightly different voice, so the group hears it as quoted.

Drill script (quick, choral → individual)
  1. Concede → narrow chain: T gives a challenge: "Isn't this just nostalgia?" S1: "That's a fair challenge, and you're right to press it." S2: "Let me draw the distinction I actually mean." S3: "I'm not saying the past was better; I'm saying we lost one thing worth keeping." New challenge, new chain.
  2. Vague → precise: T: "It was really interesting." → S: "What landed for me was the image of…, because…" T: "Maybe change the ending?" → S: "If this were mine, I might end on the image of the door, and cut the last sentence."
  3. Resist the word: T: "So you think experts are useless?" → S: "I'd resist the word 'useless'; I'd say 'overtrusted.'" T: "So technology is bad for learning?" → S: "I'd resist 'bad'; I'd say 'badly used.'"
  4. Check the question: T asks a long, slightly unclear question. S: "If I've understood you, the worry is that… Is that right?" Then S answers in one sentence.

Running a salon — the chair's order (about 10 minutes)
  1. Chair introduces (15 s): "Welcome. Our presenter is Lina. Her masterwork is an op-ed for a city magazine, arguing that every library should keep one room free of screens. Lina, you have five minutes."
  2. Presentation (4–5 min): the chair shows "1 MINUTE" and "TIME."
  3. Reflect (Respondent 1, 1 min): "What landed for me was…" (a quoted phrase or moment, and its effect).
  4. Probe (Respondent 1, 1 min): one genuine question about meaning or a choice; one short follow-up allowed.
  5. Challenge (Respondent 2, 1½ min): one fair challenge to the thesis, the evidence or a craft choice. The presenter concedes what is valid and narrows. One follow-up allowed.
  6. Offer (both respondents, 30 s): "If this were mine, I might…". The presenter says only "Thank you": no defending during offers.
  7. Response slips (1 min): everyone except the presenter writes a slip and circles the format rubric; the presenter notes the question they found hardest. Rotate.
The rubrics and the observation checklist → C26

The format rubrics (set J; the self-check version is in Workbook 8.3) each have five criteria, three shared by all formats (integration of the C2 range · precision & register · coherence across the whole) and two specific to the format (for example rhetoric performed and voice & delivery for a speech; synthesis and calibrated attribution for a research summary). The discussion rubric has three: understanding the question · concede & narrow · composure. Three levels: Mastery (the C2 standard: precise, nuanced, effortless) · Secure (strong, with occasional visible effort or near-misses) · Developing (the range is there but not yet controlled). Respondents circle the rubric for the presenter; learners self-assess; you use both for written feedback returned at C26. Don't grade C25: it's practice. Say so clearly.

The observation checklist (set L) has one row per learner and 10 can-do items. Three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with visible effort, or with slips that affect precision · ○ not yet / not observed. You can't sit through every salon: see each learner's whole presentation or at least their discussion, and one of their responses. After class, use it to:

  • Plan each learner's C26 assessment. The C2 final assessment is an individual speaking interview (about 12 minutes) plus listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's C2 final assessment. Before C26, read it and match your checklist notes to its speaking criteria: which subjects suit each learner, and where to begin the interview so that each learner starts with something they did well.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become a short review before the assessment begins.
  • Write one sentence of feedback for each learner on the returned piece, naming a real strength from the salon specifically ("Your concession to Sam's point about cost narrowed your claim and made it stronger"), and fill in their fossilization slip (two or three persistent items, with the correct form).
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who froze under challenge gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is one-to-one and friendly. No audience. You'll be fine."
Differentiation
Literacy support

Before C25 (in C24 or by message), agree a lighter piece: a 3-minute speech, or 350–450 words in any format, built directly from the planning frame's sentence starters. In the salon, allow a 3-minute presentation and ask the group's Respondent 2 to use a probe rather than a hard challenge. Give the learner four phrase cards to hold: That's a fair point · Let me draw the distinction I mean · What landed for me was… · If this were mine, I might…. As a respondent, they may write their question in advance and read it. Target: one precise quotation as a respondent, and one concede-and-narrow as a presenter.

Stretch

Answer the question behind the challenge: name its assumption ("That question assumes that access is the only barrier; I'd question that"). Reframe rather than defend. In the piece, aim for one device that carries the whole (like the door and the room) rather than several. As Respondent 2, challenge a craft choice, not only the argument ("Does the second metaphor compete with the first?"). At home: write a 150-word afterword revising the piece in light of the hardest question, and a second version of the lifelong plan for five years, not one.

L1 watch-outs
  • Where the main point goes. Some rhetorical traditions build toward the point (Japanese ki-shō-ten-ketsu, Korean gi-seung-jeon-gyeol, and some Chinese, Arabic and Persian essay conventions value context before claim). An English op-ed or briefing puts it early; a creative piece may delay it on purpose. Frame it as a choice of genre, not a better way of thinking.
  • Fossilized items at C2. The ones most likely to surface in a polished piece: articles with abstract or generic nouns (the society, the nature meaning "nature in general") · uncountables made plural (researches, advices, informations, evidences) · present perfect with a finished time (I have finished it last year) · since / for confusion · dependent prepositions (depend of, consist in for consist of, interested for) · discuss about, emphasize on, explain me. Put them on the fossilization slip, one learner at a time, never on the board with names.
  • Challenging a peer in public. Learners from cultures where questioning a peer publicly risks loss of face (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Vietnamese, Filipino, and many others) may only praise; learners from more direct traditions may challenge bluntly. The role card helps both: "The card asks for a challenge; it's your job, not your opinion." Teach the softeners: Someone might push back on… · I wonder whether….
  • Reading instead of presenting. Learners from exam systems that reward memorized recitation may read an op-ed or research summary word for word. The format rules help: op-eds perform two paragraphs, briefings are from notes. Allow a full script at home for confidence.
  • Spelling system. Written pieces often mix American and British spelling (analyze and organisation in one paragraph). Ask for one system; it's the kind of consistency a C2 reader notices.

Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, the C2 final assessment: an individual speaking interview (about 12 minutes) plus listening, reading and writing tasks — see the kit's C2 final assessment. Read it before C26 and tell learners how the class will be organized (the order of the tasks and when each learner will be interviewed), so there are no surprises. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next steps, which at C2 are the learner's own: a formal exam if a credential opens a door they want, demanding professional or academic use, teaching or mentoring, translation or interpreting pathways, and the lifelong-command plan.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You'll have a one-to-one conversation with me for about twelve minutes, and some listening, reading and writing. It's everything we've practiced. Today you presented a piece you chose and answered questions you didn't expect, from people who had read it closely. That was harder than anything in C26. Then we celebrate."

Reduce anxiety: show C2 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you have chosen between impersonation and copy for a reason, performed it, and defended the choice?" Then remind them of the longer view: for many learners this is the end of a ladder that started at A1. Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that their recorded masterwork is the best preparation for the interview.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite 2–3 volunteers to give a one-minute closing speech: "one line I'll carry home from this course", the C24 skill, with the friendliest audience of all. Learners who want to may read their "letter to my A1 self" aloud, or one line of it; nobody is asked to.
