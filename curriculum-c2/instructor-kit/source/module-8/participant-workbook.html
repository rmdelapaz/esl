8.1

The Strategist's Clinic — C2 Exam Skills

Class C23
New words — the language of the exam room
  • key word transformation: rewriting a sentence with the same meaning, using a given word you may not change
  • open cloze: a text with gaps and no options; mostly grammar words and fixed phrases
  • word formation: turning a given stem into the form the gap requires
  • gapped text: a passage from which sentences or paragraphs have been removed
  • a distractor: an option or a mention designed to attract a wrong answer
  • a breadcrumb: a reference word or connector that shows where a sentence belongs
  • band descriptors: the published criteria examiners mark against
  • task achievement: covering every part of what the prompt asks
  • an under-answered prompt: a response that ignores part of the task
  • the long turn: an extended solo answer in a speaking test
  • the collaborative task: a discussion with a partner that must reach a decision
  • to bank (the sure marks): to secure the easy points first

The Curse of Knowledge

Anyone who has asked a brilliant colleague for directions and received a baffling answer has met what psychologists call the curse of knowledge. (1) The expert has not become careless; the difficulty lies in the very thing that makes them expert.

Once we know something well, we lose access to the state of not knowing it. (2) What was once a sequence of twelve deliberate steps has been compressed, through years of practice, into a single fluent gesture.

(3) Asked to explain, the expert naturally describes the gesture rather than the steps, skipping precisely the moves a beginner most needs to see. The remedy, fortunately, is less mysterious than the problem: experts who watch a novice attempt the task, rather than simply describing it, quickly rediscover the steps they had forgotten they were taking.

A. This compression is what allows a surgeon or a pianist to work at speed, but it comes at a price.

B. Such answers are rarely the product of impatience.

C. Knowledge does not merely accumulate; it reorganizes itself.

D. Beginners, by contrast, tend to overestimate how much they have understood.

True, or true but not there? Write S (stated or clearly implied in the text) or N (it may be true, but the text doesn't say it).

Exercise 4 — The essay summary · Read · Write

A typical proficiency-style writing task: read the two texts, then write an essay that summarizes and evaluates the key points of both and gives your own view, in your own words, in about 240–280 words.

Text 1 · In Defense of Memory

It has become fashionable to dismiss memorization as a relic of the schoolroom, made obsolete by the search engine. Yet this view misunderstands how thinking works. We can only reason with what we already hold in mind: a doctor who must look up every symptom cannot see a pattern, and a reader who must search for every reference cannot enjoy a poem. Far from competing with understanding, a well-stocked memory is its precondition. What we should abandon is not memorization but its dullest methods.

Text 2 · The Scarce Skill

Schools still devote hours to material that any student can retrieve in seconds. That time would be better spent teaching learners to judge sources, to connect ideas and to recognize what they do not know. Memory has not become worthless, but the premium has shifted: in a world of abundant information, the scarce skill is not recall but evaluation. A curriculum that rewards the student who remembers most, rather than the one who questions best, is preparing young people for a world that no longer exists.

Dialogue — a collaborative task, done well · Read · Pair work

Examiner: Here are some ways a city could reduce traffic. Talk together about how effective each might be, and then decide which two to recommend.

Candidate A: Shall we start with congestion charging? On balance, I'd say it's effective but unpopular: it hits lower-income drivers hardest.

Candidate B: That's a fair point, though I'd add that the revenue can fund better public transportation, which softens the blow. What's your take on bike lanes?

Candidate A: I'm keen on them, cheap and healthy, but I see it slightly differently in that they only work where distances are short. So, are we agreed that charging plus investment in public transportation is our stronger pair?

Candidate B: Agreed. Let's recommend those two.

Label each move in the margin: I (invites the partner in) · B (builds on the partner's point) · D (differs, softly) · C (drives toward a conclusion). Which move comes last, and why must it? Why is nobody's turn longer than three sentences?

Read it in pairs, then do it again with a new task from your teacher, without the script.

8.2

The Speech Lab — Delivering with Impact

Class C24
New words — the language of the stage
  • a hook: an opening that earns attention in the first fifteen seconds
  • the spine: the signposted middle of a talk; its two or three points
  • a resonant close: an ending that resolves the talk and leaves one line behind
  • to fizzle out: to end weakly, trailing away instead of finishing
  • a beat: a short, deliberate pause
  • a breath group: a chunk of speech said on one breath, carrying one idea
  • contrastive stress: stress that picks out one word against its alternatives
  • vocal variety: changes of pace, pitch and volume that keep the ear awake
  • rapport: the sense of connection between a speaker and an audience
  • to read the room: to notice an audience's reactions and adjust
  • to field a question: to receive and answer a question, especially a difficult one
  • poise · composure: calm self-possession under pressure
  • to bridge back: to connect an answer to the talk's main thread
Grammar box — building a talk for the ear

A reader can re-read; a listener cannot rewind. A talk must therefore be simpler and more audibly signposted than an essay: tell them what you'll tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them.

Dialogue — a speech and its Q&A · Listen · Read

Speaker: Every expert you admire was once catastrophically bad at the thing they're now famous for. [pause] Every single one.

Speaker: We tell ourselves a comforting lie: that talent is something you're handed at birth. It lets us off the hook. But I want to leave you with three thoughts, and here's the first: the people we call gifted are, almost always, just the people who refused to quit while they were still terrible.

Speaker: Think about that. [pause] The gap between you and the person you admire is not talent. It is time, and the willingness to be bad in public for longer than felt comfortable.

Speaker: So if you take one thing from this room today, let it be this: don't wait to be good before you begin. [pause] Begin, and let the being-good arrive on its own schedule.

Audience member: That's inspiring, but isn't it a bit easy to say? Some people really do start with more advantages than others.

Speaker: That's a fair challenge, and you're absolutely right: the starting lines are not equal, and I'd never pretend otherwise. What I'm arguing is narrower: that whatever your starting line, quitting early guarantees you stay behind it. Advantage helps. Persistence is the part that's yours to control. Does that distinction land for you?

Audience member: It does, actually. Thank you.

Dialogue — a masterwork and its discussion · Listen · Read

Speaker: I've come to believe that fluency in a language is the least interesting thing about knowing it. [pause] Fluency is the door. What lies beyond it, the humor you can finally catch, the argument you can finally win on its own terms, the stranger who becomes a friend because you met them in their own words: that is the room. And I spent years, frankly, mistaking the door for the room.

Speaker: So when people ask me why anyone would climb all the way to mastery (why not stop at "good enough"?), I answer with a question of my own. Good enough for what? Good enough to be understood, yes. But not, perhaps, good enough to be moved, or to move someone else.

Discussant: That's a lovely image, but isn't there something a little elitist in it? Most people will never reach mastery, and they live perfectly rich lives in their language all the same.

Speaker: That's a genuinely important challenge, and you're right to press it; I'd hate for this to sound like a hierarchy of worth. Let me draw the distinction I actually mean. I'm not saying mastery makes a person richer; I'm saying it opens particular rooms: a poem in the original, a joke that doesn't survive translation. Those rooms aren't better than others. They're simply closed until you have the key.

Discussant: And for someone just beginning that climb, what would you say?

Speaker: That the summit they're dreading is closer than they think, and that the view, when they reach it, is not an ending but a door of its own.
