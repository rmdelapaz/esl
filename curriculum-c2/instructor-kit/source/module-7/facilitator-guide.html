Module 7 — Communicating Across Cultures

Three classes about what happens when fluent English meets different unwritten rules. First, cross-cultural pragmatics: politeness strategies, directness and indirectness, small talk, compliments and silence, taboo as a linguistic phenomenon (euphemism, minced oaths, the euphemism treadmill) and humor that does or doesn't travel, with a repair toolkit for when the wires cross. Then diplomacy and negotiation: acknowledge-then-counter, positions versus interests, the BATNA, the conditional trade (If you were able to…, then we could…), face-saving and the cushioned difficult message, practiced in a full negotiation simulation between invented organizations. Last, working between languages: why word-for-word fails, false friends, the untranslatable, register loss, translation versus interpreting and the ethics of interpreting, with a lab in which learners may (voluntarily) bring their own languages into the room.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 90 min per class CEFR C2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → a demanding freer task (a workshop, not a quick chat) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-7-across-cultures.html. Print materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html before C20. It holds the flashcards, the new identity cards, the Critical-Incident Clinic case cards and analysis frame, the euphemism ladders, the two Negotiation Simulation scenarios (confidential A/B briefs, prep sheet, observer checklist), the Translation & Interpreting Lab cards (ethics dilemmas, register rescue, plain-English sight texts, the untranslatables gallery), the Bridge It! board game and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has Listen buttons, so learners can hear the dialogues and model sentences again at home, and shadow the tone, which matters more than usual in this module.
  • These are workshop classes. Each class spends roughly a quarter to a third of its time on one extended performance: diagnosing and bridging invented cross-cultural incidents (C20), a full multi-issue negotiation (C21), a mediation lab with sight translation and interpreting-ethics dilemmas (C22). Protect that time. If you run late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the workshop.
  • Refinement, not a first meeting. These learners met implicature and irony in C2 Module 2 (C4–C6), register agility in 1.2 (C2), and diplomatic softening and hedging at C1 (C1 3.3). Don't re-teach "I was wondering if…". The C2 step is calibration and metapragmatic control: choosing the degree of directness for this person and moment, decoding what others' softeners are doing, repairing a misfire lightly, and being able to explain a pragmatic choice to someone else, which is exactly what a mediator does.
  • Feedback on four things. At C2 accuracy is rarely the story. Take notes in four columns: accuracy (fossilized slips worth fixing: I don't hope so, agree to disagree used as a threat, a false friend like actually for "currently"), range (well-chosen moves to praise and spread: I don't suppose there's any chance…, Where might there be room to move?, The nearest we get in English is…), appropriacy (grammatical but miscalibrated: a hedge so heavy the refusal vanished, with respect used as a sneer, a joke at a colleague's expense) and effect (what the move did to the other person: Did they relax? Dig in? Save face?). In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names.
  • Peer review, every class. Each workshop ends with a structured peer check, each with its own test: the good-faith test (C20: could both people in the incident read your analysis and feel fairly described?), the "Can they sell it upstairs?" test (C21: each negotiator states in one sentence how they will present the deal to their own side; if either can't, face wasn't saved) and the "Explain it back" test (C22: the listener explains the concept or text back in their own words; if the meaning and the feeling both arrive, the mediation worked). Reviewers always give one strength and one suggestion, in that order, and quote the words rather than summarizing.
  • Dominant speakers. C2 classes often include confident professionals: managers, lawyers, teachers, interpreters. Give them the roles that need listening: in C20 the good-faith auditor (who may only ask questions until the group has written two readings), in C21 the observer or the "board chair" who hears the deal only at the end and must be convinced by it, in C22 the listener who explains it back. Say it kindly: "Your ear is excellent. I want you on the checklist this round." In whole-class feedback, use "a new voice first" before opening the floor.
  • Useful feedback for near-native learners. Many of these learners are fluent beyond their teacher in some domains and fossilized in others. Don't praise fluency in general; praise a specific move and its effect ("Your 'Correct me if I'm wrong, but…' made him laugh and concede in the same breath"). Target fossilized items with a private written note, not a public correction. And ask for metapragmatic reasoning: "Why that softener, for that person, at that moment?" At C2, the explanation is part of the skill.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use American spelling and idioms too (humor, recognize, cozy, percent, agree to disagree, Did I get the wrong idea?). Teach the British equivalents as receptive knowledge (per cent, agree to differ, Have I got the wrong end of the stick?) and accept British forms whenever a learner uses them. This module is a good place to say it out loud: varieties of English have pragmatic differences too (understatement, for example), and none is the neutral default.

The through-line

Read the rules → negotiate across them → carry meaning between them. In C20 learners notice that the same words can follow different rules, and practice decoding a hedge, handling a delicate topic and repairing a misfire, without ever turning a person into a stereotype. In C21 they take that calibration into the pressure-cooker: a negotiation where tone decides whether the other side digs in or works with you, and where the best deal comes from interests, not positions. In C22 they cross the widest gap: between languages, where the unit of meaning is the idea, not the word, and where the mediator's ethics matter as much as their vocabulary. Every class produces something another learner hears and judges: a bridge script, a signed deal, a mediated text.

Culture-safe classroom: situations, not nationalities

This module is where a well-meant class can slide into stereotyping. Set the rules on day one (slide 2) and keep them all module. (1) Talk about situations and settings, never nationalities. Don't ask "In your country, how do people refuse?" Ask "In a setting you know well (a workplace, a family, a school, a community), what would be usual? And is everyone there the same?" (2) Nobody speaks for a culture. No learner is ever the "representative" or "example" of a country, a religion or a language community. Any learner can use an invented setting or a new identity card, with no reason given. (3) Individuals, not types. When a generalization slips out ("They're always so direct"), reframe it without shaming: "Some people, in some settings. What about the specific person in the story?" (4) Humor has limits. No jokes at the expense of nationalities, religions, accents or disabilities, and nobody performs an accent, their own or anyone else's. (5) Taboo is studied as language. We look at euphemism and mild substitutes (passed away, let go, the facilities, darn, gosh). Nobody is asked to say a swear word, name a taboo from their own language or describe what is taboo "where they come from." (6) Own languages are voluntary. In C22, learners may draw on their other languages; every task has an English-only route of equal value.

Trauma-informed: invented stakes, and the child interpreter in the room

Keep every negotiation and difficult message invented and low-stakes: room bookings, a coffee cart, a price increase, a launch date, a volunteer rota. Never role-play a firing or layoff, a salary cut, a disciplinary hearing, a medical diagnosis, a landlord eviction, a police, court or immigration interview, or a family conflict. Positions are assigned on cards; learners play the role and anyone can say "Pass." C22 needs particular care: many adult immigrants and refugees interpreted for their parents as children, or have depended on an interpreter (or a child) at a hospital, a benefits office or an immigration appointment. Those settings appear in this lesson only as ethics discussed in the abstract ("Why do professional interpreters exist?"), never as a role-play. If a learner shares such an experience, thank them, don't probe, and offer to talk after class. Journals always offer "a real one, an invented one, or one from a new identity card."

Decide groupings in advance: two negotiators per side works best (A-pair vs B-pair, plus one observer), so groups of 5; with smaller classes, 1 v 1 + observer. Keep the A and B briefs in separate envelopes: the simulation only works if the other side's points stay secret.
  • For C22: the Translation & Interpreting Lab: 8 ethics dilemma cards and 2 register rescue cards per group; the untranslatables gallery (a set of word cards on a side table); 3 plain-English sight texts (the English-only route). Say clearly: nothing personal, no letters, no medical or legal documents, and bringing nothing is completely fine.

  • The Bridge It! board game (1 board per group, 1 die, counters) is for early finishers in C22 and the C23 warmer.

Cross-Cultural Pragmatics — Politeness, Taboo & Humor

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • place a request, refusal, criticism or disagreement on a directness dial and move it up or down on purpose, using the grammar of indirectness (I don't suppose you could…? · Would there be any chance of…? · I was wondering whether…)
  • name the four politeness strategies (bald on record, positive, negative, off record) and explain which one a speaker chose and why

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: pragmatics and the directness dial. Pragmatics is the meaning that isn't in the words but in how, when and to whom they are said. The same act (a request, a refusal) can be done at many points on a dial from bald to oblique; different settings expect different points, and the listener decodes accordingly.

"I don't suppose there's any chance of the file today?" Is it a question about my beliefs? (No: it's a request.) Strong or gentle? (Very gentle.) Why the negative? (It assumes the answer might be no, so the listener isn't cornered.) · "That would be quite challenging." Is it a yes with a warning, or a no? (In many settings, a courteous no.) What happens if you hear it as "doable"? (You commit to something that won't happen, like Maya.) · Is the direct end rude? (Not necessarily: in some settings it's honest and efficient.)

Meaning: politeness strategies. A useful research framework (Brown and Levinson) treats requests, refusals and criticism as face-threatening acts, with four ways to perform them: bald on record (direct, no softening: urgent or close relationships), positive politeness (closeness: we, let's, compliments, shared ground), negative politeness (distance and deference: hedges, apologies, options, I don't suppose…) and off record (a hint the listener must infer: It's chilly in here). The frame isn't in the online lesson; it gives C2 learners the metalanguage to explain their choices.

"Watch out, the step!" Softened? (No.) Rude? (No: it's urgent.) Strategy? (Bald on record.) · "You're a genius with spreadsheets. Can we look at this together?" What's the compliment doing? (Building closeness before the ask: positive politeness.) · "Gosh, it's chilly in here." Is it about the temperature? (Maybe: it could be a hint to close the window.) Can the speaker deny it was a request? (Yes: that's why it's off record.)

Meaning: taboo as language. Every language fences off some topics (death, money, the body, illness, bodily functions). Languages respond with euphemisms (softer words: passed away), dysphemisms (deliberately harsher or jokier words: kicked the bucket) and minced oaths (disguised swear words: darn, gosh, heck, shoot). Euphemisms wear out: as a polite word takes on the associations of the thing it names, a new one replaces it (the euphemism treadmill: toilet → bathroom → restroom). We study this as language, with mild examples only.

"She passed away last spring." Softer or harder than "died"? (Softer.) Where would you use it? (A condolence card, a conversation with the family.) "He kicked the bucket." When might someone say that? (Joking, about someone not close, or to lighten a heavy moment; risky in front of family.) · "Oh, shoot!" Did the speaker swear? (No: it's a mild substitute, a minced oath.) · Why do English speakers say "restroom" in a restaurant, when nobody rests there? (It's a euphemism; the older word felt too direct.)

Meaning: humor and repair. Humor depends on a shared frame; across a gap it's the first thing to fail, and a failed joke can look like an insult. The skill is less the joke than the light repair.

A new colleague arrives late on day one. "Working part-time already?" Is it a joke? (For the speaker, yes: teasing.) What might she hear? (A real criticism.) Why? (No shared history yet: banter needs a relationship.) · "Sorry, that was meant as a joke, and not a very good one." Long or short? (Short.) Do you explain why it was funny? (No: drop it and move on.) · "Have I got the wrong end of the stick?" British or American flavor? (British. American: "Did I get the wrong idea?" / "Did I misunderstand?")

Pronunciation. Tentative frames only work with a tentative tune. Model a slow start and a fall-rise at the end of softened requests and hedges: "I don't supPOSE you could…?" ↘↗, "That would be quite CHALlenging…" ↘↗ (the fall-rise signals "there's more I'm not saying"). A flat or fast delivery turns the same words into sarcasm. In repair phrases, keep the pitch low and the pace unhurried: "That's on ME." A light laugh-voice on "and not a very good one" signals the joke is dropped. Linking: I don't suppose /aɪ ˈdəʊn(t) səˈpəʊz/ (American /doʊn(t) səˈpoʊz/), with the t nearly silent.

Drill script
  1. Dial it down (choral first, fall-rise tune): T: "Send me the file." → S: "I don't suppose you could send me the file?" T: "Move your bag." → "If it's not too much trouble, could you move your bag?" T: "Check the figures again." → "I was wondering whether you might check the figures again." T: "I disagree." → "I see it slightly differently, though I may be missing something."
  2. Decode and check: T says a hedge; S gives the likely meaning, then a check. T: "I'll see what I can do." → S: "Probably no. When you say you'll see, do you mean it's unlikely?" T: "It's an interesting idea." → "Maybe not keen. Can I check what you mean by interesting?" T: "We'll have to see." → "Uncertain or no. Just so I don't assume, are you saying it probably won't happen?"
  3. Repair it: T describes a misfire; S repairs. T: "Your joke fell flat." → S: "Sorry, that was meant as a joke, and not a very good one." T: "You talked over your colleague." → "That's on me for talking over you; let's back up." T: "Your question was too personal." → "Please tell me if I'm straying into something you'd rather not discuss."
  4. Up the ladder (euphemism): T: "died" → S: "passed away." T: "fired" → "let go." T: "old people" → "older adults / seniors." T: "the toilet" → "the restroom / the facilities." Then reverse one for humor: T: "passed away" → S (jokey, informal only): "kicked the bucket." Ask: "Where would that be risky?"
Critical-Incident Clinic — how to run it
  • What a critical incident is. A short, invented, true-to-life moment where two people behaved reasonably by their own rules and still misread each other. The cards give names and settings only, never nationalities. If a group asks "Where is he from?", answer: "It doesn't matter, and it wouldn't tell you what he meant. Work from what he said."
  • Groups of 3, with three roles: scribe (writes on the frame), voice of person 1 and voice of person 2 (each argues for the good-faith reading of their person). Give one dominant speaker the extra job of good-faith auditor: they may only ask questions until two readings are written.
  • Round 1, Diagnose (8 min): Describe (only observable words and actions: "Ravi said 'Yes, yes, I understand'", not "Ravi was evasive") · Interpret (at least two good-faith readings for each person) · Evaluate (which dimension: directness, silence, compliment, small talk/taboo, humor; what each person was probably trying to do).
  • Round 2, Bridge (7 min): write the exact words that would repair the moment afterward, or pre-empt it. Checklist: ☐ a softener ☐ a clarifying check (When you said…, did you mean…?) ☐ a realigning line (That's on me / We'll get the hang of each other) ☐ responsibility taken, not assigned. Rehearse it as a two-person scene; mind the tune.
  • Round 3, Perform and review (7 min): two groups pair up. Each performs its bridge (60–90 seconds) and reads its Interpret box. The other group runs the good-faith test: "Could both people in the incident read this analysis and feel fairly described?" Flag any sentence that judges a person or a group ("typical," "they always"). Then one strength and one suggestion, quoting the words.
  • Share: one bridge is performed for the whole class. Ask: "What did the repair cost the person who made it? (Very little.) What did it save?"
ICQs before the Clinic
Where are the people in the story from? (It doesn't say, and it doesn't matter.) In "Describe," can you write "he was rude"? (No: only what was said and done.) How many readings for each person? (At least two, both in good faith.) What three things must your bridge include? (A softener, a clarifying check, a realigning line.) In the review, what's the question? (Could both people feel fairly described?) Can you say "Pass" or use a new identity card? (Yes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Some very fluent speakers write far less easily than they speak (often learners with interrupted schooling or literacy in a non-Latin script). Give them the case card read aloud by a partner and a bridge strip of six ready frames to choose from (I think I may have misread that · When you said…, did you mean…? · Just so I don't assume… · That's on me · No harm done · Let's start again from there). They take a voice role in Round 1 (arguing a reading aloud) rather than the scribe role, and perform the bridge. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–5, Ex 3 items 1–3. Target: one good-faith reading said aloud, and one complete notice → clarify → realign repair performed with the right tune.

Stretch (toward professional mastery)

Metapragmatic commentary. Ask strong learners to take a real, public, non-personal text (a company's apology email, a customer-service reply, an out-of-office message) and annotate every politeness strategy in it, then rewrite it twice: one notch more direct, one notch more indirect, and say which reader each version would suit. In the Clinic, their bridge must also include an off-record move that the other person could safely ignore. Ask them to explain in two sentences why a strategy that works with one colleague could backfire with another, without naming any group.

L1 watch-outs (language transfer, not national character)
  • Answering negative questions: speakers of Japanese, Korean, Chinese and some other languages often answer the proposition: "You don't mind? — Yes" (meaning "Yes, that's right, I don't mind"). In English this reads as the opposite. A pure transfer of grammar that looks like a pragmatic clash. Teach: No, not at all / No, I don't mind.
  • Imperative + please as neutral: in Russian, Polish, Czech, Arabic and others, a polite imperative is ordinary courtesy, so "Please send me the report today" feels soft to the speaker and firm to many English listeners. Offer the question and distancing forms.
  • Present-tense requests: Spanish, Portuguese and Italian ¿Me pasas…? / Me passa…? → "You pass me the salt?" and "You send me the file?" Tie it to Could you…? / Would you mind…?
  • "I want" and "must": Spanish quiero, Arabic ureed, German müssen, French il faut can surface as "I want that you…" or "You must…", far stronger in English than intended. At C2 these are fossilized; flag them privately.
  • "Please feel free to not answer": split infinitives are fine in English, but the fixed courtesy frame is feel free not to. Also watch the invented "Please feel free for not answering."
  • False-friend politeness words: Spanish constipado (= has a cold) used for "constipated" can land a speaker in exactly the embarrassing territory euphemism avoids; French sensible (= sensitive); German eventuell (= possibly) → "I'll eventually come" meaning "I might come." Keep a board corner for them; they return in C22.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: diplomatic language. The content may be fixed (the answer is no, the price is higher); the tone is yours. Diplomatic language lowers the temperature so the other person can move without feeling cornered. It is honest: it changes the framing, never the truth.

"I have one or two reservations about that approach." Does she like the approach? (Not really.) Is it a verdict or a concern? (A concern: downgraded.) Why downgrade? (It leaves the other person room to improve it.) · "With respect, we may have to agree to disagree." Has anyone lost? (No.) Is the discussion over? (On this point, yes.) · "With RESpect…" said slowly through the teeth: diplomatic? (No: it's contempt in a velvet glove. The words only work if the respect is real.)

Meaning: positions, interests and BATNA. A position is the demand on the table; an interest is the need underneath it. Two positions can be incompatible while the interests fit together. The BATNA (Fisher and Ury, Getting to Yes) is what you'll do if no agreement is reached; it tells you when to walk away. The online lesson gestures at this ("naming your alternative without threat"); the kit makes it explicit.

"We need the big room every Thursday morning." Position or interest? (Position.) Ask "why?": "Our clients have to see the demo on a large screen." (Interest.) Could another room with a big screen work? (Maybe: interests open options.) · "If this doesn't work out, we'll book the hotel room at $150 a morning." What is that? (Their BATNA.) Should they accept a deal that costs them $200 a morning? (No: it's worse than the BATNA.) Should they say "Give us the room or we go to the hotel"? (No: it's a threat, and it closes the door.)

Meaning: the conditional trade and face. Every concession is tied to a return, so nothing is given away for nothing and the talk moves toward a package. Face = a person's dignity and public self-image; a person who can move without losing face will move.

"If you were able to commit to twelve months, then we could hold the rate." Is Rosa promising to hold the rate? (Only if Daniel commits.) Why "were able to" and not "can"? (More tentative: it offers without pushing.) · "With the new figures in, it'd be completely reasonable to revisit this." Was the other person wrong before? (No: the situation changed.) What does it give them? (A dignified way to change position: a golden bridge.) · "You keep missing the deadline" vs "How do we get the timeline back on track?" Which invites a defense? (The first.) Which invites a solution? (The second.)

Pronunciation. In negotiation, pace and pitch carry authority. Model a slower pace, lower pitch and falling tones on the substance ("the budget is locked ↘"), with rising or fall-rise tones only where you invite response ("What matters most to you here? ↗"). In conditional trades, put contrastive stress on the two sides of the exchange: "If YOU could move on the TIMEline, then WE could look again at the PRICE." In agree to DIFfer the stress is on differ; in reSERvations on the third syllable. For holding phrases, a deliberate pause before speaking ("Let me take a moment… before I respond to that") signals calm, not weakness.

Negotiation Simulation — how to run it
  • Set-up. Groups of 5 (A-pair, B-pair, observer) or 3 (A, B, observer). Neighboring groups get different scenarios. Hand each side its confidential brief in an envelope. Say it twice: "Your points table is secret. Don't show it. Don't compare your points with the other side's: the tables measure different things."
  • The design. Both scenarios have five issues. Some matter a lot to one side and little to the other (in The Hall: storage matters hugely to the Robotics Club and costs the Center little; cleaning matters to the Center and costs the Club little). Groups that haggle issue by issue and split every difference land around the middle; groups that explore interests and trade across issues find a package in which both sides score well above their BATNA. Don't tell them this in advance; it's the debrief.
  • Prep (8 min): same-side pairs fill in the prep sheet (Workbook 7.2): position, interests ranked, BATNA, walk-away point, the other side's likely interests, three conditional offers, one face-saving line, one holding phrase. Circulate and ask each pair: "What's your BATNA worth? So what's the worst deal you'll accept?"
  • Negotiate (12 min): open with a position, not an ultimatum. The observer ticks the checklist and writes down one quote for each move. At minute 10, call "two minutes": they must close on the deal sheet (all five issues filled in) or agree to disagree honestly.
  • Debrief (8 min): (1) The "Can they sell it upstairs?" test: each side says in one sentence how it will present the deal to its board or members ("We've secured a year of steady income and the club will handle cleaning"). If a side can't say it with a straight face, face wasn't saved. (2) Sides reveal their points and compare them with their BATNA. (3) Observer: one strength, one suggestion, quoting. (4) Whole class, 2 minutes: board the scores; ask the highest-scoring groups what they traded.
  • If a group deadlocks: that's a legitimate outcome if both sides' BATNAs really are better. Ask them to close well: "We may have to agree to disagree; let's keep the door open for the spring."
ICQs before the simulation
Are you playing yourself? (No, the brief.) Can you show your points table? (No.) Should you compare points with the other side? (No: they measure different things.) What's your BATNA? (What we do if there's no deal.) Do you accept a deal worse than your BATNA? (No.) Do you announce your BATNA as a threat? (No.) What grammar do you use for offers? (If you were able to…, then we could… / provided that.) What must be on the deal sheet at the end? (All five issues, or "agree to disagree.") What's the test? (Can each side sell it upstairs?) Differentiation
Literacy support

Pair the learner with a stronger reader on the same side, so the brief is read aloud and discussed. Give a six-line move card: "Ideally, we'd be looking at… / What matters most to you here? / If you were able to…, then we could… / We could move on…, provided… / I take your point; my only concern is… / Shall we say…?" and let them be the voice of the pair while the partner keeps the notes. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–4, Ex 4 items 1–3. Target: one complete conditional trade and one acknowledge-then-counter, said aloud in the simulation.

Stretch (toward professional mastery)

Packages and anchors. Teach multiple equivalent simultaneous offers: put two or three packages on the table at once ("We could do $55 with the storeroom, or $45 with the cabinet: which works better for you?"), then read the other side's choice as information about their interests. Discuss anchoring (the first number shapes the range) and how to re-anchor gracefully ("That's a long way from where we'd need to be; let me explain our costs"). After the simulation, they write a 120-word deal memo to their board: clear decision first, then the reasons, framed so the board's face is saved too.

L1 watch-outs
  • Conditional connectors + will: "provided you will sign," "as long as you will pay," "on condition that you will…" (common transfer from future-marked languages, including French, Spanish, Portuguese and Italian, where the subjunctive or future appears after similar connectors). English uses the present simple: provided you sign.
  • "If you would be able to…": learners over-use would in both clauses. Teach the remote pattern: If you were able to…, we could… (though If you would as a polite request, "If you'd sign here," is fine).
  • "Agree to disagree" as a door slam: learners from exam-trained systems deploy it to end a discussion. The idiom is courteous only when it keeps the door open; at C2, prefer We may have to agree to disagree on this one, for now.
  • Modal strength: German müssen, Russian нужно, French devoir → "You must lower the price," which sounds like an order in English negotiation. Offer We'd need…, It would help us if…
  • False friends in the deal: Spanish/Portuguese discutir and Italian discutere (often "argue" as well as "discuss"): "We discussed" may be heard as calmer than the speaker meant, or vice versa; French compromis and Spanish compromiso (= commitment as well as compromise); German Konsequenz (= consistency, also consequence). A compromise in English can sound like both sides losing; a package or solution doesn't.
  • Numbers under pressure: thirteen/thirty, fifteen/fifty stress (thirTEEN / THIRty); percentages ("three percent," not "three per cents"). Always confirm figures in writing: Just to confirm, that's fifty, five-zero?

Meaning: false friends. Words that look or sound alike across languages but mean different things. They're dangerous because the sentence stays grammatical and the word feels right.

"Actuellement, je travaille à Lyon" → "Actually, I work in Lyon." What's wrong? (Actuellement means "currently.") Does the English sentence look wrong? (No, and that's the danger.) · Which false friends are most dangerous for you? (Only the ones between your languages: that's why the list must be personal.) · Are all similar words false friends? (No: most cognates are true friends. Suspect the too-convenient match; don't distrust them all.)

Meaning: the untranslatable. Culture-bound concepts can't be carried by one word, but they can be conveyed: give the category, then paint the situation and the feeling.

"Saudade is a kind of longing." Enough? (No: it's just the category.) What's missing? (The situation and the feeling: longing for someone loved and absent, nostalgia with an ache in it.) · "The closest we'd get is 'chatting over coffee after dinner,' but that loses the sense that it's a whole custom." What is Tom doing? (Offering an equivalent and flagging what it loses.) Is that a failure? (No: it's honest mediation.)

Meaning: translation, interpreting, ethics. Translation = written, with time to research and revise. Interpreting = spoken, in real time: consecutive (the speaker pauses; the interpreter renders each chunk), simultaneous (almost at the same time, usually from a booth) and sight translation (a written text read aloud in another language on the spot). Professional interpreters speak in the first person ("I have two children," not "She says she has two children") and step out of it transparently only to manage the conversation ("Interpreter speaking: may I ask the speaker to repeat the number?").

A speaker makes a rude remark. Should the interpreter soften it? (No: accuracy and completeness. The listener has a right to hear what was said, and in the same register.) · The parent asks the volunteer, "What would you do?" Should the volunteer answer? (No: impartiality and role boundaries. "I'm only here to interpret. You could ask the teacher.") · After the meeting, a neighbor asks what was said. (Confidential: say nothing.) · You didn't catch a number. Guess? (Never: ask, and tell both sides you're asking.) · A child interpreting at a hospital appointment: good idea? (No: that's what professional interpreters are for.)

Pronunciation. Borrowed words: model them once and accept approximations; nobody is marked on Danish vowels. hygge /ˈhjuːɡə/ (often anglicized "HOO-guh"), Schadenfreude /ˈʃɑːdənfrɔɪdə/, saudade /saʊˈdɑːdə/ (Portuguese varieties differ), sobremesa /soβɾeˈmesa/. More important is the explanatory rhythm: a slight pause after the signal ("There isn't a single English word for it… | but"), stress on the contrast in it LITerally means…, but what we'd ACtually say…, and chunked, unhurried delivery in sight translation. Give learners time-buyers for live mediation: Let me put that another way… · What she's saying, roughly, is… (the second only in informal mediation, never in professional first-person interpreting).

Drill script
  1. Frame it (easy, explanatory rhythm): T: "hygge, no single word" → S: "There isn't a single English word for it, but the closest equivalent would be a kind of cozy contentment." T: "I hold you in the current" → "It literally means 'I hold you in the current,' but what we'd actually say is 'I'll keep you posted.'" T: "actuellement, trap" → "Careful with that word: it looks like 'actually,' but it really means 'currently.'" T: "saudade, feeling" → "Roughly, it's the feeling of longing for someone you love who isn't there anymore." T: "sobremesa, use" → "It's the word we use for the lingering talk at the table after a meal."
  2. First person, please: T reads a reported line; S renders it as an interpreter would. T: "She says she has two children." → S: "I have two children." T: "He's asking if the fee can be waived." → "Can the fee be waived?" T: "She says she's sorry she's late and the bus didn't come." → "I'm sorry I'm late. The bus didn't come." T: "He wants to know what time practice ends." → "What time does practice end?" Then T: "You missed a number." → S: "Interpreter speaking: could you repeat the number, please?"
Translation & Interpreting Lab — how to run it
  • Own languages are voluntary, always. Every part has an English-only route of equal value, and nobody is asked to translate for the class, teach a word or "represent" a language. Learners who share a language may choose to work together; nobody should be paired because of a shared language without asking.
  • Part 1, Ethics and register (8 min): each group takes two ethics dilemma cards and one register rescue card. For each dilemma: ☐ name the principle(s) at stake (accuracy/completeness · impartiality · confidentiality · transparency · role boundaries · competence) ☐ script the interpreter's exact words ☐ decide whether a professional interpreter should be there instead. For the register card: restore the original tone to a flattened rendering. Then, all together, Script 7.3-B: the teacher reads what was said and what the volunteer "interpreted"; groups count the breaches (there are six).
  • Part 2, Mediate (13 min): triads, three rounds, rotate roles. Mediator (about 2 minutes): option (a) sight translation of a short, simple, public text they brought (recipe, sign, label, children's news story), read aloud directly in natural English, not word by word; option (b) render a plain-English sight text (a dense English notice) into friendly spoken English for a neighbor; option (c) convey an untranslatable word (their own or from the gallery) with the paraphrase pattern. Listener: runs the "Explain it back" test: "So what you're telling me is…" (meaning) "…and it feels…" (feeling/tone). Checker: notes on the lab record ☐ idea not words ☐ register kept (friendly stays friendly, formal stays formal) ☐ one mediating frame used (quote it) ☐ any false-friend trap.
  • Sight-translation tips to give first: read the whole text silently first (30 seconds); chunk it into ideas; start with the main point; if a word won't cross, paraphrase and keep going; keep the tone of the original.
  • Part 3, Share (3 min): two mediators repeat their best rendering for the class; the class says what the original text was for and what tone it had.
  • Early finishers: the Bridge It! board game.
ICQs before the Lab
Do you have to use your own language? (No: there's an English route.) Can you bring a letter from the bank or the doctor? (No: public, simple, non-personal texts only.) In sight translation, word by word or idea by idea? (Idea by idea.) If a word won't cross? (Paraphrase and keep going.) What does the listener do? (Explain it back: the meaning and the feeling.) In the ethics cards, can an interpreter leave out a rude remark? (No.) Give advice? (No.) Guess a number? (No: ask, transparently.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Learners with limited literacy in their first language (or in any script) can do the lab entirely orally: option (c), conveying a word or idiom they know from speech, is just as demanding and needs no reading. Give a frame strip with the four steps of the paraphrase pattern and four frames. For the ethics cards, a partner reads the card aloud; the learner decides the principle from a picture-free list of six single words. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–6, Ex 3 items 1–4. Target: one complete signal → image → meaning → feeling explanation that a partner can explain back.

Stretch (toward professional mastery)

Back-translation and the translator's note. Learners who work in two languages sight-translate a short text into English, then a partner who shares the language (if any) back-translates it; they compare what drifted. Others write a 60-word translator's note justifying one choice (why they kept an image, dropped a pun or changed the register). Try 60 seconds of whispered simultaneous interpreting (chuchotage) of the teacher reading a plain-English sight text into simpler English, and discuss why professionals rotate every 20–30 minutes. For ethics: when is cultural brokering (explaining a cultural point) part of the job, and how do you do it transparently so both sides know?

L1 watch-outs (false friends by first language)
  • Spanish: actualmente (= currently), asistir (= attend), carpeta (= folder), éxito (= success), librería (= bookstore), constipado (= has a cold), embarazada (= pregnant), sensible (= sensitive). Portuguese: pretender (= intend), puxar (= pull), assistir (= watch/attend).
  • French: actuellement (= currently), éventuellement (= possibly), assister à (= attend), librairie (= bookstore), sensible (= sensitive). Italian: libreria (= bookstore), camera (= room), morbido (= soft), fattoria (= farm).
  • German: Gift (= poison), eventuell (= possibly), bekommen (= get/receive), Chef (= boss), sensibel (= sensitive). Polish: ewentualnie (= possibly), aktualny (= current). Russian: магазин (= shop), аккуратный (= neat, careful), фамилия (= surname).
  • Japanese and Korean loanwords that look English but aren't used that way: Japanese manshon (= apartment building), kurēmu "claim" (= complaint), sumāto "smart" (= slim), konsento "consent" (= power outlet); Korean haendeu-pon "hand phone" (= cell phone), aisyoping "eye shopping" (= window shopping), paiting "fighting!" (= good luck, go for it!).
  • Varieties, not errors: some forms that look like transfer are standard in a variety of English, such as Philippine English comfort room (= restroom) or Indian English prepone (= bring forward). Treat these as audience questions ("Will this reader know it?"), which connects to C2 5.1 (Global Englishes).
  • Collocation and grammar transfer: "do a mistake," "make a photo," "open/close the light," "I have 30 years," "arrive to," "strong in math." These are the "grammar" and "collocation" rows of the mismatch table: fix them as chunks.
