Module 7 — Communicating Across Cultures: Answers

Answers to every Module 7 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open tasks (the critical-incident bridges, the negotiation deals, the mediated texts and ethics dilemmas), and the errors you can expect to meet most.

Lessons 7.1–7.3Classes C20–C229 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

At C2, mark accuracy, range, appropriacy and effect. Most items in this module are pragmatic judgments or rewrites with many good answers, so the models below are examples, not the answer. Accept any version that (1) keeps the message (a refusal is still a refusal, a price is still a price), (2) is accurate (fixed frames intact: feel free not to, I don't suppose you could, provided + present simple, agree to disagree; accept British agree to differ), (3) fits the person and the moment, and (4) would plausibly have the intended effect on the listener. Two pragmatic cautions: never accept an answer that explains an incident by a nationality or group ("because people from X…"), and in Exercise 2 of 7.1 accept a different label when the learner can justify it: strategy labels depend on context, and a good argument is the C2 skill. American English is the model; accept British forms (humour, cosy, per cent, brilliant, Have I got the wrong end of the stick?).

7.1 · Cross-Cultural Pragmatics (C20)

Exercise 1 — A misunderstanding, repaired (Script 7.1-A)
  1. That Thursday isn't realistic: it was an indirect, face-saving refusal, not a "difficult but doable."
  2. "Ah, I… I'll certainly do my best." (a second, weaker refusal)
  3. Jun went quiet: his silence (and the hesitation before it).
  4. idea · talked · on · back · hang
  5. Monday. It's a promise Jun can keep and Maya can pass on to the client; an optimistic Thursday risked a broken deadline and lost trust.

Accept any wording for 1 that includes "no / not realistic / unlikely." Item 4a is American; point out the British equivalent, Have I got the wrong end of the stick? (common slip: "wrong side of the stick"), and accept Did I misunderstand you? as well.

Exercise 2 — Name the strategy
  1. BR (urgent warning: softening would be absurd)
  2. NP (negative question, minimizing "ten minutes")
  3. PP (compliment, "shall we," shared task)
  4. OR (a hint; the speaker can deny it was a request)
  5. NP (apology, "would you mind," "terribly")
  6. PP ("let's," "just the two of us": solidarity)
  7. BR
  8. OR (an indirect complaint; also accept NP-flavored OR: the distancing "I'd have thought" hedges it)

Polite in one setting, abrupt in another: most often 7 (fine between close colleagues on a deadline day or in some efficient workplaces; curt to a stranger or a senior colleague) and 3 (warm among peers; over-familiar, even manipulative-sounding, to a new manager). Accept 1, 6 or 8 with a sound argument. Reject any answer that assigns a strategy to a nationality.

Exercise 3 — Decode the hedge (models)
They said…It may mean…A check that doesn't accuse
1. That would be quite challenging.probably no / not realisticWhen you say challenging, do you mean it's unlikely to be possible?
2. I'll see what I can do.a courteous no, or a low-probability maybeJust so I don't assume, should I plan on it or look for another option?
3. It's an interesting idea.not convinced / not keenCan I check what you mean by interesting? I'd really value your honest reaction.
4. We'll have to see.uncertain, leaning noWould it help if I came back to you next week, or is it better to assume no for now?
5. Mm. Maybe next time.no (this time and possibly any time)No problem at all. Shall I ask you again next month, or would you rather I didn't?
6. (pause) Yes… I think that could work.reluctant or conditional yes, or a yes that means "I hear you"I sense some hesitation. Is there anything that would make it work better for you?

The check must offer an exit and take the possible misreading on the asker ("Just so I don't assume…"). Not yet: "So is that a yes or a no?" (forces bluntness), "Why can't you just say no?" (blames), "You said yes, so…" (holds them to a softener). Item 6 is the hardest: accept any reading that registers the pause as information.

Exercise 4 — The euphemism ladder
  1. kicked the bucket → died → passed away
  2. got the boot → was fired → was let go
  3. the john → the bathroom → the facilities
  4. over the hill → older → senior
  5. lying through his teeth → not telling the truth → being economical with the truth
  6. cheap → inexpensive → budget-friendly

(a) passed away (also accept died: many people prefer the plain word, and grief counselors often recommend it; a good discussion point). (b) They are minced oaths: mild, disguised substitutes for swear words, acceptable in most settings. (c) The euphemism treadmill: a polite word gradually absorbs the associations of the thing it names, so it stops feeling polite and a newer word replaces it. Accept the restroom or the toilet as the middle rung in set 3 (British toilet is neutral); accept cheap as neutral in set 6 if the learner means price only, but point out its "low quality" connotation.

Exercise 5 — When humor misfires (models)
  1. "Sorry, I meant the opposite! It's a terrible day. Bad joke. Shall we make a run for it?"
  2. "Sorry, that came out wrong. I was only joking, and it wasn't a very good one. I'm glad you made it; we're just getting started."
  3. "Can I just clarify something? When I said I'm hopeless with numbers, I was being modest. I'm actually comfortable with budgets and I'd like to keep that task, if that's all right."
  4. "Never mind, obscure reference! Anyway, where were we?"

Good repairs are short, light and forward-looking, take responsibility, and don't explain the joke. Item 3 needs a clarifying repair: self-deprecation read literally has real consequences. Not yet: "It was a joke. You don't have a sense of humor?" (blames the listener) or a paragraph explaining irony.

Dialogue — the compliment and the pause

Deflected compliment: "Oh, no, it was very rough." Lena neither argues at length nor takes it as distress; she accepts the modesty lightly ("it didn't look rushed from where I was sitting") and moves on. Silence handled well: "Take your time. I know it's a tricky one." (she doesn't fill it or push). Hedge decoded: "difficult" → Lena reads it as a likely no. Clarifying check: "So when you say 'difficult,' you'd expect a no as it stands?" "Let's draft the smaller version together" = positive politeness (shared action, "let's").

Speaking — Critical-Incident Clinic (model for Case 1, "The yes that wasn't")

Describe: Elena asked, "Can you finish the inventory by Friday?" Ravi said, "Yes, yes, I understand." On Friday the inventory wasn't finished; Ravi said he had told her it was difficult. Interpret: Ravi: (a) "yes" meant "I hear you," not "I agree"; (b) he signaled difficulty in a way that was clear by his norms (perhaps a pause, "I understand"); (c) refusing a new manager directly felt risky. Elena: (a) she heard an explicit "yes" and reasonably planned on it; (b) in her experience, anyone with a problem says so. Evaluate: directness (and the meaning of "yes"). Bridge: "Ravi, I think I may have misread you on Tuesday. When you said 'Yes, I understand,' did you mean you'd heard the request rather than that Friday was possible? That's on me for not checking. From now on, shall we agree that if something's tight, you tell me what date would work, and I'll ask rather than assume?" Models for all eight cases are on the fold-back strips of the case cards (classroom materials, set C). The good-faith test fails any analysis with "typical," "always," a nationality, or an adjective about character.

Self-check
Q1
"That might be a little difficult." The safest reading is:
A) They're just being shy; press for a firm yes.
B) It may well be a polite "no": treat the hedge as the answer.
C) It's basically a yes with a small caveat.
Why: a heavy hedge is often a face-saving refusal; the softener carries the message. Pressing (A) forces the speaker to be blunter than their norms allow; C is the classic over-reading (Maya's error).
Q2
A joke has landed wrong. Best first move?
A) Explain what made it funny.
B) Say nothing.
C) Lightly acknowledge it and move on.
Why: a short, unfussy acknowledgement repairs the moment; explaining (A) deepens the awkwardness, and silence (B) leaves any offense unrepaired.
Q3 · new
A new colleague asks how much you earn. Most graceful response?
A) "That's none of your business."
B) "Oh, enough to keep me in coffee! How are you finding the new office so far?"
C) Tell them the exact figure.
Why: the question may be ordinary friendliness by the asker's norms. B declines lightly (self-directed humor), protects both people's face and redirects to safe small talk. A shames the asker; C gives up a boundary you're entitled to keep.
Errors you will meet most (7.1)

"I don't suppose you can…" (needs could) · "feel free to not answer / for not answering" (fixed frame: feel free not to) · "Have I got the wrong side of the stick?" · answering negative questions by the proposition ("You don't mind? — Yes.") · clarifying checks that accuse ("So you lied?") · explaining a failed joke at length · euphemisms in the wrong room (kicked the bucket in a condolence message) · generalizing about groups in analysis, which is the most important thing to catch and reframe gently. For range, praise Would there be any chance of…, I'll leave it to you to say as much or as little as you like, No harm done. For effect, praise any bridge that took responsibility in the first line.

7.2 · Diplomacy, Negotiation & High-Stakes Communication (C21)

Exercise 1 — A delicate negotiation (Script 7.2-A)
  1. Her material costs have risen sharply, across the whole sector; the current rate no longer covers delivery.
  2. The budget on his side is "more or less locked": no price increase.
  3. commit · could
  4. 10 · 3
  5. Predictability and something defensible to his own bosses: he doesn't want to be "back here in six months having the same conversation."
  6. Put the deal in writing today. It gives Daniel "something solid to show his team": a locked-in rate with no surprises that he can present as stability, not a cost (a golden bridge).

Accept "ten percent / three percent" or "10% / 3%." British per cent is also correct; accept it.

Exercise 2 — From blunt to diplomatic (models)
  1. I appreciate where you're coming from, but I see it differently.
  2. I have one or two reservations about that approach.
  3. I'm afraid we won't be able to do that. / I'm not sure we're going to be able to do that, I'm afraid.
  4. With respect, we may have to agree to disagree on this one.
  5. Things didn't quite go to plan on this occasion.
  6. Correct me if I'm wrong, but I think the figures in the second column might need another look.

Not yet: 3 "I'm afraid no" · 4 "agree to disagree" said as a door slam (accept the phrase itself; it's common in American English, but check the tone) · 6 "Correct me if I'm wrong, but you're wrong" (weaponized courtesy: point out the effect) · any version so soft the message disappears ("Maybe the numbers are sort of possibly a little…").

Exercise 3 — Positions, interests and BATNAs
12345678
PIBIPBIP

Model trade: "If you were able to take the big room on alternate Thursdays, then we could take it on the others, and on your weeks we'd use the small room with the whiteboard wall for our sketching sessions." / "What if we were to split Thursday: you have nine to ten-thirty for the client demo on the big screen, and we take ten-thirty to twelve for our team meeting?" Any trade that serves both sets of interests (a big screen for clients; a weekly face-to-face meeting; a sketching wall) is correct. Note: 3 and 6 are each team's BATNA, which sets the value of any deal: a deal worse than the hotel room (or than meeting online) should be refused.

Exercise 4 — Turn a demand into a trade (models)
  1. If you were able to move a little on price, then we could keep the current delivery schedule.
  2. We could deliver early, provided (that) you pay the deposit now.
  3. We can give you a discount as long as you order 500.
  4. Supposing we reduced the scope slightly, would you consider extending the deadline?
  5. We'll add weekend cover on condition that you sign for twelve months.
  6. What if we were to extend the contract to a year, and you lowered the fee?

Check the grammar: present simple after provided / as long as / on condition that (✘ provided you will pay); past after supposing and What if we were to… for the tentative version (Supposing we reduce…, would you…? is also acceptable, slightly less tentative). Every item must give something in return: an answer to 6 that only asks ("What if we were to ask you to lower the fee?") is a demand in disguise.

Exercise 5 — The cushioned message

A. b → d → a → c.

B. Model: "I'm afraid I have some disappointing news, and I want to be straight with you. The committee has decided not to fund your street festival proposal this year. It was a genuinely strong proposal; the deciding factor was the budget, not the quality of the ideas. I'd be very glad to talk through what tipped the balance, and I hope you'll apply again in the spring round." Check: the decision appears in the first two sentences; one brief, honest reason; one forward-looking line; no more than two softeners before the decision.

Dialogue — the golden bridge

Blaming the situation: "The supplier delays have been against us from the start." Crediting the reasoning: "Your plan was sound given what we knew in March." Golden bridge: "With the new delivery figures in, it would be completely reasonable to revisit it." Conditional trade: "If you were able to take the new date to the board, I could draft the explanation for you today." Marcus saves face by reframing the change as his own plan: "phase one on Friday, full launch on the twentieth." Friday still happens; nobody "got it wrong."

Speaking — Negotiation Simulation (scoring guide)

The Hall (Center = A, Club = B). BATNAs: A 55 points (the yoga studio), B 40 points (the library room). Integrative package ($55 a night, 2 nights, the storeroom, 12 months, the club cleans): A 100 · B 100. Split-the-difference deal ($55, 2 nights, the cabinet, 6 months, shared cleaning): A 75 · B 83. Rent-only haggle (the club pushes rent down to $40 and nobody discusses storage or cleaning: $40, 2 nights, no storage, 6 months, the center cleans): A 45 · B 80. That is below the Center's BATNA of 55, so a well-prepared A should refuse, and B has missed the storeroom it needed most.

The Coffee Cart (Library = A, Co-op = B). BATNAs: A 55 (a vending machine, with no plaza to manage), B 35 (the farmers' market). Integrative package (3 days including Saturday, $250 a month, cart paused during Saturday story time, the co-op sweeps the plaza, a 3-month trial with automatic renewal to 12 months): A 107 · B 72. Split deal (3 days, $250, no story-time pause, library staff clean, 3-month trial): A 50 · B 60, below the Library's BATNA of 55, so the library should refuse it. The co-op gains most by trading the two things that cost it little (the one-hour pause and the sweeping) for the automatic renewal it needs. Points tables are on the confidential briefs (classroom materials, set E). Points are not comparable across sides; judge each side against its own BATNA.

What good negotiators said (examples): "What matters most to you in the space itself?" · "If you were able to take on the cleaning, then we could clear the storeroom for you." · "Supposing we paused the cart during story time, would that make five days possible?" · "With a trial built in, it would be completely reasonable for your board to say yes." · "Can they sell it upstairs?": "We've got a year of steady income and a cleaner hall" / "We've got storage for the robots and two nights a week."

Self-check
Q1
The classic conditional trade?
A) "If you were able to move the deadline, then we could look again at scope."
B) "Take it or leave it: that's my final offer."
C) "It'd be completely reasonable to revisit this now."
Why: A ties a concession to a return and keeps the deal moving toward a package. B is an ultimatum that invites a standoff. C is a useful face-saving bridge, but not a trade.
Q2
The pitfall of over-softening a rejection?
A) It lands too harshly.
B) It's too short.
C) The listener may not realize they were turned down and may feel misled later.
Why: if the decision is buried in cushioning, the listener misses it, and the kindness backfires when the truth sinks in. Clarity is itself a form of respect.
Q3 · new
What is a BATNA?
A) Your ideal outcome.
B) Your best alternative if no agreement is reached.
C) Your first concession.
Why: the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (Fisher and Ury) is what you'll do if you walk away. It sets your walk-away point: never accept a deal worse than your BATNA. A is your opening position (or aspiration); C is a tactic.
Errors you will meet most (7.2)

"provided you will sign / as long as you will pay" · "If you would be able to…, we would can…" · "I'm afraid no" · with respect and correct me if I'm wrong used as sneers (weaponized courtesy) · revealing the BATNA as a threat ("Give us the room or we go to the hotel") · conceding without a return ("OK, we'll lower the price") · haggling one issue at a time and splitting every difference · over-softened refusals that never state the decision · compromise used where a solution or package is meant. For range, praise Where might there be room to move?, What if we were to…?, Your logic was sound given what we knew. For effect, praise the moment a learner asked a genuine interest question and changed the direction of the talk.

7.3 · Working Between Languages (C22)

Exercise 1 — sobremesa (Script 7.3-A)
  1. sobremesa (Spanish)
  2. "over-the-table"
  3. the time you spend still sitting and talking after a meal, when the plates are cleared but nobody wants to leave
  4. not being in a hurry: the meal isn't over just because the food is
  5. "chatting over coffee after dinner"
  6. the sense that it's a whole custom (not just a chat that happens to occur)
Exercise 2 — False friends

1 pregnant · 2 currently · 3 poison · 4 bookstore · 5 to intend · 6 sensitive · 7 possibly · 8 a shop · 9 an apartment building (a condominium). The optional item: accept any genuine pair; check it with a dictionary if in doubt. English-only examples are fine (eventually vs possibly from the learner's own mistakes).

Exercise 3 — Why word-for-word fails
  1. I → I'll keep you posted. / I'll keep you in the loop.
  2. C → Can you take a photo of us?
  3. G → I'm thirty-four. / I'm thirty-four years old.
  4. C → She made a mistake in the report.
  5. C → Please turn on the light. / Please switch on the light.
  6. G → I saw her at the station yesterday.
  7. C → He's very good at cooking. / He's a great cook.
  8. I → Take your umbrella: it's pouring. / it's raining cats and dogs. (French il pleut des cordes)

Accept G for 7 if the learner focuses on the preposition. In 3, accept a "C / G" argument (it's a structural pattern with have + age). For 8, praise a learner who notices that matching the idiom with an idiom (raining cats and dogs) keeps the playful register, while it's pouring is neutral: both are correct.

Exercise 4 — Paraphrase the idiom (models)
  1. to take on more than you can realistically handle
  2. it's your turn to act or decide: the next move is up to you
  3. to be extremely expensive
  4. to share the same understanding of a situation or plan
  5. to postpone dealing with a problem, so it has to be faced later
  6. something that seems bad at first but turns out to be good

Each must state the plain meaning; the image is optional ("it's like taking a bite too big to chew"). Not yet: a paraphrase that uses another idiom ("to be in over your head" for 1 is a synonym, not a paraphrase for a literal listener).

Exercise 5 — Register and ethics

A. b. Rendering (a) keeps the facts but turns a warm, casual neighbor into an official notice: register loss. The feeling (friendly, informal, "come whenever") is part of the meaning; losing it is a failure of fidelity. Accept American or British equivalents for Pop round ("Come by / Stop by").

B. 1 F (accuracy and completeness: the listener has a right to hear what was said, in its register) · 2 T (the first-person convention) · 3 F (transparency: ask, and let both sides know you're asking) · 4 T (confidentiality) · 5 F (impartiality and role boundaries: redirect the question) · 6 T (a widely held principle; in the U.S. many public services, including hospitals and courts, must provide qualified interpreters).

Dialogue — interpreting at the community center

First person: the volunteer speaks as the speaker, so the coordinator and the parent address each other directly and nothing is filtered through the interpreter's summary. Unknown term: she steps out transparently ("Interpreter speaking: may I check a term?"), asks, then tells the parent what she asked, so both sides know everything that was said. "What would you do?": answering would break impartiality and role boundaries; she offers to pass the question to the coordinator instead.

Speaking — Translation & Interpreting Lab (ethics cards and Script 7.3-B)

Ethics cards (principle · model words): 1 advice requested at a parent–teacher conference · impartiality/role · "I'm only here to interpret; shall I ask the teacher?" 2 a rude remark at a community meeting · accuracy/completeness · render it in the same register, without comment. 3 a joke that won't cross · accuracy + transparency · "Interpreter speaking: the speaker made a play on words that doesn't translate. The meaning is…" 4 a neighbor asks what was said · confidentiality · "I'm sorry, I can't talk about that." 5 a missed number · transparency · "Interpreter speaking: could you repeat the number, please?" 6 fast speech full of jargon · accuracy/competence · "Interpreter speaking: could you pause more often, and may I check two terms?" 7 a friend's bank appointment with unfamiliar financial terms · competence · say so beforehand and suggest asking the bank for a professional interpreter. 8 "Don't translate that part" · completeness + transparency · explain that you must interpret everything said in the meeting; they can choose not to say it.

Script 7.3-B, the six breaches: (1) the third person ("She says she's been waiting…") instead of the first person ("I've been waiting…") · (2) omission: the broken water tap is left out (completeness) · (3) softening: "This is ridiculous" becomes "She's a little concerned" (accuracy; the register is lost) · (4) guessing the fee ("Fifteen or fifty? …Fifty, I think") instead of asking (transparency, accuracy) · (5) adding advice: "just water in the evening, like I do" (impartiality, role boundaries) · (6) an unreported side conversation ("Oh, nothing important") (transparency). Accept confidentiality as a seventh: the parting remark, "I'll tell everyone at the market how it went."

Plain-English sight texts (models for the English route) are printed small at the bottom of each text card (set F). The "Explain it back" test passes when the listener can say what the text is for, its key facts and its tone.

Self-check
Q1
Why does word-for-word translation of an idiom usually fail?
A) Idioms are too long.
B) An idiom's meaning isn't the sum of its words, so the literal version is opaque.
C) The grammar can never be made correct.
Why: an idiom must be matched to an expression with the same function, not decoded word by word. Length (A) is irrelevant; the grammar (C) can be perfect and the meaning still lost ("I hold you in the current").
Q2
The key difference between translation and interpreting?
A) Translation is written, with time to craft; interpreting is live speech in real time.
B) Translation is accurate; interpreting is loose.
C) Interpreting needs only speed.
Why: the difference is medium and time pressure. Both aim for fidelity (not B), and both need deep language skill (not C).
Q3 · new
The teacher uses a word you don't know. What should you do?
A) Guess and keep going.
B) Tell both people you need to check the word, then ask the teacher to explain it.
C) Skip it.
Why: transparency and accuracy. Guessing (A) risks a wrong message, and skipping (C) breaks completeness. Telling both sides keeps everyone informed about the side exchange.
Errors you will meet most (7.3)

False friends in speech: actually (= currently), eventually (= possibly), sensible (= sensitive), assist (= attend) · collocation transfer: do a mistake, make a photo, open the light · "It literally means…, but we actually say" with a tense or word-order slip · hunting for a one-word equivalent instead of paraphrasing · sight translation that follows the source word order ("Is forbidden the entry of dogs") · register loss (friendly source → officialese) · the ad hoc interpreter's reflexes: third person, summarizing, softening, adding advice. For range, praise The nearest we get in English is…, though that loses…; for effect, praise any rendering that made a listener say "Oh, I get it" and describe the feeling back.
