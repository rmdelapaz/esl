Mastery English Workbook · Module 7

Communicating Across Cultures

Three lessons on what fluent English cannot do by itself. Two people can share flawless grammar and still misread each other, because the unwritten rules for using the language differ. Here you learn to read those rules, to negotiate across them with tact, and to carry meaning, not merely words, from one language into another.

How this module works

Each heading tells you what to do: Read, Listen, Write, Say or With a partner. Most tasks have several good answers; what matters is that your choice suits the person, the moment and the purpose, and that you can explain why.

This module is about situations, not nationalities. You will never be asked to explain "how people are" in any country, or to speak for a culture or a language community. When a task draws on your own experience, you decide what to share: your real life, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good. In role-plays you play the card, not yourself, and you can always say "Pass." Using your other languages in Lesson 7.3 is welcome and entirely optional.

7.1

Same Words, Different Rules — Politeness, Taboo & Humor

Class C20
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • pragmatics — meaning built from context and convention, not from the words alone
  • an interlocutor — the person you are speaking with
  • face — a person's dignity and public self-image; a face-threatening act — a request, refusal or criticism that puts it at risk
  • a hedge / a softener — a word or phrase that reduces force (quite, a little, I wonder whether)
  • to decode — to work out what a softened or indirect message really means
  • to deflect a compliment — to wave it away modestly (Oh, it was nothing)
  • taboo — a subject a community treats as off-limits or delicate
  • a euphemism — a gentler word for something delicate (passed away); a dysphemism — a deliberately harsher or jokier one (kicked the bucket)
  • a minced oath — a disguised, mild substitute for a swear word (darn, gosh, heck)
  • self-deprecation · banter · understatement · irony — kinds of humor that depend on a shared frame
  • to misfire — (of a joke or remark) to produce the wrong effect; a repair — the move that puts it right
I don't suppose you could…?Would there be any chance of…?I see it slightly differently, though I may be missing something.Please feel free not to answer.When you said…, did you mean…?Did I get the wrong idea?That's on me.No harm done.
Grammar box — the grammar of indirectness
Negative + modal questionI don't suppose you could take another look at the figures?
Distancing (past, progressive, would)I was wondering whether… · Would there be any chance of…? · Might it be possible to…?
Conditional cushionIf it's not too much trouble, could you…?
Concede, counter, leave the door openI see where you're coming from, but… · …, though I may be missing something.
Clarifying checkWhen you said "we'll see," did you mean it's unlikely? · Just so I don't assume, are you saying…?
Offering an exitPlease feel free not to answer. · Tell me if I'm straying into something you'd rather not discuss.

Four politeness strategies for a face-threatening act:

Bald on recorddirect, unsoftened: urgency, close relationships, efficiencyWatch out, the step!
Positive politenessbuilds closeness: we, let's, compliments, common groundYou're brilliant with spreadsheets. Can we look at this together?
Negative politenessrespects distance: hedges, apologies, optionsSorry to bother you. Would you mind if I moved your bag?
Off recorda hint the listener must infer (and can ignore)Gosh, it's chilly in here.

Remember: an indirect refusal is still a refusal. That would be quite challenging and I'll see what I can do may be the whole answer, delivered in a form that saves face.

7.2

Firm on Substance, Warm on Person — Diplomacy & Negotiation

Class C21
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • a position — what a side says it wants; an interest — why it wants it
  • a BATNA — your best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you will do if no deal is reached
  • a walk-away point — the least favorable deal you would still accept
  • a concession / to concede — ground you give; to trade — to give ground only in return for ground
  • a package — a deal that settles several issues together
  • win-win — an outcome both sides can defend
  • to save face — to keep your dignity; a golden bridge — a dignified way for the other side to change position
  • to depersonalize — to move from "you vs me" to "us vs the problem"
  • a cushioned delivery — a hard message stated clearly, then softened around
  • assertive — calm, clear and firm; neither aggressive nor yielding
  • a holding phrase — words that buy a moment under pressure
  • weaponized courtesy — polite formulas used as a sneer
I appreciate where you're coming from, but…I have one or two reservations.We may have to agree to differ.Ideally, we'd be looking at…What matters most to you here?If you were able to…, then we could…Where might there be room to move?Let me make sure I understand your concern.
Grammar box — conditionals for bargaining, framing for face
Remote conditional tradeIf you were able to commit to twelve months, (then) we could hold the rate.
If you were to extend the deadline, we would absorb the extra scope.
Conditional connectorsWe could move on price, provided (that) the timeline shifts. · as long as · on condition that
Present simple after these: provided you sign, not provided you will sign.
Hypothetical proposalsWhat if we were to add Saturdays? · Supposing we added Saturdays, would you consider a longer term?
Downgraded verdictThat's a bad idea. → I have one or two reservations about that approach.
The situation as subjectThe timing has been against us. · Things didn't quite go to plan. · I don't think either of us saw this coming.
ClosingShall we say three percent, twelve months, rate frozen? · I'll put it in writing today.

The cushioned delivery: signpost (I'm afraid I have some disappointing news) → state it clearly and early → explain briefly and honestly → look forward. Softening is the wrapping, not the substance.

Read and say — Dialogue: the golden bridge

Priya: I know you've put a great deal into the Friday launch, Marcus.

Marcus: And I'd still rather keep it. Moving it now would look as if we got it wrong.

Priya: I don't think anyone got it wrong. The supplier delays have been against us from the start.

Marcus: Maybe. But the board approved Friday.

Priya: With the new delivery figures in, it would be completely reasonable to revisit it. Your plan was sound given what we knew in March.

Marcus: Hmm. If we moved it, what would we actually gain?

Priya: A launch that works the first time. And if you were able to take the new date to the board, I could draft the explanation for you today.

Marcus: All right. Let's frame it as "phase one on Friday, full launch on the twentieth."

Priya: That's a story I'd be happy to stand behind.

Find: blaming the situation, crediting the other's reasoning, a golden bridge and a conditional trade. How does Marcus save face in the end?

7.3

Working Between Languages — Translation & Interpreting Awareness

Class C22
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • to mediate / mediation — to carry meaning between people who don't share a language or a frame
  • the source language · the target language — the language you translate from · into
  • a false friend — a word that resembles one in another language but means something different
  • a cognate — a word with a shared origin and a similar meaning (a "true friend")
  • culture-bound — tied to one community's experience; untranslatable — without a one-word equivalent
  • a gloss — a short explanation of a word's meaning
  • literal ↔ idiomatic — word for word ↔ natural for the target language
  • register loss — a rendering that keeps the content but loses the tone
  • fidelity — faithfulness to the original's meaning, register and tone
  • translation (written) · interpreting (spoken): consecutive · simultaneous · sight translation (a written text read aloud in another language on the spot)
  • impartiality · confidentiality · transparency — core principles of interpreting ethics
There isn't a single English word for it, but…It literally means…, but what we'd actually say is…The closest equivalent would be…The nearest we get in English is…, though that loses…Think of it as a kind of…It's the word we use when…Careful: it looks like…, but it really means…
Grammar box — frames for mediating
Why word-for-word failsidiom: Je te tiens au courant ≠ "I hold you in the current" → I'll keep you posted
collocation: make a decision · take a photo · make a mistake
grammar: "I have thirty years" → I'm thirty
The paraphrase patternsignal the gap → give the literal image (if it helps) → state the plain meaning → paint the feeling or situation
Literal vs actualIt literally means "over the table," but what we'd actually say is "the talk after a meal."
Approximation with a costThe nearest we get is "cozy," though that loses the social side.
The interpreter's voiceProfessionals speak in the first person for the speaker: "I have two children" (not "She says she has two children"). They step out only to manage the talk, openly: "Interpreter speaking: may I check one word?"

The unit of translation is the idea, not the word. First ask what the text means and what it is doing; then find the natural way to do the same job in the target language.

Read and say — Dialogue: interpreting at the community center

A volunteer interprets between a sports coordinator and a parent at a youth-soccer sign-up. The parent's words are shown in English; in reality the parent and the volunteer are speaking another language.

Coordinator: Registration closes on Friday. The fee is forty dollars, but we waive it for anyone who asks.

Volunteer: (to the parent, in the first person) Registration closes on Friday. The fee is forty dollars, but we waive it for anyone who asks.

Parent: I have two children. Can they be in the same group?

Volunteer: (to the coordinator) I have two children. Can they be in the same group?

Coordinator: It depends on their ages. The groups are U8 and U10.

Volunteer: Interpreter speaking: may I check a term? What does "U8" mean exactly?

Coordinator: Sure, it means "under eight": players who are seven or younger this season.

Volunteer: (to the parent) The interpreter asked what "U8" means; it means under eight. It depends on their ages. The groups are for under eight and under ten.

Parent: What would you do, if they were your kids?

Volunteer: I'm only here to interpret, so I'll pass that question to the coordinator, if you like.

Why does the volunteer say "I have two children," not "She says she has two children"? How does she handle the term she doesn't know? Why doesn't she answer "What would you do?"
