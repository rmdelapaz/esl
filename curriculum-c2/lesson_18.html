🔬 Lesson 6.3: Fine Points of Usage & Punctuation

There is correct English, and there is polished English — and the gap between them is made of small things. A misplaced modifier, a comma that changes the meaning, who where whom belonged: none of these breaks communication, yet each is a fingerprint the careful reader notices. This lesson trains the fine distinctions educated writers care about — not as pedantry, but as the last, fine calibration of a voice you already command.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Command the classic usage distinctions — who/whom, fewer/less, that/which, agreement with tricky subjects, parallelism, and the subjunctive
  • Recognise and repair dangling and misplaced modifiers and faulty parallelism
  • Wield the semicolon, colon, em dash, and comma for meaning, rhythm, and rhetorical effect
  • Tell a genuine rule from a mere convention — and choose deliberately, with register in mind

Rules, Conventions & Judgement

At C2 the goal is no longer to be understood — you have long been understood — but to write and speak with the poise of an educated native. That poise lives in the fine points: distinctions that carry little information yet much signal. Get them right and a careful reader registers, often unconsciously, that they are in expert hands. Get them wrong and the same reader registers a small snag, however forgiving they choose to be.

But a master approaches these points with judgement, not fear. Some are genuine rules whose violation confuses meaning (a dangling modifier can mislead; a misplaced comma can invert a sentence). Others are mere conventions of formal register — whom, the ban on splitting infinitives, the prohibition on ending with a preposition — cherished by some readers and dismissed by linguists as folklore. The skill is to know which is which, and to choose accordingly: honouring the conventions where they buy you credibility, relaxing them where insisting on them would make you sound stiff.

🧠 Mindset

Do not learn these as commandments to obey in dread. Learn them as tools of clarity and voice. The semicolon is not a hoop to jump through; it is a way to hold two thoughts in a single balanced breath. Whom is not a trap; it is a small marker of register you deploy when register matters and drop when it doesn't. Every point in this lesson is finally about serving the reader — making meaning sharper, rhythm truer, and voice more exactly your own. Approach them with curiosity, and they become a pleasure rather than a minefield. 🌱

⚠️ Register decides how strictly a convention binds

The same choice can be right in one room and wrong in another. Whom is expected in a legal brief and faintly pompous in a chatty email; the strict that/which distinction is enforced in edited American prose and treated loosely in British and casual writing. The C2 writer reads the register first, then decides how tightly to hold each convention. Precision is knowing the rule; mastery is knowing when it applies.

Pronouns & the Classic Pairs

who vs whom

Who is a subject; whom is an object. The reliable test: answer the question with he or him — if him fits, whom is correct ("You saw whom? — I saw him"). In practice whom is retreating from speech and informal writing, where who now sounds natural even as an object ("Who did you invite?"). But in formal writing, and always after a preposition ("to whom it may concern," never "to who"), whom remains expected.

fewer vs less

Fewer for countable nouns (fewer cars, fewer errors); less for uncountable quantities (less traffic, less time). "Ten items or fewer" is the careful form the supermarket sign usually gets wrong. The exceptions are worth knowing: less is idiomatic with measures of time, money, and distance treated as a single amount ("less than five miles," "less than $20"), because these are quantities, not counts.

that vs which (restrictive vs non-restrictive)

This is the most meaning-bearing pair. A restrictive clause identifies which one you mean and takes that, with no commas: "The report that I sent yesterday is final" (of several reports, the one I sent yesterday). A non-restrictive clause merely adds information and takes which, fenced by commas: "The report, which I sent yesterday, is final" (there is one report; by the way, I sent it yesterday). The commas — not just the word — carry the difference, and changing them changes the fact.

The comma changes the meaning:

"My brother who lives in Berlin is a doctor." → I have more than one brother; the Berlin one is the doctor. (restrictive — identifies which)

"My brother, who lives in Berlin, is a doctor." → I have one brother; he happens to live in Berlin. (non-restrictive — adds a detail)

Two commas silently rewrite the family. This is why the distinction is a genuine rule, not a mere convention: the punctuation is doing semantic work, and a reader will draw a different conclusion depending on it.

💡 "between you and me"

After a preposition, pronouns take the object form: between you and me, not "between you and I." The hypercorrect "you and I" (over-applied from "you and I went," where I is a subject) is one of the commonest educated errors. Test by dropping the other person: you would say "between me and the wall," never "between I and the wall" — so it is "between you and me."

Keep these quick tests within reach — each settles a fine point in a second:

who / whom → try he / him you and me → drop the other person fewer / less → can you count it? that / which → does it identify or merely add? no commas → restrictive commas → non-restrictive if it dangles → whose action is it? read it aloud → hear the splice

Agreement, Modifiers & Parallelism

Subject–verb agreement with tricky subjects

Certain subjects trip even careful writers:

Subject typeAgreementExample
each / every / either / neithersingularEach of the plans has merit.
nonesingular or plural (context)None of it is ready. / None of them were late.
collective nouns (team, government)singular (US) / either (UK)The team is winning. (UK also: the team are…)
intervening phrasesverb agrees with the head nounThe box of chocolates is here (not "are").
neither A nor Bagrees with the nearer subjectNeither the manager nor the staff were told.

Dangling & misplaced modifiers

A modifier must sit next to what it modifies, and an introductory participle must attach to the subject of the main clause — or it dangles, producing sentences that are unintentionally comic or plainly wrong.

⚠️ Danglers — the sentence says what you didn't mean

Faulty ❌Fixed ✅
Walking to the office, the rain soaked me.Walking to the office, I was soaked by the rain.
Having finished the report, the printer jammed.Having finished the report, I found the printer had jammed.
She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates.She served the children sandwiches on paper plates.
We only sell organic eggs. (only where?)We sell only organic eggs.

In the first, the rain is walking to the office; in the third, the children are on paper plates. The grammar is intact; the attachment is wrong — and a reader momentarily pictures the absurd version before correcting it. Position only, even, and almost with care: they modify whatever immediately follows.

Parallelism

Items in a series, and both halves of a paired construction, should share the same grammatical form. Break the pattern and the sentence lurches. "She likes swimming, cycling, and to run" should be "swimming, cycling, and running." Paired connectives demand it too: "not only talented but also generous" (both adjectives), never "not only talented but also she is generous."

Faulty → parallel:

Faulty: "The role calls for someone who is diligent, with creativity, and can lead a team."

Parallel: "The role calls for someone who is diligent, creative, and able to lead a team."

Three predicate adjectives now share one form, and the sentence walks in step. Parallelism is not merely tidy — it lets the reader's ear predict the shape, which is why parallel prose reads as effortless and non-parallel prose reads as clumsy without the reader knowing why.

The Subjunctive & Other Niceties

The subjunctive were marks a hypothetical or contrary-to-fact condition: "If I were you," "I wish it were simpler," "as though he were in charge." In casual speech was is common and widely accepted, but in careful writing the subjunctive were signals a hypothetical precisely, and its loss can blur fact and supposition. It also appears in the mandative subjunctive after verbs of demand or recommendation: "I insist that he be present," "the board recommends that she step down" (base form, no -s).

ConventionThe "rule"The reality
Split infinitive"never split — to boldly go is wrong"a myth; splitting is often clearer, and forced un-splitting reads worse
Preposition at end"never end a sentence with one"a myth; "the report I told you about" is perfect English
Starting with and/but"never begin a sentence so"fine, and often crisp; used by the best writers for centuries
Subjunctive were"always use were, not was"a genuine niceness — precise in formal writing, relaxed in speech

💡 The myths worth retiring

Three "rules" you may have been taught are not rules at all — they are superstitions, several imported into English from Latin, where they made sense and here do not. You may split an infinitive ("to really understand"), end a sentence with a preposition ("something worth fighting for"), and begin one with And or But. Knowing they are myths frees you to write naturally — while knowing that some conservative readers still cling to them lets you avoid the fight in the stiffest contexts. That double awareness is exactly the C2 judgement at work.

Punctuation for Meaning & Rhythm

Punctuation is not a set of hurdles; it is the writer's control of pace and grouping — the score that tells the reader how the sentence is to be heard. The advanced marks each do a job no other does.

The semicolon

The semicolon joins two independent clauses whose closeness the writer wants to signal — a pause weightier than a comma, lighter than a full stop: "The rain stopped; the match resumed." It also separates items in a list when the items themselves contain commas, keeping the groupings clear: "We visited Paris, France; Rome, Italy; and Bern, Switzerland." Its enemy is the comma splice — two clauses joined by a mere comma ("The rain stopped, the match resumed"), which the semicolon exists to cure.

The colon

The colon announces that what follows delivers what the first part promised — a list, an explanation, an example, or a punchline: "She had one goal: to win." What precedes a colon should be a complete clause; what follows is the fulfilment of its expectation. The colon points forward, like a curtain rising.

The em dash

The em dash — that long, versatile stroke — is the most flexible mark in English. It can set off an interruption or an aside (like this one), replace a colon with more drama, or mark an abrupt turn. Used sparingly it lends energy and voice; used at every turn it makes prose breathless. It is the mark closest to speech, and the one that most carries a writer's personality.

Parentheses vs dashes vs commas — the three grades of aside

All three can enclose a parenthetical remark, but they differ in volume. Commas keep the aside in the flow of the sentence; em dashes lift it out and emphasise it; parentheses lower it to a murmur, an aside the reader may almost skip. The same aside changes character with each.

The same aside, three volumes:

Commas (neutral): "The proposal, after much debate, was approved."

Dashes (emphatic): "The proposal — after much, much debate — was approved."

Parentheses (quiet): "The proposal (after much debate) was approved."

Identical words, three different weightings of the same interruption. Choosing among them is a genuine stylistic decision — how loudly do you want the aside to sound? — and the mark of a writer who hears the sentence, not merely spells it.

The Oxford (serial) comma — where placement changes meaning

The Oxford comma is the one before the final and in a list: "red, white, and blue." Whether to use it is a house-style convention — but occasionally it is not optional at all, because its presence or absence changes the meaning.

⚠️ When the comma is not optional

"I'd like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand and God." → Without the serial comma, this reads as though your parents are Ayn Rand and God (an appositive). "I'd like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand, and God." → With it, three separate parties are thanked. When ambiguity of this kind threatens, the Oxford comma is a matter of clarity, not taste — add it.

Punctuation for Rhetorical Effect

Beyond meaning and clarity, punctuation shapes rhythm — and rhythm is a large part of what we call voice. Sentence length and the pauses within a sentence control the reader's breathing, and a skilled writer uses them the way a musician uses rests.

A run of long, comma-rich sentences builds momentum and gravity; a sudden short sentence lands like a hammer. The colon creates suspense before its payoff; the dash produces surprise; the semicolon suggests two balanced thoughts held in equilibrium. None of this is about correctness. It is about using the marks expressively — to speed, slow, group, and emphasise — so the prose is heard as you intend.

Rhythm through punctuation:

"They had planned for everything — the weather, the crowds, the delays, the sheer improbable logistics of moving ten thousand people through one narrow gate. They had planned for everything. And then the power failed."

The long, comma-laden first sentence builds and accumulates; the short repetition ("They had planned for everything.") plants its feet; the abrupt four-word close, opening with And, drops the floor out. The punctuation and sentence-length are the drama — the same content in three even sentences would fall flat. This is the final frontier: punctuation not as correctness but as music.

Editor: The argument's fine, but it reads flat. Every sentence is the same length.

Writer: They're all grammatically correct, though.

Editor: Correct isn't the same as alive. Break one of those long ones with a semicolon, let a short sentence land after it, and use a dash where you want the reader to feel the turn. Punctuation is your rhythm section.

Writer: So the marks aren't just about avoiding errors — they're how the sentence moves?

Editor: Now you're hearing it. The rules keep you correct; the rhythm keeps you read.

The editor's point completes the lesson: past the level of error, punctuation becomes an instrument of pace and voice. Correctness is the floor, not the ceiling — the C2 writer plays the marks as much as obeys them.

⚠️ Expressive is not erratic

Using punctuation for effect is not license to scatter dashes and exclamation marks. Rhetorical power comes from restraint: a short sentence lands only because the long ones set it up; a dash surprises only because they are rare. Overuse any device and it stops signalling. The expressive writer earns each effect by not overspending — one em dash in a paragraph rings; five in a paragraph is just noise.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Fix the usage

Objective: Type the corrected form of the word in question.

  1. (fewer/less + countable) The express lane is for ten items or .  [fewer / less]
  2. (object of a preposition) This is strictly between you and .  [I / me]
  3. (subject–verb agreement) Each of the proposals its merits.  [has / have]
  4. (subjunctive were) If I in charge, things would change.  [was / were]
  5. (whom, after a preposition) To should I address the letter?  [who / whom]
✅ Answer

1. fewer (items are countable). 2. me (object of the preposition "between" — test: "between me and…"). 3. has ("each" is singular). 4. were (contrary-to-fact subjunctive). 5. whom (object of "to"; answer "to him"). Each is a small marker of careful, formal English.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the punctuation for the intended meaning

Objective: Type the mark or word that produces the meaning described. Use the exact form shown in the options.

  1. (join two closely linked independent clauses, no conjunction) The rain stopped the match resumed.  [ ; / , ]
  2. (announce a list that fulfils the clause) She had one goal to win at any cost.  [ : / ; ]
  3. (I have several brothers; identify the doctor one — restrictive, so use…) My brother lives in Berlin is a doctor.  [who — no commas / , who , ] (type: who)
  4. (a list whose items contain commas — separate them with) Paris, France Rome, Italy; and Bern, Switzerland.  [ ; / , ]
  5. (restrictive clause identifying which report — use) The report I sent yesterday is final.  [that / which]
✅ Answer

1. ; (semicolon joins two independent clauses; a comma here would be a splice). 2. : (colon announces the fulfilment). 3. who with no commas (restrictive — it identifies which brother; commas would make it non-restrictive, implying only one brother). 4. ; (semicolons separate list items that themselves contain commas). 5. that (restrictive clause, no commas — it specifies which report). Notice how, in 3 and 5, the punctuation and word together carry the meaning.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Repair the danglers & the parallelism

Objective: Each sentence has a dangling/misplaced modifier or a parallelism fault. Rewrite it correctly. This is a model exercise — several good corrections exist; judge yours by whether the modifier attaches rightly and the series shares one form.

  1. "Rushing to catch the train, my coffee spilled everywhere."
  2. "The manual explains how to install, configuring, and the maintenance of the device."
  3. "We almost drove 500 miles that day." (we did drive; it was nearly 500)
  4. "To succeed you must be not only skilled but also you need discipline."
✅ Answer (model)

1. "Rushing to catch the train, I spilled my coffee everywhere." (the subject must be the one rushing — coffee cannot rush).

2. "The manual explains how to install, configure, and maintain the device." (three parallel infinitives).

3. "We drove almost 500 miles that day." (almost modifies the distance, not the verb — as written, it said we nearly drove and didn't).

4. "To succeed you must be not only skilled but also disciplined." (both halves of "not only… but also" now take the same adjective form).

In each, the meaning was recoverable but the sentence briefly said something the writer didn't intend — the hallmark of a modifier or parallelism fault.

Tips & Common Pitfalls

✅ Do's

  • Distinguish rules from conventions: honour the meaning-bearing rules always (danglers, restrictive commas), and apply register-conventions (whom, the subjunctive) as the context warrants.
  • Test the tricky pairs: he/him for who/whom, "drop the other person" for you and me, countable vs uncountable for fewer/less.
  • Read for the comma that changes meaning — restrictive vs non-restrictive, and the Oxford comma where ambiguity threatens.
  • Use the advanced marks for their jobs: the semicolon to balance two clauses, the colon to deliver a promise, the em dash for a vivid aside — sparingly.
  • Hear the rhythm: vary sentence length and let punctuation control pace, so the prose reads as alive, not merely correct.

❌ Common Pitfalls

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Hypercorrection: "between you and I," "whom shall I say is calling" — errors born of trying too hard. Over-formalising is as revealing as under-formalising.
  • The comma splice: two independent clauses joined by a comma ("It rained, we left"). Use a semicolon, a full stop, or a conjunction.
  • Danglers and stray only: the sentence says what you didn't mean. Attach the modifier to its true subject, and place only/even/almost right before what they limit.
  • Fear of the myths: contorting sentences to avoid splitting infinitives or ending on prepositions produces stiffer, worse prose than the "error" would have.
  • Punctuation overspend: a page of em dashes and exclamation marks. Expressive effect depends on restraint — a rare mark rings; a constant one is noise.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • At C2 the frontier is polish — fine points that carry little information but much signal; the skill is to tell a genuine rule from a mere convention and choose with register in mind.
  • Command the classic pairs — who/whom (he/him test), fewer/less (countable/uncountable), that/which (restrictive/non-restrictive, where the commas carry meaning), and "between you and me."
  • Watch agreement with tricky subjects, repair dangling/misplaced modifiers and stray only, keep series parallel, and use the subjunctive were for the hypothetical.
  • Wield the semicolon (balance two clauses), colon (deliver a promise), em dash (vivid aside), and the three grades of aside — commas, dashes, parentheses — for meaning and volume; the Oxford comma is sometimes a matter of clarity, not taste.
  • Past correctness, punctuation is rhythm and voice — sentence length and pause used expressively, always with restraint.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

If some of these are just conventions, why bother with them at all?

Because signals matter even when information doesn't. A convention like whom or the strict that/which carries no new meaning, but its correct use quietly tells a careful reader they're in expert hands — and its absence, in the wrong context, quietly tells them otherwise. You bother selectively: honour the convention where the register rewards it (a formal report, a legal document), relax it where insisting would sound stiff (a friendly email). Mastery is not obeying every convention; it's knowing which one this reader will notice.

How can I catch these in my own writing when I don't notice them as I write?

Almost no one catches them while composing — the mind is on meaning, not mechanics. The fixes happen in a dedicated proofreading pass, done cold (ideally the next day) and slowly. Reading aloud is the single most powerful technique: your ear catches comma splices, danglers, and clumsy rhythm that your eye slides over. Over time, the points you repeatedly catch in editing migrate into your drafting — but the editing pass never becomes optional.

Isn't caring this much about punctuation just pedantry?

It would be, if the point were "rules for their own sake." But every fine point in this lesson finally serves the reader: the restrictive comma prevents a misreading, the parallel series reads effortlessly, the well-placed semicolon controls the pace. Pedantry is enforcing a rule that serves no one (the split-infinitive taboo); craft is honouring a distinction because it makes the meaning clearer or the sentence truer. Knowing which is which is the opposite of pedantry — it's judgement.
