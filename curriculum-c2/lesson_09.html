📋 Lesson 3.3: Summarising, Paraphrasing & Synthesis

To read a demanding text and give back its essence in your own words — shorter, clearer, faithful — is one of the defining acts of an educated mind. And to take several voices on one question and weave them into a single coherent account is harder still. This lesson trains the three great condensing arts: the précis, the genuine paraphrase, and the multi-source synthesis — the very heart of Cambridge C2's reading-into-writing.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Summarise complex material accurately and concisely — find the gist, cut the detail, preserve the argument
  • Paraphrase by genuinely reformulating — rebuilding the sentence, not swapping synonyms
  • Synthesise multiple sources into one coherent account organised by idea
  • Attribute others' views accurately, conveying both their stance and your own

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Project: Take two short texts that discuss the same issue from different angles and produce (a) a one-sentence gist of each, and (b) a short synthesised paragraph combining them with attribution — then deliver it aloud.

Three Arts of Condensing

All three skills in this lesson answer one question: how do I take language that already exists and give it back transformed — shorter, reworded, or fused with other voices — without betraying its meaning? They form a natural ladder of difficulty:

  • Summarise — same meaning, far fewer words (condense)
  • Paraphrase — same meaning, same length, new words and new structure (reformulate)
  • Synthesise — several meanings, one coherent account organised by idea (fuse)

Each rests on the same discipline: read for the argument, not the words. The words are the packaging; the argument is the cargo. A master handles the cargo and lets the packaging go.

🧠 Mindset

At C2, this is not a school exercise — it is the everyday labour of thought. Every review, brief, literature survey, and honest disagreement begins with fairly restating what someone else said. The temptation is always to hide behind the original's phrasing; the discipline is to own the idea so completely that you can rebuild it from scratch. When you can summarise a page in a sentence and mean it, you have not compressed the text — you have understood it. Refinement at this level is learning to let go of the author's words while keeping faith with the author's thought. 🌱

Summarising: The Précis

A précis (or summary) reduces a text to its essentials while preserving its meaning and proportion. The first move is diagnostic: sort every sentence into main idea, supporting detail, or example. Only the main ideas survive intact; details are compressed or cut; examples usually vanish altogether.

ElementWhat it isFate in a summary
Main ideaThe claim or point being arguedKept — this is the summary
Supporting detailReasons, evidence, qualificationCompressed or generalised
Example / illustrationA specific instance of the pointUsually cut entirely
Repetition / asideRestatement, digression, hedgingDeleted

Four classic techniques do the compressing:

  • Delete — cut trivia, redundancy, and repetition
  • Generalise — replace a list with a superordinate (daffodils, tulips, roses → flowers)
  • Select or invent a topic sentence — if the text states its point, use it; if it merely implies one, supply it
  • Preserve the logical relations — keep the cause, contrast, concession, and sequence that hold the argument together

That last point is the one amateurs miss. A summary is not a bag of surviving facts; it is a miniature of the argument. If the original says "X, but therefore Y," a summary that reports "X and Y" has kept the content and lost the thought.

Dense original: "Although the new high-speed line has undeniably slashed journey times between the two cities and delighted commuters, its astronomical construction costs — nearly double the original estimate — have provoked fierce criticism, and several economists now argue that the same sum, invested in dozens of smaller regional upgrades, would have benefited far more travellers, particularly in the neglected rural districts that the flagship route bypasses entirely."

One-sentence gist: "Though the high-speed line has cut journey times, its huge overspend has led economists to argue the money would have helped more people spread across smaller regional projects."

Note what is kept: the concession (though), the main charge (overspend), and the counter-argument (better spent elsewhere). Note what is cut: the doubled estimate, the delighted commuters, the bypassed rural districts — all supporting detail. The logical spine survives; the flesh is gone.

⚠️ A summary must stay faithful

The cardinal sin of summarising is distortion. Do not add your own opinion — a summary reports the source's view, not yours. Do not sharpen a cautious claim into a bold one, or soften a bold one into a hedge. And do not over-generalise: "some economists question the cost" must not become "the project is a failure." Faithfulness of proportion matters as much as faithfulness of fact — give a minor point minor space.

Paraphrasing: Genuine Reformulation

To paraphrase is to restate a passage in your own words at roughly the same length — same meaning, wholly rebuilt expression. The test of a real paraphrase is simple: could the original author read yours and recognise the idea but not a single borrowed phrase? If chunks of the original show through, you have not paraphrased; you have copied with edits.

Genuine reformulation changes both structure and wording. The reliable techniques:

TechniqueMoveExample
Change word classNominalise or de-nominalisedecide ↔ reach a decision; the collapse of ↔ … collapsed
Change voiceActive ↔ passiveCritics attacked the plan ↔ The plan was attacked
Reorder clausesFront the result, or the causeBecause A, B ↔ B, since A
Change the subjectRe-anchor the sentence on a different nounThe study found X ↔ X, according to the study, …
Synonyms / superordinatesSubstitute meaning-safe wordsenormous → vast; cars and lorries → vehicles

Synonym-swapping is the last and least of these, not the first. Real paraphrase begins with the architecture of the sentence — change that, and the words follow naturally.

⚠️ "Patchwriting" is not paraphrase — it reads as plagiarism

Patchwriting is the counterfeit: you keep the original's sentence structure and word order, dropping a synonym into every few slots. It fools no one — an examiner or editor sees the ghost of the source at once, and in academic terms it is treated as plagiarism, not paraphrase. Genuine paraphrase rebuilds the sentence from the meaning up. The difference is stark:

Original: "The rapid expansion of social media has fundamentally altered the way young people form their political opinions."

❌ Patchwrite (bad): "The quick growth of social media has basically changed the way young individuals develop their political views."

Same skeleton — subject, verb, object, and clause order all identical; only synonyms differ. This is disguised copying.

✅ Genuine paraphrase (good): "Where young people once inherited their politics from family and community, many now form those views through the fast-growing world of social media."

The sentence has been rebuilt: a new subject and frame, a contrast introduced, the nominal expansion turned into the adjective fast-growing, the whole architecture re-poured. The meaning is faithful; nothing is borrowed.

Reporting & Attribution

The moment you handle someone else's view, your reader must know three things: whose view it is, what they claim, and how you stand toward it. Attribution language does all three at once — and the reporting verb is where most of the stance is carried. "X notes" is neutral; "X concedes" implies reluctance; "X claims" quietly signals your doubt. Choosing the verb is taking a position.

According to X, … As X points out, … X argues that … X contends / maintains … X observes / notes … X concedes / acknowledges … X refutes / rejects … X claims / alleges … In X's view, … X goes so far as to say …
FunctionVerbsStance signalled
Neutral reportstate, note, observe, remark, describeNone — you take no side
Strong assertionargue, contend, maintain, insist, holdSource is committed; you stay neutral
Agreement / supportpoint out, demonstrate, show, establishYou lean toward accepting it
Reluctant admissionconcede, acknowledge, admit, grant, allowSource yields a point against interest
Oppositionrefute, reject, dispute, counter, challengeSource pushes back on another view
Distancing / doubtclaim, allege, assert, purport, contendYou quietly withhold agreement

💡 Distancing without saying "I disagree"

Note the difference between "As Halpern shows, the policy failed" and "As Halpern claims, the policy failed." The first endorses; the second raises an eyebrow. Adverbs distance too — "the reform supposedly cut costs," "the so-called recovery" — as does the reporting frame "X would have us believe that …". At C2, these are your instruments for signalling scepticism while keeping an air of scholarly restraint. Wield them precisely: an unearned claims reads as bias.

Synthesis: Weaving Sources

Synthesis is the hardest of the three, and the true mark of C2 reading-into-writing. It means taking two or more sources and producing one coherent account organised by idea, not by source. The tell-tale sign of a weak synthesis is the source-by-source march — "Source A says… Source B says… Source C says…" — a list wearing the costume of an essay. Real synthesis asks: where do these voices agree, disagree, extend, or qualify one another? — and organises around the answer.

Both X and Y concur that … Whereas X emphasises …, Y counters that … X's view is complicated by Y's finding that … Where X sees …, Y detects instead … Y extends this, adding that … This tension between X and Y … X and Y diverge sharply on … Y qualifies X's claim, noting … Taken together, these accounts suggest … A more nuanced picture emerges when …

Source A (a technologist): "Remote work has liberated employees from the tyranny of the commute and handed them precious hours back; productivity, far from falling, has held firm or risen."

Source B (a sociologist): "The office was never merely a place of work but a community; its loss has left many workers isolated, their careers quietly stalling for want of the informal mentorship that corridors and coffee machines once provided."

Synthesised paragraph (organised by idea): "The debate over remote work turns less on productivity than on what we count as a cost. Both sides agree that output has broadly held up — Source A celebrates the reclaimed commuting hours, and even Source B does not dispute the figures. Where they diverge is on the hidden ledger. Whereas the technologist frames the office as a tyranny escaped, the sociologist counters that it was also a community lost, warning that isolation and the withering of informal mentorship may exact a slow, uncounted toll. The technologist's optimism, in other words, is complicated by the sociologist's longer view: what looks like liberation on a quarterly report may register as attrition over a career."

Note the moves: a governing idea in the first sentence (not productivity but cost); the point of agreement established first; the divergence named and organised around a single metaphor (the hidden ledger); accurate attribution throughout; and a closing sentence that holds both in tension rather than declaring a winner. Two voices, one argument.

The structural difference is worth seeing in miniature:

  • In essence, the author argues that convenience has quietly cost us our privacy.
  • Boiled down to a sentence, the whole debate turns on who bears the risk.
  • While Smith stresses the benefits, Jones counters that the costs fall unevenly.
  • To put her point another way, growth without limits is simply borrowing from tomorrow.
  • Both writers, for all their differences, converge on one uncomfortable conclusion.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Reduce to a one-sentence gist

Objective: Condense the paragraph to a single faithful sentence — and keep the logical relation intact.

"The museum's decision to charge admission for the first time in its history was defended by trustees as a regrettable necessity in an age of shrinking public subsidy. Critics, however, protested that a national collection assembled with taxpayers' money ought to remain free to the very public that owns it, and warned that the charge would deter precisely the low-income visitors the museum claims to serve."

✅ Answer (model)

"Although trustees defend the museum's new admission charge as an unavoidable response to falling subsidy, critics argue that a publicly-owned collection should stay free and that the fee will deter the low-income visitors it aims to reach." Note the preserved concession (although) and contrast (however → the two-sided structure); the examples and the "taxpayers' money" detail are compressed, but the argument's shape is kept. A version that reported only "the museum now charges admission" would be faithful to fact but false to the argument.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Judge & repair the paraphrase

Objective: Decide whether each attempt is genuine paraphrase or patchwriting — then repair the bad one.

Original: "The government's reluctance to regulate the industry has allowed dangerous practices to flourish unchecked."

  1. Attempt A: "The administration's unwillingness to control the sector has let hazardous practices thrive unrestrained."
  2. Attempt B: "Because ministers have declined to impose rules on the industry, unsafe methods have been free to spread."
✅ Answer

Attempt A is patchwriting — the skeleton is untouched (reluctance→unwillingness, regulate→control, dangerous→hazardous, flourish→thrive, unchecked→unrestrained), a pure synonym-for-synonym swap. It reads as disguised copying. Attempt B is genuine paraphrase — the nominal "reluctance to regulate" becomes a full clause ("ministers have declined to impose rules"), the subject changes, and a causal frame ("Because…") replaces the original's enabling verb ("allowed"). One repair of A: "Left unregulated by a hesitant government, the industry has seen its most dangerous habits take hold." — rebuilt, not re-skinned.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Synthesise two opposing statements

Objective: Combine the two views into a single sentence with accurate attribution and a synthesis connector.

Voss: "Standardised testing is the only fair, objective measure of what students have actually learned."

Ahmed: "Standardised tests measure little beyond a family's income and a child's skill at sitting exams."

✅ Answer (model)

"Whereas Voss defends standardised testing as the one objective yardstick of genuine learning, Ahmed counters that such tests reveal little more than a family's wealth and a talent for exam technique." Other defensible versions: "Voss's faith in testing as an impartial measure is directly challenged by Ahmed, who contends that scores track income rather than learning." Judge by three things: is each view attributed to the right person, is the opposition made explicit by the connector, and is neither view distorted? The reporting verbs (defends, counters, contends) should stay fair to both — do not slip a scornful claims onto the side you dislike.

🎯 Your Project: Gist, Synthesise, Deliver

  1. (4 min) Find two short texts that discuss the same issue from different angles — two op-eds, two reviews, two paragraphs from opposing sources. (A single search on any live controversy yields these in minutes.)
  2. (6 min) Write a one-sentence gist of each. Cut every example and aside; keep the main claim and its logical shape. Check each against its source for fidelity and proportion.
  3. (8 min) Write a short synthesised paragraph (4–6 sentences) combining them. Organise by idea: open with a governing thought, establish agreement, name the divergence, and close by holding them in tension. Attribute every claim, and choose reporting verbs that stay fair to both.
  4. (3 min) Deliver it aloud — record yourself, or present to a partner. Synthesis read aloud exposes seams: if a listener can't tell whose view is whose, tighten the attribution.
  5. (3 min) Ruthless self-edit. Delete any phrase borrowed from a source. Cut any sentence that merely repeats a view instead of relating it to the other. Is a single reader-orienting idea in charge of the paragraph?

Partner / group option: Each person synthesises the same two texts, then compares. Where you disagree on emphasis or on a reporting verb, argue it out — the disagreements are where the craft lives.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Each gist is one sentence, faithful, and keeps the source's logical relation
  • ☐ My synthesis is organised by idea, not source-by-source
  • ☐ It names where the sources agree and where they diverge
  • ☐ Every claim is attributed; reporting verbs are fair and stance-appropriate
  • ☐ No phrase is borrowed from either original — all genuine reformulation
  • ☐ I delivered it aloud and tightened whatever blurred

Tips & Common Pitfalls

✅ Do's

  • Read for the argument first; only then reach for words. If you can't state the point in a sentence, you're not ready to summarise it.
  • When paraphrasing, close the original and write from memory of the meaning — then reopen it only to check fidelity, never to borrow.
  • Organise synthesis by idea: draft your governing thought before you touch the sources' order.
  • Let the reporting verb carry your stance — and make sure it's the stance you actually hold and can defend.

❌ Common Pitfalls

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Patchwriting: keeping the source's structure and swapping synonyms. It is the single most common failure — and it reads as plagiarism, not paraphrase.
  • Editorialising in a summary: a précis reports the source's view, not yours. Save your verdict for a different sentence, clearly marked as your own.
  • Source-by-source "synthesis": a list of summaries is not a synthesis. If your paragraph could be reordered without loss, it isn't organised by idea.
  • Loaded reporting verbs: slipping claims or alleges onto the view you dislike betrays bias. Distance a source only when you can justify the doubt.
  • Losing the logical spine: dropping the but, because, or although that holds an argument together — the content survives, the thought does not.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Summarising keeps the main idea and its logical relations while cutting detail and example — and must stay faithful, never distorting or editorialising.
  • Paraphrasing rebuilds structure and wording; mere synonym-swapping is patchwriting and reads as plagiarism.
  • Attribution language — especially the reporting verb — conveys both the source's stance and your own; distancing verbs must be earned.
  • Synthesis weaves several sources into one account organised by idea, showing where they agree, diverge, extend, or qualify one another.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You've practised the reading-into-writing skills that sit at the centre of academic and professional life — and of Cambridge C2 itself. You can now take a dense argument and give back its essence in a sentence, reword a passage so thoroughly that nothing is borrowed, and fuse rival voices into a single balanced account with your own hand visible in every reporting verb. This is not summary as a school chore; it is the machinery of honest, original thought. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How is paraphrasing different from summarising, exactly?

A summary is much shorter than the original and keeps only the main ideas. A paraphrase is roughly the same length and keeps everything — it simply says it all in new words and a new structure. You summarise to compress; you paraphrase to restate a specific passage in your own voice, usually to weave it smoothly into your writing.

If I cite the source, does patchwriting still count as plagiarism?

Yes — and this surprises people. Citing tells the reader whose idea it is, but if your words and sentence structure hug the original, you have still presented their expression as your paraphrase. Proper practice is either a genuine reformulation (rebuilt, then cited) or a direct quotation in quotation marks. Patchwriting-plus-citation is the awkward, penalised middle.

How do I stay neutral in a synthesis when I have my own opinion?

You needn't be neutral — but you must be fair. Represent each source accurately and with reporting verbs it would accept, then let your own view emerge through emphasis, ordering, and an openly-marked concluding judgement, not through smuggling loaded verbs onto the side you dislike. The reader should always be able to tell the sources' claims from yours.
