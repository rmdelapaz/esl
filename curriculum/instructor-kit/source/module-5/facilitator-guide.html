Module 5 — Describing the World Around Me

Three classes that turn plain nouns into real descriptions. Learners count and color things ("two red bags"), say where things are ("It's under the table"), and talk about what people have and how they look ("She's got long dark hair"). The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 3 × 90 min CEFR A1

How to Use This Chapter

  • Each lesson block below is one 90-minute class. The stage plan is your clock. The notes under it (Meaning / Form / Pronunciation, drills, ICQs) are what you say and do.
  • Every class follows the same rhythm: warmer → lead-in → clarify (Meaning, Form, Pronunciation + CCQs) → drill → controlled practice (workbook) → freer speaking → feedback → homework.
  • Slides: slides/module-5-describing.html. Print sheet (flashcards, A/B cards, scripts, games): materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html. Answers: the Module 5 answer key.
  • This module is very visual and physical. Bring real things: a bag with 8–10 everyday objects, a small box, a ball (or an orange), and colored paper or pens. Point, touch, move, and hold things up. Learners with limited literacy follow much more easily when the words are attached to objects.
  • Teal = speaking and scripts, amber = watch-outs, dashed boxes = lines to say out loud.
The through-line

What is it like? → Where is it? → Who has got it? In 5.1 learners describe things (plural, color, adjective). In 5.2 they put those things in places (The red bag is under the table). In 5.3 they give the things to people and describe the people (She's got a red bag and long dark hair). Recycle on purpose: every freer task in 5.2 and 5.3 should include a color or an adjective from 5.1. By the end of C16 a learner can describe a room or a person in 5–6 sentences.

Before the Module

  • Mark Progress Test 1 before C14. Write a short, positive comment on every paper (one thing they did well + one thing to practice). Collect 4–5 common errors from different papers for the C14 board (no names).
  • Print from the materials sheet: color / adjective / room flashcards; the Describe & Guess cards (one set per 3–4 learners); Spot the Difference A/B cards (one A + one B per pair); What's in your bag? A/B cards and the Who is it? face board (one per pair).
  • Pack a teacher's bag: keys, a phone, two pens, a red notebook, glasses, an umbrella, an apple. You'll use it in all three classes.
  • Bring a small box and a ball or orange for the prepositions demo in C15.

Plurals, Colors & Adjectives

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • …make regular plurals (-s, -es, -ies, -ves) and say the common irregular ones (men, women, children, people, feet)
  • …name 10 colors and ask "What color is it?"

Returning Progress Test 1 (12 min) — keep it warm

1. Celebrate first (3 min). Before any paper comes out, write "We can…" on the board and list three things the whole class did well (for example: spell our names · say the time · use "there is / there are"). Say it out loud.

Four modules! You can introduce yourself, talk about your family, your day, and your home, in English. That's a lot. Clap for you.

2. Return privately (2 min). Hand papers back face-down or in folders. Never read scores aloud and never compare learners. Everyone gets a written comment: one strength + one thing to practice.

3. "Let's fix it!" (5 min). Show 4 anonymous errors from the tests (for example: She have two children. · I am 30 years. · There is two chairs. · He work in a bank.). Pairs fix them. Take answers. Praise the fixing, not the mistake-free.

4. One goal (2 min). Each learner writes one goal on a sticky note or in the workbook box: "I want to practice ___." Stick it inside the workbook cover.

A test is a map. It tells us where we are. It doesn't tell us who we are.

Some adults carry hard memories of school and tests. Keep your voice light and move on quickly. Offer a 1:1 chat at the break to anyone who struggled (below about 60%), and plan catch-up with the Module 1–4 online lessons. Don't let the test return grow past 12 minutes. The best encouragement is a good, successful lesson straight after it.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Plurals — meaning. Hold up one pen, then two pens.

One pen or more than one? (One.) And now? (More than one, two.) So we add… (s!)

Form. Rule 1: most nouns + -s. Rule 2: after s, x, ch, sh + -es (buses, boxes, watches, dishes). Rule 3: consonant + y → -ies (city → cities), but vowel + y → just -s (day → days, key → keys). Rule 4: some -f/-fe → -ves (knife → knives, leaf → leaves). Irregulars: teach as whole words with the word cards. people = the plural of person.

One child, two… (children). One person, two… (people). Is "persons" OK? (No, people.)

Pronunciation. At A1 focus on one thing: the extra syllable /ɪz/ after s, x, ch, sh. Clap it: bus (1 clap) → bus-es (2 claps), box-es, watch-es. Don't teach /s/ vs /z/ yet; just model naturally.

Colors — form. Colors are adjectives. Question: What color is it? Answer: It's blue. / It's black and white. We teach gray (American); grey (British) is also correct and appears in the online lesson.

Adjective + noun — meaning & form. Two rules: (1) the adjective goes before the noun, (2) it never takes -s.

"A car red": OK? (No.) "A red car"? (Yes.) Two cars: "two reds cars" or "two red cars"? (Two red cars.) Which word gets the s? (Cars.)

Two adjectives. Light version: opinion → size → color + noun. Most of the time one adjective is perfect.

"A nice big red bag." Which word is my feeling? (Nice.) Which word is the size? (Big.) Where is the color? (Last, next to "bag.")
Drill script
  1. Plural chain (choral): T: "One book." Ss: "Two books." · "One box." "Two boxes." · "One city." "Two cities." · "One child." "Two children." · "One person." "Two people." Then point at individuals.
  2. Backchain (the long phrase): "…bag." → "…red bag." → "…big red bag." → "…nice big red bag." → "a nice big red bag." Choral twice, then 4–5 individuals.
  3. Substitution: say "a red car," then give a cue: "two" → "two red cars" · "blue" → "two blue cars" · "small" → "two small blue cars."
ICQs — before Describe & Guess
Do you show your card? (No.) Do you say the word on the card? (No!) Do you say the color? (Yes.) How many clues? (Three.) What does your partner ask? ("Is it a…?")
Differentiation

Literacy support. Use the picture flashcards for all practice. In Ex. 1 do only the first four items. Let learners copy the plural from the board model. In the game they may use the clue words printed on the card. The clue can be just "Red. Small. Fruit."

Stretch. Two adjectives in every clue ("It's a long yellow fruit."). Add Rule 3/4 words: baby, family, leaf, wife. Write 6 sentences about their room for the project now.

L1 watch-outs
  • Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Arabic, Vietnamese speakers often put the adjective after the noun: "a car red." Romance-language speakers may also add -s to the adjective: "two reds cars." Show it clearly on the board and drill it.
  • Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Tagalog don't mark the plural on the noun, so learners drop the -s: "two book." With the number there, the meaning is still clear. Correct it gently and often.
  • Vietnamese, Cantonese speakers often drop final consonants. Plural -s and -es are hard to hear and say. Exaggerate the end sound and let learners feel it.
  • "persons," "childs," "mans" come from every L1. Keep the irregular word cards on the wall.

5.2 · C15

Where Things Are — Prepositions of Place

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • …use in, on, under, next to, between, behind, in front of, above (and understand near, opposite, below)
  • …ask Where is…? / Where are…? and answer It's… / They're…
  • …name common rooms, furniture, and everyday objects
  • …describe where 6 things are in a room; say at home, at work, at school
Materials
  • A small box and a ball (or an orange) · the teacher's bag · Workbook 5.2 · slides 9–12
  • Preposition and room / furniture flashcards · Listening script 5.2
  • Spot the Difference cards: Room A + Room B (one of each per pair)
Left: 8 simple drawings of a box and a ball: in · on · under · next to · between · behind · in front of · above
Middle: Where is my phone? → It's on the table. | Where are my keys? → They're in your bag.
Right: in front of (3 words!) ↔ behind · at home · at work · at school (✗ in home)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning (box + ball). Show each position with the ball, say the sentence, then check it. Do the ones learners know from Lesson 3.3 (under, next to, between) first. Then do the new ones.

(ball on top) Is the ball inside the box? (No.) Is it on top? (Yes.) So it's… (on the box).
(ball inside) Can you see all the ball? (No.) Is it inside? (Yes.) So it's… (in the box).
(ball in the air over the box) Is it touching the box? (No.) Is it higher? (Yes.) So it's… (above the box). Touching = on. Not touching = above.
(two boxes, ball in the middle) How many boxes? (Two.) Is the ball in the middle? (Yes.) So it's… (between the boxes).
(ball at the back) Can I see the ball? (A little / no.) Is it at the front or at the back? (Back.) So it's… (behind the box). And now? (In front of the box.)

Form. Where is + one thing → It's + preposition + the + place. Where are + two or more → They're… Short answers are natural: "On the table." in front of is always three words. at home / at work / at school are fixed phrases.

Pronunciation. Link the words: Where's‿my phone? · It's‿on‿the table. Stress the preposition when it's the new information: "It's UNder the table." Between and behind are stressed on the second syllable: be-TWEEN, be-HIND.

Drill script
  1. Choral: point to each board drawing and say the full sentence: "The ball is in the box." Ss repeat ×2. Then point in random order, Ss say only the preposition, faster and faster.
  2. Question–answer: T: "Where's the ball?" (move it) Ss: "It's under the table." Then use two balls/pens: "Where are the pens?" "They're in the bag."
  3. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 hides a pen and asks "Where's my pen?" S2 answers. Swap.
ICQs — before Spot the Difference
Do you show your picture to your partner? (No!) Are the two rooms the same? (No, a little different.) How many differences? (Six.) Do you talk or point? (Talk.) What question do you ask? ("Where is the cat?") Where do you write the differences? (In the table on the card.)
Differentiation

Literacy support. Keep the box-and-ball drawings on the board all class; learners can point at a drawing instead of reading the word. The room pictures have word labels. In Spot the Difference they may simply circle the six things that are different instead of writing sentences.

Stretch. Use near, opposite, below too. Add a color to every answer ("The red bag is on the sofa."). After the game, describe their own living room in 6 sentences without notes.

L1 watch-outs
  • Spanish / Portuguese en and Arabic fi cover both "in" and "on," so you'll hear "The phone is in the table." Use the box: inside = in, on top = on.
  • Spanish, Portuguese, French: "in home" or "in the home" (en casa). Drill at home / at work / at school as chunks.
  • Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Turkish put the location word after the noun ("table on"). Learners may say "The keys are table on." Model the English order: preposition before the place.
  • "Where are my phone?" Plural-less languages (Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog…) mix up is/are. Tie it to 5.1: one thing = is / it's, two or more = are / they're.

Q1 B The cup is between the two books · Q2 B I am at home · Q3 B Where are my keys? · Spot the Difference: lamp, cat, clock, books, cup, bag (full list in the answer key).

Have Got — People & Belongings

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • …say what people have with have got / has got (and the contractions I've got, she's got)
  • …make negatives (haven't got / hasn't got) and questions (Have you got…? Has she got…?)
  • …give short answers: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't.
  • …describe a person's hair, eyes, and look (long, short, curly, straight, dark, blond, glasses, beard, tall)
Materials

Middle: haven't got / hasn't got · Have you got a pen? → Yes, I have. / No, I haven't. · Has she got glasses? → Yes, she has. / No, she hasn't. (✗ Yes, I have got.)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. have got = have, for things we own and how people look. It is not the past tense of get.

"I've got an umbrella." Is the umbrella mine? (Yes.) Is it now or yesterday? (Now.) "I have an umbrella." Same or different? (Same.)
"She's got long hair." Is her hair long now? (Yes.) Is it "She is got"? (No, she HAS got.)

Form. I / you / we / they + have got ('ve got). He / she / it + has got ('s got). Negative: haven't got / hasn't got. Question: Have / Has + subject + got…? Short answer: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't. No got in short answers.

American English note

The online lesson teaches have got, which is common in British English and everyday speech. In the US, people more often say I have… / Do you have…? / I don't have…. Both are correct. Teach have got (it's on the homework), but model and accept "Do you have a pen?" / "Yes, I do." Learners in the US will hear the have / do form at work and at the doctor, so point it out once and move on.

People. Build it like the lesson: has got + (length) + (type/color) + hair: "She's got long straight blond hair." hair is uncountable here: long hair, not hairs. Use is for tall/short: "He's tall" (= he is).

"He's tall." Is it "he has" or "he is"? (He is.) "He's got glasses." Has or is? (Has.) How do you know? (Got!)

Pronunciation. Contractions are the natural spoken form: I've got /aɪv gɒt/ (the /v/ is short but there), she's got. Short answers are stressed: "Yes, I HAVE." "No, she HASn't." Watch /v/ in have / haven't (Spanish b/v).

Drill script
  1. Pronoun switch: T holds a pen: "I've got a pen." Point to a learner: Ss "She's got a pen." / "He's got a pen." Point to two learners: "They've got pens." Point to the class: "We've got pens."
  2. Question chain: S1 asks S2 "Have you got a phone?" S2 answers "Yes, I have." / "No, I haven't." and asks S3 about a new object. Keep it going around the room.
  3. Describing: show slide 16 people. T: "Grandfather — hair?" Ss: "He's got short gray hair." T: "Glasses?" Ss: "Yes, he's got glasses." T: "Grandmother — glasses?" Ss: "No, she hasn't."
ICQs — before What's in your bag?
Do you show your card? (No.) Do you ask "Have you got a key?" or "Has you got…"? (Have you got.) When your partner says "Yes, I have," what do you do? (Tick ✓.) How many things have you both got? (Find two.)
Privacy: bags and bodies

Use the bag cards, not learners' real bags. A bag can hold medicine, money, or documents that people don't want to show. Learners may choose to show 2–3 things from their own bag as an extra. Describe characters (the face board, the online lesson people), not classmates' bodies. If a learner wants to describe a real family member for the project, that's great. A made-up person ("new identity") is also fine.

Differentiation

Literacy support. The bag cards are all pictures. Learners point to a picture and ask "Have you got…?" with the word from the board. In Ex. 1–2 write have / has on the board with a big "he / she / it" arrow next to has. For Who is it? they can ask with gestures (point to their own glasses: "Glasses?") and you model the full question.

Stretch. Describe a person in 6+ sentences without notes, including one belonging and one negative. Use the American form as well: "Does she have glasses? — Yes, she does." Write the project model in the workbook.

L1 watch-outs
  • Tagalog, Chinese, Vietnamese, Turkish, Finnish: one spoken word for "he" and "she." When learners describe people, he/she mix-ups jump. Point to the picture: man → he, woman → she, and correct right away in the drill.
  • Spanish / Portuguese / Italian: "She has the hair long" (tiene el pelo largo) → "She's got the long hair" or "hairs." Model: She's got long hair (no "the," no -s).
  • Arabic, Russian, Chinese speakers may drop the verb: "She long hair." Point to the missing 's got.
  • Everyone: "He have got…" and "Yes, I have got." Keep the board pointer on has for he/she/it.
  • Japanese / Korean: l/r in long, curly, glasses, brown. Quick minimal-pair fun: long / wrong, glass / grass.
Homework

Closing the Module

End of C16 — "I can describe…"

Ask 3–4 volunteers to say one sentence from each lesson in a chain: "I've got two small black bags. My red bag is under the table. My sister's got long dark hair." Cheer each chain. Recap on the board: two red cars → It's under the table → She's got long hair.

Before Module 6: check the goal sticky notes from C14 with learners who want to talk about them. Anyone who missed a class: send them to the online lesson and give the workbook page to do at home.
