Module 4 — Getting Around & Doing Things

Three classes that move learners from talking about themselves to acting in the world. They say what they can do and what they like, describe what is happening right now, and then shop and find their way in a task-based review class that gets everyone ready for Progress Test 1.

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 CEFR A1 3 × 90 min

How to Use This Chapter

  • Each lesson block below is one 90-minute class. The stage plan is the clock. The notes after it give the language, the CCQs, the drill and the ICQs.
  • Same rhythm every class: warmer/review → lead-in → Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (with CCQs) → drill (choral → individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer speaking → feedback (delayed error correction) → homework.
  • C12 is different. It is a task-based review class. There is no new grammar presentation. Learners do real tasks (a map information gap and a classroom shop) while you observe with the speaking checklist. That checklist tells you who needs help in the catch-up part of C13 (Progress Test 1, Modules 1–4).
  • Print from materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html: flashcards, the Find-someone-who grid, mime cards, the A/B maps, shop price tags and shopping lists, the speaking checklist, and all the read-aloud scripts.
  • Project slides/module-4-getting-around.html (arrow keys / space). It has the picture-vocabulary, form, dialogue, CCQ and task-instruction slides for all three classes.
  • Teal = learners active, amber = L1 watch-outs, dashed boxes = lines to say out loud.
The through-line

Module 4 is about doing. In 4.1, learners say what they can do and what they like (a café is the context). In 4.2, they describe what people are doing right now, and they learn to tell it apart from the "every day" present simple from Module 3. In 4.3, they use everything: they greet, buy, ask the price, ask the way and give directions. The review class is the halfway point of the course, so celebrate it. Many adult learners arrived unable to say their name in English. Now they can shop and find the station.

Before the Module

  • C10: ability and food/drink flashcards, one Find someone who can… grid per learner, café menu cards + Server/Customer role cards (one set per pair). Bring a real cup and a spoon for the café role-play if you can.
  • C11: action flashcards, mime cards (one set per team of 4–5), the A/B "What are they doing?" park cards (one per pair).
  • C12: A and B maps (one per pair), price tags for two classroom shops, shopping-list cards, play money, and your speaking checklist on a clipboard. Arrange two "shop" tables before class starts. Real items (an apple, a pen, a bag, an umbrella) make the shop feel real and help low-literacy learners.
  • Check the audio on the online lessons works on your classroom computer (🔊 Listen buttons) in case you want a second voice.

4.1 · C10

Can / Can't — Abilities & Food

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • say what they can and can't do: I can cook, but I can't drive.
  • ask Can you…? and give short answers: Yes, I can. / No, I can't.
  • say what food and drink they like, love, don't like: I love coffee. I don't like fish.
  • ask Do you like…? → Yes, I do. / No, I don't., and answer an offer politely in a café

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. can = able to do it (a skill or ability). can't = not able to. Use your mime: swim well, guitar badly.

"I can swim." Am I able to swim? (Yes.) Am I swimming now? (No — we don't know.) Is it a skill? (Yes.)
"She can't drive." Can she drive a car? (No.) Is it possible she can learn? (Yes — "can't" is today.)

Form. subject + can / can't + plain verb. One form for every person. Three traps (write them and cross them out): She cans swim · I can to swim · He can swims. Question: Can + subject + verb? Short answers: Yes, I can. / No, I can't.

Pronunciation. In a sentence, can is weak and short /kən/: "I c'n SWIM." The main verb gets the stress. Can't is strong /kænt/: "I CAN'T SWIM." In American English the final t is often not clear, so the vowel and stress carry the difference. In short answers, can is strong: "Yes, I CAN."

Like. like / don't like / love / hate + thing. Question with do: Do you like tea? Answer with do, not like and not can.

"I love chocolate." Do I like it a little or a lot? (A lot.) "I hate onions." Do I want onions on my pizza? (No!)
"Do you like tea?" What's the short answer — "Yes, I can" or "Yes, I do"? (Yes, I do.) Why? (The question has "do".)
Drill script
  1. Hold up swim. Model ×2: "I can swim." Class ×2 (choral). Hand gesture: fist forward on can, then open hand on the verb, to show the stress.
  2. Show the card with a ❌ (cross your arms): "I can't drive." Class ×2. Exaggerate the long vowel in can't.
  3. Substitution: you say a cue, they say the sentence. "cook ✅" → I can cook. "dance ❌" → I can't dance. "she / sing ✅" → She can sing.
  4. Individual: nominate around the room, quick pace, praise, then back to choral if three people in a row struggle.
  5. Question chain: S1 asks S2 "Can you ride a bike?" S2 answers truthfully and asks S3 a new question.
ICQs — before the mingle and the café
Find someone who can… Do you write your name or your partner's name? (Partner's.) One name per box or many? (One.) Do you sit or stand? (Stand.) Do you show your paper? (No — ask!)
Café: Student A is the…? (Server.) What do you say first? ("Hello! Would you like…?") Customer — just "yes"? (No — "Yes, please.") When do you swap? (When I clap.)
Differentiation
  • Literacy support: use the picture version of the Find-someone-who grid (pictures in each box). Learners draw a tick or ask the partner to write their name. In the workbook, do Ex 1 and Ex 4 only (picture/✅❌ cues). Give a phrase strip: Can you…? · Yes, I can. · No, I can't.
  • Stretch: add …a little / …very well (I can cook very well. I can sing a little.) and a follow-up What can you cook? In the café, the customer orders for two people and asks for something not on the menu.
  • New identity option: some learners may not want to talk about skills (for example, lost jobs or qualifications). They can answer as a card character. That's always fine.
L1 watch-outs
  • "She cans / He can swims": over-using the Module 3 -s. Tap the board rule: can never changes.
  • "I can to swim": learners whose L1 uses an infinitive marker after "can" (for example Spanish puedo nadar is fine, but want to/need to interference) add to. Cross out to every time.
  • can/can't confusion in listening is universal, and it's hardest for learners of languages without weak vowels (Spanish, Japanese, many others). Drill stress, not the final t.
  • "I like the coffee" (general): Spanish, Portuguese, French, Arabic and Italian speakers use the article for general likes. In English, general likes have no article: I like coffee.
  • "Yes, I like." / "Me like…": Spanish me gusta interference. Model the short answer Yes, I do.
  • "I very like…": Chinese and Vietnamese speakers. Offer I really like… or I like… very much.
  • "a rice / breads": give a gentle correction only. The lesson says countability is "not for today."
Homework
  • Online lesson 4.1 (listen with the 🔊 buttons): rays-esl.netlify.app/curriculum/lesson_10.html, including the "I Can… I Like…" speaking project (record 6 sentences on your phone).
  • Workbook 4.1: "Write about you" + "My new words" + journal (2 sentences).

4.2 · C11

Present Continuous — Happening Right Now

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • make the present continuous: am / is / are + verb-ing (I'm eating. She's reading.)
  • describe what people are doing right now, in a picture or in the room
  • ask and answer What are you doing? and Is she working? — Yes, she is. / No, she isn't.
  • tell the difference: I work in a bank (every day) vs I'm working now (this moment)
Materials
  • Workbook 4.2 (words, grammar box, Exercises 1–5, phone-call dialogue)
  • Action flashcards · Mime cards (1 set per team) · A/B "What are they doing?" park cards (1 per pair)

Spelling: read → reading · write → writing · run → running, sit → sitting, swim → swimming
Right (timeline): EVERY DAY: I work in a bank. (usually, always)  |  NOW: I'm working now. (now, right now, at the moment)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Something is happening at this moment, while we speak. It started and it is not finished yet.

"I'm writing." Am I writing now? (Yes.) Did I finish? (No.) Is it every day? (We don't know / not important.)
"I work in a bank. Today I'm working at home." Where do I work every day? (In a bank.) Where am I now? (At home.)
"She usually walks to school, but today she's taking the bus." Is walking her habit? (Yes.) What is she doing today? (Taking the bus.)

Form. subject + am / is / are + verb-ing. Two parts, always. Negative: add not (isn't, aren't, I'm not). Question: am / is / are first. Short answers repeat am/is/are: Yes, she is. (never Yes, she's).

Spelling. Most verbs: + ing. Silent -e: drop it (write → writing). Short word, one vowel + one consonant: double (run → running). Not sure? Just add -ing.

Pronunciation. Contractions in speech: I'm, she's, they're. The ending /ɪŋ/: back of the tongue up, no hard "g." Model sing vs sin. In the question, intonation rises: Are you ↗ working?

Drill script
  1. Show read. "She's reading." ×2. Class ×2. Then the full form once ("She is reading") and back to the contraction.
  2. Subject substitution with the same picture: "I" → I'm reading. "they" → They're reading. "he" → He's reading.
  3. Picture substitution: flash cook, drink, swim, sing, watch TV. Learners say "He's cooking," etc.
  4. Negative + correction: hold swim, say "She's sleeping?" → class: "No, she isn't. She's swimming."
  5. Individual round, then open pairs: S1 "What are you doing?" S2 mimes and says "I'm drinking coffee."
ICQs — before the mime game and the info gap
Mime game: Do you show your card? (No!) Do you speak when you are acting? (Only "Yes, I am / No, I'm not.") How do you get the point? (Say the full sentence: "She's cooking.")
Info gap: Can you look at your partner's card? (No.) What's the question? ("What is ___ doing?") Where do you write the answer? (In the empty box.)
Differentiation
  • Literacy support: picture-only mime cards (use the flashcards). In the workbook, do Ex 5 (picture sentences) first. Give a strip: He's ___ing. / She's ___ing. / They're ___ing. and let learners trace the -ing.
  • Stretch: add a wear sentence (She's wearing a red coat) and the "usually … but today …" frame. In the info gap, add one extra question: "Is he happy?"
  • Private life: the phone-call adaptation is about "your home right now." Learners can invent a home and a family. Never ask who they live with.
L1 watch-outs
  • "I eating / She reading": dropping be is common for Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog and Haitian Creole speakers. Hold up two fingers: "two parts."
  • Present simple for now: Spanish, Portuguese, French, German and Russian speakers often say "What do you do now?" / "I cook now." Their L1 uses one present for both. Use the timeline and the CCQ "habit or this second?"
  • "He are working": revisit the Lesson 2.1 match-up: I → am, he/she/it → is, you/we/they → are.
  • -ing pronunciation: Spanish and Vietnamese speakers may say -in or add a hard g (sing-g). Arabic speakers may add a vowel before clusters.
  • "I'm liking / I'm wanting": stretch learners may over-apply -ing. Say simply: "like, want, know — no -ing now."
  • Tagalog / Chinese speakers and he/she: their L1 pronoun doesn't mark gender, so "He's cooking" for a woman is a slip, not a misunderstanding. Correct it lightly.

  • Workbook 4.2: "Write about now" + "My new words" + journal.

4.3 · C12

Review Project — Shopping & Directions

90 min · task-based
How this class works — task-based

This is a task-based lesson: pre-task (short language input for the two tasks) → task cycle (Task 1: map information gap · Task 2: classroom shop · Task 3: "A Day Out") → language focus (feedback on what you heard). You talk less than in any other class. Learners talk more. Your job during the tasks is to listen and fill in the speaking checklist. It is formative, so it's not a grade.

Objectives — learners can…
  • shop politely and ask the price: I'd like…, please. · How much is it? / How much are they? · How much is that altogether?
  • ask the way: Excuse me, where's the station? · Is there a bank near here?
  • give and follow simple directions: Go straight on. · Take the first left. · It's on your right, opposite the bank.
  • combine Modules 1–4 (greet, introduce, numbers, be, can, like) in one real conversation
Materials
  • Workbook 4.3 (Exercises 1–5, mini-map, "My Day Out" plan, the Modules 1–4 self-check)
  • A and B maps (1 per pair) · Shop kit: price tags for the Corner Shop and the Market Stall, shopkeeper cards, 8 shopping-list cards, play money
  • "A Day Out" Visitor / Local cards · Speaking checklist (1 copy for you, on a clipboard)
  • Slides 17–22 · Scripts: prices dictation, mini-map directions (materials)
Shop: I'd like a coffee, please. · Can I have a bag, please? · How much is it? (1) → It's $2.50. · How much are they? (2+) → They're $4. · How much is that altogether? · Cash or card? · Here's your change / receipt /rɪˈsiːt/.
Directions: Excuse me, where's the…? · ⬆ Go straight on. · ⬅ Turn left. ➡ Turn right. · Take the first / second left. · Go past the school. · It's on your left / right. · It's next to / opposite the bank. · It's on the corner.
Right corner: Today's tasks: ① Map ② Shop ③ Day Out — "Halfway! 🎓"

Pre-task language — Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Price questions. How much = the price. One thing → is it → It's…. Two or more → are they → They're…. The whole bill → How much is that altogether?

(Hold up one apple.) How much is it or how much are they? (Is it.) (Hold up shoes.) (Are they.) "How many" or "how much" for a price? (How much.)

Polite requests. I'd like… = I would like. Polite, not childish. Can I have…, please? is also polite.

"I'd like a coffee." Is it the same as "I like coffee"? (No — "I'd like" is now, a request. "I like" is always, a feeling.)

Prices. $4.50 = "four dollars fifty" or "four fifty." $13 vs $30: stress thirTEEN vs THIRty. receipt — the p is silent.

Directions (imperative). Plain verb, no you: Go straight on. Turn left. On your left / right (not in). Opposite = on the other side of the street; show it with two hands facing each other.

"It's opposite the bank." Is it next to the bank? (No.) Do I cross the street? (Yes.) "Take the second left." Do I turn at the first street? (No — the second.)
Drill script (short — this is a review class)
  1. Hold up one item, then two: class says "How much is it?" / "How much are they?" Keep a fast rhythm.
  2. Back-chain the long one: altogether → is that altogether → How much is that altogether?
  3. Directions with the body (TPR): you say it, they point; then a learner says it and you point, and you get it wrong on purpose so they correct you.
  4. Open pair: S1 "Excuse me, where's the bank?" S2 (looking at the mini-map) "Go straight on. It's on your left."
ICQs — before each task
Map: Can you see your partner's map? (No.) What do you say first? ("Excuse me…") What do you write in the "?" box? (The place.) Where do you start? (At the star — "You are here.")
Shop: Customers, what do you buy? (The things on my card.) What do you ask? ("How much is it / are they?") What do you write at the end? (The total.) Shopkeepers — do you just say "$4"? (No — "It's four dollars.")
Day Out: How many parts? (Four: hello, shop, directions, goodbye.) Do you read the card aloud? (No — look, then speak.)
The speaking checklist — how to use it
  • Write the class names in the checklist before class. Aim to observe every learner at least once across Tasks 1–3. Stand to the side; don't hover or stare at the clipboard. For anxious learners, listen from a distance.
  • Codes: ✓ does it independently · ~ does it with help / after a model · ○ not yet (or not observed, so check it again in C13). Note one example error in the last column if useful.
  • Columns cover Modules 1–4: greet & introduce · can / like · present continuous (use the Day Out small talk, or ask "What are they doing?" about the shop) · I'd like · is it / are they · prices · asks the way · gives directions · follows directions.
  • After class, circle every learner with two or more ○. They form the catch-up group in C13 (see below).
Differentiation
  • Literacy support: pair a pre-literate learner with a patient partner as Map B first (B listens and points before writing). Let them write the first letter of the place or draw its symbol in the "?" box. In the shop, they're a customer with the picture shopping list and real objects, and they can point and ask "How much is it?"
  • Stretch: in the map task, add next to / opposite / on the corner to every direction. In the shop, shopkeepers announce one item "on sale" and customers ask "Is it on sale?" or bargain politely. In Day Out, the visitor asks "Is it far?" and the local gives a time.
  • New identity: Day Out asks "Where are you from?" The Visitor card gives a name and a town to use instead. Say clearly that the card is always okay.
  • Money: use play money only. Some learners may be struggling financially, so keep prices and "budget" talk light and game-like.
L1 watch-outs
  • "How much cost?" / "How much costs it?": Spanish (¿Cuánto cuesta?), Portuguese, French and Arabic speakers translate the verb. Accept How much does it cost? but drill How much is it?
  • "How many is it?": learners mix up much/many. For prices, it's always much.
  • "Where station?" / "Where is station?": Arabic, Chinese and Russian speakers drop is and/or the article. Drill Where's the station? as one chunk.
  • "Turn to the left" / "in the right": Spanish (gira a la izquierda, a la derecha) and French speakers. Model Turn left. It's on your right.
  • teen / ty in prices: everyone (thirteen/thirty, fifteen/fifty). Vietnamese and Chinese speakers may also drop the final consonants of numbers (fif-).
  • "I want…" / "Give me…": direct requests are polite in many languages and cultures. Present I'd like…, please as "the shop sentence in English," not as a correction of manners.
  • "receipt" with a p and p/b for Arabic speakers (pay/bay, please/bleese): quick minimal-pair drill if it comes up.
Homework

  • Workbook 4.3: "My Day Out" plan + journal. Review for C13: reread workbook pages for Modules 1–4, and do the Quick Quizzes of any two earlier lessons online.

Q1 B How much are they? · Q2 A Go straight on and take the first left. It's on your right. · Q3 (new) "It's opposite the bank" → B on the other side of the street from the bank.

Bridge to C13 — Progress Test 1

Using the C12 speaking checklist in C13

C13 is Progress Test 1 (Modules 1–4) + catch-up. Before C13, count the ○s in each column of your checklist. The column with the most ○s is your whole-class five-minute review at the start of C13. Learners with two or more ○s join a small catch-up group with you while the others do an extension task. Re-check their ○ items in the group (a 1-minute chat is enough) and update the sheet. Keep the checklist: it's your evidence of speaking progress alongside the written test.

  • Typical catch-up focus areas: How much is it / are they · am/is/are in the present continuous · can + plain verb · following first / second left.
  • Tell learners in C12: "Next class there's a short test. It checks what you know, so we know what to practice. It is not a pass/fail exam." Adults with difficult school histories may worry, so say it twice.
