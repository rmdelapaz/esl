Module 3 — Everyday Life

Three classes where learners stop naming things and start describing their lives. They get their first tense, the present simple, for their own day. Then they add the clock, the calendar and the he/she -s to talk about other people. Last, they learn there is / there are to describe the place they live.

3 lessons Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C7–C9 Weeks 4–5 90 min per class CEFR A1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs) → feedback → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-3-everyday-life.html. Print materials/module-3/classroom-materials.html before C7. It holds the flashcards, the picture-sequencing cards, the clock information gap, the -s sound cards, the home cards, the listening scripts and the Find Someone Who game.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear every word and dialogue again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons sometimes show British forms (at the weekend, flat, lift, cooker). Teach on the weekend, apartment, elevator, stove, and accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

My day → your day → my home. In C7 learners describe their own routine (I / you / we / they: no -s). In C8 they describe someone else's routine with a clock and a calendar (he / she: -s). In C9 they describe the place where the day happens (there is / there are). Every class ends with the same freer task in a new form: tell a partner about a real life (yours, or a "new identity" card).

Trauma-informed: routines and homes are personal

Some learners have no job right now, work night shifts, share one room with family, or live in a shelter or temporary housing. Never ask anyone to show or explain their real situation. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a "new identity" card (in the classroom materials), or a dream / imagined life. Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Before the Module

  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board) and the routine picture-sequencing cards (one set per group of three). Card stock helps. Cut them before C7.
  • Print one clock information gap page per pair for C8 (cut A from B), and one set of -s sound cards per group.
  • Bring or make a teaching clock (a paper plate, a brad fastener and two card hands is fine). For C9, bring small realia for prepositions: a cup, a pen, a box, a toy or paper cat.
  • Print the new identity cards and the home cards and keep them on a table where anyone can take one without asking.
  • Plain paper and colored pens for C9 (draw your home).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The present simple is for habits and routines: things we do again and again. It is not for "right now." Point at the time line: every day, every week.

"I get up at seven." Is this only today? (No.) Is it every day? (Yes.) Is it a habit? (Yes.)
"I don't work on Sunday." Do I work on Sunday? (No.) On Monday? (Yes. Probably!)
"Do you get up early?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Can you answer "Yes, I do"? (Yes.) Do you say "Yes, I get up"? (No.)

Form. Three lines on the board, with the subject and verb in different colors:

PositiveI / You / We / They + base verbWe study English every day.
NegativeI / You / We / They + don't + base verbThey don't work on Sunday.
QuestionDo + I / you / we / they + base verb…?Do you cook dinner?
Short answerYes, I do. / No, I don't.(don't say the verb again)

Pronunciation. don't is one syllable with a strong o sound: /doʊnt/. Learners often say "do not" slowly. Model the natural form. In questions, Do you is weak and fast, like /də jə/: "D'you get up early?" Model it both ways. Accept the slow form in production. The stress is on the main verb: "Do you COOK?" Short answers are stressed: "Yes, I DO." "No, I DON'T."

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the model sentence: "at seven" → "get up at seven" → "I get up at seven." Choral ×3, then half the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Substitution drill (point at a flashcard, then a subject word): T: "We… 🚌" → S: "We go to work." T: "They… 📺" → S: "They watch TV." Speed up when they are ready.
  3. Negative transformation: T: "I work on Sunday." (shake head) → S: "I don't work on Sunday."
  4. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 asks S2 across the room "Do you get up early?" → S2: "Yes, I do. / No, I don't." Then all pairs together, 1 minute.
Picture sequencing — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one set of 16 sequencing cards, shuffled, picture up.
  • Round 1 (listen): read Script 3.1-B. Groups put Mia's cards in order and put the cards she does not do to one side (Mia doesn't watch TV and doesn't drink coffee).
  • Round 2 (speak): each group member points to a card and says the sentence with "I": "I get up at six." Stronger learners add a time expression.
  • Round 3 (personalize): each learner builds their own day from the cards (or from a new identity card). Keep these cards for the "Same or different?" task.

Answer (Mia): wake up → get up → take a shower → brush my teeth → get dressed → have breakfast → go to work → have lunch → come home → cook dinner → read → go to bed. Not used: drink coffee, watch TV (plus study and go to school).

ICQs before "Same or different?"
Do you ask questions or write sentences? (Ask questions.) · What is the first word of your question? ("Do.") · How many things are the same? (Three.) · Can you use a new identity card? (Yes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Picture cards do the work: learners point and say, and write only 3 phrases (copy from the board). In Ex 2, give them the cut-up sentence strips to order physically. In the project, 4 sentences is a success.

Stretch

Add a time to every sentence and link with then / after that. In the pair task, ask two "Do you…?" questions of their own about the weekend. Early finishers write their day for Ex 5 with no pictures.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I no work" / "I not work" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian; also Chinese and Vietnamese speakers): the language has no helper verb for negatives. Keep pointing at the don't on the board.
  • "You work here?" with rising intonation and no Do (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Russian). Accept for meaning; then recast: "Do you work here?"
  • "I am work in a hospital" (Arabic, Urdu, Chinese learners who have just learned "be" in Module 2). Show the two patterns side by side: I am a nurse. / I work in a hospital. Never both.
  • Adding -s too early ("I works"): learners who already know "she works." Praise the knowledge, then say: "Good, that is for next class. For I/you/we/they: no -s."
  • "Yes, I work" as a short answer. Point to "do" on the board.

If you run long

This is the busiest class. Keep the classic past/to way receptive (learners understand it, and they say the easy way). Cut Set 2 of the information gap. Do the -s sort as a whole class on the board. Never cut the freer task: it is where the -s becomes real.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Time — meaning. Past = minutes after the hour (right side of the clock). To = minutes before the next hour (left side).

3:40. Is it before four or after four? (Before.) So is it "twenty past four" or "twenty to four"? (Twenty to four.) Can you also say "three forty"? (Yes!)
"Half past seven." Is it 6:30 or 7:30? (7:30.)

He / she — meaning. Same habit meaning as last class, now about another person.

"Lina gets up at six thirty." Is this about me? (No.) About Lina, one person? (Yes.) Every day? (Yes.) What is the extra letter? (-s.)
"She doesn't work on Sunday." Does Lina work on Sunday? (No.) Where is the -s now? (On "does." Doesn't.) So "work" or "works"? (Work.)

Form.

He / She / Itverb + -sShe works. He gets up.
-esafter s, x, ch, sh, owatches · finishes · fixes · goes · does
-iesconsonant + ystudy → studies (but play → plays)
Irregularhave → hasShe has breakfast at seven.
Negativedoesn't + base verbHe doesn't work on Sunday.
QuestionDoes + he/she + base verb…?Does she get up early? — Yes, she does. / No, she doesn't.

Pronunciation. /s/ after voiceless sounds p, t, k, f: works, gets, sleeps, cooks, starts. /z/ after voiced sounds and vowels: plays, reads, goes, comes, studies, has, lives. /ɪz/ after s, z, sh, ch, x, ge (hissing sounds): watches, finishes, fixes, relaxes, brushes, washes. This adds a syllable, so clap it: watch (1) → watch-es (2). The /s/ vs /z/ difference is small, and nobody misunderstands it. The /ɪz/ extra beat and saying the -s at all matter much more. Also: does /dʌz/ and says /sɛz/ change their vowel.

Drill script
  1. Clock drill: move the teaching clock. "What time is it?" (all) → "It's four thirty." Choral, then individuals. Every third time, ask for the classic way from a stronger learner.
  2. I → she transformation: T: "I work." S: "She works." T: "I watch TV." S: "She watches TV." T: "I have lunch." S: "She has lunch." Choral, then individuals, then fast.
  3. Negative: T: "She works on Sunday?" (shake head) → S: "She doesn't work on Sunday."
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "Does Lina get up early?" S2: "Yes, she does." S2 → S3: "Does Lina work on Sunday?"… around the room.
  5. /ɪz/ clap drill: learners clap the beats: watch (1) / watch-es (2), fix / fix-es, finish (2) / fin-ish-es (3).
ICQs before the information gap and the freer task
Information gap: Can you show your card? (No.) Who asks first? (A.) What do you draw on the empty clock? (The hands.) What do you say if you don't understand? ("Can you say it again, please?")
Partner's day: First you ask your partner, or you tell a new partner? (Ask first.) When you tell the new partner, do you say "I" or "he/she"? (He/she.) What letter do you listen for? (-s.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Times only on the hour and half hour (o'clock and thirty). In the information gap, write digits (4:30) instead of drawing hands if needed. Give a days-of-the-week strip to copy from. For -s, the aim is to hear and say it; the spelling rules are for stretch.

Stretch

Produce the classic way (twenty to four). Add noon/midnight and months with in. In the freer task, compare: "Ana gets up at six, but I get up at seven." Use on the weekend and frequency words they may know (always, never).

L1 watch-outs
  • "Half seven" means 6:30 in German, Dutch, Russian, Polish, Hungarian and some others (half toward seven). In British English "half seven" means 7:30! Teach "seven thirty" and "half past seven" and check with a clock every time.
  • Dropping the -s ("He work at night"): very common for Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Korean and Japanese speakers (no verb endings in their L1). Vietnamese speakers also often drop final consonants in general. Pull the -s out with your fingers ("works-sss") and accept progress, not perfection.
  • He/she mix-ups (Tagalog siya, Chinese tā, Turkish o, Persian u, Finnish hän are one word for both). Use two picture cards (a man, a woman) and point while you drill. Correct gently; it is a habit, not a lack of knowledge.
  • Days without capitals (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian, Russian write lunes, lundi, segunda-feira in lower case). "In Monday" (Spanish en, Portuguese na) and "at the seven" (Spanish a las siete). Spanish and French speakers may also add "the": "on the Monday."
  • 24-hour clock: many learners use 17:00 at work. Accept "five o'clock" and teach in the afternoon / in the evening to replace "p.m." in speech.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. There is / there are says that something exists in a place. We use it for new information: "There's a cat in my kitchen!", not "A cat is in my kitchen."

"There is a bed in my room." How many beds? (One.) "There are two chairs." One chair? (No, two.) So one = is, two or more = are.
"There isn't a TV." Is there a TV? (No.) "Are there any shops near the school?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Answer with the same words: (Yes, there are. / No, there aren't.)
"There is some water." Can you count water, one water, two water? (No.) So is or are? (Is.)

Form.

One thing / uncountableTwo or more
+There is (There's) a sofa. / some water.There are two bedrooms. / some plants.
−There isn't a garden.There aren't any windows.
?Is there a balcony?Are there any chairs?
ShortYes, there is. / No, there isn't.Yes, there are. / No, there aren't.

Pronunciation. There's /ðɛrz/ is one syllable; drill it, because it sounds natural. Do not contract there are in writing; in fast speech it sounds like /ðɛrər/. The th /ð/: tongue between the teeth, voice on. Many learners say "dere" or "zere." Accept it for meaning and show the tongue once. Short answers use the full form, stressed: "Yes, there IS." in front of is linked: /ɪn ˈfrʌnt əv/ ("in-FRUN-tuv").

Drill script
  1. Realia drill: put things on your desk. T: (1 pen) "There's a pen." (choral) → (3 pens) "There are three pens." Learners call the sentence as you add or take away items.
  2. Negative: T holds up an empty hand: "a phone?" → S: "There isn't a phone." "books?" → S: "There aren't any books."
  3. Question and answer, open pairs: S1: "Is there a clock in this room?" S2 (looks): "Yes, there is." S2 → S3: "Are there any plants?"…
  4. Where's the cat? Move the paper cat; the room answers: "It's under the box." Then learners move it for each other.
ICQs before "Draw your home"
Do you show your picture to your partner? (No, not yet!) · Does it need to be your real home? (No. Any home, or a card.) · A talks and B…? (Draws and asks questions.) · What question can B ask? ("Is there a…? Are there any…?") · When do you show the pictures? (At the end.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Drawing is the task, so pre-literate learners can succeed fully. Give them labeled furniture picture cards to place on their drawing and point to while speaking. Target 4 sentences with "There is / There are" and 2 prepositions (on, next to).

Stretch

Describe two rooms and connect them ("The kitchen is next to the living room"). Add adjectives they know (big, small, beautiful). Use some/any and between. Tell the class about the partner's home in the third person: "In Omar's home there are…"

L1 watch-outs
  • "There have a table" / "Have a table in my room": Portuguese tem, Chinese yǒu, Tagalog may / mayroon, Vietnamese có, Thai mii use "have" for "there is." Mark the contrast on the board: I have a table. (mine) / There is a table. (in the room).
  • "There is two chairs": Spanish hay, French il y a, Italian c'è/ci sono (partly) use one form for singular and plural. Point at the number.
  • "In the kitchen table" (no "there is"): Arabic, Russian, Turkish and Korean often start with the place and leave out "there is." Accept word order at first, but insist on There's.
  • Missing articles: "There is bed" (Russian, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, many Slavic learners). Recast with "a."
  • in / on: Spanish and Portuguese en / em is both in and on, so "The lamp is in the table." Use the realia: inside the box vs. on top.
  • th /ð/: "dere is" / "zere is" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, German, Russian, Arabic, Chinese). Show the tongue once; don't stop the fluency task for it.
