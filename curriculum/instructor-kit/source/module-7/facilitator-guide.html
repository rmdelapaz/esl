Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 7 — Out in the World

Three classes that take learners out of the classroom and into the places they go every week: the kitchen and the grocery store, the café, and the clothing store on a rainy day. Learners sort food into "can count / can't count," order politely with I'd like…, and buy a jacket in the right size, at the right price, for the weather.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 CEFR A1 90 min per class

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The stage plan is the clock. The notes under it are the content: Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs), a drill script, instruction-checking questions (ICQs), differentiation, and L1 watch-outs.
  • Same rhythm every class: warmer → lead-in → clarify (M/F/P + CCQs) → drill (choral → individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs / groups) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • This is a role-play module. Each class ends with a real-world simulation: a kitchen information gap (C20), a classroom café (C21), and a clothing store (C22). The props are in materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html. Print and cut them before Week 10.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework (each has 🔊 Listen buttons). The classroom is for speaking.
  • Teal = hands-on / role-play, amber = watch-outs, dashed boxes = lines to say out loud.
The through-line

One journey through a normal week: check the kitchen → go to the café → go shopping. The grammar travels with the learner. In C20, some is for positives and any for negatives and questions. In C21, the polite exception appears: Would you like some milk? In C22, this / these and How much is it? finish the shopping trip. Every class recycles numbers and prices from Modules 1 and 4, so learners leave able to handle real money in English.

American English in the classroom

This kit teaches American English. The online lessons use British words first, with the US word in brackets (bill / check, trousers / pants, jumper / sweater, take away / to go, £ prices). Teach the US words in class, and accept both every time. Say it plainly: "Both are correct. In the US we say check. In the UK they say bill."

Before the Module

  • Print (card stock if you can): picture flashcards, fridge A/B cards (C20), café menus, order pads and customer cards (C21), price tags and shopper cards (C22), and the Module 7 food bingo cards.
  • Realia helps a lot at A1: an empty milk carton, a bag of rice, a few pieces of plastic fruit, paper cups, play money (or paper bills you draw), a scarf, a hat, a pair of gloves, an umbrella. Real objects beat pictures for low-literacy learners.
  • Food and culture: keep examples inclusive. Avoid pork and alcohol in your own examples (the course uses chicken, fish, and beef as "meat"). Some learners fast at certain times of year. Food talk is about words, not what anyone must eat.
  • Money and prices: use dollars in class. Keep prices small and realistic ($2.50 coffee, $35 jacket). Learners on a tight budget should never feel judged. The shopper cards give everyone the same invented budget.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Countable / uncountable. Meaning: countable = separate things you can count one by one. Uncountable = one mass, no plural, no a/an. Form: countable has singular/plural (apple → apples). Uncountable takes is, never are. Trick: containers make it countable: a glass of water, a cup of coffee, a bottle of milk, a piece of bread.

(Hold rice.) Can I count this? One, two, three…? (No.) So do we say "rices"? (No.) Do we say "a rice"? (No.)
(Hold two apples.) Can I count these? (Yes.) One apple, two…? (Apples.)
Milk. Can you count it? (No.) So: a glass of milk, or two glasses of milk? (Both OK! We count the glasses.)

some / any. Meaning: a number or amount; we don't say exactly how much. Form: (+) some, (−) and (?) any, with uncountable nouns and plural countable nouns. With one countable thing, use a / an. Pronunciation: some is weak in a sentence: /səm/, "There's s'm milk." Link is there any → "iz-ther-EN-ee."

"There isn't any bread." Is there bread? (No.) Is it zero or a little? (Zero.)
"There's some milk." Do I know how much milk? (No, not exactly.) Is there milk? (Yes.)
"Is there ___ juice?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Some or any? (Any.)

How much / how many. Meaning: both ask about amount. Form: how much + uncountable, how many + plural countable. The -s on the noun is the clue. A lot of = a big amount, with both. Not much + uncountable, not many + countable. Pronunciation: a lot of → "uh-LOT-uv."

"How many eggs?" Can I count eggs? (Yes.) So much or many? (Many.)
"There isn't much milk." Is there a lot of milk? (No.) Is there zero milk? (No, a little.)
Drill script (choral → individual)
  1. Model ×2, class repeats ×2: "There's some milk." → "There are some eggs." → "There isn't any bread." → "Is there any juice?" (rising at the end).
  2. Back-chain the long one: "…juice?" → "…any juice?" → "Is there any juice?"
  3. Substitution drill (flashcards): Show rice + ✅ → learners: "There's some rice." Show apples + ❌ → "There aren't any apples." Show cheese + ❓ → "Is there any cheese?"
  4. Individual: go around the room fast, one card each. Praise, then re-model any error and have the learner repeat. Don't explain again.
  5. Question chain: "How many eggs are there?" → the next learner answers "There are a lot of eggs," then asks the next person a new question.
Freer practice — The Fridge (information gap)

Pairs sit face to face, cards hidden. A's fridge and B's fridge each have 10 foods, and 6 are different. Ask with Is there any…? / Are there any…? / How much…? / How many…? Circle the differences. Then agree on a shopping list of 5 things. Model with a strong learner first.

ICQs: Can you show your card to your partner? (No.) Do you ask questions or tell? (Ask.) Is milk "Is there" or "Are there"? (Is there.) What do you write at the end? (A shopping list.)

Monitor for: Are there any milk?; There aren't some eggs; How much eggs?; two breads / rices. Write them down for the feedback stage. Don't stop the talk.

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: learners work from the picture cards only. Give them a strip with the two frames already written: "Is there any ___?" / "Are there any ___?" They point at the picture and say the word. In Ex 1, give them the 6 words with pictures and let them sort picture cards into two piles before writing C/U.
  • Stretch: add containers (two bottles of milk, a kilo of rice, a piece of cake) and quantities to the shopping list. Ask them to explain the rule to a partner in their own words.
L1 watch-outs
  • Uncountable in English, countable in L1. Many languages count bread, fruit, or information (Spanish panes, French des fruits). Expect breads, fruits, rices. Correct gently and point to the NO column.
  • No plurals / no articles (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese; Russian and Arabic have no a/an): expect I want apple, there is egg. Drill an egg / some eggs as fixed chunks.
  • Final -s dropped (Vietnamese, Chinese, Thai): eggs sounds like egg, so learners can't tell countable plurals apart when they listen. Exaggerate the -s in the drill: "eggzzz."
  • Some ≠ Spanish/Portuguese algunos in negatives. No hay algunos is wrong in Spanish too, but learners still produce There aren't some. Use the board rule: (−) and (?) = any.
Homework

Online lesson rays-esl.netlify.app/curriculum/lesson_19.html (🔊 Listen to the new words and the Nadia & Ben dialogue). Do the kitchen project: say 6 sentences about your kitchen out loud. Workbook 7.1: "My new words" + the 2-sentence journal.

Check — quick quiz

Answers: Q1 correct sentence = B) There's some rice. (no plural, no a with rice); Q2 "How ___ eggs?" = B) many (eggs has -s, so countable); Q3 (new) uncountable word = C) rice.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

I'd like… / Can I have…? Meaning: I want, but polite. Form: I'd like + a / an / some + noun (I'd like a tea). I'd = I would. Can I have + noun + please? Pronunciation: the /d/ in I'd is small but important: "I'd like" /aɪd laɪk/. Intonation: I'd like… falls ↘ (a statement). Can I have…? rises ↗. Please is warm, not sharp.

"I'd like a coffee." Is this the same as "I like coffee"? (No!) Which one is ordering now? (I'd like.) Which one is about every day? (I like.)
"I want a coffee." "I'd like a coffee, please." Same meaning? (Yes.) Which is more polite? (I'd like… please.)

Would you like…? Meaning: a polite offer: "Do you want…?" Form: Would you like + some / a / anything + noun? Answers: Yes, please. / No, thank you. (never just silence). Pronunciation: "Would you" links → "wuh-joo."

"Would you like some milk?" Is the server asking or offering? (Offering.) Some or any here? (Some. It's a polite offer.)

Prices. $4.50 = "four fifty" or "four dollars and fifty cents." Stress: fifTEEN (15) vs FIFty (50). How much is that? (never How much it is?).

Drill script (choral → individual)
  1. Back-chain: "…please." → "…coffee, please." → "…a coffee, please." → "I'd like a coffee, please." Tap the table on 'd so learners hear it.
  2. Question: "…please?" ↗ → "…a muffin, please?" → "Can I have a muffin, please?"
  3. Substitution: show a flashcard; learners order it: cake → I'd like a piece of cake, please. water → Can I have a bottle of water, please?
  4. Offer / answer pairs: you: "Would you like some sugar?" Half the class: "Yes, please." Other half: "No, thank you." Swap.
  5. Individual: fast round with a flashcard for each learner. Re-model; don't re-explain.
Freer practice — The Classroom Café

Set up 3–4 tables. Each has a menu and a server with an order pad and play money for change. Customers pick a customer card (e.g., "You are hungry. You have $10. You don't drink coffee."). The server greets, takes the order, offers something (Would you like…?), gives the price, and takes the money. After 7 minutes: swap servers and cards. Customers can visit a different table.

ICQs: Customers, what do you say first? (I'd like… please.) Servers, what do you write? (The order.) Can you say "I want"? (Better: I'd like.) When the server offers, what do you answer? (Yes, please / No, thank you.) When do we swap? (When I ring the bell / clap.)

Monitor for: I like a coffee (missing 'd), missing please, How much it is?, silence after an offer, fifteen / fifty confusion on the order pad.

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: use a picture menu (the flashcards on the table) and let the learner point + say "A coffee, please." That is a full, successful order. Servers with low literacy tick pictures on the order pad instead of writing words. Prices can be shown with play money only.
  • Stretch: give stretch customers a "problem card": "They bring the wrong drink" → Excuse me, I'd like a tea, not a coffee. Or they order for two people: We'd like two coffees and a table for two.
L1 watch-outs
  • "I like a coffee." The biggest error in this lesson, across all L1s. The 'd disappears, and so does the meaning. Tap the table on it in every drill.
  • Direct forms from L1. Spanish Me da un café, Arabic ʿaṭīnī, Russian Дайте кофе are polite at home but become Give me a coffee in English, which sounds rude. Say it kindly: "In your language it's polite. In English, I'd like or Can I have."
  • Flat intonation (Arabic, Russian, some Chinese speakers) can sound unfriendly in English. Model a lift on please and a smile. Keep it light. It's about sounding friendly, not about accent.
  • Final /d/ dropped (Vietnamese, Cantonese, Thai): I'd → I. Link it forward: "I-dlike."
  • Tipping culture varies. "Keep the change" is optional. Some learners come from places where tipping is rude, others where it's expected. Teach the phrase, don't teach the custom as a rule.
Homework

Online lesson rays-esl.netlify.app/curriculum/lesson_20.html: 🔊 listen and repeat the café conversation 3 times (slow → normal → friendly). Note: the online lesson uses £ and bill / take away. Tell learners it's British English, and both are fine. Workbook 7.2: journal (your own café conversation).

Check — quick quiz

Answers: Q1 most polite = C) I'd like a coffee, please.; Q2 you don't want more = B) No, thank you. That's all.; Q3 (new) "Would you like some milk?" and you want milk = A) Yes, please.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

this / these. Meaning: the thing(s) near me, often while pointing. Form: this + singular noun + is; these + plural noun + are. Adjective before the noun (this warm coat) or after be (this coat is warm). Pair clothes are always plural: these jeans, these pants, a pair of shoes. Pronunciation: this /ðɪs/ short vowel, /s/ · these /ðiːz/ long vowel, /z/. Smile wide for these.

(Hold one glove.) This or these? (This.) (Hold two.) This or these? (These.)
"These jeans are nice." How many jeans? One pair or two? (One pair.) So why "these"? (Jeans are always plural.)

Shopping phrases. Teach as chunks, not grammar. Try it on: the little word goes in the middle (try it on, not try on it). I'll take it = I want to buy it (don't teach will yet).

"Can I try it on?" Do I buy it now? (No, not yet.) Where do I go? (The changing room / fitting room.)
"It's too big." Is it good for me? (No.) What do I ask for? (A small / a smaller size.)

Weather. What's the weather like? (like here does not mean "love"). Answer: It's + adjective (sunny, cloudy, windy, cold, hot, warm) or It's + -ing (raining, snowing). Always start with It's. Wear a coat when it's cold: imperative + when.

"What's the weather like?" Am I asking "Do you like the weather?" (No.) I'm asking: how is the weather? (Yes.)
"It's raining." Is it raining now? (Yes.) Do I need an umbrella? (Yes!)
Drill script (choral → individual)
  1. Minimal pair: "this — these, this — these." Learners hold up 1 or 2 fingers when you say one of them at random.
  2. Substitution: you show a clothing card + an adjective card: shoes + nice → "These shoes are nice." jacket + expensive → "This jacket is expensive."
  3. Back-chain: "…on?" → "…try it on?" → "Can I try it on?" (link: try-i-ton).
  4. Weather chain: show a weather card → class: "It's snowing." → one learner: "Wear boots when it's snowing."
  5. Individual: each learner points at something a classmate is wearing: "I like these shoes!" / "I like this sweater!" Adults love a compliment round, and it's great for the mood.
Freer practice — The Clothing Store

Stores: 3–4 "shops" with 6–8 price-tagged items each (tags show item, sizes S/M/L, price). Shoppers take a shopper card: "Next week: rainy and cold. You need 2 things. Your size: medium. You have $60." Shoppers greet, ask, try on, say too big / too small when the tag's size doesn't match, ask the price, and buy. Assistants use: Can I help you? · What size are you? · Here you are. · It's $25.

ICQs: Shoppers, how much money do you have? (Look at your card: $60.) Can you buy everything? (No, only what you need.) What do you ask before you buy? (Can I try it on? How much is it?) Assistants, do you help or buy? (Help.)

Monitor for: this shoes, a pant / a jean, Can I try on it?, It's rain, Is cold (missing It's).

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: shopper cards have pictures (umbrella, coat) and a price number only. The learner can buy with two phrases: "How much is it?" and "I'll take it." In Ex 2, weather emojis are the cue; learners copy the word from the word bank.
  • Stretch: assistants recommend things: These boots are warm. Wear them when it's snowing. Shoppers compare two items: This coat is nice, but it's expensive. This jacket is cheap.
L1 watch-outs
  • Dropped dummy subject (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Chinese): Is cold. Is raining. English always needs It's. Hold up an "IT" card and point to it every time.
  • this / these vowel (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Japanese, Vietnamese have no /ɪ/–/iː/ contrast): this and these sound the same. Use the finger drill. Accept the /z/ ending as the clue.
  • /ð/ in this / these / clothes / weather: often dis / dese or zis. Fine for A1 if meaning is clear. Show the tongue between the teeth once and move on. Clothes is one syllable /kloʊz/.
  • p / b (Arabic): pants → bants. Hold a paper strip in front of your mouth: it moves for /p/.
  • "a pant / a jean" (Spanish un pantalón, French un jean): singular in L1, always plural in English.
