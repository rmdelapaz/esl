Module 1 — First Words & Meeting People

The first three classes of the course: welcoming a brand-new group, the classroom language they need to survive, the alphabet for learners with very different literacy backgrounds, greetings and introductions, and numbers, age and personal information, handled with care.

Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 3 × 90 min CEFR A1

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block below = one 90-minute class. The stage plan is the clock. The notes under it are what you say and what to watch for.
  • Every class follows the same rhythm: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation (with CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs) → feedback → homework. Learners come to trust the routine, and that lowers anxiety.
  • Dashed "Say:" boxes are lines to say out loud, usually concept-checking questions (CCQs) or instruction-checking questions (ICQs). Teal "Read aloud" scripts are in materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html too, so you can read from a printed sheet.
  • The online lesson is the homework. Each lesson has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear every word again at home. Show them the site on a phone in C1.
  • Teal = hands-on / pair work, amber = watch-outs and L1 notes.
The through-line

Module 1 answers one question: "Who are you?" By the end of C3 every learner can walk up to a stranger, say hello, give a name and spell it, and give a phone number and email slowly and clearly. These are the exact exchanges of a clinic front desk, a job center, a school office, or a first day at work. Each class adds one layer: letters (C1) → names and greetings (C2) → numbers and details (C3). By C3 the whole first-meeting conversation is in place.

Personal information: a trauma-informed default

From the very first class, learners are asked for personal details. For many adults (refugees, asylum seekers, people without papers, survivors of violence) these questions can feel unsafe. Make this a standing rule, said out loud in C1: in any "about you" task, a learner may use a new identity card (fictional name, age, phone, email; printed in the classroom materials). Age is always optional. Never ask for a real address, immigration status, or documents in class. Nobody has to explain why they choose a card.

Before Class 1

  • Print: the Module 1 workbook pages (one per learner), the name tents, the new-identity cards, the six classroom-language phrase cards (to tape on the wall for all 24 classes), the classroom-object picture flashcards, and the C1 spelling cards (A/B). All are in materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html.
  • Bring: thick markers for name tents, a pack of index cards, a few mini-whiteboards or scrap paper, tape, and a phone or speaker for the online lesson's 🔊 audio.
  • Room: chairs in a horseshoe or small tables of 3–4, so everyone can see the board and each other. Save front seats for anyone with low vision or hearing.
  • Learn about your group from the intake form if you have one: first languages, and whether each person reads their own language and a Latin-script language. This tells you who needs the literacy route in C1 (see Lesson 1.1).
  • Open the slides slides/module-1-first-words.html and the online course (rays-esl.netlify.app/curriculum/) on the classroom screen.

The first-class routine
  1. At the door. Smile, shake hands or nod (follow the learner's lead; not everyone shakes hands across genders), and say "Hello! I'm ___." Point to yourself. Most learners will copy you: that is the whole of Lesson 1.2 in miniature.
  2. Name tents. A folded card on the desk, name facing the room. Model yours first: "My name is ___. Please call me ___." Learners write the name they want to be called. Offer the new-identity cards quietly and without fuss: "You can use your name, or a new name. Both are OK." For learners who cannot yet write their name in Latin letters, write it for them lightly in pencil and let them trace it over in marker.
  3. Say their names right. Ask each person to say their name, repeat it, and ask "Is that right?" Do not shorten or anglicize a name unless the learner offers a short name. Getting names right is the single biggest trust-builder you have in C1.
  4. Seating and pairs. Seat people with a different first language next to each other where possible, so English is the shared language. Put a confident reader beside a learner on the literacy route, as a quiet helper, not a translator. Tell the class you will change partners often: "New class, new partner."
  5. What the course is. Keep it to one slide and five minutes. The only must-knows: when class is, the workbook stays with them, the online course is homework, and mistakes are welcome here.
    In this class, mistakes are OK. Mistakes are how we learn. Everybody here is new to English.
  6. The wall. Tape the six classroom-language cards to the wall where everyone can see them. Point to them all course, every time a learner needs one.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation: the alphabet

Letter names vs letter sounds. A letter has a name (B = "bee"), used for spelling out loud, and a sound (B = /b/ in book), used for reading. This lesson teaches names, because the goal is spelling. Learners on the literacy route also need sounds (see the two routes below). Do not mix both on the board for everyone. It overloads.

Teach the names in sound groups, not A to Z. Letters that rhyme are easier to learn together, and the groups show learners why E and I, or G and J, are different:

Two routes for a mixed-literacy class (last 5 min of the alphabet stage)

Route 1: Latin-script readers (they already read Spanish, French, Portuguese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Swahili…). They know the shapes. They need the English names, and to unlearn their L1 names (see the watch-outs). Pairs: one points at letters on the workbook alphabet chart at random, the other says the name. Then they spell three words from the wall to each other.

Route 2: new to Latin script or new to reading (Arabic, Dari/Farsi, Pashto, Amharic, Tigrinya, Chinese, Burmese, Nepali readers, or learners with little schooling). Start with the letters of their own name only. Work on shape, direction (left → right; this matters for Arabic and Farsi readers) and one sound per letter with a picture: B /b/ book · D /d/ door · P /p/ pen · W /w/ window. Trace, then copy. 4–6 letters is a great first day.

Route 2 learners join the rest of the class for spelling: they spell their own name (the letters they practiced) instead of new words, and in the dictation game they hold and point to the picture card. Nobody sits out. Watch for b/d/p/q reversals; give them a mnemonic ("b has a belly, d has a door handle") rather than a correction.

Spelling out loud & ICQs

Model the dialogue with a confident learner (or the slide). Then pairs: A reads, B reads; swap. Then B uses their own (or new-identity) name.

A: Hello! What's your name?

B: My name is Anna.

A: How do you spell it?

B: A – N – N – A. Anna.

A: And your last name?

B: Costa. C – O – S – T – A.

A: Thank you!

ICQs before "Spell yourself" (mingle):

Do you sit or stand? (stand) · How many people? (four) · Do you write the names? (yes) · Can you use a new name? (yes)
Game — "Spell It, Find It" (spelling dictation, 12 min)
  1. Stick the 9 classroom-object picture cards on the board, without words. Teams of 3–4, each with a mini-whiteboard or paper.
  2. Spell a word from the script, clearly, once: "p – e – n." Teams write it, then one runner touches the right picture. First correct team gets a point. Learners may say "Can you repeat, please?" (you repeat, no penalty).
  3. Round 2: learners take your role. One volunteer from each team spells a word to the other teams.
  4. Finish with workbook Ex 4 (listen and write), using the second half of the script.

Mixed literacy: in every team, the Route 2 learner is the "finder" who touches the picture when the team's word is written; readers write. Stretch: spell names from the class (from the name tents) instead of objects.

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: Route 2 above; the copy-your-name line in workbook Ex 1; only the letters of their name in Ex 2; a copy of the alphabet chart on the desk all class; pair with a patient reader.
  • Stretch: spell a street name, a city, or an email address; add What does ___ mean? and Can you help me, please?; be the teacher in the dictation game.
L1 watch-outs
  • Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Tagalog speakers: the vowel letter names shift. Their e sounds like English A, and their i sounds like English E. Expect "ee" for I and "ay/eh" for E. Drill E/I with the lesson's tip: "email is E, I am I."
  • French speakers: G and J are exactly reversed (French G ≈ English J, French J ≈ English G). Also Spanish G/J cause confusion. Drill G "jee" / J "jay" as a pair.
  • Arabic speakers: P and B (Arabic has no /p/). Hand on throat: B buzzes, P puffs. Put a paper strip in front of the lips for P. Also reading direction for learners new to Latin script.
  • Japanese and Korean speakers: L "el" vs R "ar". Spanish, Japanese and Korean speakers: B/V. Show teeth on lip for V.
  • Vietnamese, Chinese speakers: final consonants drop in ef, el, em, en, ess, eks, so F sounds like "e". Exaggerate the end. Some Chinese speakers add a vowel ("ef-u").
  • H: "aych" (US). Some learners say "hash" or "ahche" from their L1 names; accept the attempt, model "aych."
Feedback (delayed error correction)

During the mingle, note 2–3 errors on a card. Don't stop the activity. At the end write them on the board without names: e.g., G – I – N – A said "jay – ee…", or "Can you repeat?" without please. Ask the class: "Right or wrong? How do we say it?" Then drill the correct form once.

  • Workbook 1.1: Ex 5 (write about you), the checklist, "My new words," and the journal (why do you want to learn English? any language is fine).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation: greetings

Meaning. Hello and Hi work any time (Hi is informal). The three Good… greetings follow the clock. Good night and Goodbye are for leaving.

It's 9 a.m. I arrive at work. Good morning or good evening? (good morning)
It's 8 p.m. I arrive at a party. Good evening or good night? (good evening) … It's 11 p.m. I go home. Now? (good night)
"Good night": hello or goodbye? (goodbye!)
Hi: for my friend or for my boss? (my friend) Hello? (both)

Pronunciation. Good morning is two words with one stress: good MORning. Link Nice to meet you → "nice-tuh-MEE-chuh" (don't teach the spelling of that, just let them hear it). Rising intonation on And you?; falling on What's your name? (wh-questions fall).

Clock game (3 min): everyone stands. Call a time and a place: "7 a.m., the bus!" Learners greet a neighbor with the right phrase. "11 p.m., leaving!" → "Good night!" Anyone who says Good night on arrival sits down, then gets back up next round.

Formal vs informal (culture note)

Formal: boss, doctor, teacher, older person, stranger → Good morning + title + last name (Mr. Lee, Ms. Park), often a handshake. Informal: friends, classmates → Hi + first name. Ms. ("miz") is the safe title for any woman. When unsure, be a little more formal.

"Ms. Park": first name or last name? (last name) · "Mr. David"? Right or wrong? (wrong: Mr. Lee, or just David)

Tell the class what you want to be called. Many learners will call you "Teacher," which is polite in their cultures; accept it warmly. Don't correct it as an error.

Freer practice: meeting cards & ICQs

Each card gives a time, a place and who you are (e.g., A: 9 a.m., new job, meet your boss Mr. Diaz / B: You are Mr. Diaz). Pairs act it out: greet → name → nice to meet you → how are you → goodbye. Swap cards with the next pair. Last round: stand up and meet 3 classmates; write their names in workbook Ex 5.

Do you read the card to your partner? (no, just look and speak) · First you say…? (a greeting) · Last you say…? (goodbye) · Formal card: first name or Mr./Ms.? (Mr./Ms.)
Differentiation
  • Literacy support: the clock pictures (☀️ / 🌙) on the greeting cards carry the meaning; learners can do the freer practice from memory without reading the card (you whisper the situation); workbook Ex 1 as circling, not writing.
  • Stretch: add See you tomorrow · Please, come in · Excuse me; introduce a partner to a third person ("This is Rosa."); play the formal card with a title and handshake.
L1 watch-outs
  • "Good night" as hello: Spanish buenas noches, Portuguese boa noite, Tagalog magandang gabi, Italian buonasera/buonanotte confusions: many languages use one phrase for evening hello and goodbye. Expect it. The clock game fixes it.
  • Arabic, Chinese, Russian, many others: dropping am/is: "I Ana," "My name Ana," "What your name?" Point at the missing word with a gesture (a finger in the gap) and let them self-correct.
  • Title + first name ("Ms. Maria," "Mr. David") is common for Spanish, Tagalog, South Asian and West African speakers, where it's polite. Teach title + last name as the US norm, gently.
  • "How are you?" as a real question: some learners answer at length or with health news. Teach it as a greeting ritual; the answer is short.
  • Apostrophes: "Im," "Whats." Also you're vs your. Point at the missing letter.
Feedback (delayed error correction)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation: numbers

0–20. Learners know the concepts, just not the words ("You already count every day. Today: new words, same numbers."). The hard sounds: three (tongue between the teeth, not "tree"), twelve (one syllable), thirteen (th + -TEEN).

-teen vs -ty: it's all stress. fourTEEN /ˌfɔːrˈtiːn/ vs FORty /ˈfɔːrti/. Also, American English often says the t in forty, thirty like a quick /d/ ("FOR-dee"), and the -teen keeps a clear, long /t/. Give a hand signal: hand up on the stressed syllable.

(say "fourTEEN") Loud at the start or the end? (the end) So, 14 or 40? (14)
I'm not sure: 15 or 50? What can I ask? ("Fifteen, one-five? Or fifty, five-zero?")

Tens and spelling traps: forty (no u), eighty (one t), ninety (keeps the e), fifty (not fivety), thirty (not threety). Form of 21–99: ten + one with a hyphen: twenty-one, forty-eight.

Drill: pairs chorally (13–30, 14–40 … 19–90) with the hand signal, then random individuals. Pair game: A says one number of a pair, B points to the card (13 or 30). Swap.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation: phone numbers and email

Phone numbers are said one digit at a time, in groups, with a small pause: (702) 555-0148 = "seven – oh – two … five – five – five … oh – one – four – eight." 0 is often oh; a repeated digit can be double (double five). Email: @ = at, . = dot; spell anything unclear: "ben, B – E – N, at mail dot com."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. To be links a person to a fact about them: who they are, where they are from, how they feel. Show it on the board with an equals sign: Sofia = Mexican → Sofia is Mexican.

She is from Japan. Is Japan her country? (Yes.) Is "from Japan" about now, yesterday, or always? (Now / in general.) She is Japanese. Is "Japanese" a country or a person? (A person — a nationality.)
I'm not from Spain. Am I from Spain? (No.) Is "Yes, I'm." OK? (No. "Yes, I am.")

Form. Three groups: am (I) · is (he, she, it, one person's name) · are (you, we, they). Negative: add not (isn't, aren't, I'm not; never "amn't"). Question: swap subject and verb (Is she…? Are you…?). Short answers: never contract a positive (Yes, I am.), but negatives may contract (No, I'm not. No, he isn't.). Country after from; nationality with no from.

Pronunciation. Contractions are one beat: /aɪm/ /hiːz/ /ʃiːz/ /ðeə(r)/. Where are you from? falls at the end ↘; yes/no questions rise ↗ (Are you from Brazil? ↗). Stress on nationalities moves: BRAzil → BraZILian, ITaly → ITALian, Egypt → EGYPtian, CHIna → ChiNESE, JaPAN → JapaNESE. Clap it.

Drill script
  1. The beat (choral). T claps a slow 3-beat rhythm: "I am · he-she-it IS · you-we-they ARE." ×3, getting faster. Half the room / the other half / women / men / everybody.
  2. Substitution (choral → individual). T says a subject, learners say the sentence: "She… from Mexico." → "She's from Mexico." Cue: they → They're from Mexico. I → I'm from Mexico. Leo → Leo's from Mexico. Point to individuals after 3–4 choral rounds.
  3. Question chain. T → S1: "Are you from Brazil?" S1: "No, I'm not." (or Yes, I am.) S1 → S2 with any country. Keep it moving. Any true or imagined answer is fine.
  4. Stress drill. Hold up two fingers per syllable; learners clap and say: Japan – Japanese, Italy – Italian, Brazil – Brazilian.
Freer practice: world-map mingle (15 min)
  1. Choose (3 min). Each learner puts one sticky dot on the map on any country they like: their home, a country they love, or a country they want to visit. Hand out identity cards to anyone who wants one. Say it lightly: "You can be you, or you can be a new person today."
  2. Mingle (8 min). Stand up, meet 5 people: Hi, I'm ___. Where are you from? — I'm from ___. I'm ___. — Nice to meet you. Then one yes/no question: Are you ___? Learners write each name and country in the workbook speaking grid.
  3. Report (4 min). Back at tables, learners say two sentences about someone else: "This is Amira. She's from Canada. She's Canadian." Now the third-person is gets practiced.

ICQs before you start: "Do you have to say your real country?" (No, any country.) "How many people do you meet?" (Five.) "Do you sit or stand?" (Stand.) "What do you write?" (Name and country.)

Fast finishers: the 2.1 information-gap cards. A and B each know half of a table of four people; they ask Where is Kenji from? Is he Japanese? to complete it.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. This / these = near me (I can touch it). That / those = far from me (over there). A / an = one, any one. Plural = more than one.

(Holding a pen.) This is a pen. Is it near me or far? (Near.) One pen or many? (One.)
(Pointing to windows across the room.) Those are windows. Near or far? (Far.) One or many? (Many.) Can I say "those window"? (No — those windows.)
An apple. A pen. Why "an"? (Apple starts with a vowel sound.) Can I say "a pens"? (No. "A" means one.)

Form. this / that + singular noun + is; these / those + plural noun + are. a before a consonant sound, an before a vowel sound (it's about the sound: a university, an hour; mention only if asked). Plurals: add -s; add -es after s, x, ch, sh; consonant + y → -ies (but vowel + y → -s: keys, boys). Answers: It's a… (one) · They're… (many).

Pronunciation. this /ðɪs/ (short, hissing s) vs these /ðiːz/ (long "ee", buzzing z). Stretch your lips into a smile for these. th /ð/: tongue between the teeth, voice on; learners may say dis, dat, zis. Let them touch their tongue tip. a is weak /ə/. an links: an‿apple /ə‿næpl/. Plural endings: /s/ after voiceless sounds (books, cups), /z/ after voiced sounds (pens, keys), and an extra syllable /ɪz/ after s, x, ch, sh (box-es, watch-es, bus-es). Count syllables on fingers.

Drill script
  1. Article drill (choral → individual). T holds up an object; class says it with the article: a cup · an apple · a key · an orange · an umbrella · a book. Speed up. Then T says a bare noun, individuals add the article.
  2. Near / far drill. T stands next to the desk and touches an object: learners chorus "This is a cup." T walks to the back of the room and points at it: "That is a cup." Same with a group of pens: These are pens. / Those are pens.
  3. Question-and-answer chain. S1 holds an object: "What's this?" S2: "It's a key." S2 picks up two things: "What are these?" S3: "They're pens." Pass the objects on.
  4. Plural syllable drill. T says the singular, learners say the plural and show the syllables on fingers: box (1) → boxes (2); pen (1) → pens (1).
Freer practice: mystery bag & "What's on the desk?" (15 min)
  1. Mystery bag (5 min). Groups of 4–5. One learner closes their eyes, puts a hand in the bag, and feels one object. The others ask: "Is this a key?" The feeler guesses: "Yes, it is!" or "No, it isn't. It's a…" Pull it out to check. Pass the bag. (If you have only one bag, run it as a whole class with volunteers.)
  2. Information gap (10 min). Pairs, A and B cards (materials). Each card shows a desk of nine numbered things; three are a "?". A asks about B's objects, and B asks about A's: "Number 2. What are these?" — "They're keys." "Number 4. What's this?" — "It's an umbrella." They write the missing words with a / an or a plural. Then compare the cards.

ICQs: "Can you look in the bag?" (No, eyes closed.) "Who asks the question, the feeler or the group?" (The group.) In the info gap: "Can you show your card to your partner?" (No, not until the end.) "Do you write or only speak?" (Both. Speak, then write the word.)

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: work from the objects and picture flashcards, not the workbook text. Give a copying card: a pen · an apple · a book · an egg. Do workbook Ex 1 (items 1–4) and Ex 3 (items 1–3) only. In the info gap, they point and say the word; the partner writes it.
  • Stretch: introduce an hour / a university and let them explain the "sound, not spelling" rule. Add colors from their own knowledge (This is a red cup). Ask about a partner's bag: "What's that in your bag?" Irregular plurals as a bonus: man → men, child → children.
L1 watch-outs
  • No articles (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese, many Slavic and African languages): *It's pen. *This is book. Tap the table twice for "missing word". Expect this for months, and praise every correct a.
  • No plural -s (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai): *three pen, *These are key. Vietnamese speakers may know the plural but not pronounce the final -s. Exaggerate the hiss.
  • this / these sound the same (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Italian, French): the short/long vowel contrast is new. Do the smile trick.
  • *This are books / *These is (all L1s): match one ↔ one, many ↔ many.
  • Near/far systems differ. Spanish, Japanese, and Korean have three demonstratives, for example (este / ese / aquel). English has only two, and "that" covers both of the far ones.
  • th → d / z / s (most L1s). Accept intelligible versions and don't over-correct at A1. Model and move on.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A possessive adjective shows who something belongs to, or who a person is connected to. It matches the owner, never the thing.

Tom has a sister. His sister or her sister? (His.) Why "his"? The sister is a woman! (Because Tom is the owner. Tom is a man.)
Anna's brother. Who is the brother connected to, Anna or someone else? (Anna.) Is Anna the brother? (No.)
(Pointing to the tree.) Rosa is Carlos's mother. Is Carlos Rosa's son? (Yes.) Is Rosa older or younger? (Older.)

Form. possessive + noun (my mother, never *mother my or *the mother of me). It does not change for plural (my brother / my brothers). Owner + 's + thing (Tom's phone); plural owner ending in -s → s' (my parents' house). have with I / you / we / they; has with he / she / it and one name. Watch the look-alikes: your / you're, its / it's.

Pronunciation. 's follows the plural rules from Class 5: Pat's /s/, Tom's /z/, Rose's /ɪz/ (extra syllable). his /hɪz/ (short) vs he's /hiːz/ (long). daughter /ˈdɔːtər/, and the "gh" is silent. American aunt /ænt/ sounds like "ant" (/ɑːnt/ is also heard). mom (US) / mum (UK): this is an American course, so model mom. their sounds like there.

Drill script
  1. Pairs drill (choral). T shows a flashcard and says half; class completes: mother → father, sister → brother, son → daughter, aunt → uncle.
  2. Pronoun → possessive (choral → individual). T: "I" → "my." "she" → "her." "they" → "their." Fast, mixed order.
  3. His/her substitution. T points to a man in the tree: "… mother is Rosa." → "His mother is Rosa." Points to a woman → "Her mother is Rosa." Then T points at real people in the room with real objects: "his bag, her pen, their books."
  4. 's chain. S1 picks up S2's object: "This is Lina's phone." Hand it back with "Here you are." "Thank you."
Freer practice: family trees (17 min)
  1. The Rivera family information gap (7 min). Pairs, A and B cards. The same family tree, but A and B have different names missing. They ask "Who is Carlos's wife?" "What's Luis's sister's name?" and write the missing names. Check by comparing.

No one says which one they chose.

  4. Present (5 min). Pairs, then swap partners once: "This is my sister. Her name is… She has two children." Partner asks one question: "Who's this?" Listener then tells the class one fact: "Omar's brother is a driver."

L1 watch-outs
  • he / she, his / her mix-ups (Tagalog, Chinese, Vietnamese, Turkish, Persian, Finnish, Hungarian, Yoruba, Somali and others with one pronoun for both): *My mother, he is a nurse. *Anna and his brother. This persists for a long time. Gesture (♂/♀ card) rather than stop the speaker.

If a learner shares a loss, a simple "I'm sorry" and moving on is respectful. Don't turn it into a class topic.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The present simple is for habits and routines: things we do again and again. It is not for "right now." Point at the time line: every day, every week.

"I get up at seven." Is this only today? (No.) Is it every day? (Yes.) Is it a habit? (Yes.)
"I don't work on Sunday." Do I work on Sunday? (No.) On Monday? (Yes. Probably!)
"Do you get up early?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Can you answer "Yes, I do"? (Yes.) Do you say "Yes, I get up"? (No.)

Form. Three lines on the board, with the subject and verb in different colors:

PositiveI / You / We / They + base verbWe study English every day.
NegativeI / You / We / They + don't + base verbThey don't work on Sunday.
QuestionDo + I / you / we / they + base verb…?Do you cook dinner?
Short answerYes, I do. / No, I don't.(don't say the verb again)

Pronunciation. don't is one syllable with a strong o sound: /doʊnt/. Learners often say "do not" slowly. Model the natural form. In questions, Do you is weak and fast, like /də jə/: "D'you get up early?" Model it both ways. Accept the slow form in production. The stress is on the main verb: "Do you COOK?" Short answers are stressed: "Yes, I DO." "No, I DON'T."

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the model sentence: "at seven" → "get up at seven" → "I get up at seven." Choral ×3, then half the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Substitution drill (point at a flashcard, then a subject word): T: "We… 🚌" → S: "We go to work." T: "They… 📺" → S: "They watch TV." Speed up when they are ready.
  3. Negative transformation: T: "I work on Sunday." (shake head) → S: "I don't work on Sunday."
  4. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 asks S2 across the room "Do you get up early?" → S2: "Yes, I do. / No, I don't." Then all pairs together, 1 minute.

Time — meaning. Past = minutes after the hour (right side of the clock). To = minutes before the next hour (left side).

3:40. Is it before four or after four? (Before.) So is it "twenty past four" or "twenty to four"? (Twenty to four.) Can you also say "three forty"? (Yes!)
"Half past seven." Is it 6:30 or 7:30? (7:30.)

He / she — meaning. Same habit meaning as last class, now about another person.

"Lina gets up at six thirty." Is this about me? (No.) About Lina, one person? (Yes.) Every day? (Yes.) What is the extra letter? (-s.)
"She doesn't work on Sunday." Does Lina work on Sunday? (No.) Where is the -s now? (On "does." Doesn't.) So "work" or "works"? (Work.)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. There is / there are says that something exists in a place. We use it for new information: "There's a cat in my kitchen!", not "A cat is in my kitchen."

"There is a bed in my room." How many beds? (One.) "There are two chairs." One chair? (No, two.) So one = is, two or more = are.
"There isn't a TV." Is there a TV? (No.) "Are there any shops near the school?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Answer with the same words: (Yes, there are. / No, there aren't.)
"There is some water." Can you count water, one water, two water? (No.) So is or are? (Is.)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. can = able to do it (a skill or ability). can't = not able to. Use your mime: swim well, guitar badly.

"I can swim." Am I able to swim? (Yes.) Am I swimming now? (No — we don't know.) Is it a skill? (Yes.)
"She can't drive." Can she drive a car? (No.) Is it possible she can learn? (Yes — "can't" is today.)

Form. subject + can / can't + plain verb. One form for every person. Three traps (write them and cross them out): She cans swim · I can to swim · He can swims. Question: Can + subject + verb? Short answers: Yes, I can. / No, I can't.

Pronunciation. In a sentence, can is weak and short /kən/: "I c'n SWIM." The main verb gets the stress. Can't is strong /kænt/: "I CAN'T SWIM." In American English the final t is often not clear, so the vowel and stress carry the difference. In short answers, can is strong: "Yes, I CAN."

Like. like / don't like / love / hate + thing. Question with do: Do you like tea? Answer with do, not like and not can.

"I love chocolate." Do I like it a little or a lot? (A lot.) "I hate onions." Do I want onions on my pizza? (No!)
"Do you like tea?" What's the short answer — "Yes, I can" or "Yes, I do"? (Yes, I do.) Why? (The question has "do".)
Drill script

  4. Individual: nominate around the room, quick pace, praise, then back to choral if three people in a row struggle.
  5. Question chain: S1 asks S2 "Can you ride a bike?" S2 answers truthfully and asks S3 a new question.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Something is happening at this moment, while we speak. It started and it is not finished yet.

"I'm writing." Am I writing now? (Yes.) Did I finish? (No.) Is it every day? (We don't know / not important.)
"I work in a bank. Today I'm working at home." Where do I work every day? (In a bank.) Where am I now? (At home.)

Pronunciation. Contractions in speech: I'm, she's, they're. The ending /ɪŋ/: back of the tongue up, no hard "g." Model sing vs sin. In the question, intonation rises: Are you ↗ working?

Pre-task language — Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Price questions. How much = the price. One thing → is it → It's…. Two or more → are they → They're…. The whole bill → How much is that altogether?

(Hold up one apple.) How much is it or how much are they? (Is it.) (Hold up shoes.) (Are they.) "How many" or "how much" for a price? (How much.)

Polite requests. I'd like… = I would like. Polite, not childish. Can I have…, please? is also polite.

"I'd like a coffee." Is it the same as "I like coffee"? (No — "I'd like" is now, a request. "I like" is always, a feeling.)

Prices. $4.50 = "four dollars fifty" or "four fifty." $13 vs $30: stress thirTEEN vs THIRty. receipt — the p is silent.

Directions (imperative). Plain verb, no you: Go straight on. Turn left. On your left / right (not in). Opposite = on the other side of the street; show it with two hands facing each other.

"It's opposite the bank." Is it next to the bank? (No.) Do I cross the street? (Yes.) "Take the second left." Do I turn at the first street? (No — the second.)

  • Write the class names in the checklist before class. Aim to observe every learner at least once across Tasks 1–3. Stand to the side; don't hover or stare at the clipboard. For anxious learners, listen from a distance.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Plurals — meaning. Hold up one pen, then two pens.

One pen or more than one? (One.) And now? (More than one, two.) So we add… (s!)

Form. Rule 1: most nouns + -s. Rule 2: after s, x, ch, sh + -es (buses, boxes, watches, dishes). Rule 3: consonant + y → -ies (city → cities), but vowel + y → just -s (day → days, key → keys). Rule 4: some -f/-fe → -ves (knife → knives, leaf → leaves). Irregulars: teach as whole words with the word cards. people = the plural of person.

One child, two… (children). One person, two… (people). Is "persons" OK? (No, people.)

Pronunciation. At A1 focus on one thing: the extra syllable /ɪz/ after s, x, ch, sh. Clap it: bus (1 clap) → bus-es (2 claps), box-es, watch-es. Don't teach /s/ vs /z/ yet; just model naturally.

Colors — form. Colors are adjectives. Question: What color is it? Answer: It's blue. / It's black and white. We teach gray (American); grey (British) is also correct and appears in the online lesson.

Adjective + noun — meaning & form. Two rules: (1) the adjective goes before the noun, (2) it never takes -s.

"A car red": OK? (No.) "A red car"? (Yes.) Two cars: "two reds cars" or "two red cars"? (Two red cars.) Which word gets the s? (Cars.)

Two adjectives. Light version: opinion → size → color + noun. Most of the time one adjective is perfect.

"A nice big red bag." Which word is my feeling? (Nice.) Which word is the size? (Big.) Where is the color? (Last, next to "bag.")
Drill script

  3. Substitution: say "a red car," then give a cue: "two" → "two red cars" · "blue" → "two blue cars" · "small" → "two small blue cars."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. have got = have, for things we own and how people look. It is not the past tense of get.

"I've got an umbrella." Is the umbrella mine? (Yes.) Is it now or yesterday? (Now.) "I have an umbrella." Same or different? (Same.)
"She's got long hair." Is her hair long now? (Yes.) Is it "She is got"? (No, she HAS got.)

Form. I / you / we / they + have got ('ve got). He / she / it + has got ('s got). Negative: haven't got / hasn't got. Question: Have / Has + subject + got…? Short answer: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't. No got in short answers.

American English note

The online lesson teaches have got, which is common in British English and everyday speech. In the US, people more often say I have… / Do you have…? / I don't have…. Both are correct. Teach have got (it's on the homework), but model and accept "Do you have a pen?" / "Yes, I do." Learners in the US will hear the have / do form at work and at the doctor, so point it out once and move on.

People. Build it like the lesson: has got + (length) + (type/color) + hair: "She's got long straight blond hair." hair is uncountable here: long hair, not hairs. Use is for tall/short: "He's tall" (= he is).

"He's tall." Is it "he has" or "he is"? (He is.) "He's got glasses." Has or is? (Has.) How do you know? (Got!)

Pronunciation. Contractions are the natural spoken form: I've got /aɪv gɒt/ (the /v/ is short but there), she's got. Short answers are stressed: "Yes, I HAVE." "No, she HASn't." Watch /v/ in have / haven't (Spanish b/v).

  3. Describing: show slide 16 people. T: "Grandfather — hair?" Ss: "He's got short gray hair." T: "Glasses?" Ss: "Yes, he's got glasses." T: "Grandmother — glasses?" Ss: "No, she hasn't."

Module 7 — Out in the World

Three classes that take learners out of the classroom and into the places they go every week: the kitchen and the grocery store, the café, and the clothing store on a rainy day. Learners sort food into "can count / can't count," order politely with I'd like…, and buy a jacket in the right size, at the right price, for the weather.

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. The stage plan is the clock. The notes under it are the content: Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs), a drill script, instruction-checking questions (ICQs), differentiation, and L1 watch-outs.

  • Teal = hands-on / role-play, amber = watch-outs, dashed boxes = lines to say out loud.
The through-line

One journey through a normal week: check the kitchen → go to the café → go shopping. The grammar travels with the learner. In C20, some is for positives and any for negatives and questions. In C21, the polite exception appears: Would you like some milk? In C22, this / these and How much is it? finish the shopping trip. Every class recycles numbers and prices from Modules 1 and 4, so learners leave able to handle real money in English.

American English in the classroom

This kit teaches American English. The online lessons use British words first, with the US word in brackets (bill / check, trousers / pants, jumper / sweater, take away / to go, £ prices). Teach the US words in class, and accept both every time. Say it plainly: "Both are correct. In the US we say check. In the UK they say bill."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Countable / uncountable. Meaning: countable = separate things you can count one by one. Uncountable = one mass, no plural, no a/an. Form: countable has singular/plural (apple → apples). Uncountable takes is, never are. Trick: containers make it countable: a glass of water, a cup of coffee, a bottle of milk, a piece of bread.

(Hold rice.) Can I count this? One, two, three…? (No.) So do we say "rices"? (No.) Do we say "a rice"? (No.)
(Hold two apples.) Can I count these? (Yes.) One apple, two…? (Apples.)
Milk. Can you count it? (No.) So: a glass of milk, or two glasses of milk? (Both OK! We count the glasses.)

some / any. Meaning: a number or amount; we don't say exactly how much. Form: (+) some, (−) and (?) any, with uncountable nouns and plural countable nouns. With one countable thing, use a / an. Pronunciation: some is weak in a sentence: /səm/, "There's s'm milk." Link is there any → "iz-ther-EN-ee."

"There isn't any bread." Is there bread? (No.) Is it zero or a little? (Zero.)
"There's some milk." Do I know how much milk? (No, not exactly.) Is there milk? (Yes.)
"Is there ___ juice?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Some or any? (Any.)

How much / how many. Meaning: both ask about amount. Form: how much + uncountable, how many + plural countable. The -s on the noun is the clue. A lot of = a big amount, with both. Not much + uncountable, not many + countable. Pronunciation: a lot of → "uh-LOT-uv."

"How many eggs?" Can I count eggs? (Yes.) So much or many? (Many.)
"There isn't much milk." Is there a lot of milk? (No.) Is there zero milk? (No, a little.)
Drill script (choral → individual)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

I'd like… / Can I have…? Meaning: I want, but polite. Form: I'd like + a / an / some + noun (I'd like a tea). I'd = I would. Can I have + noun + please? Pronunciation: the /d/ in I'd is small but important: "I'd like" /aɪd laɪk/. Intonation: I'd like… falls ↘ (a statement). Can I have…? rises ↗. Please is warm, not sharp.

"I'd like a coffee." Is this the same as "I like coffee"? (No!) Which one is ordering now? (I'd like.) Which one is about every day? (I like.)
"I want a coffee." "I'd like a coffee, please." Same meaning? (Yes.) Which is more polite? (I'd like… please.)

Would you like…? Meaning: a polite offer: "Do you want…?" Form: Would you like + some / a / anything + noun? Answers: Yes, please. / No, thank you. (never just silence). Pronunciation: "Would you" links → "wuh-joo."

"Would you like some milk?" Is the server asking or offering? (Offering.) Some or any here? (Some. It's a polite offer.)

Prices. $4.50 = "four fifty" or "four dollars and fifty cents." Stress: fifTEEN (15) vs FIFty (50). How much is that? (never How much it is?).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

this / these. Meaning: the thing(s) near me, often while pointing. Form: this + singular noun + is; these + plural noun + are. Adjective before the noun (this warm coat) or after be (this coat is warm). Pair clothes are always plural: these jeans, these pants, a pair of shoes. Pronunciation: this /ðɪs/ short vowel, /s/ · these /ðiːz/ long vowel, /z/. Smile wide for these.

(Hold one glove.) This or these? (This.) (Hold two.) This or these? (These.)
"These jeans are nice." How many jeans? One pair or two? (One pair.) So why "these"? (Jeans are always plural.)

Shopping phrases. Teach as chunks, not grammar. Try it on: the little word goes in the middle (try it on, not try on it). I'll take it = I want to buy it (don't teach will yet).

"Can I try it on?" Do I buy it now? (No, not yet.) Where do I go? (The changing room / fitting room.)
"It's too big." Is it good for me? (No.) What do I ask for? (A small / a smaller size.)

Weather. What's the weather like? (like here does not mean "love"). Answer: It's + adjective (sunny, cloudy, windy, cold, hot, warm) or It's + -ing (raining, snowing). Always start with It's. Wear a coat when it's cold: imperative + when.

"What's the weather like?" Am I asking "Do you like the weather?" (No.) I'm asking: how is the weather? (Yes.)
"It's raining." Is it raining now? (Yes.) Do I need an umbrella? (Yes!)
Drill script (choral → individual)
  1. Minimal pair: "this — these, this — these." Learners hold up 1 or 2 fingers when you say one of them at random.
  2. Substitution: you show a clothing card + an adjective card: shoes + nice → "These shoes are nice." jacket + expensive → "This jacket is expensive."
  3. Back-chain: "…on?" → "…try it on?" → "Can I try it on?" (link: try-i-ton).
  4. Weather chain: show a weather card → class: "It's snowing." → one learner: "Wear boots when it's snowing."
  5. Individual: each learner points at something a classmate is wearing: "I like these shoes!" / "I like this sweater!" Adults love a compliment round, and it's great for the mood.
Freer practice — The Clothing Store

Stores: 3–4 "shops" with 6–8 price-tagged items each (tags show item, sizes S/M/L, price). Shoppers take a shopper card: "Next week: rainy and cold. You need 2 things. Your size: medium. You have $60." Shoppers greet, ask, try on, say too big / too small when the tag's size doesn't match, ask the price, and buy. Assistants use: Can I help you? · What size are you? · Here you are. · It's $25.

ICQs: Shoppers, how much money do you have? (Look at your card: $60.) Can you buy everything? (No, only what you need.) What do you ask before you buy? (Can I try it on? How much is it?) Assistants, do you help or buy? (Help.)

Monitor for: this shoes, a pant / a jean, Can I try on it?, It's rain, Is cold (missing It's).

Differentiation
  • Literacy support: shopper cards have pictures (umbrella, coat) and a price number only. The learner can buy with two phrases: "How much is it?" and "I'll take it." In Ex 2, weather emojis are the cue; learners copy the word from the word bank.
  • Stretch: assistants recommend things: These boots are warm. Wear them when it's snowing. Shoppers compare two items: This coat is nice, but it's expensive. This jacket is cheap.
L1 watch-outs
  • Dropped dummy subject (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Chinese): Is cold. Is raining. English always needs It's. Hold up an "IT" card and point to it every time.
  • this / these vowel (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Japanese, Vietnamese have no /ɪ/–/iː/ contrast): this and these sound the same. Use the finger drill. Accept the /z/ ending as the clue.
  • /ð/ in this / these / clothes / weather: often dis / dese or zis. Fine for A1 if meaning is clear. Show the tongue between the teeth once and move on. Clothes is one syllable /kloʊz/.
  • p / b (Arabic): pants → bants. Hold a paper strip in front of your mouth: it moves for /p/.
  • "a pant / a jean" (Spanish un pantalón, French un jean): singular in L1, always plural in English.

Module 8 — Bringing It All Together

The last teaching class of the course, and the dress rehearsal for the final assessment. No new grammar system: learners use everything from Modules 1–7 in two real-world tasks. First a visit to the doctor's office (booking an appointment, describing a problem, understanding advice), then a "My Week" talk that joins the present simple and going to. You observe, take notes, and plan C24.

1 lesson · review project Lesson 8.1 Class C23 (+ C24 preview) Week 12 CEFR A1 90 min

How to Use This Guide

  • C23 is task-based. The shape is pre-task (the language learners need, taught fast) → task (they do it with a partner or group) → report (a few share) → language focus (you fix the errors you noticed). You run this cycle twice: the doctor's office, then My Week.
  • New language is small and practical: body parts, I have / I've got a headache, My back hurts, I feel tired, and You should / shouldn't for advice. Everything else is review.
  • While learners do the two tasks, you observe with the checklist (in materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html). Your notes tell you who is ready for the C24 speaking assessment, who needs a gentler role-play card, and what to review in the C24 warm-up.
  • This is also an emotional class: learners realize the course is nearly over. Name their progress out loud. Many adults have never had a teacher tell them "You did this."
  • Teal = tasks, amber = watch-outs and sensitivity notes, dashed boxes = lines to say out loud.
The through-line

Use it all → show what you can do → celebrate. The doctor's office recycles I'd like… (7.2), times and days (3.2, 6.3), spelling your name (1.1), and How often? (6.1). My Week recycles daily routines (3.1), frequency adverbs (6.1), likes (6.2), and going to plans (6.3). C23 practices exactly the kind of speaking C24 will check, so nothing in the final class is a surprise.

Sensitivity: health is private

Learners never need to talk about their real health. Every patient in this class is a character on a role card with invented symptoms. Never ask "Are you sick?" or "What's wrong with you?" Ask "What's wrong with Sam?" (the card). Some learners have had frightening medical experiences, live with illness or disability, or come from places where clinics were dangerous. If someone volunteers real health information, thank them warmly, don't ask follow-up questions, and offer to talk privately after class, or point them to your program's support staff. Mime pain lightly and with humor, never realistically. Say at the start: "Today we are actors. Nobody talks about their real body."

My Week is personal too. Some learners are unemployed, in shelters, waiting for asylum decisions, or caring for sick family. Offer the "new identity" week cards (a ready-made invented week) to everyone, with no reason needed. Say: "You can talk about your week or a card's week. Both are great practice."

Before the Class

  • Print: body-parts flashcards, patient / receptionist / doctor role cards (one set per group of 3), My Week planners (in the workbook) and new-identity week cards, the Find-Someone-Who sheet, and two copies of the observation checklist (one for the doctor task, one for My Week, or one combined).
  • Set up a "clinic": two chairs and a desk for the reception, and two chairs for the doctor's room in each corner. A clipboard and a toy stethoscope, if you have one, get a laugh and lower anxiety.
  • Know your C24 plan. Tell learners this class what C24 will look like (see "Looking Ahead" below). Check your program's final assessment materials before class so what you say matches them.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Health problems. Three fixed patterns. (1) I have a / I've got a + problem (headache, cold, cough, sore throat, temperature, stomachache). In the US, I have is most common. The online lesson teaches I've got. Both are correct. (2) My + body part + hurts (one) / hurt (two or more): My back hurts. My feet hurt. (3) I feel + adjective: sick, tired, dizzy, better. Pronunciation: headache /ˈhɛdeɪk/, "ache" = "ake." Stomach /ˈstʌmək/, "STUM-uk." Cough /kɔf/ rhymes with "off."

"I have a cold." Am I cold, like winter? (No.) Am I sick? (Yes, a little. Nose, throat.)
"My feet hurt." One foot or two? (Two.) So "hurt" or "hurts"? (Hurt.)
"I feel tired." Is this a body part? (No.) It's how my whole body feels. (Yes.)

should / shouldn't. Meaning: advice, a good idea (not a rule or an order). Form: subject + should + base verb. Same for every person, no to, no -s: She should rest. Negative shouldn't. Question Should I take medicine? Pronunciation: the l is silent: /ʃʊd/ rhymes with "good." Shouldn't /ˈʃʊdnt/.

"You should rest." Is it a good idea or a rule? (A good idea.) Do I have to rest? (No, but it's a good idea.)
"You shouldn't go to work." Is going to work a good idea today? (No.)
"He should rests" or "He should rest"? (He should rest. No -s after should.)

Two time frames (review). Every week = present simple + frequency adverb (I usually work on Mondays; he works, with -s). Next week = be going to + verb (I'm going to visit my sister). The time word goes first, so the listener knows: usually, every day, on Saturdays vs. next week, tomorrow, on Friday night.

"Next week I'm going to start a class." Now or future? (Future.) Is it a plan? (Yes.)
"I usually cook on Sundays." One time or every week? (Every week.)
Drill script (choral → individual)
  1. Touch and say: "Head." (touch) → class: "head" … fast: "hand – foot – leg – stomach – back – throat." Then learners lead.
  2. Back-chain: "…ache." → "…headache." → "I have a headache." Then substitution with flashcards: stomach → I have a stomachache; throat → I have a sore throat; back → My back hurts.
  3. Problem → advice chain: you: "I have a cold." Class: "You should rest!" You: "I have a toothache." Class: "You should see a dentist!" Then learners give the problems.
  4. The appointment line: "…appointment." → "…make an appointment." → "I'd like to make an appointment." Say it with a smile on the phone (hand to ear).
  5. Individual: each learner picks a flashcard and says one problem sentence. The next learner gives advice. Re-model errors; don't explain again.
TASK 1 — The doctor's office (groups of 3)

Round: (1) The patient phones the receptionist, asks for an appointment, and spells their name. The receptionist offers a day and a time from their card. (2) The doctor asks What's the matter? The patient describes the problem from their card. The doctor asks one question (Does your stomach hurt? How do you feel?) and gives 2 pieces of advice from the doctor card. (3) The patient thanks the doctor. Then rotate roles and take a new patient card.

ICQs: Are these your real problems? (No! From the card.) Who talks first on the phone? (The receptionist: "Good morning, how can I help you?") How many pieces of advice does the doctor give? (Two.) When do you change roles? (When I clap.)

Observe for (checklist items 1–4): a clear problem sentence, understanding and using should, day + time with on / at, spelling a name, repair language (Sorry, can you say that again?).

TASK 2 — My Week (groups of 3)

Plan: the workbook planner, 4 sentences about a normal week (present simple + usually / always / often / sometimes / never) and 4 about next week (going to). Literacy support: draw it (see below). Tell: each speaker talks for 1–2 minutes without reading word by word. Glancing at notes is fine. Each listener asks one question. Then the next speaker.

ICQs: How many sentences? (Eight or more.) Every week: which grammar? (I usually work…) Next week? (I'm going to…) Listeners: what do you do at the end? (Ask one question.) Can you use a week card? (Yes, if you want.)

Observe for (checklist items 5–8): present simple with a frequency adverb, going to + verb, time words at the start, answering a follow-up question, and overall fluency and clarity.

The observation checklist → C24

Print it from the classroom materials. One row per learner, 8 can-do items, three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / didn't try. You won't see every learner do everything. That's fine. Aim to observe each learner in at least one task. After class, use the sheet to:

  • Choose C24 role-play cards: learners with mostly ✓ can take any card (café, store, or doctor). Learners with ~ / ○ on items 1–4 should get the role-play they did best today.
  • Plan the C24 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become the 10-minute review before the assessment starts.
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who didn't speak in Task 2 gets a quiet word at the end of C23: "You can do this. Next class is friendly, and I'll help you."
  • Don't grade C23. It's practice. Tell learners so. The checklist is for your planning, not their record.
Differentiation
  • Literacy support: patient cards have a picture (the flashcard) plus one sentence to say. The My Week planner can be pictures: learners draw or stick My Week picture prompts on a 7-day strip, then talk from the pictures. Receptionists with low literacy use the clock face on their card instead of written times. Accept 5–6 sentences instead of 8.
  • Stretch: patients ask a question back (How often should I take it?, recycling 6.1), and doctors add You should … for three days. In My Week, add a third frame with he / she: "My sister usually works on Saturdays. Next week she's going to…" (recycles the -s). Add a "because" to one plan.
L1 watch-outs
  • "I have cold" / "I am cold" (Spanish tengo frío = I'm cold, tengo un resfriado = I have a cold; Portuguese, French similar). English: I'm cold (temperature) vs I have a cold (sick). Draw a snowflake and a tissue on the board.
  • "It hurts me the head" (Spanish me duele la cabeza, Portuguese dói-me a cabeça, Italian mi fa male la testa). English uses the possessive: My head hurts.
  • "should to" / "should rests": all L1s, especially where modals take an infinitive marker (German, Russian). Drill should + verb as one chunk.
  • Dropped "be" in going to (Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese): I going to visit. Board: I'm going to, and tap the 'm.
  • he / she -s in the stretch task (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai: no verb endings; Tagalog, Chinese speakers may also mix he / she). Keep the -s on the board.
  • "Stomach" spelled as said ("stomak"), and ache pronounced "a-chay" (Spanish/Italian reading). Model /eɪk/.

Looking Ahead — C24: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C23 (slide 13)

C24 has two parts. First, a short, friendly final assessment: a one-to-one speaking check with you (a few questions about you and your week, and one role-play like today's: café, store, or doctor), plus the short listening / reading / writing tasks in the kit's final assessment. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next step, the A2 course.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You talk with me for about five minutes, like today. No surprises: it's everything we practiced. Then we celebrate!"

Reduce anxiety: show the course's first lesson on screen and ask, "Week 1: could you say 'I usually work on Mondays and next week I'm going to visit my family'?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them the online lessons stay open for review.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C24, great, and it recycles Module 7! Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite learners who want to to give their My Week talk to the whole group as a "graduation speech."

Look at your checklist and answer three questions: Which two can-dos need a C24 warm-up? Which learners need a quiet word of encouragement? Which role-play card will each learner get in C24? Write your answers on the back of the checklist.
