1.1 · The English Alphabet & First Sounds

Exercise 1 · Write the small letter

B → b · D → d · E → e · G → g · H → h · J → j · M → m · Q → q · R → r

Copy-your-name line: check the capital first letter and left-to-right order. Watch for b/d and q/p reversals from learners new to Latin script; point, don't mark wrong.

Exercise 2 · Listen and write the letter (script 1.1 A)

1 G · 2 J · 3 E · 4 I · 5 H · 6 W · 7 Y · 8 R

Capital or lowercase are both fine. Expect G↔J, E↔I and W↔Y swaps: that's the point of the item set. Replay those pairs.

Exercise 3 · Circle the vowels

book · name · hello · pen · desk · window · teacher

Learners often skip the silent e in name or one letter of oo / ea. All vowel letters count, silent or not. w is a consonant here.

Exercise 4 · Listen and write the word (script 1.1 B)

1 pen · 2 book · 3 desk · 4 door · 5 window · 6 teacher

Top errors: bok (missed "double o"), dor, tiecher (E/I confusion). Literacy route: accept a correct picture-point for any word they can't yet write.

Exercise 5 · Write about you

Open answer (real or new-identity name). Check: capital first letter in first and last name; the spelled line uses letters separated by dashes or spaces (e.g., A – N – N – A, or A – double N – A: both correct).

Speaking · Spell yourself

Open. Success = four names written correctly. The best evidence is that learners used Can you repeat, please? or Slowly, please at least once.

Quick check 1
Which are all vowels?
A) A, B, C
B) A, E, I
C) E, R, T
Why: the five main vowels are A, E, I, O, U. B, C, R and T are consonants.
Quick check 2
How do you spell "Bill"?
A) capital B – i – double l
B) capital B – double i – l
C) capital P – i – l – l
Why: a name starts with a capital; two L's together = "double l." (B has two i's; C starts with P, a common Arabic-speaker slip.)
Quick check 3 · new
The teacher speaks very fast. You say…
A) Slowly, please.
B) Goodbye.
C) Double L.
Why: classroom language for asking for help. "Can you repeat, please?" is also a good answer if a learner writes it.
Top errors to expect in 1.1

E said "ee"/I said "ee" (Spanish, Portuguese, Tagalog) · G and J swapped (French, Spanish) · P said as B (Arabic) · F/L/M/N/S/X losing the final sound (Vietnamese, Chinese) · spelling a double letter twice ("n – n") instead of "double n" (fine, both are understood) · forgetting "capital."

1 What's · 2 I'm · 3 name · 4 too · 5 And · 6 later

Exercise 4 · Write the short form

1 I'm · 2 What's · 3 you're · 4 isn't · 5 What's your name?

Mark the apostrophe: Im, Whats, youre are wrong. your for you're is the classic error.

Exercise 5 · Meet 3 people

Open. Check the sequence: greeting → name → Nice to meet you (too) → How are you? / And you? → goodbye. "Formal or informal" depends on the meeting card: boss / doctor / Mr. or Ms. cards = formal; classmate / neighbor cards = informal.

Quick check 1
You arrive at a party at 8:00 p.m. You say…
A) Good night
B) Good evening
C) Good morning
Why: you are arriving, so it's a greeting. "Good night" is only for leaving or going to bed.
Quick check 2
Short form of "What is your name?"
A) What's your name?
B) Whats your name?
C) You're your name?
Why: the apostrophe replaces the missing i of is. B has no apostrophe; C uses you're (= you are).
Quick check 3 · new
A person says "Nice to meet you." You say…
A) Good night.
B) I'm fine.
C) Nice to meet you, too.
Why: you give the same phrase back and add too. "I'm fine" answers "How are you?".
Top errors to expect in 1.2

"Good night!" on arrival · dropped be: "My name Rosa," "What your name?" · "Nice to meet you" without too in the reply · title + first name ("Ms. Maria") · missing apostrophes (Im, Whats) · your/you're · long, literal answers to "How are you?".

1.3 · Numbers, Age & Personal Information

Exercise 1 · Listen and circle (script 1.3 A)

1 30 · 2 14 · 3 50 · 4 16 · 5 70 · 6 18 · 7 90

If most of the class misses an item, say the pair again with exaggerated stress, then ask the class the confirmation question: "Sixteen, one-six? Or sixty, six-zero?"

Exercise 2 · Write the word or the number

15 = fifteen · 50 = fifty · 13 = thirteen · 40 = forty · 67 = sixty-seven · 84 = eighty-four

nineteen = 19 · ninety = 90 · thirty-two = 32 · seventy-seven = 77

Accept sixty seven without the hyphen at this level, but point it out. Not acceptable: fourty, fivety, threety, eightty.

Exercise 3 · Make the number

twenty-one · forty-eight · ninety-six · sixty-three

Exercise 4 · Listen and write (script 1.3 B)

1 (702) 555-0148 · 2 (702) 555-0163 · 3 (213) 555-0199 · 4 mei.chen@mail.com · 5 carlos27@mail.com

Any grouping or punctuation is fine (7025550148, 702-555-0148) if the digits are right. Top errors: 01 heard as O1 letter (fine: same thing on a phone), 0199 written as 199 (lost "oh"), meichen without the dot, carlos 27 with a space.

Quick check 1
You hear "four-TEEN." The number is…
A) 14
B) 40
C) 4
Why: -teen numbers are stressed at the end; -ty numbers (FORty) at the front.
Quick check 2
You are 30. You say…
A) I have 30 years.
B) I'm 30 years old.
C) It's 30 years.
Why: English uses be for age: I'm 30 / I'm 30 years old. "I have 30 years" is a translation from Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian and others.
Quick check 3 · new
Phone number 707-0155. You can say 0 as…
A) "at"
B) "oh"
C) "dot"
Why: in phone numbers, 0 is very often "oh" ("zero" is also fine). "At" and "dot" are for email: @ and the "." sign.
Top errors to expect in 1.3

"I have 30 years" · "I 30" / "She 25" (no be) · 13/30 … 19/90 confusion, both hearing and saying · "fourty" · "tree" for three · phone numbers said as big numbers ("five hundred fifty-five") · final-consonant drop in eight, five, six (Vietnamese) · "@" read as "a" or "arroba" · "I'm 25 years."

Marking at A1

Accept full forms or contractions anywhere, unless the exercise asks for one (she is not / she isn't / she's not are all correct). Capital letters on countries and nationalities count: mark them, but gently. For personal answers (write about you, family tree), there is no single answer; check the grammar, not the facts. A learner using a new identity is doing the task correctly.

  2. is
  3. are

  5. is
  6. Are (capital A)
  7. is

Items 7–8 are the new ones: a name = he/she → is; "You and Sofia" = you (plural) → are.

Exercise 2 · negatives
  1. She isn't from Japan. / She's not from Japan.
  2. They aren't American. / They're not American.
  3. I'm not from Spain. (only one contraction; never "I amn't")
  4. You aren't a student. / You're not a student.

Full forms (She is not…) are also correct.

Exercise 3 · match
1 Chinac Chinese
2 Spaine Spanish
3 Germanya German
4 the UKb British

Exercise 4 · word order
  1. Where are you from?
  2. She is from Japan. / She's from Japan.
  3. Is he Brazilian?
  4. I'm not from Italy.

Check the capital letter at the start, the capital on the country or nationality, and the ? on questions.

Exercise 5 · short answers

1 I am · 2 she isn't · 3 they are · 4 he isn't. Items 1 and 3 are the "no contraction in a positive short answer" rule. No, she's not would also be correct in item 2, but it isn't one of the options.

Speaking & writing tasks

World-map mingle grid / Write about you: answers vary. Model: I'm Ana. I'm from Mexico. I'm Mexican. / This is Omar. He's from Egypt. He's Egyptian. Check: am/is/are present; from + country; no from before a nationality; capitals. 2.1 information gap (materials): Kenji, Japan, Japanese · Amira, Egypt, Egyptian · Lucas, Brazil, Brazilian · Priya, India, Indian. A's card holds Kenji's and Lucas's details, B's card holds Amira's and Priya's.

Self-check Q1
Choose the correct sentence.
B) She is from Japan.
Why: she takes is (A is wrong), and after from we need the country, Japan, not the nationality (C is wrong).
Self-check Q2
"Are you Brazilian?" You say yes.
A) Yes, I am.
Why: never contract a positive short answer ("Yes, I'm" sounds unfinished), and I takes am, not are.
Self-check Q3 (new)
Which is correct?
C) I'm from Spain. I'm Spanish.
Why: from + country; I'm + nationality. A mixes them up; B drops the verb to be, the most common A1 error.
Top errors to expect · 2.1

*I from Brazil. (missing am) · *She are… / *They is… · *Yes, I'm. · *I'm from Spanish. / *I'm Spain. · *i'm mexican (lower case) · *I amn't · *Is he from Japan? — Yes, he's. · *Where you are from? (no inversion).

Exercise 3 · this / that / these / those
  1. This (near, one)
  2. Those (far, many)
  3. These (near, many)
  4. That (far, one)
  5. This (near, one)

Capital letter at the start of the sentence.

Exercise 4 · look and write
  1. They're keys. / They are keys.
  2. It's an umbrella.
  3. No, it isn't. It's a cup. (also: No, it's not.)
  4. Yes, they are.

Item 3: "No, it isn't." alone is acceptable; the correction is a bonus. Item 4: *Yes, they're is wrong (positive short answer).

Exercise 5 · circle

1 These (keys = many) · 2 is (one window) · 3 an (orange starts with a vowel sound) · 4 boxes (those = many).

Speaking & writing tasks

"What's on the desk?" information gap (materials): A's card is missing a notebook, keys, an egg; B's card is missing an umbrella, a cup, pencils. Both complete desks show: a cup, keys, a notebook, an umbrella, an egg, pencils, a phone, an apple, a book. Object tour: answers vary. Check that this/that go with is + singular + a/an, and that these/those go with are + plural and no a.

Self-check Q1
Which sentence is correct?
B) It's an apple.
Why: apple starts with a vowel sound → an. C is wrong because a/an means one, so it can't go with a plural.
Self-check Q2
Some keys are next to you.
C) These are keys.
Why: near + many = these, with are. Those is for far away; This is keys mixes one and many.
Self-check Q3 (new)
Three buses are far away, on the street.
A) Those are buses.
Why: far + many = those + are; bus ends in s → buses. B mixes one and many; C uses near and a wrong plural.
Top errors to expect · 2.2

*It's pen. (no article) · *a apple · *a pens · *This are books. / *These is… · *boxs, watchs · *babys / *keies · this and these pronounced the same · "that" for something in the learner's hand · plural -s not pronounced (three pen).

Family-tree speaking task

Answers vary, and a real, famous, or invented family is equally correct. Check: at least 4 people; 5 sentences; my, his, and her each used once, matched to the owner; one have/has; This is my… / These are my…. Model: This is my mother. Her name is Rosa. Self-check Q1
Anna has a brother.
B) her brother
Why: the owner (Anna) is a woman, so it's her. The word matches the owner, not the brother.
Self-check Q2
The house of two parents.
B) my parents' house
Why: the owner is plural and already ends in -s, so the apostrophe goes after the s. A = one parent. C mixes in you're (= you are).
Self-check Q3 (new)
Which sentence is correct?
C) She has two sons.
Why: he / she / it take has. B uses the possessive her as a subject; the subject is she.
Top errors to expect · 2.3

*Anna and his brother / *My mother, he is… (he/she mix-up) · *the mother of me · *mother my · *She have a brother. · *It's name is Rex. · *Your kind. (for You're kind) · *brother's Anna (owner second) · *my parent's house for two parents (writing only). Also: daughter pronounced with a hard gh.

Module 3 — Everyday Life: Answers

Marking principle

At A1, mark meaning first. A sentence with the right structure and a small spelling slip ("studys") is a success with a note, not a wrong answer. American English is the model; British forms (at the weekend, flat, lift, half seven meaning 7:30) are acceptable when a learner uses them.

Find someone who: report with the third-person -s: "Ahmed loves cooking." "Mei hates shopping." "Ana doesn't mind cleaning." Write about you: "I like to cook" is also correct. Accept it, and note the -ing form as the target.

Q1
Which sentence is correct?
B) I love cooking.
Why: after love/like/hate we use verb + -ing. A has no -ing, and C keeps the e ("danceing").
Q2
"Do you like these books?" — "Yes, I love ___."
B) them
Why: "these books" = many things → them. One thing → it. Him is for a man.
Q3
Which sentence is correct?
C) She loves dancing.
Why: two endings work together: she → loves, and the activity → dancing.
Top errors to expect (6.2)

"I love cook." · "I like danceing." · "I enjoy swiming." · "I like they." · "She love reading." · "Do you like cooking? — Yes, I like." (needs Yes, I do. or Yes, I like it.) · "I don't mind" misunderstood as negative. Check it with a CCQ.

Task 1 — Doctor's office: what success looks like

The patient (1) opens the call politely (I'd like to make an appointment), (2) accepts or changes a day/time using on + day / at + time, (3) spells their card name, (4) gives at least two problem sentences (I have a… / My … hurts / I feel…), and (5) shows they understood the advice (repeats it, or says OK, I should rest. Thank you). The doctor gives two should / shouldn't sentences. It doesn't need to be perfect: a learner who communicates the problem and the appointment with a few grammar slips has done the task.

Task 2 — My Week: model talk & success profile

Model (≈ 10 sentences): "Let me tell you about my week. I usually get up at six thirty. From Monday to Friday I work at a supermarket. In the evening I often cook dinner with my daughter. On Wednesday I'm going to meet my friend for coffee. And on Saturday I'm going to buy a warm coat, because it's cold now!"

Success at A1: 8+ sentences (5–6 with literacy support) · at least 2 frequency adverbs · at least 2 correct going to plans · time words that make the time frame clear · answers one listener question. Typical good-but-imperfect talk: occasional missing -s or missing 'm in I'm going to; this is fine if meaning is clear.
