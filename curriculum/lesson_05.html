🔎 Lesson 2.2: This & That, a/an & Everyday Objects

Look around you right now. A pen, a phone, a cup, some keys — the world is full of everyday objects, and today you'll learn to name them and point them out in English. You'll master this / that / these / those, choose between a and an, and turn one thing into many.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use this, that, these, and those correctly for near/far and one/many
  • Choose a or an before a noun by listening to its first sound
  • Make regular plural nouns (pen → pens, box → boxes, baby → babies)
  • Ask and answer "What's this?" and "What are these?" about real objects

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Point to 8 objects around you and describe each one out loud with "This is a…" or "These are…" — record yourself, or have a partner quiz you.

Pointing at the World

When you don't know a word, you can still communicate — just point and ask! "What's this?" is one of the most powerful questions a beginner has. It turns every object around you into a free English lesson: point at a thing, hear its name, and repeat it.

To point in English, you need two small tools. First, the demonstratives — this, that, these, those — which tell people which thing and how far away it is. Second, the articles — a and an — the tiny words that sit in front of a single object: a pen, an apple. They feel small, but they appear in almost every English sentence you will ever say.

🧠 Mindset

Little words like a, an, and this cause big worries for beginners — and that's okay. Native speakers use them without thinking, but they spent years absorbing them. You will make mistakes with articles for a long time, and people will still understand you perfectly. Communication first, perfection later. Every "an apple" you get right is a small win. Collect the wins. 🌱

This, That, These, Those

Demonstratives answer two questions at once: Is it near or far? and Is it one or many? That's it — just four words fill the whole grid.

Near me 👉Far from me 👉👉
One (singular)this — this penthat — that pen
Many (plural)these — these pensthose — those pens

Read the grid like a map. THIS and THESE are close — something you can touch. THAT and THOSE are far — across the room or over there. And the plural words both end in the same sound, which is a handy memory hook.

✅ Memory Trick

The words with the "s" letter hiding inside — these and those — are the plural (many) ones. These and those = more than one. This and that = just one.

Here they are in full sentences. Notice that singular uses is and plural uses are — the verb to be from the last lesson is back at work.

  • This is a book. (near, one)
  • That is a door. (far, one)
  • These are keys. (near, many)
  • Those are windows. (far, many)
💡 Note: After this/that we use a singular noun (this cup). After these/those we use a plural noun (these cups). The demonstrative and the noun always agree — both one, or both many.

The Articles: a / an

Before a single, countable object we use a small word called an indefinite article: a or an. It just means "one" of something not yet specific: a pen = one pen, any pen.

📖 The Simple Rule

Use a before a consonant sound: a pen, a book, a cup, a table.

Use an before a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u): an apple, an egg, an orange, an umbrella.

Why? Because "an" makes those vowel words easier to say. Try saying "a apple" — it bumps! "An apple" flows. 😊

Use a (consonant sound)Use an (vowel sound)
a penan apple
a bookan orange
a cupan umbrella
a bagan egg
a tablean idea

💡 It's about SOUND, not spelling

The rule follows the first sound, not the first letter. A few tricky words prove it:

  • a university — "university" starts with a "you" sound /juː/, which is a consonant sound, so we say a.
  • an hour — the "h" is silent, so it starts with a vowel sound /aʊ/, so we say an.
  • an honest man — silent "h" again → an.

Don't stress about these today. For 95% of words, "vowel letter → an, consonant letter → a" works. Just remember: when in doubt, listen to the first sound.

⚠️ Articles are for singular only

We say a pen (one) but never "a pens." Once a noun is plural, drop a/an: pens, three pens, some pens. The article a/an means "one," so it can't sit in front of "many."

One or Many: Plural Nouns

A plural noun means more than one. Most English plurals are easy: just add -s. A few groups need a small change so the word is still easy to say.

RuleWhenExamples
Add -smost nounspen → pens, book → books, key → keys
Add -esafter s, x, ch, shbus → buses, box → boxes, watch → watches, dish → dishes
Change y → iesconsonant + ybaby → babies, city → cities, story → stories

Why the -es group? Say "buss" quickly — you can't hear the extra s! Adding -es gives it an extra "iz" sound (bus-iz) so the plural is clear. The same reason lies behind boxes and watches.

✅ One small warning about "y"

The y → ies change only happens after a consonant (baby, city). If a vowel comes before the y, just add -s the normal way: key → keys, boy → boys, day → days. Never "keies."

💡 Remember: English also has a few "wild" plurals that break all rules — man → men, woman → women, child → children, foot → feet. We'll meet those later. For now, focus on the three regular rules above.

Ask & Answer

Now put it together. These are the exact question patterns you'll use to name any object. Keep them in your pocket — they work everywhere.

What's this? What's that? What are these? What are those? It's a phone. They're keys. Is this a pen? Yes, it is. No, it isn't.

💡 The pattern in one line

Singular: "What's this?" → "It's a phone."  (What's = What is · It's = It is)

Plural: "What are these?" → "They're keys."  (They're = They are)

Yes/No: "Is this a pen?" → "Yes, it is." / "No, it isn't. It's a pencil."

Listen to a whole object-guessing game. Read both parts out loud — it's the fastest way to make the patterns feel natural.

A: What's this?

B: It's a cup.

A: And what's that, over there?

B: That's a window.

A: Okay. What are these?

B: They're keys.

A: Is this an apple?

B: No, it isn't! It's an orange. 🍊

A: Ha! You're right. Thank you!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose a or an

Objective: Pick the correct article by the first sound.

Write a or an before each word.

  1. ___ book
  2. ___ apple
  3. ___ umbrella
  4. ___ chair
  5. ___ orange
  6. ___ phone
  7. ___ egg
  8. ___ key
✅ Answer

1. a book · 2. an apple · 3. an umbrella · 4. a chair · 5. an orange · 6. a phone · 7. an egg · 8. a key

The an words all begin with a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u).

🏋️ Exercise 2: One ↔ Many

Objective: Make the plural (or the singular) form.

Change each word. If it's singular, make it plural; if it's plural, write the singular.

  1. pen → ?
  2. box → ?
  3. watch → ?
  4. baby → ?
  5. bus → ?
  6. keys → ? (singular)
  7. cities → ? (singular)
  8. notebook → ?
✅ Answer

1. pen → pens · 2. box → boxes · 3. watch → watches · 4. baby → babies · 5. bus → buses · 6. keys → key · 7. cities → city · 8. notebook → notebooks

Remember: -es after s/x/ch/sh, and consonant + y → ies.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Choose this / that / these / those

Objective: Match the demonstrative to near/far and one/many.

Read the situation and choose the right word.

  1. A pen is in your hand. → "___ is a pen."
  2. Two chairs are across the room. → "___ are chairs."
  3. Some apples are right in front of you. → "___ are apples."
  4. A door is far away. → "___ is a door."
  5. A cup is next to you on the table. → "___ is a cup."
✅ Answer

1. This is a pen. (near, one) · 2. Those are chairs. (far, many) · 3. These are apples. (near, many) · 4. That is a door. (far, one) · 5. This is a cup. (near, one)

🎯 Your Project: "This is a…" Object Tour

Time to describe the real world around you. Do it out loud — to a partner, a class, or your phone's voice recorder.

  1. (3 min) Find 8 objects around you. Try to include some near and some far, and at least two plural groups (e.g. keys, books).
  2. (5 min) Point to each one and say a full sentence: "This is a cup." · "That is a window." · "These are keys." Use the correct a/an!
  3. (3 min) Now ask and answer about four of them: "What's this?" — "It's a book."
  4. (2 min) Partner option: have your partner point and ask "What's this?" / "What are these?" and you answer. Then swap.
    Solo option: record all 8 sentences, then listen back once.
  5. (2 min) Note any object whose name or article felt hard. Save it for your journal.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Listen to the first sound before choosing a/an, not just the first letter.
  • Match the demonstrative to the noun: this cup (one), these cups (many).
  • Practice with real objects at home — say them as you touch them.
  • Read the dialogues out loud until "What's this?" comes automatically.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "a" with a plural: ❌ a pens → ✅ pens or a pen. The article a/an means "one."
  • Wrong verb: ❌ This are a book → ✅ This is a book. Singular takes is; plural takes are (These are books).
  • Mixing near/far: pointing at something in your hand and saying "that". If you can touch it, it's this / these.
  • Forgetting -es: ❌ boxs, watchs → ✅ boxes, watches.

🌍 Culture & Register Note

In shops and daily life, pointing while you ask "What's this?" or "How much is this?" is completely normal and polite in English. If you don't know a word, don't be shy — point, ask, and you'll get your answer. Most people are happy to help a learner. 😊

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • This/that = one thing; these/those = many things. This/these = near, that/those = far.
  • Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound — it's about the sound, not the spelling.
  • Regular plurals: add -s; add -es after s/x/ch/sh; change consonant + y → ies.
  • Ask "What's this?" → "It's a phone." and "What are these?" → "They're keys."

Those are the exact building blocks of everyday English conversation — and you'll use them in almost every lesson from here on. Well done! 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When do I use "the" instead of "a/an"?

Great question! A/an means "one, not specific" (a pen = any pen). The means "the specific one we both know" (the pen on the table). We'll explore the in a later lesson. For now, a/an is your go-to for naming single objects.

Is it really "an hour" and not "a hour"?

Yes! The "h" in hour is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound ("our"). That's why we say an hour. Trust your ears: say the word and listen to the first sound.

What's the difference between "this" and "these" again?

This is for one thing (this book). These is for more than one (these books). A quick trick: these and those hide an "s" — like the "s" on plural nouns.
