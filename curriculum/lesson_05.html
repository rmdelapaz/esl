🔎 Lesson 2.2: This & That, a/an & Everyday Objects

Look around you now. A pen, a phone, a cup, some keys. There are many everyday things. Today you learn their names in English. You learn this / that / these / those. You choose a or an. You make one thing into many.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use this, that, these, and those for near/far and one/many
  • Choose a or an before a word. Listen to the first sound.
  • Make plural nouns (pen → pens, box → boxes, baby → babies)
  • Ask and answer "What's this?" and "What are these?" about real things

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Point to 8 things near you. Say each one out loud with "This is a…" or "These are…" Record yourself, or ask a partner to quiz you.

Pointing at the World

You don't know a word? You can still talk. Just point and ask! "What's this?" is a very useful question. You point at a thing. You hear its name. You say it again. Every thing near you is a free English lesson.

You need two small tools. First, the demonstratives: this, that, these, those. They tell people which thing. They also tell how far it is. Second, the articles: a and an. These little words go before one thing: a pen, an apple. They are small, but you use them in almost every sentence.

🧠 Mindset

Little words like a, an, and this worry many beginners. That's okay. Native speakers learned them over many years. You will make mistakes for a long time. That is normal, and people still understand you. Talk first, be perfect later. Every "an apple" you say right is a small win. Collect the wins. 🌱

This, That, These, Those

Demonstratives answer two questions. Is it near or far? and Is it one or many? Just four words do all the work.

Near me 👉Far from me 👉👉
One (singular)this — this penthat — that pen
Many (plural)these — these pensthose — those pens

Read the grid like a map. THIS and THESE are near — you can touch them. THAT and THOSE are far — over there. The two plural words end in the same sound. That helps you remember them.

✅ Memory Trick

The words with an "s" inside — these and those — are the plural (many) ones. These and those = more than one. This and that = just one.

Here they are in full sentences. One thing uses is. Many things use are. This is the verb to be from the last lesson.

  • This is a book. (near, one)
  • That is a door. (far, one)
  • These are keys. (near, many)
  • Those are windows. (far, many)
💡 Note: After this/that we use one thing (this cup). After these/those we use many things (these cups). Match them: both one, or both many.

The Articles: a / an

Before one thing, we use a small word: a or an. We call it an indefinite article. It means "one" — but not a special one: a pen = one pen, any pen.

📖 The Simple Rule

Use a before a consonant sound: a pen, a book, a cup, a table.

Use an before a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u): an apple, an egg, an orange, an umbrella.

Why? Because "an" makes vowel words easier to say. Try to say "a apple" — it is hard! "An apple" is easy. 😊

Use a (consonant sound)Use an (vowel sound)
a penan apple
a bookan orange
a cupan umbrella
a bagan egg
a tablean idea

💡 It's about SOUND, not spelling

The rule uses the first sound, not the first letter. Look at these words:

  • a university — "university" starts with a "you" sound /juː/. That is a consonant sound, so we say a.
  • an hour — the "h" is silent. The word starts with a vowel sound /aʊ/, so we say an.
  • an honest man — silent "h" again → an.

Don't worry about these today. For almost all words, "vowel letter → an, consonant letter → a" works. Not sure? Listen to the first sound.

⚠️ Articles are for singular only

We say a pen (one). We never say "a pens." For many things, do not use a/an: pens, three pens, some pens. a/an means "one." It can't go before "many."

One or Many: Plural Nouns

A plural noun means more than one. Most plurals are easy: just add -s. A few words need a small change. This keeps them easy to say.

RuleWhenExamples
Add -smost nounspen → pens, book → books, key → keys
Add -esafter s, x, ch, shbus → buses, box → boxes, watch → watches, dish → dishes
Change y → iesconsonant + ybaby → babies, city → cities, story → stories

Why the -es group? Say "buss" fast — you can't hear the extra s! -es adds an "iz" sound (bus-iz). Now the plural is clear. It is the same for boxes and watches.

✅ One small warning about "y"

The y → ies change happens only after a consonant (baby, city). Is there a vowel before the y? Then just add -s: key → keys, boy → boys, day → days. Never "keies."

💡 Remember: English also has a few "wild" plurals. They break the rules — man → men, woman → women, child → children, foot → feet. We learn those later. For now, use the three rules above.

Ask & Answer

Now put it together. Use these questions to name any thing. Remember them — they work everywhere.

What's this? What's that? What are these? What are those? It's a phone. They're keys. Is this a pen? Yes, it is. No, it isn't.

💡 The pattern in one line

Singular: "What's this?" → "It's a phone."  (What's = What is · It's = It is)

Plural: "What are these?" → "They're keys."  (They're = They are)

Yes/No: "Is this a pen?" → "Yes, it is." / "No, it isn't. It's a pencil."

Listen to an object-guessing game. Read both parts out loud. This is the fastest way to learn the patterns.

A: What's this?

B: It's a cup.

A: And what's that, over there?

B: That's a window.

A: Okay. What are these?

B: They're keys.

A: Is this an apple?

B: No, it isn't! It's an orange. 🍊

A: Ha! You're right. Thank you!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Choose a or an

Objective: Pick the right article. Use the first sound.

Write a or an before each word.

  1. ___ book
  2. ___ apple
  3. ___ umbrella
  4. ___ chair
  5. ___ orange
  6. ___ phone
  7. ___ egg
  8. ___ key
✅ Answer

1. a book · 2. an apple · 3. an umbrella · 4. a chair · 5. an orange · 6. a phone · 7. an egg · 8. a key

The an words all begin with a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u).

🏋️ Exercise 2: One ↔ Many

Objective: Make the plural (or the singular) form.

Change each word. One thing → make it many. Many → write the one.

  1. pen → ?
  2. box → ?
  3. watch → ?
  4. baby → ?
  5. bus → ?
  6. keys → ? (singular)
  7. cities → ? (singular)
  8. notebook → ?
✅ Answer

1. pen → pens · 2. box → boxes · 3. watch → watches · 4. baby → babies · 5. bus → buses · 6. keys → key · 7. cities → city · 8. notebook → notebooks

Remember: -es after s/x/ch/sh, and consonant + y → ies.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Choose this / that / these / those

Objective: Match the demonstrative to near/far and one/many.

Read each one and choose the right word.

  1. A pen is in your hand. → "___ is a pen."
  2. Two chairs are across the room. → "___ are chairs."
  3. Some apples are right in front of you. → "___ are apples."
  4. A door is far away. → "___ is a door."
  5. A cup is next to you on the table. → "___ is a cup."
✅ Answer

1. This is a pen. (near, one) · 2. Those are chairs. (far, many) · 3. These are apples. (near, many) · 4. That is a door. (far, one) · 5. This is a cup. (near, one)

🎯 Your Project: "This is a…" Object Tour

Now describe the world around you. Do it out loud — to a partner, a class, or your phone.

  1. (3 min) Find 8 things near you. Get some near and some far. Get at least two plural groups (e.g. keys, books).

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Listen to the first sound before you choose a/an. Not just the first letter.
  • Match the demonstrative to the noun: this cup (one), these cups (many).
  • Practice with real things at home. Say them as you touch them.
  • Read the dialogues out loud. Say "What's this?" until it is easy.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "a" with a plural: ❌ a pens → ✅ pens or a pen. The article a/an means "one."
  • Wrong verb: ❌ This are a book → ✅ This is a book. One thing takes is. Many things take are (These are books).
  • Near/far mix-up: You point at a thing in your hand and say "that". If you can touch it, use this / these.
  • Forgetting -es: ❌ boxs, watchs → ✅ boxes, watches.

🌍 Culture & Register Note

In shops and daily life, you can point and ask "What's this?" or "How much is this?" This is normal and polite in English. You don't know a word? Don't be shy. Point, ask, and you get your answer. Most people are happy to help a learner. 😊

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • This/that = one thing; these/those = many things. This/these = near, that/those = far.
  • Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound — it's about the sound, not the spelling.
  • Regular plurals: add -s; add -es after s/x/ch/sh; change consonant + y → ies.
  • Ask "What's this?" → "It's a phone." and "What are these?" → "They're keys."

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now go into any room and say what is in it. You can point at one thing or many. You can point near or far. You can choose the right article. You can turn one thing into a group. These are the building blocks of everyday English. You will use them in almost every lesson. Well done! 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When do I use "the" instead of "a/an"?

Great question! A/an means "one, any one" (a pen = any pen). The means "the special one we both know" (the pen on the table). We learn the in a later lesson. For now, use a/an to name one thing.

Is it really "an hour" and not "a hour"?

Yes! The "h" in hour is silent. The word starts with a vowel sound ("our"). That's why we say an hour. Trust your ears: say the word and listen to the first sound.

What's the difference between "this" and "these" again?

This is for one thing (this book). These is for many things (these books). A quick trick: these and those have an "s" — like the "s" on plural nouns.
