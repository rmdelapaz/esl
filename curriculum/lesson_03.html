🔢 Lesson 1.3: Numbers, Age & Personal Information

Numbers are everywhere — your age, your phone, your address, prices, times, and dates. In this lesson you'll count from 0 to 100, say how old you are the correct English way, and give your phone number and email out loud with confidence.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Say and read numbers 0–100 in English
  • Ask and answer "How old are you?" the correct way
  • Say a phone number one digit at a time
  • Give basic personal information — name, age, phone, and email

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Say aloud your age, your phone number (digit by digit), and spell your email address.

Why Numbers Matter

Think about your day. You check the time, you pay a price, you give someone your phone number, you fill in a form with your age and address. All of these are numbers. If you can count and say numbers clearly, you can handle a huge slice of real, everyday English.

The good news: English numbers follow simple patterns. Once you learn 0–20 and the tens, you can build every number up to a hundred like clicking two bricks together. Let's start counting. 🧱

🧠 Mindset

You already know numbers — you count in your own language every single day. You are not learning numbers from zero; you are just learning new words for ideas you already have. That's a much smaller job. Say them out loud, get a few wrong, laugh, and try again. Mistakes are just reps at the gym for your brain. 💪

💡 The tricky sounds

Three of these trip up almost everyone at first:

  • three /θriː/ — put your tongue lightly between your teeth for the "th", don't say "tree."
  • twelve /twɛlv/ — one word, not "twoelve."
  • thirteen /ˌθɜːrˈtiːn/ — "th" again, and the stress lands on the -teen.

⚠️ The classic trap: -teen vs. -ty

The numbers 13–19 end in -teen and the tens (30, 40, 50…) end in -ty. They sound almost the same, so listeners get confused all the time. The secret is stress — which part of the word is louder and longer:

  • fourTEEN (14) — stress on the end: /ˌfɔːrˈtiːn/
  • FORty (40) — stress on the front: /ˈfɔːrti/

Say the pair out loud, exaggerating the loud part: "four-TEEN … FOR-ty." When you're not sure what someone said, it's fine to ask: "Fourteen, one-four? Or forty, four-zero?"

⚠️ Spelling watch-out

forty has no "u". The number four has a "u" (f-o-u-r), but forty does not (f-o-r-t-y). This one catches native speakers too. Write it a few times: forty, forty, forty.

Building 21–99

Here's the magic pattern. To make any number between the tens, just say the ten first, then the single digit, joined with a hyphen when you write it:

📖 The pattern

tens word + - + ones word

21 = twenty + one = twenty-one
35 = thirty + five = thirty-five
99 = ninety + nine = ninety-nine

Talking About Age

One of the first questions people ask is how old you are. Here is the standard question and the two natural ways to answer.

AskAnswer (short)Answer (full)
How old are you?I'm 25.I'm 25 years old.
How old is he?He's 30.He's 30 years old.
How old is she?She's 19.She's 19 years old.

Notice that "I'm" is short for "I am," "He's" for "He is," and "She's" for "She is." You can stop after the number ("I'm 25") or add "years old" — both are completely correct and common.

⚠️ Very common mistake: use BE, not HAVE

In many languages you literally say "I have 25 years." In English this is wrong. English uses the verb to be:

  • ❌ I have 25 years.
  • ✅ I'm 25. / I'm 25 years old.

If you remember only one thing from this lesson, remember: for age, English says "I am…" not "I have…"

A: How old are you?

B: I'm 22. And you?

A: I'm 22 years old too! Same age.

B: Nice! And how old is your brother?

A: He's 30.

🌍 Culture note: In some cultures, asking someone's age directly can feel impolite — especially with older people or strangers. In casual English among friends and peers it's usually fine. If you're unsure, it's polite to let the other person share their age first, or simply skip the question.

Phone Numbers & Email

Phone numbers work differently from other numbers. You don't say them as big numbers — you say each digit one at a time.

💡 Two handy rules for phone numbers

  • Zero is very often said "oh" (like the letter O). So 0 = "oh".
  • When a digit repeats, you can say "double". So 77 = "double seven".

Example number: 077 8123 4567

Say it: "oh — double seven — eight — one — two — three — four — five — six — seven."

Say each digit slowly, with a small pause between groups. That makes it easy for the other person to write down.

Now the phrases you need to ask for and give a number:

What's your phone number? My number is… It's… Can you say that again? Slowly, please.

📧 Saying your email address

Email addresses use two special little words:

  • @ — say "at"
  • . — say "dot"

So anna@mail.com is said: "anna — at — mail — dot — com." If a letter might be unclear, spell it out (remember Lesson 1.1!): "A — double N — A, at mail dot com."

A: What's your phone number?

B: It's oh — seven — nine — double four — two — three — one — oh — five — six.

A: Slowly, please!

B: Sure. Oh — seven — nine … four — four … two — three — one … oh — five — six.

A: Got it. And your email?

B: It's ben, B — E — N, at mail dot com.

A: Thank you!

🗂️ Personal information word list

  • name — what people call you
  • age — how old you are
  • phone number — your telephone digits
  • email address — your address for messages
  • address — where you live
  • at — the @ sign
  • dot — the . in an email/website

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Numbers ↔ words

Objective: Drill the tricky -teen vs. -ty contrast.

Part A — Write these numerals as words:

  • 15
  • 50
  • 13
  • 40
  • 67
  • 84

Part B — Write these words as numbers:

  • nineteen
  • ninety
  • thirty-two
  • seventy-seven
✅ Answer

Part A: 15 = fifteen · 50 = fifty · 13 = thirteen · 40 = forty (no "u"!) · 67 = sixty-seven · 84 = eighty-four

Part B: nineteen = 19 · ninety = 90 · thirty-two = 32 · seventy-seven = 77

🏋️ Exercise 2: Read three phone numbers aloud

Objective: Say each digit separately, using "oh" for zero and "double" for repeats.

Read each number out loud, slowly, one digit at a time:

  • 555 0199
  • 077 4402 8830
  • 0800 12 12 12
✅ Answer

555 0199 = "double five — five — oh — one — double nine" (or "five — five — five — oh — one — nine — nine")

077 4402 8830 = "oh — double seven — double four — oh — two — double eight — three — oh"

0800 12 12 12 = "oh — eight — double oh — one — two — one — two — one — two"

Any clear version is correct — the goal is that a listener can write down the exact digits.

🎯 Your Project: Say Your Details

This is your speaking task. Do it out loud — to a partner, a teacher, or your phone's voice recorder.

  1. (2 min) Count out loud from 0 to 20, then say the tens: 10, 20, 30 … 100.
  2. (2 min) Say your age: "I'm ___ years old." (Remember: am, not have!)
  3. (3 min) Say your phone number, digit by digit. Use "oh" for zero and "double" for any repeats.
  4. (3 min) Spell your email address out loud, using "at" for @ and "dot" for the dot.
  5. (2 min) If you recorded yourself, listen back once. Which numbers felt hard? Note them for the journal.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Practice the -teen / -ty pairs out loud: "thirteen–thirty, fourteen–forty, fifteen–fifty."
  • Say phone numbers slowly, one digit at a time — speed is not the goal, clarity is.
  • Use real numbers: your age, your phone, house numbers you pass on the street.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I have 25 years": Wrong. Age uses to be — say "I'm 25."
  • "fourty": Wrong spelling. 40 = forty, with no "u".
  • Mixing -teen and -ty: If you're unsure, confirm the digits: "Fifteen, one-five? Or fifty, five-zero?"
  • Saying a phone number as one big number: Don't. Say each digit separately.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Learn 0–20 and the tens — then build any number by joining a ten and a ones digit with a hyphen (twenty-one, thirty-five).
  • -teen (13–19) stresses the end; -ty (30, 40…) stresses the front. And 40 = forty, no "u".
  • For age, English uses to be: "I'm 25 (years old)," never "I have 25 years."
  • Say phone numbers digit by digit; 0 = "oh", and repeats can be "double". Email uses "at" (@) and "dot" (.).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now count to a hundred, tell people how old you are the correct English way, and give your phone number and email so clearly that anyone could write them down. These are real, daily-use skills — the kind you'll reach for at a shop, a clinic, a hotel, or a first meeting. Well done. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I have to add "years old," or is "I'm 25" enough?

Just "I'm 25" is perfect and very common in everyday speech. "I'm 25 years old" is the fuller form. Both are correct — use whichever feels natural.

Is it "oh" or "zero" for 0 in a phone number?

Both are understood. In phone numbers, "oh" is extremely common and natural. "Zero" is clearer if there's any risk of confusion — use it when you really need to be precise.

I keep mixing up thirteen and thirty. Is that bad?

Not at all — even native speakers ask "one-three or three-zero?" to be sure. Practice the stress difference, and when in doubt, confirm the digits. Confusion here is completely normal.
