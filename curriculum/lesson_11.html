🎬 Lesson 4.2: Present Continuous — Happening Right Now

Look around you right now. Someone is reading. Someone is talking. Maybe you are drinking coffee ☕. In this lesson you'll learn the tense that describes exactly this — what is happening at this very moment — and how it's different from the "every day" English you already know.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the present continuous with am / is / are + verb-ing
  • Describe what is happening right now
  • Ask and answer "What are you doing?"
  • Tell the difference between now (present continuous) and every day (present simple)

What Is "Right Now" English?

In Lesson 4.1 you learned how to talk about your daily life: "I work in a bank. She drinks coffee. They play football." That is the present simple — it's for habits and things that are true every day.

But English has a second "present." When you want to say what is happening at this exact moment — while you are speaking — you use the present continuous. Compare:

  • Every day: I drink coffee. ☕ (my habit)
  • Right now: I am drinking coffee. (this moment)

Both sentences are correct — they just mean different things. This lesson is all about the second one: the "happening now" tense.

🧠 Mindset

You might worry: "Two present tenses? That's confusing!" Here's the good news — you already carry the idea of "now" versus "usually" in your own language. You're not learning a new idea, only a new shape for it in English. Take it slowly, say the sentences out loud, and the pattern will start to feel natural. One step at a time. 🌱

The Form: am / is / are + -ing

The present continuous has just two parts:

subject + am / is / are + verb-ing

The first part is the verb to be — the same am / is / are you met back in Lesson 2.1. If you know that, you're already halfway there! You just add -ing to the action verb.

Subjectto beverb-ingFull sentenceShort form
IameatingI am eating.I'm eating.
YouareworkingYou are working.You're working.
HeisreadingHe is reading.He's reading.
SheisreadingShe is reading.She's reading.
ItisrainingIt is raining.It's raining.
WeareplayingWe are playing.We're playing.
TheyareplayingThey are playing.They're playing.

✅ The one thing to remember

Never forget the to be word! A very common beginner mistake is to say "I eating". You always need both parts: am / is / are and the -ing verb. → "I am eating." ✅

Which "to be" word do you use? It depends on the subject — exactly like Lesson 2.1:

💡 Quick match-up

  • I → am  (I'm)
  • He / She / It → is  (he's / she's / it's)
  • You / We / They → are  (you're / we're / they're)

-ing Spelling Rules

Most of the time you just add -ing to the verb. But a few verbs change their spelling first. Here are the four patterns you need.

RuleWhat to doExamples
Most verbsjust add -ingread → reading · play → playing · eat → eating · watch → watching
Verbs ending in silent -edrop the e, add -ingwrite → writing · dance → dancing · make → making
Short vowel + one consonantdouble the last consonant, add -ingrun → running · sit → sitting · swim → swimming
Verbs ending in -iechange ie to y, add -inglie → lying · die → dying

⚠️ Watch out: don't double every consonant

We only double the last consonant after a short vowel sound in a short word: run → running, sit → sitting. But we do not double in longer words like read → reading (two vowels) or walk → walking (two consonants). When in doubt, just add -ing — that's the most common rule.

Negatives & Questions

To make it negative, add not after am / is / are. To make a question, put am / is / are first. Nothing else changes — the -ing stays the same.

➖ Negative

Full formShort form
I am not working.I'm not working.
He is not sleeping.He isn't sleeping. / He's not sleeping.
They are not listening.They aren't listening. / They're not listening.

❓ Questions & short answers

QuestionYes answerNo answer
Are you working?Yes, I am.No, I'm not.
Is she working?Yes, she is.No, she isn't.
Are they playing?Yes, they are.No, they aren't.

The most useful "now" question of all is "What are you doing?" Answer it with I'm + verb-ing:

💡 The key question

  • "What are you doing?" → "I'm cooking." 🍳
  • "What is he doing?" → "He's sleeping." 😴
  • "What are they doing?" → "They're playing football." ⚽

Now vs. Every Day

This is the most important idea in the whole lesson. English has two "presents," and the difference is time:

Present Simple
(every day)		Present Continuous
(right now)
Meaninga habit, a routine, a facthappening at this moment
Formverb / verb + sam / is / are + verb-ing
Time wordsevery day, usually, alwaysnow, right now, at the moment
ExampleI work in a bank.I 'm working now.
ExampleShe walks to school.She 's taking the bus today.

💡 The key insight — remember this!

Ask yourself one question: Is this a habit, or is it happening this second?

👉 "I work in a bank." = my job, every day. (present simple)

👉 "I'm working now." = this moment, at my desk. (present continuous)

The classic example: "She usually walks to school, but today she's taking the bus." The habit is walking; the "right now" is taking the bus. Same person, two tenses, two different times. 🕐

🗣️ Register note: In real conversation, English speakers love the short forms — "I'm cooking," not "I am cooking." The short form sounds natural and friendly. Use it whenever you're speaking.

Action Verbs & Phrases

The present continuous needs action verbs — things you can see a person doing. Here are the most useful ones. Practice saying each with -ing: eat → eating.

  • eat → eating 🍽️
  • drink → drinking 🥤
  • read → reading 📖
  • write → writing ✍️
  • cook → cooking 🍳
  • run → running 🏃
  • walk → walking 🚶
  • play → playing ⚽
  • watch → watching 📺
  • listen → listening 🎧
  • talk → talking 💬
  • wear → wearing 👕
  • sit → sitting 🪑
  • stand → standing 🧍
  • sleep → sleeping 😴
  • study → studying 📚
  • use (a phone) → using 📱
  • wait → waiting ⏳

Here are the everyday phrases that go with "right now" English. Keep them handy:

What are you doing? I'm …-ing Right now At the moment He's …-ing Is she …-ing?

Now listen to a real phone call. Read both parts out loud — this is exactly how you'll use the present continuous every day.

A: Hi! What are you doing?

B: I'm cooking dinner. 🍝

A: Oh, nice! Is Maria there?

B: Yes, she's watching TV. And the kids are doing their homework.

A: Are you busy? Can I call later?

B: No problem — I'm not busy. Let's talk now!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Add -ing correctly

Objective: Apply the spelling rules.

Write the -ing form of each verb. Watch out for the doubling and drop-e rules!

  1. run → __________
  2. write → __________
  3. play → __________
  4. sit → __________
  5. cook → __________
✅ Answer

1. run → running (double the n) · 2. write → writing (drop the e) · 3. play → playing (just add -ing) · 4. sit → sitting (double the t) · 5. cook → cooking (just add -ing)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Make present continuous sentences

Objective: Build full sentences from prompts.

Use the subject + verb to make a present continuous sentence. Use the short form.

  1. She / read a book → __________
  2. They / play in the park → __________
  3. I / drink water → __________
  4. He / not / sleep → __________
  5. you / watch TV? → __________
✅ Answer

1. She's reading a book. · 2. They're playing in the park. · 3. I'm drinking water. · 4. He isn't sleeping. (or He's not sleeping.) · 5. Are you watching TV?

🏋️ Exercise 3: Every day or right now?

Objective: Choose present simple (every day) or present continuous (now).

Pick the correct verb form for each sentence.

  1. Every morning, she (drinks / is drinking) tea.
  2. Look! The baby (sleeps / is sleeping) now.
  3. I (work / am working) in a school every day.
  4. Be quiet — they (study / are studying) at the moment.
  5. We usually (walk / are walking) to work.
✅ Answer

1. drinks (every morning = habit) · 2. is sleeping (now) · 3. work (every day = habit) · 4. are studying (at the moment) · 5. walk (usually = habit)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Always use two parts: am / is / are and the -ing verb.
  • Use the short forms when speaking — I'm, he's, they're — they sound natural.
  • Ask yourself: habit or this second? That tells you which present to use.
  • Practice out loud by describing what people around you are doing right now.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Forgetting "to be": "I eating" ❌ → "I am eating." ✅ You always need am/is/are.
  • Wrong "to be" word: "He are working" ❌ → "He is working." ✅ Match it to the subject.
  • Using "now" tense for habits: "I am working here every day" ❌ → "I work here every day." ✅ Every day = present simple.
  • Spelling slips: "runing," "writeing" ❌ → "running," "writing." ✅ Double after a short vowel; drop the silent e.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The present continuous = am / is / are + verb-ing, and it describes what is happening right now.
  • Add -ing to most verbs; drop the silent -e (write → writing); double the consonant after a short vowel (run → running).
  • Negative: I'm not working. Question: Is she working? → Yes, she is / No, she isn't.
  • Use present simple for every day, present continuous for right now. Ask: habit or this second?

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now describe the living, moving world in front of you — not just what happens every day, but what is happening this very second. You can ask "What are you doing?" and answer it, spell the tricky -ing forms, and — most importantly — you know which "present" to choose. That's a huge step toward real, natural conversation. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Why do we need two present tenses?

Because "usually" and "right now" are different ideas. "I read books" (I'm a reader) is not the same as "I'm reading a book" (this book, this moment). Many languages show this difference too — English just uses a special form for "now."

When exactly do I double the last consonant?

Only in a short word that ends in one vowel + one consonant, like run, sit, swim. Not when there are two vowels (read → reading) or two consonants (walk → walking). If you're ever unsure, remember most verbs simply add -ing.

Can I use short forms like "I'm" in writing?

Yes, in friendly and everyday writing — texts, chats, emails to friends. In very formal writing you might write the full form (I am, she is), but for speaking and normal messages, short forms are perfect and sound natural.
