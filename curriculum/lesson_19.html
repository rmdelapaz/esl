🍎 Lesson 7.1: Food & Drink — some & any

Everybody eats and drinks. In this lesson you learn the words for food and drink, and a very useful idea in English: some things we can count (one apple, two apples) and some things we cannot count (rice, water, milk). Once you see the difference, the words some, any, how much, and how many become easy.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell the difference between countable and uncountable nouns (an apple / rice)
  • Use some in positive sentences and any in negatives and questions
  • Ask how much (uncountable) and how many (countable)
  • Use a lot of, not much, and not many to say the amount

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Check your kitchen and make a shopping list out loud, using some / any and how much / how many in 6 or more sentences.

Food, Drink & Counting

Food is one of the first things you talk about in a new country. You shop for food. You order food. You cook and share meals with friends. So the words in this lesson are very useful in real life.

There is one small idea that makes English food words easy. Look at these two groups. One apple, two apples, three apples — you can count them. But rice? You cannot say "one rice, two rices." You cannot count rice like that. This is the big idea of the lesson: some nouns are countable, and some are uncountable.

💡 You saw this before

In Lesson 3.3 you met some and any for the first time ("There are some plants," "Are there any shops?"). Today we look more closely and add food. If it feels familiar, that is good news — you already have a head start! 🙂

🧠 Mindset

Do not worry about being perfect. If you say "I want two milks," people will still understand you. That is a win! 🌱 Small mistakes are normal for every learner. Today you will simply notice the difference between things you count and things you do not. Noticing is the first step. The correct habit comes with practice, a little at a time.

Countable vs Uncountable

Let's make the two groups clear.

  • Countable nouns are separate things you can count: an apple, two apples, three eggs, four bananas. They have a singular form (apple) and a plural form (apples). You can use a or an with one of them.
  • Uncountable nouns are things you see as one mass, not separate pieces: rice, water, milk, bread, cheese, sugar. They have no plural — we do not say "rices" or "waters." We do not use a or an with them.
CountableUncountable
Can you count it?Yes (one, two, three)No (it is one mass)
Plural form?Yes: apple → applesNo: rice (never "rices")
Use a / an?Yes: an egg, a bananaNo: a water → some water
Examplesapple, egg, banana, carrot, onion, biscuitrice, water, milk, bread, cheese, sugar, meat, coffee, tea
⚠️ Important Note: Never add -s to an uncountable noun. Say "I like rice," not "I like rices." Say "There is bread," not "There are breads." Uncountable nouns always stay in the singular, and they take is, not are.

💡 A friendly trick for uncountable food

To count uncountable food, use a container or a piece word: a glass of water, a cup of coffee, a bottle of milk, a piece of bread, a slice of cheese, a kilo of rice. Now you can count them — you count the glasses, cups, or pieces! 🥛

💬 One word, two ideas

A few words are uncountable as a group but countable as one piece. Vegetables and eggs are countable (you can count them). But fruit is usually uncountable ("I eat a lot of fruit"), even though one apple is countable. Don't worry about the tricky ones now — just learn the common patterns. 🍎🍌

Some & Any (a Closer Look)

Now the useful part. When we do not say an exact number, we often use some or any. The rule is short and easy.

📖 The simple rule

Use SOME in positive sentences: There's some milk in the fridge.

Use ANY in negatives and questions: There isn't any bread. · Is there any juice?

We use some and any with uncountable nouns (milk, bread, rice) and with plural countable nouns (apples, eggs). With one countable thing, use a / an instead (an egg, a banana).

Sentence typeWordExample
Positive (+)someThere's some milk. / There are some eggs.
Negative (−)anyThere isn't any bread. / There aren't any apples.
Question (?)anyIs there any juice? / Are there any tomatoes?

💡 A polite exception (for later)

When you offer something or ask for something politely, English uses some even in a question: "Would you like some tea?" or "Can I have some water, please?" You will practice this in the next café lesson. For now, remember the main rule: some for positive, any for negatives and questions. 🙂

How Much & How Many

To ask about the amount of something, we use how much or how many. Which one? It depends on countable or uncountable — the big idea from the start of the lesson!

how much + uncountable · how many + countable (plural)

Question wordUse withExample
How muchuncountable nounsHow much milk is there? How much rice do we need?
How manycountable (plural) nounsHow many eggs are there? How many apples do we need?

An easy way to remember: MANY is for things you count (one, two, many eggs). MUCH is for things you cannot count (much water, much rice).

✅ Pro Tip

Look at the noun right after the question word. If the noun has a plural -s (eggs, bananas), use how many. If the noun has no plural (milk, bread, water), use how much. The -s is your clue! 🔎

A Lot Of, Not Much, Not Many

To answer "how much?" or "how many?" without an exact number, use these easy phrases.

PhraseMeaningUse withExample
a lot ofa big amount 👍bothThere's a lot of rice. / There are a lot of eggs.
not mucha small amountuncountableThere isn't much milk.
not manya small numbercountableThere aren't many apples.

See the pattern again? Not much goes with uncountable (milk), and not many goes with countable (apples). And a lot of is the easy one — it works with both! When you are not sure, a lot of is a safe choice. 😊

⚠️ Watch out: We usually use much and many in negatives and questions. In a positive sentence, a lot of sounds much more natural. Say "There's a lot of water," not "There's much water."

Dialogue & Phrases

Here are Nadia and Ben. They are checking the kitchen and making a shopping list before dinner. Read both parts out loud. Look for every some, any, how much, and how many.

Nadia: Let's make dinner. Is there any rice?

Ben: Yes, there's some rice. But there isn't much — only a little.

Nadia: OK, put rice on the list. How many eggs are there?

Ben: Let me see… There are only two eggs. That's not many.

Nadia: We need six. And how much milk is there?

Ben: There isn't any milk! The bottle is empty.

Nadia: No milk? OK, we need some milk too. Is there any bread?

Ben: There's a lot of bread, and there's some cheese. And there are a lot of tomatoes.

Nadia: Good. So the list is: rice, eggs, and milk. Are there any apples?

Ben: No, there aren't any. Let's buy some fruit as well.

Here are the best phrases from the conversation. Keep them ready for your project.

Is there any…? There's some… There isn't any… How much milk is there? How many eggs are there? There isn't much. That's not many. We need some… Let's buy some… Put it on the list.

🎯 Your Project: Check the Kitchen & Make a Shopping List

Now do this in real life! Go to your kitchen, or imagine one, and make a shopping list out loud. Say 6 or more sentences using some / any and how much / how many.

  1. (3 min) Look in your fridge or cupboard. Say what there is: "There's some rice. There are some eggs."
  2. (3 min) Say what there isn't: "There isn't any milk. There aren't any apples."
  3. (3 min) Ask yourself how much / how many for two foods: "How many eggs are there? How much bread is there?"
  4. (3 min) Make your list: "I need some milk, six eggs, and a lot of fruit."
  5. (3 min) Say the whole list out loud. Solo? Record it and listen once. With a partner? Take turns and check each other's some/any.

Model answer: "Let me check the kitchen. There's some rice and there's a lot of bread. There are some eggs, but not many — only two. There isn't any milk, and there isn't any cheese. How much fruit is there? Not much. So my shopping list is: some milk, six eggs, some cheese, and a lot of fruit."

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Ask yourself: can I count it? Yes → countable. No → uncountable. This choice guides everything else.
  • Use some in positive sentences and any in negatives and questions.
  • Look for the plural -s: how many apples, but how much water.
  • Use a lot of in positive sentences — it works with both countable and uncountable.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "three rices" / "two breads" ❌ — Uncountable nouns have no plural. Say some rice, a lot of bread. ✅
  • "There are some milk." ❌ — Milk is uncountable, so it takes is. Say There is some milk. ✅
  • "How much apples?" ❌ — Apples are countable. Say How many apples? ✅
  • "There aren't some eggs." ❌ — Use any in negatives. Say There aren't any eggs. ✅
  • "I want a water." ❌ — Water is uncountable. Say some water or a glass of water. ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Countable nouns can be counted and have plurals (an apple, two apples). Uncountable nouns cannot (rice, water) and have no plural.
  • Use some in positive sentences; use any in negatives and questions.
  • How much goes with uncountable nouns; how many goes with countable (plural) nouns.
  • A lot of = a big amount (both); not much = a small amount (uncountable); not many = a small number (countable).

You can ask how much and how many. This is everyday, useful English that you will use in shops, kitchens, and cafés. Well done! 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know if a word is countable or uncountable?

Ask: "Can I count it one by one?" You can count eggs and apples (countable). You cannot count water or rice one by one — it is one mass (uncountable). When you are not sure, listen to how English speakers use the word, and learn it with practice.

Can I use "some" in a question?

Usually we use any in questions. But when you offer or ask for something politely, English uses some: "Would you like some tea?" You will practice this polite some in the next café lesson.

Is it wrong to say "much water" in a normal sentence?

In a positive sentence, a lot of sounds much more natural: "There's a lot of water." We usually keep much and many for negatives and questions: "There isn't much water," "How much water is there?"
