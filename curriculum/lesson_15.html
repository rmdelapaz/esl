👀 Lesson 5.3: Have Got — People & Belongings

What have you got? A phone? Brown eyes? A big family? In this lesson you learn have got and has got — an easy, friendly way to talk about the things you own and the way people look. Soon you can describe your friends and family in English. Let's take a look! 👨‍👩‍👧

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use have got and has got to talk about belongings and looks
  • Make negatives (haven't got, hasn't got) and questions (Have you got…?)
  • Give short answers (Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't.)
  • Use description words for hair, eyes, and appearance

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe a family member or friend in 6 or more sentences using have got / has got and description words.

🔤 New Words in This Lesson

Here are some useful words from this lesson. Tap 🔊 to hear a word.

  • curly — hair with lots of curls
  • straight — hair with no curls
  • long — not short (long hair)

  • beard — hair on a man's face
  • glasses — you wear them to see better
  • tall — high, like a big building
  • eyes — you see with them

What Is "Have Got"?

We use have got to talk about two things: possession (things you own — "I've got a car") and description (how people look — "She's got blue eyes"). It is a very common and friendly way to speak.

Here is the good news: have got means the same as have. Both are correct! "I've got a sister" and "I have a sister" mean exactly the same thing. So you are not learning a new idea — just a new, natural way to say it. 🙂

💡 have got = have

Have got is very common in British and everyday English, especially in speaking. Have (on its own) is also correct everywhere, and American English often just uses have. So "Have you got a pen?" and "Do you have a pen?" are both fine. Use the one you like. 🌍

🧠 Mindset

You already know how to describe a friend in your own language. Today you just get the English words. Don't worry about "have" versus "have got" — pick one and use it! If you say "He have got brown hair," people still understand you perfectly. Speak first, and the small grammar comes with practice. You are ready. 🌱

Affirmative & Contractions

The rule is easy. There are only two forms. Use have got for I, you, we, they. Use has got for he, she, it.

I/you/we/they + have got  ·  he/she/it + has got

SubjectFormExample
Ihave gotI have got a new phone.
Youhave gotYou have got nice eyes.
Wehave gotWe have got two cats.
Theyhave gotThey have got a big house.
Hehas gotHe has got short hair.
Shehas gotShe has got a brother.
Ithas gotIt has got four legs.

When we speak, we almost always make it shorter with a contraction. This sounds natural and easy.

Full formShort form (contraction)
I have gotI've got
You have gotYou've got
We / They have gotWe've got / They've got
He / She has gotHe's got / She's got

💡 What does 's mean?

In "She's got brown eyes," the 's means has (= she has got). Don't confuse it with "She's happy," where 's means is. If got comes after, then 's = has. 👍

Negatives

To say you do not have something, add not to have/has. We almost always use the short forms haven't got and hasn't got.

haven't got  ·  hasn't got

SubjectNegativeExample
I / You / We / Theyhaven't gotI haven't got a car.
He / She / Ithasn't gotShe hasn't got a beard.

So "I haven't got a car" means "I don't have a car." And "He hasn't got glasses" means "He doesn't have glasses." Easy!

⚠️ Important Note: Use haven't for I/you/we/they and hasn't for he/she/it. Say "She hasn't got a car," not "She haven't got a car." Match the word to the subject.

Questions & Short Answers

To ask a question, put Have or Has at the start. Then the subject, then got.

Have/Has + subject + got …?

QuestionYes answerNo answer
Have you got a pen?Yes, I have.No, I haven't.
Have they got children?Yes, they have.No, they haven't.
Has she got long hair?Yes, she has.No, she hasn't.
Has he got a beard?Yes, he has.No, he hasn't.

Look at the short answers. We don't say got again. Someone asks "Have you got a pen?" You just say "Yes, I have." or "No, I haven't." Short and natural.

✅ Pro Tip

In the short answer, don't say got. Say "Yes, I have," not "Yes, I have got." And match the word: "Has she got…?" → "Yes, she has." / "No, she hasn't." The little words have and has do all the work. 💪

Describing People

Now let's use have got to describe how people look. Read each word out loud. Then you can describe your friends and family.

💇 Hair

  • long hair — not short
  • short hair — not long
  • curly hair — with curls
  • straight hair — no curls
  • blond hair — light yellow
  • dark hair — brown or black
  • grey hair — often older people

👤 Face & body

  • blue / brown / green eyes
  • a beard — hair on the face
  • a moustache — hair above the mouth
  • glasses — to see better
  • tall — high (a tall man)
  • short — not tall

💡 How to build a description

Put the words together like this: She has got + (length) + (type/color) + hair. For example: "She's got long straight blond hair." Or simply: "He's got short dark hair and brown eyes." You can also add: "He's got a beard and glasses." 👓

⚠️ Watch out: Hair for the head is usually uncountable — say "She has got long hair," not "long hairs." Also, we say "She's got blue eyes," not "she is blue eyes."

Dialogue & Phrases

Here are two friends, Nadia and Sam. Sam wants to meet Nadia's brother at the station. Read both parts out loud. Look for every have got / has got.

Sam: I'm at the station. How can I find your brother?

Nadia: OK. He's tall, and he's got short dark hair.

Sam: Has he got a beard?

Nadia: Yes, he has. And he's got glasses too.

Sam: Has he got a bag?

Nadia: Yes — he's got a big blue backpack.

Sam: I think I can see him! Tall, dark hair, glasses… blue backpack. Is that him?

Nadia: That's him! His name is Omar. Say hello!

Sam: Great. We haven't got much time, so I'll go now. Thanks, Nadia!

Here are the best phrases from the conversation. Keep them ready for your project.

He's got short dark hair. Has he got a beard? Yes, he has. / No, he hasn't. She's got blue eyes. I've got a big blue bag. We haven't got much time.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use has got for he/she/it, and have got for I/you/we/they.
  • Use the short forms when you speak: I've got, she's got, he hasn't got.
  • In short answers, don't say got: "Yes, I have," "No, she hasn't."
  • Remember: have got = have. Both are correct, so pick one!

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "He have got…" ❌ — For he/she/it, use has got. ✅
  • "She haven't got…" ❌ — For she, use hasn't got. ✅
  • "Yes, I have got." ❌ — In short answers, drop got: Yes, I have. ✅
  • "She's got long hairs." ❌ — Hair is uncountable here: long hair. ✅
  • Mixing 's: In "She's got," 's = has, not is. If got follows, it means has. ✅

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What is the difference between "have got" and "have"?

There is no difference in meaning! "I've got a car" = "I have a car." Have got is very common in British and everyday speaking. Plain have is correct everywhere, and American English often prefers it. Use the one you like.

Does "She's got" mean "she is got"?

No. When got comes after, the 's means has: "She's got" = "She has got." Be careful — "She's happy" is different; there 's means is. The word got is your clue.

Do I say "got" in the short answer?

No. In short answers, drop got. Say "Yes, I have" or "No, she hasn't," not "Yes, I have got." The little words have and has are enough.
