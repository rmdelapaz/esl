Body Parts

To talk to a doctor, you need words for your body. Read each word out loud and point to that part of your body as you say it.

  • head — the top, with your face 🧑
  • eye / ear / nose / mouth — parts of your face
  • throat — inside your neck
  • tooth — in your mouth (many = teeth)
  • arm — shoulder to hand
  • hand — at the end of your arm
  • stomach — where food goes; your belly
  • back — behind you, neck to hips
  • leg — hip to foot
  • foot — you stand on it (two = feet)

💡 Two special plurals

Most body words just add -s (arms, legs, ears). But two are irregular: tooth → teeth and foot → feet. Say "one tooth, two teeth" and "one foot, two feet." These are common little exceptions in English. 🦷🦶

Health Problems

Now, how do you say something hurts? English has a few common patterns. Here are the most useful ones. Tap 🔊 on the model sentences and repeat them.

1. "I've got a…" (a problem)

Use I've got (= I have) for many common problems:

I've got a headache. I've got a cold. I've got a temperature. I've got a stomachache. I've got a sore throat. I've got a cough.

2. "My … hurts."

Use hurts to say a body part is in pain. For one part, say hurts; for two or more, say hurt:

My leg hurts. My back hurts. My tooth hurts. My feet hurt.

3. "I feel…"

Use I feel for how your whole body feels:

I feel sick. I feel tired. I feel dizzy. I feel better today.

💡 A friendly note on "-ache"

English joins a body part with -ache (= a pain) for some common problems: head + ache = headache, stomach + ache = stomachache, tooth + ache = toothache, back + ache = backache. We usually say "I've got a headache." Learn these as whole words. 🤕

Advice with Should

A doctor gives advice — good ideas to help you feel better. The easiest way to give advice is with should. Put should before the base verb. It's the same for every subject.

subject + should + base verb

Advice (should)Advice not to (shouldn't)
You should rest.You shouldn't work today.
You should drink water.You shouldn't drink coffee.
You should see a doctor.You shouldn't go to work.
You should stay in bed.You shouldn't eat a lot.
⚠️ Watch out: After should, use the plain verb — never add to or -s. Say "You should rest," not "You should to rest" or "You should rests."

✅ Real health advice, from real doctors

This lesson teaches English words, not medical care. If you are really ill, always see a real doctor, nurse, or pharmacist. This lesson helps you talk about health in English — it is not medical advice. Take care of yourself first. ❤️

Listening: At the Doctor

Here is a full conversation between a patient and a doctor. This is a listening & speaking task. Read both parts out loud, then use the buttons to listen and repeat.

Doctor: Good morning. Come in and sit down. What's the matter?

Patient: Good morning, doctor. I don't feel well. I've got a headache and a sore throat.

Doctor: I see. How long have you had it?

Patient: Since yesterday. Last night I had a temperature, too. I feel tired.

Doctor: Okay. Does your stomach hurt?

Patient: No, it doesn't. But my back hurts a little.

Doctor: It sounds like a cold. It's not serious. You should rest and drink a lot of water.

Patient: Should I take some medicine?

Doctor: Yes, you should take this for your temperature. And you shouldn't go to work for two days.

Patient: Thank you, doctor. I feel better already!

🔊 Listen & Repeat

This is a speaking lesson, so use your ears and your mouth! Tap the 🔊 Listen button (or the speaker names) to hear each line, then say it out loud after the voice. Try to copy the sound and the music of the sentence. Go slowly. Repeat each line two or three times until it feels comfortable in your mouth. This is how real speaking grows. 🎧🗣️

Now check that you understood the conversation.

📝 Comprehension questions (tap to check)
  1. What problems has the patient got? — A headache and a sore throat (and last night, a temperature).
  2. Does the patient's stomach hurt? — No, it doesn't. But their back hurts a little.
  3. What does the doctor say it is? — A cold. It's not serious.
  4. What advice does the doctor give? — You should rest, drink a lot of water, and take some medicine. You shouldn't go to work for two days.

Keep these health phrases ready for a real visit.

What's the matter? I don't feel well. I've got a headache. My back hurts. I feel tired. You should rest. You should drink water. You shouldn't go to work. I feel better already!

Review: Your Three Time Frames

Before the big project, let's quickly review the three ways you can talk about time. You learned all of these in A1! Here they are side by side.

TimeGrammarExample
Every week (present)present simple + frequency adverbI usually work from Monday to Friday.
Next week (future)going to / present continuousNext week I'm going to visit my family.
Last week (past)past simpleLast week I went to the cinema.

💡 Time words help your listener

Little time words tell people which time frame you mean: usually, every day, on Mondays (present); next week, tomorrow, tonight (future); last week, yesterday, two days ago (past). Start your sentence with one of these, and your meaning is clear. ⏰

Ana: Tell me about your week, Leo!

Leo: Well, I usually work in the morning and study English in the evening. Last week I visited my grandmother, and next week I'm going to start a new class!

Ana: Wow, past, present, and future in one breath! Your English is great.

Leo: Thank you! One year ago I couldn't say any of this.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Mixed tenses — my week

Objective: Choose the right form for present, future, and past.

Write the correct word in each gap. The time word tells you which tense to use.

  1. Every day I up at seven. (get)
  2. I usually from Monday to Friday. (work)
  3. Next week I'm going to my family. (visit)
  4. Yesterday I a good film. (watch)
  5. Last weekend I to the beach. (go)
✅ Answer

1. get (present) · 2. work (present) · 3. visit (base verb after "going to") · 4. watched (past, regular) · 5. went (past, irregular)

🎯 Capstone Project: My Week

This is your A1 capstone — the big one! Give a short talk about your week, out loud, to a partner, a teacher, or your phone. Use all three time frames. Say 8 or more sentences.

  1. (4 min) Present: Write 3 sentences about your typical week. Use the present simple and a frequency adverb (usually, always, often, sometimes). Example: "I usually get up at seven."
  2. (4 min) Future: Write 2–3 sentences about your plans for next week. Use going to or the present continuous. Example: "Next week I'm going to start a course."
  3. (4 min) Past: Write 2–3 sentences about last week. Use the past simple, with at least one irregular verb. Example: "Last week I went to the market."
  4. (3 min) Put your 8+ sentences in a clear order: present → future → past (or tell it like a story). Add time words to guide your listener.
  5. (5 min) Say your whole talk out loud, or record it and listen once. With a partner? Take turns, then ask each other one question, like "What are you going to do next week?"

Model answer: "Let me tell you about my week. I usually get up at seven and go to work. In the evening I always study English for one hour. Next week is special — I'm going to start a new job on Monday, and I'm going to meet my old friends on Saturday. Last week was busy. I visited my grandmother, and we cooked dinner together. On Sunday I went to the park and played football with my son. It was a great week, and next week is going to be even better!"

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I said 8 or more sentences about my week
  • ☐ I described my typical week (present simple + a frequency adverb)
  • ☐ I described my plans for next week (going to / present continuous)
  • ☐ I described what I did last week (past simple, with one irregular verb)
  • ☐ I used clear time words (usually, next week, last week)
  • ☐ I said it all out loud (recorded or to a partner)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use I've got a… for common problems (a headache, a cold) and My … hurts for a body part.
  • Give advice with should + plain verb (You should rest).
  • Use time words (usually, next week, last week) so your listener knows the time frame.
  • Listen and repeat out loud — copy the sound of the voice. Speaking grows with practice.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "You should to rest." ❌ — No to after should. Say "You should rest." ✅
  • "My legs hurts." ❌ — Two legs → hurt. Say "My legs hurt." ✅ (One leg → "My leg hurts.")
  • "Next week I went…" ❌ — Next week is future. Say "Next week I'm going to…" ✅
  • "Yesterday I go…" ❌ — Yesterday is past. Say "Yesterday I went…" ✅

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

I still make mistakes. Am I really ready to move on?

Yes! Making mistakes is a normal, healthy part of learning — even advanced speakers make them. If you can talk about the present, future, and past, and have simple everyday conversations, you have a strong A1 foundation. A2 will help you make fewer mistakes over time.

How can I keep practicing after A1?

Keep speaking out loud every day, even for five minutes. Describe your day, your plans, and your past. Watch simple videos, read easy texts, and use your journal. A little practice every day is better than a lot once a week. Keep going!

What is the difference between A1 and A2?

A1 gave you the foundation — the basic building blocks. A2 (Elementary) builds on top: more tenses and detail, longer conversations, and more natural, connected speech. You are ready for it. It's the perfect next step.
