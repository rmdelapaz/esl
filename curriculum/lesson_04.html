🌍 Lesson 2.1: The Verb "To Be" — Countries & Nationalities

This is one of the most important lessons in the course. The verb "to be" — am, is, are — is the little engine behind many English sentences. Today you learn all three forms. You use them to say where you are from and what your nationality is.

📚 What You'll Learn

After this lesson, you can:

  • Use "to be" with every person — in yes-sentences, negatives, and questions
  • Ask and answer "Where are you from?"
  • Say your country and your nationality the right way
  • Ask and answer yes/no questions and give short answers

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Talk about yourself and two other people (real or imagined) — name, country, and nationality — in full sentences with "to be."

Why "To Be" Matters

In Lesson 1 you learned to greet people and say your name. To do more — to say who you are, where you are from, how you feel, or where something is — you need the verb "to be." It is the most common verb in English. When you know it, you can make many new sentences:

  • "I am Maria." — who you are
  • "She is from Japan." — where someone is from
  • "We are happy." — how you feel
  • "The book is on the table." — where a thing is

Here is the good news: "to be" has only three forms in the present — am, is, are. Learn which one goes with which person, and you are ready.

🧠 Mindset

Grammar tables can look scary — all those rows and boxes! But take a breath. The verb "to be" is just three little words: am, is, are. That is all. You are not learning a hundred things. You are learning where three words go. A child does this with no table at all. The table is just a map to help you, and you know how to read a map. 🌱

Affirmative: am / is / are

"Affirmative" just means a positive, yes-sentence (not a negative and not a question). Here is the full picture. The Full form column is what you write in careful, formal English. The Contraction column is the short form you hear in everyday speech.

PersonFull formContraction
II amI'm
you (one person)you areyou're
hehe ishe's
sheshe isshe's
itit isit's
wewe arewe're
you (a group)you areyou're
theythey arethey're

Look at the pattern. It is simple. There are only three groups:

Use this form……with these subjects
amI
ishe, she, it (one person or thing)
areyou, we, they

💡 A tiny memory trick

am goes with I only. is goes with the "one" words — he, she, it. Everything else — you, we, they — takes are. Say it with a beat: "I am · he-she-it is · you-we-they are."

Some people learn best with a picture. Here is the same rule as a small map:

graph LR
    I["I"] --> AM["am"]
    HE["he / she / it"] --> IS["is"]
    YOU["you / we / they"] --> ARE["are"]

Making It Negative

To make a "to be" sentence negative — to say not — just add the word not after am / is / are. That is the whole rule. Most of the time, we then join the words into a contraction.

PersonFull negativeContraction(s)
II am notI'm not
youyou are notyou aren't · you're not
hehe is nothe isn't · he's not
sheshe is notshe isn't · she's not
itit is notit isn't · it's not
wewe are notwe aren't · we're not
theythey are notthey aren't · they're not

Both contractions mean the same thing. "He isn't" and "he's not" are both correct and both common. Use the one that is easier to say.

✅ Notice the one special case

For I, there is only one contraction: I'm not. English never says "I amn't." So: "I'm not from Spain." ✅

Questions & Short Answers

To make a "to be" sentence into a yes/no question, change the order of the first two words. Put am / is / are before the subject. We call this inversion.

StatementQuestion (inverted)
I am late.Am I late?
You are ready.Are you ready?
He is from Japan.Is he from Japan?
She is a teacher.Is she a teacher?
They are here.Are they here?

When someone asks a yes/no question, you usually give a short answer. You do not say the whole sentence again. Here is the pattern:

QuestionYes, …No, …
Am I right?Yes, you are.No, you aren't.
Are you Brazilian?Yes, I am.No, I'm not.
Is he from Mexico?Yes, he is.No, he isn't.
Is she a student?Yes, she is.No, she isn't.
Are they friends?Yes, they are.No, they aren't.

⚠️ Watch Out: never contract a short "Yes" answer

In a positive short answer, keep the verb full. Say "Yes, I am." — never "Yes, I'm." ❌ The same for the others: "Yes, she is." ✅ not "Yes, she's." And "Yes, they are." ✅ not "Yes, they're."

Why? A contraction like "I'm" needs a word after it. Alone, it sounds unfinished. But negative short answers can use a contraction: "No, I'm not." and "No, she isn't." are both fine. ✅

"Where Are You From?"

Now let's use "to be" in the most useful question when you meet someone new: "Where are you from?" This is a Wh-question (it starts with a "wh" word). To answer, use "I'm from + country."

Where are you from? I'm from Brazil. Where's he from? He's from Japan. Where are they from? They're from Italy. Nice to meet you!
QuestionAnswer
Where are you from?I'm from Brazil.
Where's he from?He's from Japan.
Where's she from?She's from Mexico.
Where are they from?They're from France.

"Where's" is just the short form of "Where is." Use it with he, she, it. Now read a real meeting out loud. Say both parts.

A: Hi! I'm Leo.

B: Hello, Leo. I'm Sofia. Nice to meet you.

A: Nice to meet you too. Where are you from, Sofia?

B: I'm from Mexico. I'm Mexican. And you?

A: I'm from Italy. I'm Italian.

B: Oh, nice! Is your friend Italian too?

A: No, he isn't. He's from France. He's French.

Countries & Nationalities

Your country is the place (a noun): Brazil, Japan, Mexico. Your nationality is the word for a person from there (an adjective): Brazilian, Japanese, Mexican. English changes the end of the country word to make the nationality. There is no single rule, so study this table.

CountryNationality (adjective)
BrazilBrazilian
JapanJapanese
MexicoMexican
FranceFrench
ChinaChinese
EgyptEgyptian
ItalyItalian
SpainSpanish
GermanyGerman
the USAAmerican
the UKBritish
KoreaKorean
IndiaIndian

⚠️ Always capitalize countries AND nationalities

In English, country names and nationality words always start with a CAPITAL letter — even in the middle of a sentence. Write "I'm Brazilian," not "i'm brazilian." Many languages do not do this, so it is an easy mistake. 💡 When you are not sure, use a capital letter.

The two sentence patterns

Use these two little patterns together to say where you are from:

  • I'm from + country. → I'm from Mexico.
  • I'm + nationality. → I'm Mexican.

Put them together and you have a good, natural introduction:

"Hello! I'm Ana. I'm from Mexico. I'm Mexican."

💡 Notice the difference

After from, use the country: "I'm from Spain." ✅

With no "from", use the nationality: "I'm Spanish." ✅

Do not mix them: "I'm from Spanish" ❌ and "I'm Spain" ❌ are both wrong.

🎯 Your Project: Three Introductions

Talk about yourself and two other people. They can be real (family, friends, famous people) or made up. For each person, say their name, their country, and their nationality in full sentences with "to be." Do it out loud — to a partner, a teacher, or your phone's voice recorder.

  1. (3 min) Introduce yourself: "I'm ___. I'm from ___. I'm ___." (name, country, nationality)
  2. (3 min) Introduce person two using he or she: "This is ___. He's/She's from ___. He's/She's ___."
  3. (3 min) Introduce person three the same way.
  4. (2 min) Now make each one negative for practice: "I'm not from Japan. I'm from ___."
  5. (2 min) Partner/class option: swap with a partner and ask each other "Where are you from?" and one yes/no question ("Are you ___?"). Give short answers.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Say the beat every day: "I am · he-she-it is · you-we-they are."
  • Use contractions when you speak — I'm, you're, she's — they sound natural.
  • Use a capital letter for every country and nationality word, always.
  • Answer yes/no questions with a short answer, not silence and not the full sentence.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Leaving out "to be": Do not say "I from Brazil." ❌ Every sentence needs the verb: "I am from Brazil." ✅
  • Wrong form: "She are…" ❌ and "They is…" ❌ are common slips. Match the person: she is, they are.
  • Contracting a positive short answer: "Yes, I'm." ❌ → say "Yes, I am." ✅
  • Country vs. nationality: "I'm from Spanish" ❌ → "I'm from Spain." ✅ / "I'm Spanish." ✅
  • Small letter for nationalities: "i'm italian" ❌ → "I'm Italian." ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • "To be" has just three present forms: am (I), is (he/she/it), are (you/we/they).
  • Make it negative by adding not: isn't, aren't, I'm not.
  • Make a question by inversion: Is he…? Are you…? — and reply with a short answer ("Yes, I am." / "No, I'm not.").
  • Answer "Where are you from?" with "I'm from + country" and "I'm + nationality" — both always capitalized.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You just learned the engine of English — the verb "to be" — in all three forms: positive, negative, and question. Now you can introduce yourself, say where you are from, name your nationality, and answer real questions. This one verb is in almost every conversation. That is a big step. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I have to use contractions like "I'm" and "she's"?

No — the full forms (I am, she is) are always correct. But people use contractions in normal speech. When you hear and use them, you sound natural and you understand others better.

What's the difference between "isn't" and "'s not"?

Nothing important — "he isn't" and "he's not" mean the same thing and are both common. Pick the one that is easier to say. Only "I" has no choice: it is always "I'm not."

Why can't I say "Yes, I'm."?

A contraction like "I'm" needs a word after it. Alone, it sounds unfinished. So in a positive short answer, we keep the verb full: "Yes, I am." Negatives are fine with a contraction: "No, I'm not."
