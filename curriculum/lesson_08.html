🕒 Lesson 3.2: Telling the Time, Days & He/She (-s)

Now you can talk about your own daily routine. Let's add a clock and a calendar — and learn to talk about other people. In English, when we say what he, she, or it does, we add a little -s to the verb. Learn these three things and you can describe anyone's day.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell the time in English — the easy digital way and the classic "past/to" way
  • Name the seven days of the week and spell them with capital letters
  • Use the present simple third person -s for he / she / it
  • Choose the right preposition of time: at, on, or in

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe a friend's or family member's daily routine out loud, using he/she + the time + the days of the week (e.g. "She gets up at seven. On Monday she works…").

  • quarter — fifteen minutes
  • half past — thirty minutes after the hour

Why Time, Days & He/She?

In the last lesson you learned to say what you do every day: "I get up at seven." But you often need two more things: a clock and a calendar. When does the shop open? What day is the class? What time does the bus leave? These are everyday questions. Today you can answer them.

You also take a big step in grammar. Talking about yourself is one skill. Talking about other people is another. When we say what he, she, or it does, English adds a small -s to the verb: I work → she works. It is a tiny letter, but it is one of the most important patterns in English.

🧠 Mindset

That little -s is the number-one thing beginners forget — and that is fine. Even people who speak English for years sometimes drop it when they speak fast. You will not learn it in one day, and you don't need to. Notice it, practice it, and be kind to yourself when you miss it. Every time you catch the mistake, your brain is learning. Progress, not perfection. 🌱

Telling the Time

To ask the time, we say: "What time is it?" To answer, we start with "It's…" There are two ways to say the time. Good news: you can always use the easy one.

The easy way: just say the numbers (digital)

Read the clock just as you see it, left number then right number. This always works, and everyone understands it.

  • 3:00 → "three o'clock"
  • 3:30 → "three thirty"
  • 3:15 → "three fifteen"
  • 3:45 → "three forty-five"
  • 7:05 → "seven oh five"
  • 9:20 → "nine twenty"

💡 A small note

For minutes under ten, English speakers often say "oh" for the zero: 7:05 is "seven oh five." The "oh" is the sound of the letter O. It stands for zero.

The classic way: o'clock, past & to

You will also hear people use these words. It is good to understand them, even if you like the easy way when you speak.

  • o'clock — only for a full hour: 3:00 = "three o'clock."
  • quarter past — 15 minutes after: 3:15 = "quarter past three."
  • half past — 30 minutes after: 3:30 = "half past three."
  • quarter to — 15 minutes before the next hour: 3:45 = "quarter to four."
  • past — minutes after the hour: 3:10 = "ten past three."
  • to — minutes before the next hour: 3:40 = "twenty to four."

⚠️ The big idea: "past" goes up, "to" goes down

For the first half hour (minutes 1–30), we count past the hour we just had. After the half hour (31–59), we count how many minutes to the next hour. So 3:40 is "twenty to four" — it is twenty minutes before four o'clock.

✅ Noon & midnight

12:00 in the day is noon (also called midday). 12:00 at night is midnight. So "let's meet at noon" means twelve in the middle of the day. "Midnight" is twelve when everyone is asleep.

Days of the Week

There are seven days. Say them out loud in order — the rhythm helps you remember them. The pronunciation hint shows the sound.

  • Monday /ˈmʌndeɪ/ — MUN-day
  • Tuesday /ˈtjuːzdeɪ/ — TYOOZ-day
  • Wednesday /ˈwɛnzdeɪ/ — WENZ-day
  • Thursday /ˈθɜːrzdeɪ/ — THURZ-day
  • Friday /ˈfraɪdeɪ/ — FRY-day
  • Saturday /ˈsætərdeɪ/ — SAT-ur-day
  • Sunday /ˈsʌndeɪ/ — SUN-day

⚠️ Days always start with a CAPITAL letter

In English, we always write the days of the week with a big first letter: Monday, not monday. This is true even in the middle of a sentence: "I work on Friday." Also watch the spelling of Wednesday — we say "WENZ-day," but there is a silent d in the middle.

💡 Weekday vs. weekend

The five working days — Monday to Friday — are the weekdays. Saturday and Sunday are the weekend. So "I work on weekdays and rest at the weekend" is very natural to say.

At / On / In — Prepositions of Time

Small words, big job. English uses three little words to add a time to a sentence. Each one has its own rule.

WordUse it for…Examples
ata clock time; night; the weekend (UK)at 7 o'clock · at 8:30 · at noon · at night · at the weekend
ona day or dateon Monday · on Friday · on Saturday morning · on July 4th
ina part of the day; a month; a season; a yearin the morning · in the afternoon · in July · in summer · in 2026

💡 A memory trick

Think of the words getting bigger: at a tiny point (a clock time) → on a single day → in a big block of time (a morning, a month, a year). Learn this pair together: "in the morning / in the afternoon / in the evening" but "at night." Night is the odd one out! 🌙

He / She / It + -s

Here is the heart of today's grammar. In the present simple, when the subject is he, she, or it, we add -s to the verb. For everyone else (I, you, we, they), the verb stays plain.

SubjectVerb "work"Example
IworkI work in a shop.
YouworkYou work hard.
HeworksHe works at night.
SheworksShe works on Monday.
ItworksThe clock works.
WeworkWe work together.
TheyworkThey work at home.

Spelling rules for the -s

Most verbs just take a plain -s. A few verbs need a small change. Here are the three rules.

RuleWhenExamples
Add -smost verbswork → works · play → plays · read → reads · start → starts
Add -esafter s, x, ch, sh, owatch → watches · go → goes · do → does · finish → finishes · fix → fixes
Change -y → -iesconsonant + ystudy → studies · try → tries · fly → flies

⚠️ One irregular verb to remember: have → has

have does not follow the rule. For he/she/it it becomes has: "She has breakfast at eight." (Not "haves.") Just learn this one.

Negatives & questions: the -s "disappears"

Here is the surprise for almost every learner. When you make a negative or a question, the -s moves onto a helper word (does). The main verb goes back to its plain base form.

TypeFormExample
Positivehe/she + verb-sShe works on Monday.
Negativedoesn't + base verbShe doesn't work on Sunday.
QuestionDoes + subject + base verb?Does he get up early?
Short answerYes, … does / No, … doesn'tYes, he does. / No, he doesn't.

⚠️ Never say "worksn't" or "doesn't works"

The -s can be in only one place at a time. In a negative or question, does/doesn't already has it. So the main verb is plain:

  • ✅ She doesn't work on Sunday.   ❌ She doesn't works.
  • ✅ Does he get up early?   ❌ Does he gets up early?

Phrases & Dialogue

Here are ready-made phrases to ask and answer about time and routines. Say each one out loud.

What time is it? It's half past nine. What time do you get up? What time does she start work? He starts at nine. on Monday at the weekend in the morning Does he work on Sunday? No, he doesn't.

Now listen to two friends talk. See how Mia talks about herself ("I start…") and then about her brother ("He starts…"). Read both parts out loud.

A: What time do you start work?

B: I start at eight in the morning. And I finish at four.

A: And your brother? What time does he start?

B: He starts work at nine. He works in a hospital.

A: Does he work on Sunday?

B: No, he doesn't. He rests at the weekend. But he works on Saturday.

A: When does he get up?

B: Early! He gets up at half past six and he has breakfast at seven.

Model answer — describe one person: "My sister is a teacher. She gets up at six thirty. She has breakfast at seven and she starts work at eight. On Monday and Wednesday she finishes late. She doesn't work at the weekend — she relaxes on Sunday."

  4. 10:45

  • 6:30 — six thirty / half past six
  • 8:15 — eight fifteen / quarter past eight
  • 10:45 — ten forty-five / quarter to eleven

🏋️ Exercise 2: Add -s correctly (he/she)

Objective: Use the spelling rules to make the he/she form.

Write the right he/she/it form of each verb:

  1. study
  2. go
  3. watch
  4. play
  5. finish
✅ Answer
  • study → studies (consonant + y → ies)
  • go → goes (add -es after o)
  • watch → watches (add -es after ch)
  • play → plays (vowel + y, so just add -s — not "plaies")
  • finish → finishes (add -es after sh)

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fill in at / on / in

Objective: Choose the right preposition of time.

  1. She starts work 9 o'clock.
  2. He doesn't work Sunday.
  3. I read the evening.
  4. My birthday is July.
  5. We relax the weekend.
  6. The cat sleeps night.
✅ Answer
  • She starts work at 9 o'clock. (clock time)
  • He doesn't work on Sunday. (a day)
  • I read in the evening. (part of the day)
  • My birthday is in July. (a month)
  • We relax at the weekend. (UK) — American English often says on the weekend.
  • The cat sleeps at night. (night is the odd one out!)

🎯 Your Project: Describe Someone's Day

Pick one person you know — a friend, your mother, a brother, a co-worker. You will describe their daily routine out loud. Use he or she with the -s form, plus times and days. Do it to a partner, a teacher, or your phone.

  1. (3 min) Choose your person. Write down 5 things they do each day, with a time (e.g. "gets up — 7:00").
  2. (3 min) Turn each one into a full sentence with -s: "She gets up at seven."
  3. (3 min) Add at least two days: "On Monday she works. On Sunday she rests."
  4. (3 min) Add one negative: "He doesn't work at the weekend."
  5. (3 min) Say the whole routine out loud, start to finish. Did you record it? Listen and check that every verb has its -s.

  • ☐ I included at least two times (e.g. "at seven")
  • ☐ I named at least two days of the week (with capital letters)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • When you speak, the easy digital way of telling time ("three forty-five") is always correct. Use it when you are not sure.
  • Say a whole sentence out loud and listen for the -s on he/she verbs. Train your ear.
  • Write days of the week with a capital letter, every time.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Dropping the -s: ❌ He work at night. → ✅ He works at night. This is the #1 beginner slip.
  • Doubling the -s: ❌ Does he works? → ✅ Does he work? The -s is on does, so the verb stays plain.
  • "have" → "has": ❌ She haves lunch. → ✅ She has lunch.
  • Wrong preposition: ❌ in Monday / on the morning → ✅ on Monday · in the morning · at night.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • To tell the time, ask "What time is it?" and answer "It's…" — the digital way (three forty-five) always works.
  • Past counts up in the first half hour; to counts down to the next hour. 12:00 = noon (day) or midnight (night).
  • The seven days start with a capital letter; Monday–Friday are weekdays, Saturday–Sunday are the weekend.
  • Use at for clock times, on for days, in for parts of the day/months — but at night.
  • For he/she/it, add -s to the verb (have → has). In negatives & questions, doesn't/does takes the -s and the main verb stays plain.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now add a clock and a calendar to your English. Even bigger — you can talk about other people, not just yourself. You can describe someone's whole day with the right times, days, and that important -s. That is real, useful English. It is a big step. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I say "three forty-five" or "quarter to four"?

Both are correct and both are common. The digital way ("three forty-five") is simpler and never wrong. Use it when you speak. Learn the "past/to" way so you understand it when others use it.

I keep forgetting the -s on he/she. Is that a problem?

It is the most normal beginner mistake there is — even fluent learners drop it sometimes. People will still understand you. Keep noticing it and saying it. Slowly it becomes easy. Confusion is part of learning, not a sign you are failing.

Is it "at the weekend" or "on the weekend"?

Both! British English says "at the weekend" and American English says "on the weekend." Choose the one for the English you study, and use it every time.
