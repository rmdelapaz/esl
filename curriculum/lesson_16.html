🔁 Lesson 6.1: How Often? — Adverbs of Frequency

You already talk about your daily routine. Now you learn to say how often you do things — always, usually, often, sometimes, hardly ever, and never. With these small words, your English about your life becomes much richer. You can tell people exactly how many times something happens.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use adverbs of frequency on a scale (always → never) to say how often you do things
  • Put the adverb in the correct position — before the main verb, but after be
  • Ask and answer "How often…?" questions with frequency phrases (once a week, twice a day, every morning)
  • Talk about jobs and workplaces and how often people do things at work

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Talk about your week out loud in 6 or more sentences, using at least four different frequency words in the correct position.

🔤 New Words in This Lesson

Here are some useful words from this lesson. Tap 🔊 to hear a word.

  • always — every time, 100% of the time
  • usually — most of the time, but not always
  • often — many times
  • sometimes — a few times, now and then
  • hardly ever — almost never, very few times
  • never — not at any time, 0% of the time
  • nurse — a person who helps sick people
  • driver — a person who drives a car, bus, or truck
  • teacher — a person who teaches in a school
  • chef — a cook in a restaurant

  • waiter — a person who brings food in a restaurant
  • doctor — a person who helps sick people get well

How Often Do You…?

In an earlier lesson, you learned to say what you do every day: "I drink coffee. I go to work. I watch TV." That is great. But real life is not so simple. Some things you do every day. Some things you do almost never. Today you learn the words that show the difference.

These words are called adverbs of frequency. "Frequency" means how often. When you say "I always drink coffee" or "I never drink coffee," you give a much clearer picture of your life. This is a small change with a big result. 🔁

🧠 Mindset

You already know these ideas in your own language. You know the difference between "every day" and "almost never." The only new thing today is the English words and where they go in the sentence. That is a small, friendly step. Do not worry about being perfect. Say your sentence, put the word in, and keep going. Little words like these make you sound much more natural. 🌱

The Frequency Scale

Think of the adverbs as a line, from the top (100%, every time) to the bottom (0%, no times). This is called a frequency scale. Read it from top to bottom.

AdverbHow often (%)Example
always100% — every timeI always have breakfast.
usuallyabout 90% — most timesI usually walk to work.
oftenabout 70% — many timesI often cook at home.
sometimesabout 40% — some timesI sometimes watch TV at night.
hardly everabout 10% — almost neverI hardly ever eat fast food.
never0% — no timesI never drink coffee at night.

💡 "Never" already means "no"

Be careful with never. It already means "no times," so we do not add don't. Say "I never smoke," not "I don't never smoke." One "no" word is enough. The verb stays plain and positive: never smoke, never eat, never go. 🚫

✅ Pro Tip

You do not need to remember the exact percentages. They are only a picture to help you feel the meaning. What matters is the order: always is the most, never is the least, and the others sit in between. Learn the order, and you can choose the right word every time. ⏰

Where to Put the Adverb

This is the most important rule in the lesson, and it is easy. There are just two cases.

1. BEFORE the main verb

With normal verbs (drink, work, go, eat), put the adverb before the verb.

SubjectAdverbVerbFull sentence
IalwaysdrinkI always drink coffee.
TheyusuallyworkThey usually work late.
WesometimesgoWe sometimes go out.
ShenevereatsShe never eats meat.

2. AFTER the verb be

With the verb be (am, is, are), the adverb goes after it, not before.

SubjectbeAdverbFull sentence
SheisalwaysShe is always late.
IamusuallyI am usually tired at night.
TheyareoftenThey are often busy.
HeisneverHe is never angry.
⚠️ Important Note: Remember the difference. With a normal verb: "She always works" (adverb before the verb). With be: "She is always late" (adverb after is). If you can find the verb be, put the adverb right after it. If not, put it before the verb.

💡 A simple memory trick

Say to yourself: "be after, verb before." The adverb comes after the word be (am/is/are), and before every other verb. Repeat that little phrase a few times, and the rule will stay with you. 🧠

Frequency Phrases: Once a Week

Sometimes you want to be exact. Instead of "often," you can say how many times. For this, English uses frequency phrases with once, twice, and times.

PhraseMeaningExample
once a weekone time in a weekI go shopping once a week.
twice a daytwo times in a dayI brush my teeth twice a day.
three times a monththree times in a monthThey visit family three times a month.
every morningeach morningShe runs every morning.
on Fridayseach FridayWe eat out on Fridays.

Notice the special words: once means "one time," and twice means "two times." After that, we just say the number + times: three times, four times, five times.

✅ Where do these phrases go?

Good news — frequency phrases like once a week and every morning are more relaxed than single adverbs. They usually go at the end of the sentence ("I clean the house once a week") or at the start ("Every morning, I make tea"). Both are correct and natural. 👍

Asking "How Often…?"

To ask about frequency, start with How often, then a normal question. This is how you learn about other people's habits.

How often + do + subject + verb …?

QuestionPossible answer
How often do you cook?I usually cook. / I cook every evening.
How often do they travel?They travel twice a year.
How often does she work at night?She often works at night.

You can answer in two ways: with an adverb ("I usually cook") or with a phrase ("I cook every evening"). Both are correct. Use the one that fits your meaning best.

💡 Don't forget "does" for he/she/it

When you ask about he, she, or it, use does, not do: "How often does she work?" And after does, the verb stays plain: work, not works. You learned this pattern before — it works the same way with "How often." 🙂

Jobs & Workplaces

Different jobs have different routines. A baker gets up very early. A nurse often works at night. Let's learn some common jobs. We can use them to practice our frequency words. Read each one out loud.

  • nurse — helps sick people (works in a hospital)
  • driver — drives a car, bus, or taxi
  • teacher — teaches students (works in a school)
  • chef — cooks food (works in a restaurant)
  • mechanic — fixes cars (works in a garage)
  • waiter — brings food to tables (works in a restaurant)
  • doctor — helps sick people get well
  • cleaner — cleans rooms and buildings
  • shop assistant — helps customers (works in a shop)

Now let's put jobs and frequency words together. Read these sentences out loud:

  • A nurse usually works at night.
  • A teacher always starts work in the morning.
  • A chef often works late in the evening.
  • A driver sometimes works on the weekend.
  • A mechanic hardly ever works at night.
  • A shop assistant is usually busy on Saturdays.

💡 "a" before a job

When you say someone's job, put a (or an) in front: "She is a nurse," "He is a driver," "I am an engineer." Use an before a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u). Don't say "She is nurse." — always add the little word a. 👍

Dialogue & Phrases

Here are two friends, Ben and Lucy. Lucy is a nurse. They talk about how often they do things. Read both parts out loud. Look for every frequency word.

Ben: You look tired, Lucy. How often do you work at night?

Lucy: I'm a nurse, so I usually work at night. About three times a week.

Ben: Three times a week! Do you sleep in the day, then?

Lucy: Yes, I always sleep in the morning after a night shift. But I'm often tired.

Ben: I understand. I'm a teacher, so I never work at night. I start at eight every morning.

Lucy: Lucky you! How often do you exercise?

Ben: I sometimes go running, maybe once a week. I hardly ever go to the gym.

Lucy: Me too. On my days off, I usually just relax at home.

Here are the best phrases from the conversation. Keep them ready for your project.

How often do you…? I usually work at night. About three times a week. I always sleep in the morning. I never work at night. I sometimes go running. I hardly ever go to the gym. I'm often tired.

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the adverb in the right place

Objective: Write the adverb in the correct position — before a normal verb, but after be.

Type only the adverb in the gap where it belongs. The adverb to use is in brackets.

  1. (always) I drink tea in the morning.
  2. (never) She eats meat.
  3. (usually) He is late for work.
  4. (often) We go to the beach.
  5. (sometimes) They are busy on Fridays.
  6. (hardly ever) I watch TV.
✅ Answer

1. always (before the verb drink) · 2. never (before eats) · 3. usually (after is) · 4. often (before go) · 5. sometimes (after are) · 6. hardly ever (before watch)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Choose the right frequency word

Objective: Pick the adverb or phrase that matches the meaning.

Read the clue, then write the best frequency word in the gap.

  1. 100% of the time, every time → I lock the door. (always / never)
  2. 0% of the time, not at any time → I smoke. (never / usually)
  3. almost never, only 10% → She eats sweets. (hardly ever / often)
  4. one time in a week → I clean the house a week. (once / twice)
  5. two times in a day → I take the medicine a day. (once / twice)
  6. most of the time, about 90% → We walk to school. (usually / hardly ever)
✅ Answer

1. always · 2. never · 3. hardly ever · 4. once · 5. twice · 6. usually

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Put the adverb before a normal verb: I always work, they never eat.
  • Put the adverb after the verb be: She is usually happy, we are often busy.
  • Learn the order of the scale, from always down to never.
  • Use frequency phrases for exact numbers: once a week, twice a day, every morning.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I don't never work." ❌ — Never already means "no." Say I never work. ✅
  • "I drink always tea." ❌ — The adverb goes before a normal verb. Say I always drink tea. ✅
  • "She always is late." ❌ — With be, the adverb goes after. Say She is always late. ✅
  • "I go to the gym two times." — This is not wrong, but twice a week sounds more natural for "two times."
  • "He is nurse." ❌ — Add the little word a: He is a nurse. ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Adverbs of frequency say how often: always (100%) → usually → often → sometimes → hardly ever → never (0%).
  • Position: before a normal verb (I always work), but after the verb be (She is always late).
  • Never already means "no" — don't add don't.
  • Frequency phrases: once a week, twice a day, three times a month, every morning, on Fridays.
  • Ask about habits with "How often do/does…?"

🎉 What You've Accomplished

Your English about daily life just got much richer. Before, you could say what you do. Now you can say how often you do it — and that small difference means a lot. You can talk about your week, ask other people about their habits, and describe how often people do things at work. These little words make you sound natural and real. Be proud of this step. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can frequency adverbs go at the start or end of a sentence?

Sometimes and usually can sometimes go at the start ("Sometimes I cook"). But the safe, correct place for all of them is the middle — before the main verb, or after be. Start there. Longer phrases like once a week are the ones that go at the end.

What is the difference between "hardly ever" and "never"?

Never means zero times — not at all, ever. Hardly ever means almost never — a very small number of times, but not zero. So "I never eat fast food" means never, and "I hardly ever eat fast food" means once in a long while.

Do I say "two times a week" or "twice a week"?

Both are understood, but twice a week is more natural for two times. For one time, say once. For three or more, use the number + times: three times a week, four times a month.
