🗣️ Lesson 4.1: IELTS Speaking Parts 1–3 — Fluency & Strategy

The Speaking test is the one part of IELTS that feels like a real conversation — because it is. For 11 to 14 minutes you sit with an examiner and talk. That's less time than it takes to make a coffee, yet nerves make many capable speakers freeze, give one-word answers, and score below their true level. This lesson maps the three parts and gives you the core survival skill: extending your answers so you never dry up.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the three parts of the Speaking test and what each one demands
  • Extend your answers — never one word — using the "answer + why + example" pattern
  • Deliver the Part 2 long turn using the cue card and your one prep minute
  • Handle Part 3's abstract discussion with opinion, support, and speculation

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Record full answers to a sample Part 1 set, a Part 2 cue card (1 min prep, ~2 min talk), and Part 3 questions — then self-review for extension and fluency.

The Interview & Its Three Parts

IELTS Speaking is an 11–14 minute face-to-face interview with a trained examiner — in person or by video call, never with a machine. It's recorded, and it always follows the same three-part shape. That predictability is a gift: you can't know the exact questions, but you can rehearse the format until it feels routine. You prepare for the test; you do not memorise it.

PartTimeWhat happensWhat it tests
Part 1
Interview		4–5 minIntro + ID check, then questions on familiar topics: home, work or study, hobbies, daily lifeShort but extended answers on everyday subjects
Part 2
Long turn		3–4 minA cue card with a topic + prompts; 1 minute to prepare and make notes, then speak ~2 minutes uninterrupted; a rounding-off questionSustaining a monologue; organising ideas
Part 3
Discussion		4–5 minAbstract discussion linked to the Part 2 topic: opinions, comparisons, speculationDeveloping ideas; abstract, analytical language

💡 The topic thread

Parts 2 and 3 are linked. If your Part 2 cue card is "Describe a skill you would like to learn," Part 3 will widen out — "Why do some skills disappear over time?", "Should schools teach more practical skills?" The examiner moves from the personal and concrete to the general and abstract. Expecting that shift stops it surprising you.

Extending Answers — The Core Skill

If you learn one thing from this lesson, learn this: never give a one-word or single-sentence answer. Short answers starve the examiner of language to assess and cap your fluency score — you simply don't give enough evidence to mark you highly. The fix is a habit you can build in a day: the answer + why + example / detail pattern.

⚠️ The band-limiting mistake: the one-word answer

Answering "Do you like cooking?" with "Yes, I do." and stopping is the single most common way to score below your level. It gives the examiner nothing to reward — no vocabulary, no grammar range, no fluency. Under nerves, candidates shrink their answers exactly when they should extend them. Train the opposite reflex.

Ex: Do you enjoy cooking?

Weak: Yes, I do. (Full stop. The examiner waits, then has to ask another question — you've wasted an opportunity.)

Ex: Do you enjoy cooking?

Strong: Yes, I really do — it's become my main way to unwind after work (why). I mostly cook Filipino dishes my mum taught me, like chicken adobo, which only takes half an hour (example + detail). Honestly, the chopping and stirring feel almost meditative.

The strong answer is only three sentences, but it delivers a reason, a concrete example, and a personal reflection — plenty for the examiner to mark. Here is the pattern and the ways to grow an answer:

MoveWhat you addExample continuation
AnswerDirectly respond first"Yes, I love it…"
WhyA reason or cause"…because it lets me relax."
ExampleA specific instance"For instance, last weekend I…"
ContrastCompare with something else"Unlike baking, which I find stressful…"
ExperienceA personal story"I remember the first time I…"
HypotheticalAn imagined case"If I had more time, I'd…"

📏 How far to extend?

Part 1: aim for 2–3 sentences — extended, but don't ramble; the examiner has many questions to get through. Part 3: develop fully — three or four sentences that build an argument. Match the depth to the part.

Phrase bank — extending your answer:

…because… The main reason is… For example / For instance… A good example of this is… What I mean is… This is especially true when… Unlike…, I find… I remember once…

Part 2: The Cue-Card Long Turn

Part 2 frightens people because you speak alone for two minutes with no help. But it's the most controllable part — you get a full minute to prepare and a pencil and paper for notes. Use them well and the two minutes look after themselves.

✅ Use the prep minute deliberately

Don't try to write sentences — you can't in 60 seconds. Instead, jot keywords for each bullet plus a personal angle (a real or invented memory to anchor the talk). Then structure the two minutes: a short intro, cover each prompt in turn, and end with a feeling or reflection. It is completely fine to invent details — you're marked on English, not honesty.

📋 Sample cue card

Describe a skill you would like to learn.

You should say:

  • what the skill is
  • why you want to learn it
  • how you would learn it

and explain how this skill would change your life.

🗒️ My prep-minute notes (keywords only):

skill = playing the piano · why = always loved music, calming, regret from childhood · how = app + weekend lessons, second-hand keyboard · change = confidence, play at family parties, share with kids one day

🎙️ Model long turn (~2 minutes)

"The skill I'd most love to learn is playing the piano. I've always been drawn to music, but I never had the chance as a child, and that's something I genuinely regret.

The main reason I want to learn it is that playing an instrument seems incredibly calming — a way to switch off from screens and just be present. There's also something about creating music yourself, rather than only listening, that really appeals to me.

As for how I'd go about it, I'd probably start with one of those learning apps to pick up the basics, then buy a cheap second-hand keyboard so I could practise at home. Ideally I'd take a proper lesson at the weekend too, because a real teacher spots the bad habits an app can't.

And honestly, I think it would change my life in small but meaningful ways. I'd feel more confident, I could play at family gatherings instead of just watching, and one day I'd love to teach my own children. So yes — the piano is definitely at the top of my list."

Notice: an intro that names the skill, each bullet covered in order, discourse markers signposting the structure, and a warm reflection to finish — filling the full two minutes.

Phrase bank — buying thinking time & structuring the long turn:

Let me start by saying… The skill I'd choose is… There are a couple of reasons for this… As for how I'd… Another thing worth mentioning is… Coming back to… And finally… So, all in all…

💡 Keep going until the examiner stops you

Don't finish at 45 seconds and sit in silence. If you run out of your bullets early, add more — another reason, an example, a comparison. The examiner will say "Thank you" when your time is up; that's your cue to stop, not before.

Part 3: The Discussion

Part 3 is a mini-discussion of the ideas behind your Part 2 topic. The questions turn abstract — "Why do…?", "Do you think…?", "How might… change in future?" — and one-line answers won't survive here. Treat each question as a small argument: give an opinion, justify it, consider another view, and where useful compare or speculate about the future.

💬 Part 3 question + strong answer

Examiner: Do you think schools should teach more practical skills, like cooking or managing money?

Candidate: "I'd definitely say so (opinion). So many young people leave school able to solve equations but with no idea how to budget or cook a healthy meal, and those are the skills you actually use every single day (justification). That said, I can see the counter-argument (other view) — teachers are already stretched, and academic subjects matter for university. So perhaps the answer isn't replacing anything but weaving practical skills into the existing timetable (resolution). In fact, I suspect we'll see more of this in future (speculation) as the cost of living pushes financial literacy up the agenda."

Opinion → reason → concession → resolution → a forward-looking prediction. That's Band-7-plus reasoning.

Phrase bank — opinion, hedging & speculation:

I'd argue that… Personally, I tend to think… It depends largely on… On the one hand… on the other… Some people would say…, but… I suppose it's possible that… In the long run, I imagine… It's likely that… will…

💡 "Hedging" makes you sound more advanced, not less sure

Words like tend to, arguably, it's likely, I suppose let you handle uncertain, abstract ideas gracefully. They show lexical range and let you take a position without over-claiming. Sprinkle them in — but don't hide behind them so much that you never actually answer.

Fluency & Coherence

Fluency & Coherence is one of the four Speaking criteria (with Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, and Pronunciation — the full detail comes next lesson). Fluency is not about speed. It's about keeping going at a natural pace, without long freezes or constant self-correction, and linking your ideas so the listener can follow.

The trick when your mind goes blank is to keep the flow with a discourse marker rather than stopping dead. A short filler buys you a second to think and sounds far more natural than silence:

Phrase bank — natural fillers to keep flowing (don't overuse):

Well, that's an interesting question… I suppose… The thing is… Let me think for a second… To be honest… Actually, now I think about it… I mean…

⚠️ Do NOT memorise scripted answers

Examiners are trained to spot rehearsed, memorised speeches — the rhythm changes, the eyes drift, the answer doesn't quite fit the question — and it hurts your score. Prepare topics and vocabulary, not scripts. A slightly imperfect, genuine answer beats a fluent-sounding recital every time. Rehearse the skill of extending and structuring, and let the words come fresh on the day.

🧠 Mindset

Speaking is a trainable skill, not a verdict on how "naturally talkative" you are. Quiet people pass with high bands all the time — because fluency here means developed, connected answers, not chattiness or a perfect accent. Every time you turn a one-word reply into a three-sentence one, you're literally practising the thing being scored. Record yourself, wince a little, and do it again. That loop is where the band comes from. 🌱

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back, copying the rhythm and intonation. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These are natural spoken answers of the kind that keep you fluent across Parts 1 to 3.

  • That's an interesting question — I'd have to say I really enjoy spending time outdoors.
  • The main reason is that it helps me relax after a long and busy week.
  • To be honest, I'd never really thought about it, but I suppose I'd choose the summer.
  • I'd argue that both options have their advantages, though I tend to prefer the first.
  • In the long run, I imagine cities will become far greener than they are today.

Practice & Your Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Turn short answers into extended ones

Objective: Apply the answer + why + example pattern.

Each reply below is too short. Rewrite it as a proper 2–3 sentence Part 1 answer.

  1. Ex: Do you use public transport? — "Yes, sometimes."
  2. Ex: Is your hometown a good place to live? — "Yes, it's nice."
✅ Answer (models — judge by extension, not exact words)

1. "Yes, quite often, actually — I take the metro to work most days (answer + detail) because driving in the city centre is a nightmare and parking costs a fortune (why). This morning, for example, I read half a book on the way in (example)."

2. "It really is — I'd say it's a great place to live (answer). It's small enough that everyone's friendly, but it still has decent cafés and a park by the river (why + detail). Unlike the big city where I studied, I actually know my neighbours here (contrast)."

🏋️ Exercise 2: Plan a Part 2 talk from the bullets

Objective: Practise the prep minute — keyword notes only.

You have 60 seconds. Make keyword notes (not sentences) for this cue card:

Describe a place you like to visit in your free time. You should say: where it is · how often you go · what you do there · and explain why you like it.

✅ Answer (a model note-set)

where = the old harbour, 20 min from home · how often = most Sunday mornings · what = walk, coffee, watch boats, photos · why = quiet before crowds, clears my head, memories of dad taking me as a kid → reflection: my "reset button"

Notice it's keywords, not sentences, one cluster per bullet, plus a personal angle (dad) and a closing reflection to grow the talk to two minutes.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Answer a Part 3 question fully

Objective: Opinion + justification + another view.

Answer aloud, then check your answer contains all three moves:

Do you think people travel too much these days?

✅ Answer (model)

"It's a real double-edged sword (opinion/hedge). On one hand, cheap flights have let ordinary people see the world and understand other cultures, which is surely a good thing (justification). On the other hand, the environmental cost is enormous, and some beautiful places are being ruined by sheer numbers of tourists (other view). So I wouldn't say people travel too much exactly, but perhaps too thoughtlessly — and I suspect we'll need to travel more responsibly in future (speculation)." Check yours has: a clear stance, at least one reason, and a counter-view.

🎯 Your Task: Record a Full Speaking Run

Do this under real timing, recording on your phone so you can review it.

  1. (4 min) Part 1: Record extended answers to: Where do you live? Do you work or study? What do you do in your free time? Do you prefer mornings or evenings? Aim for 2–3 sentences each.
  2. (1 min prep + 2 min talk) Part 2: Write your own cue card (e.g. "Describe a skill you would like to learn"), take your prep minute of keyword notes, then talk for the full two minutes.
  3. (4 min) Part 3: Record fuller answers to: Why do some skills become less common over time? Should schools teach more practical skills?
  4. (5 min) Self-review: Play it back. Did any answer stop at one line? Did you fill the two minutes? Where did you freeze — and could a filler have kept you flowing?

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Every Part 1 answer had at least a reason or example (no one-word replies)
  • ☐ I used my prep minute for keyword notes, not full sentences
  • ☐ My Part 2 long turn filled roughly the full two minutes
  • ☐ My Part 3 answers gave an opinion and support (and a counter-view where I could)
  • ☐ I listened back and noted one thing to improve next time

Partner/tutor option: Have someone act as examiner and ask the questions live, then give you one piece of feedback per part. A live listener recreates the pressure — and the useful nerves — of the real thing.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Extend every answer — answer + why + example — so the examiner always has language to reward.
  • Use your prep minute for keyword notes and a personal angle; then keep talking until you're stopped.
  • Treat Part 3 as a discussion — opinion, justification, a counter-view, a little speculation.
  • Use discourse markers to keep flowing when you need a moment to think.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • One-word answers: the number-one score-limiter — you give the examiner nothing to mark.
  • Memorised scripts: examiners spot rehearsed speeches and penalise them; prepare topics and vocab, not scripts.
  • Stopping early in Part 2: silence before "Thank you" leaves marks on the table — always have one more reason ready.
  • Freezing in Part 3: don't panic at abstract questions; buy a second with a filler, then give an opinion and back it up.
  • Chasing speed: fluency is smooth, connected speech — not fast speech. Slow, clear, and developed beats fast and thin.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Speaking is an 11–14 min interview in three parts: familiar topics, a cue-card long turn, and an abstract discussion.
  • The core skill is extending answers — answer + why + example — never one word.
  • Part 2: use the prep minute for keyword notes + a personal angle, structure the talk, and fill the full two minutes.
  • Part 3: give an opinion, justify it, consider other views, and speculate — a mini-discussion, not a quiz.
  • Never memorise scripts; prepare topics and vocab, and use fillers to stay fluent, not fast.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You now know the shape of the whole Speaking test and — more importantly — the one habit that lifts most candidates: turning thin answers into developed ones. You've planned a cue-card talk, built abstract Part 3 answers with real reasoning, and recorded yourself to hear the difference. That self-review loop is exactly how examiners' bands are earned. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Does my accent affect my score?

Not in the way people fear. You're marked on being understood, not on sounding British or American. A clear accent from any background can reach Band 9. Pronunciation is one criterion — we cover it fully next lesson — but it's about clarity, stress, and intonation, not erasing where you're from.

Can I ask the examiner to repeat or explain a question?

Yes — politely asking for repetition ("Sorry, could you say that again?") is fine and natural, especially in Part 3. In Part 1 you can also ask what a word means. It won't lower your score; freezing in confused silence would. Don't overuse it, though.

What if I run out of things to say in Part 2?

Add a reason, an example, or a comparison to something else — the extension techniques from this lesson work in the long turn too. It's fine to invent details. Keep going until the examiner says "Thank you."
