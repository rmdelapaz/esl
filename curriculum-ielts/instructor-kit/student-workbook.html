Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Write, Listen, Say, Read, Timed or With a partner. In Timed tasks, work to the clock and then mark your own answers honestly: that is how you learn where your marks go.

per-skill minimum = the lowest band you may have in any one skill (for example, "no band below 6.0") · transfer time = extra minutes to copy answers onto the answer sheet · to prioritize = to put first

Grammar box — The test map
Listeningabout 30 min · 4 recordings · 40 questionsYou hear each recording once.
Reading60 min · 3 long passages · 40 questionsNo extra transfer time: about 20 min per passage.
Writing60 min · Task 1: describe a graph, chart, table, map or process (150+ words) · Task 2: an essay (250+ words)Task 2 counts twice as much: about 20 + 40 min.
Speaking11–14 min · Part 1 interview · Part 2 talk from a cue card (1 min to prepare, up to 2 min to speak) · Part 3 discussionAlways with a real examiner.

The overall band = the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band.

Average ends in….0 / .5 → stays.125 → down (6.125 → 6.0).25 → up to .5 (6.25 → 6.5).375 → up (6.375 → 6.5).625 → down (6.625 → 6.5).75 → up to the next whole band (6.75 → 7.0)

Say scores like this: "six point five" or "six and a half." Not: "six comma five."

Three strategic principles: 1. Manage the clock: a hard question is worth one mark, the same as an easy one. 2. Never leave a blank: there is no penalty for a wrong answer. 3. Answer exactly what was asked: "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" means a three-word answer scores zero.

Grammar box — The paraphrase ladder

Base sentence: Young people pollute the air when they drive cars. Climb at least two rungs.

5 · General wordcars → private transport · apples and pears → fruit
4 · Structurewhen they drive → by driving · Although… → …; however, …
3 · Voicedrivers cause pollution → pollution is caused by drivers
2 · Word classpollute (verb) → pollution (noun) · decide → make a decision
1 · Synonymyoung people → adolescents · cars → vehicles

Result: Adolescents contribute to air pollution through their use of vehicles. (rungs 1, 2 and 4)

Two failures. Too little: one word swapped, same sentence. Too much: the meaning changes (some → all, may → will, affordable → cheap). Keep numbers, quantities and hedges exactly.

Word families. The sentence decides the form. Listen for the stress: it often moves.

Grammar box — Range and accuracy

What the grammar criterion rewards (paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors):

BandRangeAccuracy
5mostly simple sentences; complex ones are rareerrors are frequent and can make reading difficult
6a mix of simple and complex sentencessome errors, but the meaning stays clear
7a variety of complex structuresmany sentences have no errors at all
8a wide range, used flexiblymost sentences are error-free; only occasional slips

From 5 to 6: add range. From 6 to 7: keep that range accurate.

The toolkit.

Subordinate clauseAlthough car use rose, bus use also increased. (Not: Although…, but…)
Relative clauseStudents who work part-time… (defining, no commas) · The bridge, which opened in 1932, … (non-defining, commas)
ConditionalsIf cities invest in cycling, congestion will fall. · If fees were lower, more people would enroll. (Not: If it will continue)
Participle clauseHaving doubled in a decade, the population then stabilized.
Noun clauseWhat is clear is that demand will keep growing. · The fact that… · One reason why… is that…
ComparisonSpending was far higher in 2020 than in any earlier year.

Tense by task. Task 1: read the dates first (2000–2015 → rose · 2012 to now → has risen · forecast → is expected to rise) · a process → present passive (is heated) · Task 2 → present simple for opinions, conditionals for possibilities.

The band-limiting seven: articles · subject–verb agreement (The number of students is) · prepositions (an increase in, responsible for, an effect on) · comma splices · uncountables (information, advice, research) · word form (affect = verb, effect = noun) · one structure on repeat.

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Listen, Read, Write, Say or With a partner. Some exercises are timed: your teacher will tell you the time limit, and you work alone and in silence, as in the real test. After every timed exercise you mark your answers strictly and fill in the error tally. Marking strictly may feel hard, but it shows you exactly where your marks are going, and that is the fastest way to win them back.

In the partner tasks, you use an invented identity card, so you never need to share your own personal details. In discussions you can always say "Pass."

Paths of Desire: What Shortcuts Reveal About Cities

A  Walk across almost any park, campus or housing development and you will find them: narrow strips of worn earth cutting across the grass, often ending exactly where a paved path turns a corner. Planners call these informal routes "desire paths" or "desire lines," because they record where people actually want to go rather than where designers expected them to walk. For decades, they were treated as a maintenance problem, to be fenced off or re-seeded. Increasingly, however, researchers see them as a free and remarkably honest source of data.

B  The reasons people create such paths are less mysterious than they first appear. The most obvious is distance: a pedestrian facing a right-angled path will usually cut the corner if doing so saves more than a few steps. Yet distance is not the whole story. A study of three university campuses led by the urban geographer Hana Kowalski found that paths also formed where the official route passed through poorly lit areas or alongside busy parking lots, suggesting that walkers weigh comfort and perceived safety alongside time. In a small number of cases, paths appeared even though they were longer than the paved alternative.

C  Once a path begins, it tends to reinforce itself. Grass that has been walked on a few times becomes thinner and easier to see, and a visible line invites the next walker to follow it. Kowalski's team estimated that on dry ground as few as fifteen crossings a week could stop a path from recovering, while on wet clay soil the figure was lower still. Walkers rarely notice that they are doing this; asked afterwards, most say they simply followed the obvious route. This feedback effect means that an entire route can be established within a single semester, long before any complaint reaches a maintenance office.

D  Some institutions have turned this process to their advantage. When a new medical campus opened in the city of Aldport in 2011, its designers deliberately delayed laying most footpaths. For the first year, the grounds were simply planted with grass, and aerial photographs were taken each month. Only after the main desire lines had become clear were they paved, and a follow-up survey found that far fewer shortcuts appeared than on comparable sites. The approach was not free, however: temporary lighting and signs had to be installed, and some staff complained about muddy shoes during the wet winter months.

E  Digital technology has since made the method cheaper. Anonymous location data from phones, collected with users' consent, can reveal walking patterns across a whole district in weeks rather than a year, and drones can photograph worn grass far more cheaply than aircraft. In one district, a single month of phone data identified more than forty informal routes that no survey had recorded. Critics warn, however, that such data may over-represent younger people, who are more likely to carry smartphones and share their location, and may therefore miss the routes used by older residents or by people with limited mobility, for whom a shortcut across uneven ground may be no shortcut at all.

F  Perhaps the most important lesson of desire paths is not technical but philosophical. They suggest that people are not obstacles to good design but participants in it. A planner who treats every worn strip of grass as disobedience will spend years putting up fences; one who treats it as feedback may discover, at little cost, how a place is really used. As one landscape architect put it, "The public has already drawn the plan. Our job is to read it."

Lighting the Night Differently

A  For more than a century, street lighting followed a simple rule: brighter was better. Lamps were judged by how much light they produced, and a well-lit street was assumed to be a safe one. In the last twenty years, however, that assumption has been questioned from several directions at once, and cities around the world are now rethinking how, where and when they light the night.

B  The first push came from technology. Light-emitting diodes, or LEDs, use far less electricity than the older sodium lamps they replace, and they last several times longer. For city budgets, the argument was hard to resist. The engineer Marcus Hale, who advised on the replacement of 30,000 streetlights in the city of Brenton, estimates that the switch cut the city's lighting bill by more than half within five years, although the initial purchase cost was considerable.

C  Yet the new lamps brought an unexpected problem. Early LEDs produced a cold, bluish-white light, and many residents found it harsh; complaints about light shining into bedrooms were common. The sleep researcher Dr. Priya Nair argues that blue-rich light at night can delay the body's release of melatonin, a hormone that helps regulate sleep, and she recommends warmer-colored lamps for residential streets. She stresses, however, that the effect of streetlights is small compared with that of phones and computer screens used in bed.

D  Wildlife researchers have raised similar concerns. Many insects are drawn to artificial light, and some studies have linked brighter streets with fewer moths and other night-flying insects in the surrounding area. Bats that hunt in darkness may avoid lit routes altogether. For ecologists, the question is not only how bright a lamp is, but also which way its light travels: a lamp that sends light sideways and upward affects a far wider area than one that points straight down.

E  Perhaps the most surprising finding concerns safety itself. When Brenton dimmed its lights by forty percent after midnight on quieter streets, some residents feared a rise in crime. According to the city council, reported crime on those streets did not rise over the following two years. Hale is cautious about the result: he notes that crime was already falling across the country during the same period, and that busy streets and the town center were never dimmed.

F  What is emerging is a more flexible approach. Instead of treating light as a fixed utility, some cities now use "smart" lamps that brighten when a pedestrian or cyclist approaches and dim again when the street is empty. Brenton council plans to install such systems on all residential streets by 2030. Nair welcomes the idea but argues that residents should be consulted street by street, since the needs of a hospital entrance and a quiet dead-end street are very different.
