C · Part 2 Cue Cards with Part 3 Questions (4.1 · 4.2 · 4.3)

Ten original cue cards. Print the card on the front and the examiner's section (rounding-off question + Part 3) on the back, or cut them apart and staple the examiner strip to the card. The candidate sees only the cue card. None of these cards asks about a hard time, a journey to a new country, or a family member far away, and every card can be answered with invented details.

Cue card 1

Describe a teacher or trainer who helped you learn something.

You should say: who this person was · what they taught you · how they taught it
and explain why you still remember this person.

Examiner: Rounding off: Do you still keep in touch with this person? · Part 3: What makes a good teacher? · Will online learning ever replace classroom teachers? · Should adults keep studying after they finish school?
Cue card 2

Describe a time you had to wait for something.

You should say: what you were waiting for · where you were · how long you waited
and explain how you felt while you were waiting.

Examiner: Rounding off: Are you usually a patient person? · Part 3: Are people less patient than they were in the past? · Which services in cities should be faster? · Can waiting ever be useful?
Cue card 3

Describe a park or green space you like to visit.

You should say: where it is · how often you go there · what you do there
and explain why you like it.

Examiner: Rounding off: Would you recommend it to a visitor? · Part 3: Why do cities need green spaces? · Who should pay for parks: the city, businesses or local people? · How might city parks be different in fifty years?
Cue card 4

Describe a skill you learned from a friend, neighbor or colleague.

You should say: what the skill is · who taught you · how you learned it
and explain how useful this skill has been.

Examiner: Rounding off: Have you taught this skill to anyone else? · Part 3: Which practical skills do young people need most today? · Do people learn more from screens than from other people now? · Should practical skills be taught in schools?
Cue card 5

Describe a piece of advice you found useful.

You should say: what the advice was · who gave it to you · when you received it
and explain why it was useful.

Examiner: Rounding off: Do you often ask other people for advice? · Part 3: Why do people ask strangers online for advice? · Is advice from experts always better than advice from friends? · What kind of advice do young people need most?
Cue card 6

Describe a day out that you enjoyed.

You should say: where you went · who you were with · what you did
and explain why you enjoyed it so much.

Examiner: Rounding off: Would you go there again? · Part 3: Why do people travel for leisure? · How does tourism affect the places people visit? · Will virtual tours ever replace real visits?
Cue card 7

Describe a meal you enjoyed sharing with other people.

You should say: what you ate · where you ate it · who you shared it with
and explain why the meal was special.

Examiner: Rounding off: Do you often eat with other people? · Part 3: Are shared meals less common than in the past? · How has fast food changed the way people eat? · Should schools teach young people to cook?
Cue card 8

Describe a goal you would like to achieve in the next few years.

You should say: what the goal is · why it matters to you · what you need to do to achieve it
and explain how you'll feel if you achieve it.

Examiner: Rounding off: Have you told other people about this goal? · Part 3: Is it important for people to set goals? · Do young people care more about work-life balance than career success now? · Why do many people give up on their goals?
Cue card 9

Describe a building in your town or city that you find interesting.

You should say: where it is · what it looks like · what it's used for
and explain why you find it interesting.

Examiner: Rounding off: Have you ever been inside it? · Part 3: Should old buildings be preserved or replaced? · How do buildings affect how people feel? · What will homes look like in the future?
Cue card 10

Describe a website or app that helps you in everyday life.

You should say: what it is · how you found it · what you use it for
and explain why it's so helpful to you.

Examiner: Rounding off: Do you think you'll still use it in five years? · Part 3: Does technology make life simpler or more complicated? · Should children have limits on screen time? · What jobs might disappear because of technology?

D · Mock-Examiner Kit (4.1 · 4.3)

The examiner script is written in our own words; real examiners follow their own fixed frame. What matters is the shape: friendly, neutral, no teaching, no chatting.

Examiner script

Say only these words

Opening: "Good morning / afternoon. My name is ___. Can you tell me your full name, please? … Thank you."

Part 1: "In this first part, I'd like to ask you some questions about yourself." (Ask the card's questions. If an answer is very short, wait two seconds, then ask "Why?" once.)

Part 2: "Now I'd like you to speak about a topic for up to two minutes. Here's your card, some paper and a pencil. You have one minute to prepare." (After 1 min:) "Please start speaking now." (At 2 min:) "Thank you." (Then the rounding-off question.)

Part 3: "We've been talking about ___. Now I'd like to ask you some more general questions about this." (Ask the Part 3 questions. You may ask "Why do you think that is?")

End: "Thank you. That's the end of the speaking test."

Nerves toolkit card (keep it)

Calm, prepared, on strategy

  1. Breathe out longer than in: in for 4, out for 6, three times.
  2. Self-talk: "One mark each: guess, mark it, move on." · "The recording moves on; so do I." · "It's a conversation, not an interrogation."
  3. If–then plans: decide now what you'll do if something goes wrong.
  4. Routine: a normal breakfast, arrive early, no last-minute cramming.
  5. Remember: nerves are normal. They mean you care.
Teacher's note: check-in, results and remarks (factual, general)

Check-in usually includes an ID check and a photograph, and at some centers a fingerprint scan. Say this neutrally and in advance; don't role-play it. Results are released online first and then as a Test Report Form; computer-delivered results usually arrive sooner than paper-based ones. A candidate who believes a score is wrong can ask the test center for a remark (an "enquiry on results") within a limited period after the test; they can usually choose which sections, a fee is charged, and it is refunded if the band score goes up. Candidates can retake the full test at any time, and many locations offer a one skill retake. Receiving organizations set their own rules on what they accept. Don't quote fees, deadlines or turnaround times: they vary and change. Send learners to their test provider's official information.

H · Game — Keep Talking! (C14 warmer · early finishers)

How to play (groups of 3–4, one die, one counter each): roll and move. Do the task on your square aloud. The group judges with the cheat sheet: good = stay; not yet = go back 1. 4.1-B · A model long turn (C12 strategy stage · slide 5)

Cue card: Describe a piece of technology you find useful in your daily life. You should say: what it is · how long you have had it · how you use it · and explain why it is so useful to you. Learners tick each bullet as you cover it and note the signposts. About two minutes.

The piece of technology I'd like to talk about isn't a phone or a laptop, which might surprise you. It's my rice cooker, and in particular the one with a timer that I bought about three years ago, when I started working longer hours. Before that, I had a very basic one, and honestly, I didn't think a kitchen gadget could make much difference to my life. As for how I use it, it's pretty simple. In the morning, before I leave for work, I rinse the rice, add the water, and set the timer for six o'clock. So when I get home in the evening, tired and hungry, the rice is already hot and ready. On weekends I've also started using it for other things, like steaming vegetables or even making porridge, which I didn't know was possible until a friend showed me. The main reason it's so useful is that it saves me time and, to be honest, it saves me money too. Without it, I'd probably order takeout three or four nights a week, simply because I'd be too tired to cook. Now I just make something quick to go with the rice, and I eat much more healthily. Another thing worth mentioning is that it's incredibly reliable: in three years, it's never let me down once. So, all in all, it's not the most exciting piece of technology in the world, but it's definitely the one that has made the biggest difference to my everyday routine.

Signposts to notice: The piece of technology I'd like to talk about… · As for how I use it… · The main reason… · Another thing worth mentioning is… · So, all in all… The talk adds a comparison (the old cooker), a small story (the friend) and a hypothetical (Without it, I'd probably…) to fill two minutes.

4.1-C · A Part 3 exchange (C12 listening stage · read once; learners call out the moves)

Examiner: Do you think technology makes people's lives simpler or more complicated? · Candidate: I'd argue that, on the whole, it makes everyday tasks simpler. Things like paying bills or finding directions used to take real effort, and now they take seconds. That said, I can see why some people feel the opposite, because we're expected to be available all the time, which adds a different kind of pressure. So perhaps the answer is that technology simplifies tasks but complicates our time. In the long run, I imagine people will get better at setting limits, the way we've learned to manage other habits. · Examiner: How might homes change in the next twenty years? · Candidate: That's an interesting question. I suspect they'll become much more energy-efficient, mainly because heating and electricity keep getting more expensive. Some people would say homes will be full of smart devices, but personally, I think a lot of people will want the opposite: somewhere quiet, without screens. It wouldn't surprise me if both kinds of homes became popular.

Moves in answer 1: opinion (I'd argue that…) → reason (used to take real effort…) → other view (That said…) → resolution (So perhaps…) → future (In the long run…). Answer 2 opens with a time-buyer, then speculation (I suspect…), another view (Some people would say…) and a second speculative structure (It wouldn't surprise me if…).

4.2-A · Same idea, two bands (Workbook 4.2, Ex 1)

Read version 1 slowly and flatly, with even stress. Read version 2 with natural chunks, stress and a real pause before the paraphrase. Second listening: version 2 only.

Version 1 (about band 5): I like to go to a market near my house. It is a very nice place. There are a lot of things and it is good. I go there with my friends and we buy things and we talk. The prices are good because you can talk about the price. I like it because it is nice and it is not boring. · Version 2 (about band 7): There's a little street market about ten minutes from my apartment, and it's become my go-to place on Saturday mornings. It's a really bustling spot: dozens of stalls, fresh fruit piled high, that sort of thing. I usually go with a couple of close friends; we grab a coffee, wander around and catch up on the week. I vividly remember the first time I went: I couldn't believe the wide range of spices. What I love most is that thing — I don't know the exact word — where you argue a bit about the price and both sides end up happy. After an hour there I feel completely recharged.

Answers: 1 go-to · 2 bustling · 3 catch up · 4 vividly · 5 wide range · 6 recharged. Paraphrase: "that thing… where you argue a bit about the price and both sides end up happy" = haggling (or bargaining). Version 1 repeats nice, good, a lot of, things and uses only simple sentences; version 2 has less common words, collocations, an idiomatic expression, a paraphrase and varied structures.

4.3-A · The test-day briefing (Workbook 4.3, Ex 1)

Read in a calm, friendly teacher's voice. Learners complete the six checklist gaps.

OK, everyone, a few reminders for test day. First, and most important: bring the ID you registered with. Not a different card, not a photocopy, but the exact document you used when you booked. Without it, you can't take the test. Second, arrive well before the reporting time printed on your confirmation. Check-in takes a while: there's an ID check, a photo and, at some centers, a fingerprint scan, so give yourself plenty of time. Third, what you can take in. Water is usually allowed, but only in a clear, label-free bottle. Everything else, your phone switched off, your watch and any notes, goes in the locker. Now, the clock. In Reading, you have sixty minutes for three passages, so that's about twenty minutes per passage, and remember, there's no extra time to copy answers at the end. In Writing, spend about twenty minutes on Task 1 and about forty minutes on Task 2, because Task 2 is worth more. And finally, breathe. You've done the work. Treat the Speaking test as a conversation, not an interrogation.

Answers: 1 registered with · 2 reporting time · 3 clear (label-free) · 4 locker · 5 twenty · 6 forty.
