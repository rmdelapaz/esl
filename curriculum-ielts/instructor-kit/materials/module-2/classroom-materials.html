E · Game — Trap Bingo (2.1 review)

How to play (7–8 minutes, whole class). Each learner gets a grid. Read the calls below in any order, once each, at natural speed. Every call contains a trap: a correction, a -teen / -ty pair, a spelling, or an answer that depends on the question. Learners cross out a square only if it matches the final answer. Each grid also contains some distractor squares (the first thing said, before the correction): anyone who crosses those out may get a "false bingo." First to complete a line (across, down or diagonal) calls "Bingo!" and reads the line back; check it against your list. If it contains a distractor, the game goes on. Repeat grids in a large class: several winners are fine.

2.1-E · Part 4: the urban heat lecture (Workbook 2.1, Ex 4 · one voice · TIMED: once only, no pause)

Good morning. Today I want to look at a problem that affects most large cities: the urban heat island. Put simply, cities are warmer than the countryside around them, and the difference is greatest not in the afternoon, as many people assume, but at night. In the city our team measured, the gap on summer nights reached seven degrees. · So what causes it? The main factor is surfaces. Dark materials, roads of course, but above all roofs, because there are so many of them, absorb heat during the day and release it slowly after sunset. · The second factor is the loss of trees. Trees cool the air in two ways: they provide shade, and they release moisture through their leaves, which cools the air around them, rather like sweat cools your skin. · Then there's what we call waste heat. People often blame air-conditioning units first, and they do contribute, but in our measurements the bigger source was traffic. · Now, the good news. One simple solution is to change the color of roofs. In our trial, we painted the roofs of forty apartment buildings white. We considered a light gray, which is cheaper, but it reflected far less sunlight. · We'd hoped that temperatures on the top floors would fall by five degrees. In fact, the average drop was three: smaller than we wanted, but for residents without air conditioning, a real difference.

Answers: 31 roofs · 32 moisture · 33 traffic · 34 white · 35 3 / three. Distractors: roads (already in the notes), air conditioning, gray, five, seven.
