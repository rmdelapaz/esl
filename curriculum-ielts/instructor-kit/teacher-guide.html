Module 1 — The Test & Foundations

Four classes that give learners a map, a starting point and two language foundations. The course opens with an orientation class and a short diagnostic (C1), run calmly and framed as a map, not a verdict. Then three workshops: the format, bands and scoring of IELTS Academic, with the myths that waste learners' time and a personal study plan built on a realistic target (C2); academic vocabulary and paraphrase, the skill behind almost every Listening and Reading answer and a quarter of the Writing and Speaking marks (C3); and grammar for a high band: a controlled range of complex sentences, the right tense for each task, and an honest look at error density (C4).

3 lessons + orientation Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C4 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class Target band 6.0–7.5

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C1 is the orientation and diagnostic class; C2–C4 teach online lessons 1.1–1.3. These are exam-skills workshops, so every lesson class follows the same shape: warmer (review) → lead-in → strategy or language clarified as Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → guided practice (workbook) → timed practice → self-marking against a key or against criteria paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors → a freer pair task → targeted feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes. If you're running late, cut the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the timed practice or the self-marking: learning to work to the clock and to judge their own answers is what this course sells.
  • Present from slides/module-1-foundations.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the picture and word flashcards, the new identity cards, the C1 icebreaker and speaking-sample interviewer card, the C2 format information-gap cards and Myth or Fact? cards, the C3 Paraphrase Relay prompt cards, the C4 Part 3 frame cards with observer tallies, the Paraphrase Bingo game and all the listening scripts.
  • The diagnostic paper is assessment/diagnostic.html; its key, the raw-score → band conversion tables and the paraphrased Writing and Speaking criteria are in assessment/answer-keys.html. Both are written separately from this guide. Always follow the timings and instructions printed on the paper itself. The C1 plan below budgets about 50 minutes for its three written parts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home. The online course is self-paced; the classroom is where learners practice under time, get feedback and learn to mark themselves.
  • Feedback on three things. In every freer or timed task, take notes in three columns: accuracy (errors to fix: the number of students are, an increase of, informations, six comma five), range (well-chosen language to praise and spread: a considerable proportion of, give rise to, Although…, which…) and task (the exam habit: a blank left on the answer sheet, three words in a two-word answer, a copied prompt, a Task 1 chart described in the wrong tense). The task column is new for most learners and often the quickest band gain. In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, never with names.
  • Self-marking is a skill, so teach it. Learners mark their own timed practice from a key (C2, C3) or against short criteria (C4). Model it once: "Right answer, wrong spelling? In IELTS that's wrong. Right meaning, three words when the limit was two? Wrong." Honest self-marking now makes the Practice Test debriefs in C9 and C16 far more useful.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use British spellings and words (practise, recognise, prioritise, programme, centre, urbanisation, levelled, full stop). Teach practice, recognize, prioritize, program, center, urbanization, leveled, period. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them. IELTS accepts British and American spelling equally; the only rule is to be consistent within one answer, so tell learners to choose one and stick to it.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Know the test → know the words → control the grammar. C1 and C2 replace rumor with facts: what the four sections are, how bands are calculated, and where marks are actually lost (blanks, word limits, the clock, the question not answered). C3 builds the one language skill that runs through all four sections: paraphrase, in both directions (recognizing it in Listening and Reading, producing it in Writing and Speaking). C4 adds the other half of a high band: a reliable handful of complex structures, used accurately. Every class ends with something learners keep: a target and a study plan (C1–C2), a paraphrase bank (C3), a personal error log (C4).

High stakes, kindly: test anxiety and realistic expectations

For many learners this test decides something big: a university place, registration in their profession, a job, where their family will live. Some have taken IELTS before and paid for it more than once. Treat that with respect, and never use fear as motivation ("If you don't do this, you'll fail"). Say instead: "This is a skill we can train, and we'll train it together."

Be honest about gains. In eight weeks, a gain of about half a band is common for learners who come to class and do the homework. A full band is possible for some, especially when most of the gap is technique (timing, word limits, answering the question), but it is not guaranteed, and nobody in the room should promise it, including you. (Online lesson 1.1 mentions a rule of thumb of "about 12 weeks per half band at higher levels." Both statements fit: technique gains come early and fast; vocabulary and grammar gains come slowly.) If a learner's target is 1.5 bands above their diagnostic and their test is in six weeks, talk to them privately about options: a later test date, or a plan that focuses on the one or two skills with the biggest gap.

Scores are private. Hand back diagnostic and practice-test results on folded slips. Never rank learners aloud, never display a class list of bands, and never ask anyone to share a score. The workbook band tracker is for the learner alone.

Personal information is optional. Learners never have to say why they need IELTS, which country they come from or what happens if they miss their target. For every personal task (the course contract intro, the study-plan interview, speaking topics) offer a new identity card. Migration, visas and immigration policy are never discussion topics in this course.

Using the diagnostic and the two practice tests diagnostically

The course has three measurement points: the diagnostic (C1), Practice Test 1 (C8: timed Listening + Reading) and Practice Test 2 (C15–C16: all four skills). Present every one of them as information, not judgment. The diagnostic is short and "cold" (learners haven't met the format yet), so it usually underestimates learners; say so in advance. What matters is the pattern: which skill is weakest, which question types cost the most marks, and whether the problem is language (didn't understand) or technique (ran out of time, broke the word limit, misread the instruction). Learners record each result in the workbook band tracker and write one "next focus" line each time. Band results from the diagnostic are estimates: say "about 6.0" or "5.5 to 6.0," never a single number stated as fact.

A test-anxiety-aware orientation — what to say and do
  • Name it, then normalize it. "Almost everybody feels nervous before IELTS. Nervous energy can help you concentrate; we just don't want it to drive." Never tell a learner "Don't worry." Say "It makes sense that you're worried; this matters."
  • Your four promises (read them aloud, and they're printed in the workbook contract): 1. Your scores are private: I'll never share or compare them. 2. I'll tell you honestly where you are and what will help most. 3. Every practice test is practice: it's information for us, not a judgment of you. 4. You never have to share personal information to take part.
  • The one-breath routine (teach it once; use it before every timed task in the course): "Feet flat. Breathe in slowly for four. Hold for two. Out slowly for six. Read the first instruction." It takes 15 seconds and gives nervous learners a starting ritual. Do it with them, without comment.
  • Be careful with the stakes. Don't ask "Why do you need IELTS?" in front of the class. The My target card has an optional line ("I need IELTS for: study / work / professional registration / other / prefer not to say"); nobody reads it but you.
  • If a learner becomes distressed during the diagnostic, let them stop, step outside or simply sit. Say quietly: "That's fine. We'll finish it another time or skip it; it doesn't matter today." The diagnostic is replaceable; their trust is not.
Running the speaking sample
  • Use the questions printed in assessment/diagnostic.html and the interviewer card in the materials (set C). Three minutes per learner: about one minute of familiar questions, then a short long-turn or opinion question, as the paper describes. Keep a phone timer on the table where the learner can't see it.
  • Sit at the side of the table, not opposite (it feels less like an interrogation). Smile, but don't nod "yes" to every answer: a real examiner is neutral, and you want a natural sample. Take notes on the four areas only after the learner leaves.
  • Class of up to about 6: you'll reach everyone during the writing sample. Larger class: reach about 5 in C1, then take the rest at the side table during C2's study-plan stage and C3's controlled-practice stage (never during timed practice). Alternatively, a learner can record the sample at home: a friend or family member reads the questions from the interviewer card, or the learner reads them aloud and answers, and brings the recording on their phone.
  • With the learner's permission, record the sample on your phone so you can mark it later and play a few seconds back to them in Module 4 as a "before" recording. Delete recordings at the end of the course and say that you will.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Some learners are strong speakers but slow readers or writers, often because they learned to read in a non-Latin script or had interrupted schooling. Tell them privately before the diagnostic: "If you don't finish Reading, that's useful information, not failure. Answer what you can and guess the rest." Offer a large-print copy if you can. In the course contract, read every line aloud. On the My target card, a learner can dictate the requirement to you. Note (privately) who ran out of time: reading speed is a strategy target for Module 2.

Stretch

Learners who finish a section early should check, not relax: "Check every answer for spelling and the word limit." In the target stage, stronger learners (aiming for 7.0–7.5) add a second line: "The one sub-skill I think will be hardest to lift: ___" (for example, Reading passage 3, Task 2 coherence, Part 3 abstract answers). Ask them to bring one example of a university or professional body's exact requirement to C2 to share (the requirement, not the reason).

Watch-outs for the first class
  • Previous IELTS results. Some learners arrive with a real score from a previous test and feelings about it. Acknowledge it privately and use it: a real score is better data than our diagnostic. Never ask about it in front of the class.
  • Exam cultures. Learners from systems where tests were ranked publicly (common in many East Asian, South Asian, Middle Eastern and Eastern European schools) may expect you to post results or may ask classmates for their score. Say early and clearly: "In this class, scores are private."
  • Copying the prompt in the writing sample and memorized answers in the speaking sample are very common and tell you a lot. Don't correct them today; note them for C2 (the myth "memorized answers score well") and C3 (paraphrasing the prompt).
  • Decimal commas. On the My target card, learners who use a decimal comma (Spanish, Portuguese, French, German, Russian, Vietnamese and others) may write "6,5." Accept it today; teach "six point five" in C2.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the overall band. Not a pass or fail: a profile of four skill bands plus their average, rounded to the nearest half band. Institutions often set two numbers: an overall band and a minimum for every skill.

"Overall 6.5, no band below 6.0." Listening 7.5, Reading 7.0, Writing 5.5, Speaking 6.5: overall? (6.625 → 6.5.) Is that enough? (No! Writing is below 6.0.) So which skill do you study first? (Writing.) · Can a great Reading score rescue a low Writing score? (For the overall, a little; for the minimum, no.)
L 6.0, R 6.5, W 6.0, S 6.5. Average? (6.25.) Is 6.25 closer to 6.0 or 6.5? (Exactly in between.) What does IELTS do with .25? (Rounds up: 6.5.) And 6.125? (Down: 6.0.) And 6.75? (Up: 7.0.)

Meaning: the word limit. The limit is part of the instruction. An answer that breaks it scores zero even if the meaning is right.

"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER." Can I write one word? (Yes.) Three words? (No.) "two weeks": how many words? (One word and a number: fine.) "well-known": one word or two? (One: hyphenated words count as one.) "the library": is "the" a word? (Yes! Often you can drop it: "library.")

Meaning: technique vs language. Two learners with the same English can score half a band apart because of timing, blanks and instructions. That part improves fastest.

A learner knew the answer but wrote three words. Is that a language problem or a technique problem? (Technique.) Which is faster to fix? (Technique.) Does that mean language doesn't matter? (No: technique helps you show the language you have.)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: paraphrase. The same meaning in different words and a different structure. A good paraphrase is not shorter or longer on purpose; it is equal in meaning.

"Young people pollute the air when they drive cars." → "Adolescents contribute to air pollution through their use of vehicles." Same meaning? (Yes.) How many rungs? (Synonym: adolescents, vehicles; word class: pollute → pollution; structure: when they drive → through their use.) · "Young people pollute the air when they drive vehicles." Is that a paraphrase? (Not really: one word changed.) · "All teenagers are destroying the planet with their cars." Paraphrase? (No: the meaning has changed. "All," "destroying" = drift.)

Meaning: synonym trap. A trap sentence repeats a word from the question but says something different, or uses a near-synonym that is stronger or weaker.

Question: "Visitor numbers doubled." Text: "Visitor numbers rose considerably." Same? (No: rose is not doubled.) Text: "Visitors doubled their spending." Same word; same meaning? (No: spending, not numbers.) Text: "Twice as many people visited." (Yes: twice as many = doubled.) · "cheap" and "affordable": same? (Close, not the same: cheap can mean low quality; affordable housing is a fixed phrase.)

Meaning: word family. One root, several word classes. The grammar of the sentence decides which one you need.

"The ___ of the data took three weeks." Noun, verb or adjective? (Noun: analysis.) "She is very ___." (Adjective: analytical.) · "economic growth" vs "an economical car": which is about the economy? (Economic.) Which one saves money? (Economical.)

Paraphrase Relay + No-Echo — how to run it
  • Pairs, A and B. Each prompt card has a Task 2-style prompt (original, neutral topics: cycle lanes, online degrees, school gardens, four-day weeks, museum fees, shared workspaces…) and three "don't repeat" words printed underneath for the checker.
  • Round 1 (8 min): A reads the prompt aloud. B has 30 seconds to think, then says a one- or two-sentence introduction that restates the prompt and (optionally) gives a position. A listens for the "don't repeat" words and says which rungs B used. Swap after each card. Aim for three cards each.
  • Round 2 (6 min): A is the examiner with a No-Echo card (Part 1 questions: study, work, hometown or a new identity card's town, free time, food, technology). B answers in 2–3 sentences. A taps the table once for each echoed phrase. Swap after five questions.

  • Keep it honest: a shorter, accurate paraphrase beats a long, risky one. If B freezes, A can offer a starter from the card ("It is sometimes argued that…").

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: range and accuracy. Range = how many different structures you use; accuracy = how many of your sentences have no errors. The criterion needs both; neither alone lifts the band.

Paragraph A: five short, perfect sentences. Range? (Low.) Accuracy? (High.) Paragraph B: long sentences with many errors. Range? (Some.) Accuracy? (Low.) Is B better than A? (Not necessarily: the errors make it hard to follow.) What does band 7 want? (A variety of complex sentences, and many of them error-free.)

Meaning: error density. Not "Did I make a mistake?" but "How many of my sentences are completely correct?" One error in a long script is normal at band 7; errors in most sentences hold a script around band 5–6.

A paragraph has eight sentences; three are error-free. Is that "most"? (No, fewer than half.) What should this writer work on first: more range or more accuracy? (Accuracy.) Another writer: eight sentences, seven error-free, all simple. Next step? (Range: add complex structures she can control.)

  2. Kill the double connector: T: "Although it was expensive, but it worked." → S: "Although it was expensive, it worked." T: "Because prices rose, so people bought less." → "Because prices rose, people bought less." (or "Prices rose, so people bought less.")
  3. Date → tense: T: "1995 to 2005, rise." → S: "rose." T: "2010 to now, rise." → "have risen / has risen." T: "2030, forecast, reach." → "is expected to reach." T: "process, grind." → "is ground."
  4. Frame it (T shows a frame card and a topic; S speaks): "What is clear is that… / cycling" → "What is clear is that cycling is becoming more popular in cities." "The fact that… / screen time" → "The fact that children spend so much time on screens worries many parents." "Not only… but also…" (stretch: inversion) → "Not only does cycling reduce traffic, but it also improves health."
Grammar Upgrade workshop — how to run it
  • Groups of 3 with roles: writer (holds the pen), checker (reads every new sentence aloud and asks "Is it correct?") and counter (ticks structures on the checklist and, in Round 2, counts error-free sentences). Rotate the pen each round. Give the checker role to a confident learner who tends to over-reach: it teaches control.
  • Round 1 (8 min): Ex 5 paragraph (neighborhood library, six short error-free sentences). Checklist: ☐ 4+ different complex structures ☐ no new errors ☐ same facts ☐ no sentence longer than about 30 words. A 3-sentence answer that is correct and ranged beats a 1-sentence monster.
  • Round 2 (6 min): Ex 4 paragraph (remote study, eight sentences, nine errors from the band-limiting seven). Mark ✔ / ✘ per sentence first, then write the error density (✔ ÷ all), then correct. Groups check with the key on the slide at the end of the round.
  • Round 3 (6 min): Part 3 frame cards. The observer's tally has two columns: structures heard and errors that blurred meaning (not every slip: only ones that made the listener unsure). Each speaker gets one strength and one suggestion.
  • Share: one group reads its upgraded paragraph; the class names the structures and checks for new errors. Praise the group that removed a risky structure because they weren't sure of it: that is the strategy.
ICQs before the workshop
In Round 1, how many different complex structures? (At least four.) Can you add new facts? (No.) What's worse: a short correct sentence or a long broken one? (The long broken one.) In Round 2, what do you count? (The error-free sentences.) In Round 3, does the observer write every mistake? (No, only errors that made the meaning unclear.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a frame strip with four frames only (Although…, … · … who … · If …, … will … · far more … than …) and ask for two structures in Round 1, not four. In Round 2, underline the sentences with errors in advance so the learner's job is to find and fix, not to find. For the audit, sit with the learner and count together; point to one error type only for the error log. Target: two accurate complex sentences, written and then said aloud.

Stretch (toward 7.5+)

Flexibility, not just variety. Ask strong learners to write the same idea three ways with different structures (Although… / Despite + -ing… / …; however, …) and to say which one fits the paragraph best. Introduce inversion for emphasis (Not only does…, Rarely have…) and mixed conditionals (If the city had invested earlier, traffic would be lighter today), with the warning that one well-placed example is enough. Their error-density target is "almost all sentences error-free."

L1 watch-outs
  • "Although…, but…" and "Because…, so…": Chinese (虽然…但是 · 因为…所以), Vietnamese (tuy…nhưng · vì…nên), Korean, Japanese and Thai use paired connectors. It is one of the most frequent errors in IELTS writing. One clause, one connector.
  • Articles: speakers of Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Persian, Hindi-Urdu and Turkish drop them ("Government should build hospital"); Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian and Arabic speakers may add the to general statements ("The pollution is a problem"). Teach one rule at a time; the error log helps.
  • Comma splices: Spanish, Portuguese, Vietnamese, Chinese and Arabic writing traditions allow long chains joined by commas or and (Arabic wa). In English, two sentences need a period, a semicolon or a connector.
  • "If it will…": many languages use a future after if (for example, Russian, Polish, Chinese, Korean, Arabic). Drill "If + present, will."
  • Tense with dates: Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian/Malay mark time with words, not verb forms, so a whole Task 1 answer may stay in the present. "Read the dates first" is the fix.
  • Uncountables: "informations, advices, researches, equipments, knowledges" transfer from French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Russian and others where these nouns are countable.
  • Subject–verb agreement across long subjects: "The number of students who study abroad are…" The verb agrees with the head noun (number), not the nearest one. Everyone does this, including advanced writers.

The through-line

Hear it coming → find it fast → prove it. In C5 learners stop listening to everything and start listening for something: they predict the answer type, hear the signpost, wait for the correction and write the final version exactly. In C6 they stop reading every word: they skim to build a map, then scan for a keyword's paraphrase, and they spend their minutes where the marks are. In C7 they learn to defend each answer with evidence: point to the words that agree (True), the words that clash (False), or show that the text is silent (Not Given). The same idea runs through all three: the answer is almost never in the question's words, so every class trains learners to recognize paraphrase under time pressure. C8 puts all of it under exam conditions, and C9's warmer turns the results into a personal plan.

Stakes, nerves and realistic expectations

Your learners are sitting a high-stakes test: a university place, professional registration, a visa. Many have taken IELTS before and been a half band short of what they needed; some have spent a lot of money on it. Anxiety is normal, and it shows up most in Listening ("It goes too fast, I panic and lose everything"). Say plainly and often that these are trainable habits, not a judgment of intelligence or of their English. Never say "you'll fail" or "that's a band 5 answer"; say "that's one mark we can win back." Be honest about progress, too: across an 8-week course, a gain of about half a band is common and realistic; bigger jumps happen but are not guaranteed, and they depend on many hours of practice outside class. Listening and Reading usually move first, because technique alone recovers marks, so these two classes are a good place to build confidence. Scores are private: never read them aloud, never rank learners, and let learners keep their results to themselves if they want to. For learners who have been through interviews or exams that decided where they could live, a timed test can bring back difficult feelings. Tell the class what will happen before every timed set, keep your tone calm, and allow anyone to put the pen down and breathe for a moment.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: prediction. Before the recording starts, the gap already tells you most of what you need: the grammar around it gives the word class, and the context gives the type (a time, a price, a place).

"The library closes at ____ on weekdays." What kind of answer? (A time.) Could it be "Monday"? (No: the sentence already says "weekdays.") · "Bring two forms of ____." Singular or plural? (Plural or uncountable: "two forms of identification / ID.") · Do you predict the exact word? (No, the type. The exact word comes from the recording.)

Meaning: distractor and correction. A distractor is a possible answer that the speaker says and then rejects or changes. It is deliberately placed to test whether you follow the whole idea.

"The deposit is fifty dollars — sorry, they've just put it up to sixty." How much is the deposit? (Sixty.) Is "fifty" in the recording? (Yes.) Is it the answer? (No.) Which word told you it was changing? (Sorry.) · "Most people pick yoga, but the included class is actually Pilates." Which class is included? (Pilates.) Why is "yoga" there? (To catch people who write the first thing they hear.)

Meaning: the word limit. "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" is part of the marking, not advice.

The answer you hear is "student identification cards." The limit is two words. Can you write all three? (No: zero marks.) Can you write "card"? (No: it's plural, and you lost a word.) What's safe? ("identification cards" or "ID cards.") · A price, and "$" is already printed. Do you write "$70"? (No need: just "70." Writing it again can break the limit.) · Do hyphenated words and numbers count? "Build the trap" information gap — how to run it
  • Pairs, two rounds. Learner A takes a caller identity card (Set B: six invented people, each with a name to spell, an address number, a phone number, a date, a choice between two options and a built-in correction). Learner B takes a receptionist form for the same business (a sports center, a language school, a clinic, a bike rental, a museum tour, a community garden).
  • The call (3 min): B runs the call ("Good morning, how can I help?"), asks for each detail and writes it on the form. A reads naturally, spells the name, says the phone number in groups ("oh-seven-seven…") and must deliver the correction printed on the card, in natural words.
  • The check (1 min): B reads the completed form back; A compares it with the card. Any mismatch? Find out why: a letter, a number, the correction?
  • Swap with a new card. In round 2, A may invent one extra trap of their own (a -teen / -ty pair is the favorite).
  • If a learner prefers not to speak much, they can be the receptionist in both rounds: it is the listening role, and the one that practices the test.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Most IELTS candidates read well, but some learners are strong speakers with slow reading or handwriting in the Latin alphabet (for example, learners schooled mainly in Arabic, Chinese, Amharic or Tamil script). L1 watch-outs
  • Letter names: Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and many other speakers hear English E /iː/ as their "I" and English A /eɪ/ as their "E", so Ferreira becomes "Farreira" or "Firreira." French and Spanish speakers often swap G and J (their names for these letters are roughly the reverse of English). Arabic speakers may confuse P/B and V/F when writing a spelled name; Japanese and Korean speakers may confuse R/L. Drill the pairs, and in any spelled answer, tell learners to write the letters as they hear them, then read them back.
  • "Actually" is a false friend: Spanish actualmente, French actuellement, Portuguese atualmente, Italian attualmente, German aktuell and Russian актуально mean "currently." In English, actually usually introduces a correction or surprise, one of the most important cues in the test. Make this explicit.
  • Large numbers: Chinese, Japanese and Korean group large numbers by ten thousand (wàn / man), and Hindi and Urdu speakers may think in lakh (100,000) and crore. "Two hundred and forty thousand" can come out as 24,000. Practice writing big numbers from dictation. Many European languages use a decimal comma (3,5): in the test, write 3.5.
  • Final consonants and plurals: Vietnamese, Cantonese, Mandarin, Thai and some other speakers may not hear (or write) a final -s or -ed, which turns batteries into battery (zero marks). Use the grammar around the gap: "spare ____" + "bring" = probably plural.
  • Dates: American month/day and British day/month orders both appear. 3/5 is ambiguous; tell learners to write dates with the month as a word: 19 March or March 19. Both are accepted.
  • British words in the recording: car park, ground floor / first floor, queue, postcode, timetable, flat. Learners who know American English may not recognize them. In the real test a "first floor" room is one level above the entrance.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: skimming and scanning. Skimming is reading fast for the gist and the structure (which paragraph does what). Scanning is moving your eyes fast to find one thing, then reading closely only there.

"I read the first and last sentence of each paragraph." Skim or scan? (Skim.) Am I answering questions yet? (No: making a map.) · "I'm looking for the year 2011." Skim or scan? (Scan.) Do I read the sentences around it? (Only when I find it.) · Should you read the whole passage carefully before the questions? (No: that's the biggest time-sink.)

Meaning: keyword and paraphrase. The keyword is the part of the question that is hard to change: a name, a number, a place, a technical term. The rest of the question is usually paraphrased in the text.

"Critics are worried that data may not show how all groups of people walk." Which is the best word to scan for? (Critics, or data.) Will the text say "all groups of people"? (Probably not: maybe "older residents," "people with limited mobility.") · The question says "permission." The text says "consent." Same meaning? (Yes.) So is the sentence with "consent" a good place to look? (Yes.)

Meaning: the word-match trap. An option that repeats the text's exact words is not automatically right; it is often the distractor.

Question 5, option C: "appear mainly on university campuses." Does the text contain "university campuses"? (Yes.) Does it say paths appear mainly there? (No: the study was done there.) So is C right? (No: it's a word-match trap.)

Literacy support

L1 watch-outs
  • The "read every word" habit: learners from education systems built on close reading, translation or memorization (common in many countries) may feel that skimming is lazy or even dishonest.   • Script direction and speed: readers whose first literacy is in Arabic, Persian or Urdu (right to left) or in a logographic script such as Chinese may read English more slowly at first even with strong vocabulary.   2. Stronger or weaker? (choral) T: "some" → S: "all is stronger." T: "may" → "will is stronger." T: "often" → "always is stronger." T: "suggests" → "proves is stronger." T: "one of the main reasons" → "the main reason is stronger."
  3. Evidence frames (individual, fast): T gives a label and a clue; S builds the sentence. T: "False · three years / more than three years" → S: "It's False: the text says three years, whereas the statement says more than three." T: "Not Given · women" → S: "It's Not Given: the text talks about the learners, but there's no mention of how many were women."
"Prove it" debates — how to run it
  • Pairs, three cards each. Each Prove it card (Set D) gives a statement about Passage A or B and two assigned positions: Speaker A argues one label, Speaker B argues another (for example, True vs Not Given). The positions are assigned, so nobody has to "lose" an argument they believe.
  • Round (3 min per card): 30 seconds' silent preparation (find the evidence) → A speaks for 30 seconds → B speaks for 30 seconds → together they decide the real answer and write one evidence sentence on the card's line. The answer and reason are on a fold-back strip: check it only after deciding.
  • Evidence rules: you must quote or point to the passage (sentence number in Passage A, paragraph letter in Passage B). "I think" and "I know" don't count as evidence.
  • Swap positions on the next card. If a pair finishes early, they write a new statement of their own that is Not Given and test it on the next pair.
ICQs before the Prove it debates
Do you choose your position? (No: the card gives it.) Can you say "I know this is true"? (No: point to the passage.) How long does each speaker have? (30 seconds.) When do you look at the answer strip? (After we decide.) · Before matching headings: will you use all eight headings? (No.) Which paragraphs do you do first? (The easy ones.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Passage A is short and has numbered sentences on purpose (a classroom scaffold: the real test has none). For learners who read slowly, set statements 1–6 only and give them the "locate" step for free: the sentence number next to each statement. For headings, give a reduced list (the five right headings plus one trap) and let them do three paragraphs. Keep the T / F / NG cards: the drill is fully oral, so it is where they shine.

Stretch (band 7+)

Strong readers are the ones who over-infer ("It must be True, because…"). Give them the job of writing statements: three for Passage B, one of each label, with at least one qualifier trap, to test on a partner. Then introduce Yes / No / Not Given with Passage B's paragraph F: "Does the writer think cities should consult residents? (Not the writer: Nair does. So a statement about the writer's view would be Not Given.)" That distinction, writer vs quoted person, is a band 8 skill.

L1 watch-outs
  • Reading between the lines: learners from school systems and cultures that reward inference ("What does the writer really mean?") will turn Not Given into True or False. Present IELTS logic as a game with strict rules, not as the right way to read: in this game, only stated meaning counts.
  • Answering negative questions: Japanese, Korean, Chinese, and some other speakers answer a negative question by agreeing with it: "The text doesn't mention farmers?" "Yes" (= "right, it doesn't"). In pair debates this causes real confusion. Teach "Right, it doesn't" / "No, it doesn't" and model it.
  • "Not Given" as a concept: many school tests only have true/false. Some learners also come from exam systems with negative marking and leave blanks when unsure. Say it plainly: IELTS never takes marks away for a wrong answer, so always write a label.
  • Qualifier translation: few vs a few (Spanish pocos / unos pocos, French peu / quelques) changes the meaning: "few learners improved" is negative; "a few learners improved" is positive. Also hardly (= almost not, not "hard"). Put these on the qualifier flashcards.
  • Stress in contrast: learners whose languages mark contrast with word order or particles rather than stress (French, Japanese, Korean, Tagalog, and others) may argue flatly in the debates. The contrastive stress drill above helps listeners follow their argument.

Reading the Listening aloud — how to do it well
  • Rehearse it the day before, aloud, with a stopwatch. Mark every pause in the script ("You now have 30 seconds to look at Questions 11 to 15"), and time the pauses exactly in class. Learners use those seconds to predict; shortening them changes the test.
  • Record it if you can. A recording made on a phone in a quiet room (you, or you and a colleague for the two-voice parts) is fairer than live reading: every class hears exactly the same speed, you can't accidentally stress an answer, and you can keep it for future groups. Test the volume from the back row before class. If you read live, stand still at the front, face the class, and don't walk around while reading.
  • Two or more voices. For conversations, ask a colleague or a volunteer who is not taking the test. If you are alone, change your voice and position slightly for each speaker and say the speaker's name the first time they speak only if the script does.
  • Natural speed, flat emphasis. Read at the speed of a normal, clear conversation (not slower "for learners"). Read the distractors and the corrections exactly as written, with no extra stress on the answer and no pause before it. Include the fillers and hesitations in the script ("um," "let me see"): they are part of the test.
  • Once only. No repeats, no answers to questions during the recording. If a learner asks "Can you repeat?", smile and shake your head. You can explain afterwards: in the real test the recording doesn't stop, and learning to let one answer go is part of the skill.
  • If something goes wrong (a fire alarm, loud noise outside, a phone ringing): keep going if you can. If a sentence was genuinely inaudible to the whole room, note the question numbers affected on your copy and remove those items from everyone's score in C9 (say so, and adjust the total). Never replay a section for one learner.
  • Accents. The real test uses a range of English accents (British, North American, Australian, New Zealand and others). Your own accent is fine for practice; tell learners so, and point them to the online lessons' UK / US voice switch for variety at home.
  • Latecomers enter only at a break between parts, quietly, and start from the current part. Write "joined at Part __" on their answer sheet so their C9 score is read in context.
What to say (and not say) about nerves
"You will miss some answers. Everyone does, including people who get band 8. When you miss one, let it go and catch the next one." · "Feeling nervous is normal; it means this matters to you. If your heart is racing, put your pencil down, breathe out slowly, and carry on. The clock keeps going, like in the real test, so one or two breaths, then back in." · "This is practice. The score is information for you and me, not a label. Nobody else will see it unless you show them."

Avoid: "This is really important," "Don't panic," "If you get under 25 you're in trouble," or comparing classes ("Last year's group did better"). If a learner becomes distressed, let them step outside for a moment with the calm card; they can come back and continue, and the time lost is noted, not counted against them in your feedback. Have tissues and water available without comment.

Invigilating
  • Walk slowly and quietly during the Reading, not the Listening. Check that answers are going on the answer sheet (a common first-test error is writing everything on the booklet in Reading and running out of time to copy it). You may point silently at the answer sheet; say nothing else.
  • Don't help with vocabulary or explain a question, even a little. If a learner asks, say "Do your best: I can't help in the test." Note which questions caused questions: useful for C9.
  • Note, privately, anything you see that the scores won't show: who stopped to re-read Passage 1 for a long time, who never turned the page to Passage 3, who froze in Listening Part 4. It makes your C9 feedback specific and kind.
Differentiation
Literacy and access

A practice test should show a learner's real starting point, so keep the conditions the same for everyone, with two exceptions. (1) A learner with a documented need (visual impairment, dyslexia, a hearing difficulty) should get the arrangement they would request from the test center: an enlarged-print copy, a seat at the front, extra time noted on the tracker. Encourage them to apply for special arrangements with the test center early. (2) A learner whose literacy is clearly well below the test (who could not finish Passage 1) should still take the test, but you might tell them privately beforehand: "Do Passage 1 carefully; that's your target today." Their C9 goal is then about Passage 1 accuracy, not the total.

Early finishers

Anyone who finishes Reading early stays seated and checks: every answer on the sheet? every blank filled? word limits? spelling copied exactly? True/False labels (not Yes/No) where the instructions ask for them? Tell strong learners in advance that finishing early usually means there is checking left to do. They must not leave the room or start other work.

Watch-outs: test habits from other systems
  • Negative marking: learners who took exams where wrong answers lose points will leave blanks. Say it in the briefing: IELTS never deducts marks. Fill every blank.
  • Collaboration norms: in some classrooms, helping a neighbor quietly during a test is tolerated or even expected. Explain the rule without judgment before the test starts, and seat friends apart.
  • Writing answers on the booklet only (Reading): there is no transfer time. Remind them in the briefing and at the 10-minute warning.
  • Capital letters and handwriting: learners with less practice in Latin handwriting can write answers in CAPITALS (accepted in the real test) to avoid unclear letters.
Returning the results: the C9 warmer (error-type tally)

Do not mark the tests between C8 and C9 (a quick look for missing names is fine). Learners mark their own. The Module 3 guide begins C9 with this warmer; here is the full procedure, which takes about 10 minutes in class, with the tally finished at home.

  1. Frame it (1 min). "Today you'll mark your own test. The score is useful, but the most useful thing is why you lost each mark. You're looking for your two biggest leaks."
  2. Mark (5 min). Return the answer sheets. Learners mark with a colored pen as you project or read the key from assessment/answer-keys.html, section by section. Strict rules, stated once: spelling must be exact (British or American both fine), plurals count, over the word limit = wrong, True on a Yes/No question = wrong, a blank = wrong. Where the key lists alternatives, accept them. Remove any "void" items (see above) from everyone's total.
  3. Score and band (1 min). Learners total each section out of 40 and read the approximate band from the conversion table in the answer-keys document. Say clearly: "This is an estimate from a practice test that I read aloud, not an official score. Treat it as a range, about half a band either side."
  4. Tally (3 min in class, finish at home). Each learner takes an error-type tally sheet (Set F) and writes a code beside every wrong answer on their answer sheet, then counts the codes. They start in class with the Listening section (the codes are fresh from C5) and finish Reading at home.
  5. Two leaks. At home, they circle their two most frequent codes and write their two-leak plan on the tally sheet (the sheet suggests a practice task for each code). They bring the sheet to C10.
  6. Class picture, no names. In C10 (or at the end of C9), ask for a show of hands for each code and board the totals. Plan short, targeted listening or reading "clinics" in later warmers for the two biggest class-wide codes. Keep the individual scores private: learners record them on their own results tracker and you record them on yours.

If the Module 3 C9 plan cannot spare 10 minutes, do steps 1–3 in class (7 minutes) and set the whole tally as homework. If your program allows, a 15-minute version lets pairs compare their codes (never their scores) and share one fix.

Using the results diagnostically
  • The codes tell you what to teach. Lots of D and L: build more "take the correction" and "let it go" practice into warmers (Trap Bingo is ready). Lots of S / N: 3-minute dictations of numbers, dates and spelled names at the start of classes. Lots of T: timed skim-and-scan drills with a strict 20-minute passage rule. Lots of F / K / Q: one T / F / NG statement a day with evidence. Lots of H: one-line paragraph summaries.
  • The section pattern tells you where the band is. Many learners find that Listening and Reading are closer to their target than Writing and Speaking, which the next two modules address. For others, Reading speed is the main barrier. Tell each learner honestly which section is furthest from their target and what the fastest win is.
  • Comparing with Practice Test 2 (C15). Use the same timing and the same marking rules. A rise of a few raw marks, or a change from "lots of D" to "a few D," is real progress even if the estimated band stays the same. Remind learners that a 0.5 band gain over the course is a good, realistic result, and that some will gain more and some less.
  • Privacy. Never display scores, rank learners, or share one learner's result with another. A learner who does worse than they hoped needs a quiet conversation and one specific next step, not public reassurance.

The through-line

Report it → argue it → judge it. In C9 learners report: a neutral, selective summary of a chart with a clear overview and not a word of opinion. In C10 they argue: read exactly what the question asks, take a position and develop two ideas properly. In C11 they judge: they sit in the examiner's chair, band-estimate three anonymous scripts against paraphrased criteria, compare their estimates with an examiner-style commentary, then turn the same lens on their own essay. Each class produces a piece of writing another person reads: a timed Task 1 report, a planned Task 2 opening, and a set of panel judgments plus a targeted rewrite.

Stakes, anxiety and realistic expectations

Your learners are not practicing for fun: a band decides a university place, a professional license, a visa. Writing is the paper most of them fear most, and C9 begins with their first real practice-test result. Say these things explicitly, in the first ten minutes of C9 and again whenever you hand back writing:

  • A result is a map, not a verdict. "Your score tells us where to dig. It doesn't tell us who you are or what you'll get on test day."
  • Realistic gains. Over an 8-week course, a gain of about half a band in a skill is common and worth celebrating. Bigger jumps happen, especially when a learner fixes one "band-limiter" (no overview, no paragraphs, under length), but they aren't guaranteed. Never promise a band.
  • Writing moves more slowly than Reading and Listening, because it depends on habits that take weeks to build. The fastest writing gains in this module come from structure (overview, question type, paragraphs, word count), which learners can control on test day even when they are nervous.
  • Never say "you'll fail" or "that's a band 5 essay" in front of the class. Talk about the script, not the person, and always name a criterion and a next step: "This essay's Task Response is strong. Its Coherence is held back by the linking. Here's the fix."
  • Anxiety in timed writing often shows as a blank page. Give everyone the same starter routine (Task 1: underline, overview, then numbers; Task 2: type, position, two ideas) so there is always a first move. Allow learners who freeze to start with the plan only; a good plan is a real achievement.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: the overview. The overview is the big picture: the one or two things a reader would remember if they saw the chart for five seconds. It is not a summary of every line, and it is not a conclusion at the end.

"Overall, cycling became much more popular in Northport and Riverton, while it fell slightly in Eastvale." Is there a number? (No.) Could I say this after looking for five seconds? (Yes.) Where does it go: the start or the end? (Near the start, paragraph 2; many good writers put it straight after the introduction.) · "In 2004, 14% of commuters in Eastvale cycled." Overview or detail? (Detail: it has a figure and one year.) · Can I write "This is because Northport built bike lanes"? (No: the chart doesn't say why. That's an invented cause.)

Meaning: degree and speed. The adverb or adjective must match the size of the change on the chart.

From 10% to 12% in ten years: rose sharply or slightly? (Slightly.) From 80 to 15 in one year? (Plummeted / fell dramatically.) 50, 50, 50, 50? (Remained stable / leveled off.) Up, down, up, down with no direction? (Fluctuated.) · "Riverton drew level with Eastvale in 2016." Same number or different? (The same, 11%.) · "Northport overtook Eastvale." Who was ahead before? (Eastvale.) After? (Northport.)

Meaning: prepositions of change. to = the new level; by = the size of the change; at = a point; of = the size after a noun; in = the thing that changed.

"Paid work rose to 12 hours." How many hours at the end? (12.) "Paid work rose by 6 hours." How many at the end? (We need the start: 6 + 6 = 12.) · "There was a rise of 6 hours in paid work." Which word gives the amount? (of.) Which introduces the topic? (in.) · "Solar accounted for 12% of electricity." Is that a change over time? (No: a proportion at one moment.)

Form.

Verb + adverbsubject + rose / fell / climbed / declined + sharply, steadily, slightlyCycling in Northport rose steadily until 2012.
Adjective + nounThere was a / an + sharp, steady, slight + rise / fall / increase + in + topicThere was a sharp rise in cycling after 2012.
Irregular verbsrise – rose – risen · fall – fell – fallen · grow – grew – grown · (raise needs an object)✘ rised · ✘ was risen · ✔ has risen

Tense. Past data → past simple (rose, fell); a point before another past point → past perfect (had overtaken by 2016); no time / a process → present simple. Projections (is expected to rise, is predicted to reach) only if the chart shows future years.

Pronunciation. Noun vs verb stress: an INcrease / to inCREASE, a DEcrease / to deCREASE. Percentages: perCENT, "thirty-one percent," never "thirty-one percents." Fractions: a third, a quarter, three fifths /fɪfθs/. Rose /roʊz/ vs rows; fell vs fall: learners who confuse them in speech often confuse them in writing too.

Drill script
  1. Verb → noun: T: "Cycling rose sharply." → S: "There was a sharp rise in cycling." T: "Coal use fell slightly." → "There was a slight fall in coal use." T: "Paid work increased dramatically." → "There was a dramatic increase in paid work." (Stress the noun: an INcrease.)
  2. Noun → verb: T: "There was a gradual decline in study hours." → S: "Study hours declined gradually." T: "There was a steady growth in wind power." → "Wind power grew steadily."
  3. To or by? T: "6 hours, then 12 hours. Use to." → S: "It rose to 12 hours." T: "Use by." → "It rose by 6 hours." T: "14%, then 10%. From… to." → "It fell from 14% to 10%."
  4. Approximate it: T: "26%" → S: "just over a quarter." T: "48%" → "just under half." T: "62%" → "roughly three fifths" / "well over half."

Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Precision and flexibility. Ask strong learners to (1) vary the subject: The figure for Riverton… · Riverton's rate… · The proportion of cyclists in Riverton…; (2) use one past perfect and one participle clause (…rising to 31%, the highest rate shown); (3) compare within a sentence (whereas, while, compared with, respectively); (4) finish the report at 160–170 words, not 220: selection is a band-7 skill. L1 watch-outs
  • "the 60% of people": Spanish el 60%, Portuguese os 60%, French les 60%, Italian il 60%. Teach 60% of people and the proportion / percentage of people. Also percent (with a number) vs percentage (without): ✘ "the percent of cyclists."
  • Articles: Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese speakers drop or add them: "Number of cyclists rose," "There was sharp rise." Drill the chunk there was a sharp rise in as one unit; and the number of + plural noun + singular verb: The number of cyclists was… (✘ were).
  • Tense: Chinese and Vietnamese don't mark tense on the verb, so learners often describe 2004 data in the present ("In 2004, Eastvale has 14%"). Board a timeline and ask "Is 2004 finished?"
  • Rise / raise / arise: all L1s, especially Spanish (subir / aumentar) and Arabic speakers. Raise needs an object (the company raised prices); data rises.
  • "increase of" vs "increase in": French une augmentation de, Spanish un aumento de, Russian рост + genitive. A rise of 5 points (amount) in cycling (topic).
  • Plural -s dropped in writing: Vietnamese, Chinese, Thai speakers ("five activity," "12 hour"). The Practice Test 1 tally probably showed it in Listening too: link the two.
  • Giving reasons: learners from school systems where "explain the data" was rewarded will add causes. Praise the thinking, then: "Great for Task 2. In Task 1, the examiner can't reward what the chart doesn't show."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: question type = the job. The instruction words tell the writer what kind of answer counts. Writing a good essay about the topic but not the question is the most common reason for a low Task Response score.

"Discuss both views and give your own opinion." How many jobs? (Three: view A, view B, my opinion.) If I only write about the view I agree with? (Task Response is capped: one job is missing.) · "To what extent do you agree or disagree?" Do I have to agree completely? (No: I can partly agree, but I must say so clearly and hold that position.) · "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" Is a list of advantages and a list of disadvantages enough? (No: I have to weigh them and give a verdict.) · "Why is this happening? What can be done?" How many body paragraphs, at least? (Two: one per question.)

Meaning: thesis vs topic sentence. The thesis (end of the introduction) states the answer to the whole question. A topic sentence (start of a body paragraph) states the one idea of that paragraph.

"This essay will discuss both sides." Does it tell me the writer's position? (No: it's a plan, not a thesis. Add "…before arguing that…".) · "Sitting all day causes several problems." Topic sentence: one idea or many? (Too general: which problem? Better: "The most serious problem is the effect on physical health.") · Does the conclusion add a new idea? (No: it restates the position.)

Meaning: development. A developed point answers "why?" and "so what?" and proves it with a specific example. Listing gives four claims and no proof.

"Working from home is convenient. It saves time. People like it." How many ideas? (Three.) How many are explained? (None.) · "…which means workers start the day rested." Is this a why or a consequence? (Consequence: so what.) · Does my example have to be a real statistic? (No: it should be specific and plausible, like "a company that let staff start at 10." Don't invent exact data.)

Form.

Thesis (opinion)While / Although + concession, I believe / I would argue that + positionWhile education has value, I believe businesses bear greater responsibility.
Thesis (discussion)This essay will examine both views before arguing that……before arguing that cooking should be compulsory.
Topic sentenceThe most serious / compelling / obvious + noun + is (that)…The most serious problem is the effect on physical health.
WhyThis is because… · since… · as…This is because the body burns very little energy.
So what…, which means (that)… · As a result, … · This can lead to……, which means they take more sick days.
ExampleFor example, … · A clear example of this is… · …such as…A small firm that holds walking meetings, for example, …
Hedgingcan · may · tends to · many / some (not all) · oftenLong hours at a desk can harm concentration.

Register. No contractions (do not, not don't), no you addressing the reader, no slang, no rhetorical questions in a row. Memorized openers ("In this modern era of technology…") are recognized by examiners and earn nothing.

Pronunciation (for the Plan Pitch and the dictation). Contrastive stress in a thesis: "While education has value, I believe businesses bear greater responsibility." A slight pause after the concession clause. outweigh /aʊtˈweɪ/, silent gh; thesis /ˈθiːsɪs/, plural theses /ˈθiːsiːz/.

"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" → "Advantages–disadvantages — verdict!" "What are the causes, and what solutions can you suggest?" → "Problem–solution — two parts!"

Plan Pitch — how to run it
  • Pairs, one set of prompt cards face down. A draws a card and has 2 minutes to fill the plan template in the workbook. No full sentences except the thesis.
  • Pitch (1 min): A says the plan aloud: "This is a ___ question, so I need to ___. My position is ___. Body 1: ___ because ___, for example ___. Body 2: …" B listens with the listener checklist (type right · every part answered · one idea per body · a specific example · no new idea in the conclusion) and asks "so what?" once, on the weakest point. A answers.
  • Swap with a new card. If time allows, a third round with a new partner.
  • Why speak a written plan? Saying it aloud exposes a missing part faster than reading it. Tell learners that's the only purpose; accuracy of speech isn't being judged.

  • "Although… but…": Chinese (虽然…但是…), Vietnamese (mặc dù… nhưng…), Korean and Arabic speakers double the connector. Keep one: Although X, Y. or X, but Y.
  • Delayed position: learners from Arabic, Persian, Japanese and some Latin American essay traditions often build up to the opinion at the end, or leave it implicit as a courtesy. Explain that the IELTS reader expects the answer in the introduction; it isn't rude, it's the genre.
  • Long coordinated sentences and comma splices: Arabic (wa…wa…), Spanish and Russian writers join many clauses with and or commas. One idea per sentence in the chain; show a comma splice and its three repairs (full stop, semicolon, subordinator).
  • "On the contrary" used to mean "on the other hand": French au contraire, Spanish por el contrario, Portuguese pelo contrário. On the contrary rejects the previous statement; for a second side, use On the other hand or However.
  • "People is / Everybody are": Spanish la gente es, Portuguese o povo é. People is plural: people are.
  • Proverbs and set phrases as openers, common in Chinese, Arabic, Persian and Indian English school writing ("As the saying goes…"). They aren't wrong, but they spend words without answering the question. Paraphrase the prompt instead.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: four separate judgments. Each task is judged on four criteria that count equally; the task band is roughly their average, and Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1 in the Writing band. A brilliant criterion can't fully rescue a weak one.

An essay has excellent vocabulary but answers a different question. Which criterion suffers most? (Task Response.) Can the vocabulary save it? (Only partly: TR drags the average down.) · So which criterion should you practice first: your best or your weakest? (The weakest.) · Are the bands in the workbook table official? (No: they're paraphrased from the public descriptors, a guide for estimating.)

Meaning: coherence vs cohesion.

An essay has perfect linkers, but the ideas jump around. Coherent or cohesive? (Cohesive but not coherent.) · Each paragraph has one clear idea in a sensible order, but the sentences feel like separate islands. (Coherent but not very cohesive.) · Is paragraphing coherence or cohesion? (Coherence, and it's the cheapest gain: no paragraphs caps this criterion around band 5.)

Meaning: referencing with a summary noun. This / these + noun points back to a whole idea and names it, so the reader never wonders what this means.

"Cities face traffic, pollution and crowded trains. This makes action essential." What does this refer to? (All three problems, but it's vague.) Better? (These pressures make action essential.) · "Governments could raise fuel taxes. This measure would reduce car use." Measure = ? (Raising fuel taxes.) · Does "more linkers = higher band" hold? (No: the descriptors reward linking that is natural and accurate; overuse is marked down.)

Pronunciation (for reading aloud, the best self-check for cohesion). Linkers at the start of a sentence take a slight pause after them: "However, | the trend carries a risk." Reading aloud slowly reveals over-linking: if you hear a signpost on every sentence, the reader will too. Stress the summary noun: "These pressures make action essential."

Drill script
  1. Name it with a summary noun: T reads a sentence; S starts the next sentence with This / These + noun. T: "Many companies now allow staff to choose their hours." → S: "This trend…" / "This approach…" T: "Cities could add bus lanes and cut fares." → "These measures…" T: "Some people believe libraries should only lend books." → "This view…" T: "Book budgets may shrink too far." → "This risk…"
  2. Cut the signpost: T reads an over-linked pair: "Firstly, cycling is cheap. Moreover, it is healthy." → S combines: "Cycling is both cheap and healthy." T: "In addition, it causes no pollution. Consequently, cities support it." → "Because it causes no pollution, cities support it." / "It also causes no pollution, which is why cities support it."
  3. Which criterion? (fast): T: "no paragraphs" → S: "CC!" · "eleven times important" → "LR!" · "no position" → "TR!" · "only simple sentences" → "GRA!" · "missing articles" → "GRA!" · "every sentence starts with a linker" → "CC!"
Examiner Panel — how to run it (30 min)
  • Groups of 4, four roles (role cards, Set E): the TR examiner, the CC examiner, the LR examiner and the GRA examiner. Each reads the script once for meaning, then again for their criterion only, and uses the paraphrased criteria table in the workbook. Rotate roles for each script so everyone judges at least two criteria. In groups of 3, one person takes LR and GRA together.
  • All three scripts answer the same prompt (public libraries adding new services; a positive/negative question). They are original, anonymous and presented in mixed order: learners are not told which is strongest. They were written to sit at roughly bands 5.5, 6.5 and 7.5, but don't reveal this until the panels commit.
  • Each examiner reports (1 minute each): a band for their criterion (whole bands: 5, 6, 7, 8) and one quotation from the script as evidence ("Paragraph 2: every sentence starts with a linker, so CC 5"). The panel writes the four bands and the average in the Workbook grid. Average .25 → round up to the next half band; .75 → up to the next whole band. (Say it's approximate.)
  • Timing: Script A 9 minutes (the first one is slower), Scripts B and C 7 minutes each. Keep a visible timer. If time is short, panels do A and C only (the two furthest apart) and do B for homework.
  • Reveal (7 min): turn over the examiner commentary. Panels compare criterion by criterion and circle any gap of a whole band or more. Whole-class: "Where did you disagree with the commentary, and what's your evidence?" A panel can argue for a different band if it quotes the script; that's exactly the skill. Point out that the commentary itself says "about": two trained examiners can differ by half a band.
  • Dominant speakers and anxious learners. Give your most confident speaker the role that needs the most careful reading (GRA, finding error-free sentences). Anxious learners often feel relieved to judge someone else's writing first; the scripts are anonymous and invented, so nobody's work is exposed. Keep the tone professional: "The script has…" never "The student is…."
ICQs before the Examiner Panel
How many criteria does each person judge per script? (One.) What must you give with every band? (A quotation from the script.) Do you know which script is best? (No.) When do we turn over the commentary? (After all panels commit.) Is the commentary the "right answer"? (It's an expert estimate; we can disagree with evidence.) Do we talk about the writer or the script? (The script.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Reading three 250–300-word scripts is heavy. Give these learners the CC role first: it needs structure-spotting more than close reading (count paragraphs, circle every sentence-initial linker, underline this / these + noun). Pre-highlight the topic sentences on their copy. In the four-pass self-assessment, they do two passes only (TR and CC, which are the most reliable to self-judge) and rewrite one topic sentence rather than a whole paragraph.

Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Examiner-level precision. Strong learners write a three-sentence commentary for one criterion of Script A in the style of the reveal sheet (one strength, one limitation, band + reason). Then: "What exactly would move Script C from CC 7 to CC 8?" (seamless cohesion, flexible referencing, no over-used linker). In their own rewrite, target one flexible reference chain across a paragraph (remote work → this arrangement → it → such flexibility).

L1 watch-outs
  • Linkers as good style: many school systems (Chinese 首先…其次…最后, Arabic أولاً… ثانياً, Spanish en primer lugar…, Russian во-первых…) teach heavy signposting as a mark of a good essay. Learners genuinely believe it raises their score. Show the over-linked vs natural versions side by side and let them hear the difference (Script 3.3-A) rather than just saying "don't."
  • Vague this / it: learners from pro-drop and topic-prominent languages (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean) often use this or it for a whole paragraph of ideas. The summary-noun drill is the fix.
  • Repetition as clarity: Chinese, Korean, Japanese and some formal Arabic writing repeat the full noun on purpose. English readers hear it as limited vocabulary (LR) and weak referencing (CC).
  • Paragraphing conventions: some learners (for example, from Arabic, Persian or Vietnamese school traditions) write very long paragraphs or indent without a new idea. One idea = one paragraph; leave a blank line between paragraphs in the paper test so the examiner can see them.
  • Peer criticism and face: in many cultures, criticizing a classmate's work openly is uncomfortable, and judging a teacher-provided text feels presumptuous. The anonymous scripts and the "quote the script" rule help. Model a kind, evidence-based comment first.

The through-line

Say more → say it better → deliver it on the day. In C12 learners stop giving the examiner too little: every answer grows (answer + why + example), the long turn fills two minutes, and Part 3 becomes a small argument. In C13 they learn how the examiner turns talk into a number, and they lift the two criteria candidates neglect most: pronunciation (chunking, stress, intonation) and lexical resource (precise, natural, sometimes idiomatic). In C14 they rehearse the whole test and plan the run-up to test day, including the parts no textbook practices: the check-in, the clock, the nerves, the results. C15–C16 turn that plan into evidence: a second full practice test, a band profile set beside Practice Test 1, and a personal next step.

The stakes are real: handle them kindly

Your learners need a band for a university place, a professional license or a visa. Some have already taken IELTS and missed their target; some are sitting it within weeks of C16. Speak honestly and gently about what an 8-week course can do. A gain of about half a band in 8 weeks is common; bigger jumps happen but are not guaranteed, and strategy gains (timing, question types, extending answers) come faster than language gains (range, accuracy). Never say "you'll fail" or "you're not ready." Say instead: "Here's where you are today, here's the gap, and here's the one change with the biggest payoff." Practice-test bands move up and down by about half a band from one day to the next for the same person; treat a single result as a snapshot, not a sentence. If a learner is clearly far from their target, talk to them privately about timing their test date, never in front of the group.

Trauma-informed: personal questions and official checks

Part 1 asks about home, family, work and hometown, and several cue cards invite personal memories. For some learners (refugees, people separated from family, people who have lost work or status) these questions are painful. Every personal task in this module offers three equally good options: your real life, a new identity card, or something invented. Say it every class: "In the test you can invent details. You're marked on your English, not on your life." None of our cue cards asks about a journey to a new country, a hard time, or a family member who is far away. Test day also brings ID checks, photographs and sometimes a fingerprint scan. For learners who have been through border or immigration interviews, that can feel very different from how it feels to others. In C14 describe check-in neutrally and in advance, so nothing is a surprise; never role-play an ID check, and let anyone skip that part of the discussion.

Mock-examiner trios — how to run it
  • Roles. The examiner reads only from the examiner script and the cards: no chatting, no helping, a friendly neutral face. The timekeeper-observer runs the phone timer, says "Thank you" at 2 minutes in Part 2, and fills the observer sheet (ticks for extended answers, the Part 2 bullets covered, the Part 3 moves heard, one quote to praise, one to fix). The candidate speaks. Give dominant speakers the examiner or timekeeper job first: it asks them to listen.
  • One round (8 min): Part 1, examiner takes one Part 1 card and asks three questions (1½ min) → Part 2, examiner hands over a cue card and says "You have one minute to prepare" (1 min), then "Please start speaking now" (up to 2 min), then one rounding-off question (e.g. "Would you recommend it to others?") → Part 3, examiner asks the two Part 3 questions linked to that cue card (2 min) → feedback: observer gives one strength and one next step, both quoted (1½ min).
  • Call the parts for the whole room. Everyone moves on your timer ("Part 2, prep minute starts… now"), so no trio drifts. If a talk ends early, the timekeeper writes the time on the sheet: that's useful data, not a failure.
  • Cue cards. Deal each trio four different cue cards face down. The candidate never chooses: in the test you can't. Anyone may use a new identity card for Part 1, and invent every detail in Part 2.
  • You sit in with a different trio each round and take three-column notes. Don't interrupt a candidate. If an examiner starts helping, whisper "Just the script."

Literacy support

Some learners speak much more confidently than they write, and the prep minute's note-taking can slow them down. Offer picture notes (a quick sketch per bullet is fine: the test only needs notes you can read) or the 4-box note frame from Workbook Ex 4 (what · who · how long · feeling). Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Band 7+ candidates speak at length without sounding prepared. Ask strong learners to (1) answer a Part 3 question with a concession that changes their position a little (I used to think…, but now I'd say…); (2) use two different speculative structures in one answer (I suspect… · It wouldn't surprise me if… · There's every chance that…); and (3) in Part 2, add a flashback or a comparison the bullets don't ask for, so the talk fills two minutes naturally. As examiners, they must ask one unscripted follow-up in Part 3 ("Why do you think that is?").

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am agree" / "I am not agree" (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, Portuguese estou de acordo, French je suis d'accord, Arabic and Farsi patterns): the most frequent Part 3 error. Board it every class: I agree · I don't agree · I'm not sure I agree.
  • Tense with since / for: "I live here since two years" (Spanish, French, German, Russian, Polish use a present tense). Part 1 is full of these: I've lived here for two years.
  • Dropped be and articles (Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese): "My hometown very beautiful," "I am student." In speech these errors are frequent but don't usually block meaning; target them in feedback, but praise the extension first.
  • He/she in stories about people (Tagalog siya, Chinese tā, Farsi u, Finnish, Turkish, Yoruba): long Part 2 stories about a teacher or friend expose it. Ask the observer to listen for it.
  • Very short "polite" answers: learners from cultures where talking at length to an authority figure is rude (many East and Southeast Asian classrooms, some rural or formal cultures) may give short answers out of respect, not lack of English. Name it: "In this test, talking more is the polite thing to do."
  • Memorized openings: learners from exam systems that reward memorization may arrive with a rehearsed "Let me introduce my hometown…" paragraph. Treat it kindly: keep the vocabulary, drop the script.

L1 watch-outs
  • Syllable-timed rhythm (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Turkish, Tagalog, Hindi-Urdu, Cantonese, many West African languages): every syllable gets equal weight, so sentence stress and chunks disappear.   • Extra vowels: Spanish and Portuguese speakers add e- before s + consonant (es-tudy, es-peak); Arabic and Farsi speakers insert vowels in clusters (pi-lease); Japanese speakers add final vowels (desk-u). Only fix these if they cause confusion.
  • Dropped final consonants (Vietnamese, Cantonese, Mandarin, Thai): walk / walked, like / likes merge, which also hides grammar and can cost Grammatical Range & Accuracy. Target the -ed and -s endings in the long turn.
  • Specific sounds that cause real confusion: p / b (Arabic: people / beeble), l / r (Japanese, Korean: collect / correct), v / w (Hindi-Urdu, German), th → s / t / f (many L1s). Work only on the pair that affects each learner.
  • Flat or falling intonation that sounds uninterested (German, Russian, Polish, some Chinese speakers when nervous): the examiner may hear boredom. Practice the rise-fall of enthusiasm: It was absolutely ↗ aMAzing ↘.
  • Cognate stress: Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian speakers carry their L1 stress into cognates (comforTAble, developMENT, ecoNOmy, phoTOgraph). The Ex 2 word list targets exactly these.
  • "I am very interesting in…" (-ed / -ing adjectives: Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Russian): frequent in Part 1 hobby answers.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: timing plans. The clock is part of the test. A plan decides in advance what to do when a question takes too long.

In Reading, is there extra time at the end to copy answers? (No: write them on the answer sheet as you go.) About how long for each passage? (About 20 minutes.) A question is taking five minutes. What do you do? (Guess, mark it, move on.) Is a hard question worth more? (No: one mark each.) · In Writing, which task first? (Either, but most people do Task 1 first, in about 20 minutes.) Which task is worth more? (Task 2.) So which one must never be short? (Task 2.)

Computer-delivered and paper-delivered: the general picture. Describe differences in general terms and send learners to their test provider's official information for the details of their own booking. Content, question types, timing and difficulty are the same in both formats, and the Speaking test is face to face with an examiner in both. The practical differences: on computer, answers are typed or clicked straight in, Writing shows a word count, text can usually be highlighted, and Listening gives a short check time at the end rather than time to transfer answers; results usually arrive sooner. On paper, Listening and Reading answers go on an answer sheet (in Listening there's extra time at the end to transfer them; in Reading there isn't), Writing is handwritten so legibility matters and you estimate your own word count (count the words in three lines, average them, multiply). Practice in the format you've booked.

Is the computer test easier? (No: same content, same difficulty.) Is Speaking done with a computer? (No: with a person, in both formats.) On paper, how do you know your word count? (Estimate: words per line × lines.) Which format should you practice in? (The one you've booked.)

Results, remarks and retakes: factual. Results are released online first and then as a Test Report Form; computer-delivered results usually arrive faster than paper-based ones. Many universities and employers accept results for about two years, but each organization sets its own rules. If a candidate believes a score doesn't reflect their performance, they can ask their test center for a remark (officially an "enquiry on results") within a limited period after the test; they can usually choose which sections are re-marked, a fee is charged, and it's refunded if the band score goes up. Candidates can take the full test again whenever they like. In many locations there's also a one skill retake option for a single section. Check with the test center and with the receiving organization before relying on either. Don't quote fees, deadlines or turnaround times in class: they vary by country and change. Show learners where to find them.

Can you ask for a remark for any reason, any time? (No: within a limited period, through the test center.) Is it free? (No, but the fee is returned if your score goes up.) Does every university accept a one skill retake? (Not necessarily: check first.) Where do you find the exact rules and dates? (The test provider's official information and your test center.)

Full speaking mock — how to run it
  • Real conditions. Rearrange the room so pairs face each other across a table, as in the test. The examiner uses the examiner script word for word, including the opening ("Good afternoon. My name is… Can you tell me your full name, please?" — learners may give a new-identity name).

Swap.
  • Odd numbers: make one trio with an observer who times and takes quotes; the observer is the candidate in round 3 if time allows, or does their mock with you during the countdown-plan stage.
  • You walk the room and listen to every candidate for at least a minute, noting one strength per learner for the C16 debrief. If a candidate freezes, catch the examiner's eye and mouth "Next question," exactly as a real examiner would move on.
  • Afterwards: ask "How did the nerves feel compared with C12? What helped?" Normalize: nerves are expected, and they fade with each rehearsal.

L1 watch-outs
  • Dates and times: on + date (on May 14), at + clock time (at 8:30), in + month (in May). Spanish, Portuguese and French speakers often say "in the 14 of May"; Arabic and Farsi speakers may mix in / on; Chinese and Vietnamese speakers may drop the preposition.
  • Future with present simple ("Tomorrow I study Reading," Spanish, German, Russian, Chinese): for plans, push I'm going to / I'll.
  • Word count habits: learners from systems that reward long answers may think "more is always better" in Task 1. Remind them: 150+ words, but quality and an overview matter more than 250 words of detail.
  • Asking the examiner to repeat feels rude in some cultures (many East Asian, South Asian and West African classrooms). Say it plainly: "Asking for repetition is normal and doesn't lower your score. Silence does more harm."
  • Ordinal numbers in dates and on answer sheets: twenty-first, thirty-second. Spelling errors in Listening answers cost marks: Wednesday, February, accommodation, environment, government.

Paraphrased criteria are in the practice-test key and on the band card.
  • Trauma-informed: use a new-identity name without comment if a learner gives one, and skip any Part 1 question a learner visibly doesn't want to answer ("Let's talk about something else: what do you do in your free time?").
ICQs (before Writing): How long for Writing? (60 minutes.) Which task is worth more? (Task 2.) When I call your name, what do you write on your slip? (The time I leave and the time I come back.) Do you lose those minutes? (No, you get them back at the end.) How long is the interview? (About six minutes.)
How to read a band profile (say this, kindly)
  • "This is a snapshot, not a sentence." The same person can score half a band higher or lower on a different day. Look at the trend across Diagnostic, Practice Test 1 and Practice Test 2, not one number.
  • "Half a band in eight weeks is a real gain." Many learners improve by about that much in a course of this length; some improve more, some hold steady while their technique improves and the band catches up later. Bigger jumps are possible but not guaranteed.
  • "Your overall band is the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band" (an average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band). Check your target: many universities and professional bodies also set a minimum for each skill. One low skill can matter more than a high average.
  • "Compare yourself with you." No one reads anyone else's profile. The only question is: what's your gap, and what's the one change with the biggest payoff?

Using the two practice tests diagnostically. Ask learners to look beyond the band. For Listening and Reading, list every wrong answer by question type (form completion, multiple choice, map or plan labeling, True/False/Not Given, matching headings…) and by error code (the Module 2 codes. Listening: D distractor · S spelling or plural · W word limit / wrong form · N number, letter or date misheard · L lost my place / froze · P missed the paraphrase · X transfer error. Reading: P · M word-match trap · S copied a word wrongly · W · O looked in the wrong place · T ran out of time / left a blank · F False vs Not Given · K own knowledge · Q missed a qualifier · H heading matched a detail · X). Compare with Practice Test 1: a question type that's wrong in both tests is the clearest target. For Writing and Speaking, compare criterion by criterion: a criterion that stays lowest across both tests is the "one change." The Workbook's Practice Test 2 page has a table for this.

Returning Writing bands. Mark the Writing scripts against the paraphrased Writing criteria in the practice-test key (Task Achievement / Task Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy; Task 2 counts for more than Task 1). Return each script with a band estimate given as a range, one strength, one next step, and one rewritten sentence of the learner's own as a model. If you promised email, send it within a week, while the next-step plan is still fresh.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Sit with these learners during marking and help with the raw score → band table (a ruler under the row helps). For the next-step plan, offer the circle-the-option version on the band profile card (three choices per box) and let them tell you their plan aloud while you or a peer write the key words. The celebration round can be spoken or shown (a thumbs-up card with a drawing is fine).

Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Strong learners often look only at the lowest skill. Ask them to find the cheapest half band: which skill is closest to the next band boundary (raw score two or three marks short)? Ask them to set a plan that protects their strong skills (one maintenance session a week) while working on the weakest, and to write one sentence on what they'd do differently in the real test's first ten minutes.

Watch-outs
  • Comparing bands across the room: learners may ask "What did you get?" Keep profiles private; the band profile card folds in half.
  • A lower band than Practice Test 1: it happens, especially when a learner changed technique. Look at the question types together: often the new technique is working and one section went badly. Never say "you got worse."
  • "Should I cancel my test?" Don't answer in class. Talk privately, look at the gap and the test date together, and offer options (keep the date and focus on the weakest skill; move the date if the provider's rules allow). The decision is the learner's.
  • Food at the celebration: check allergies, dietary rules and fasting periods before anyone brings food. A celebration can be just words, certificates and good-luck cards.
After the course

Send (or hand out at a pickup session) the Writing bands and feedback. Point learners to the full online course for review (https://rays-esl.netlify.app/curriculum-ielts/), their workbook toolkit table and their countdown plan. If your program has a "results day," invite learners to come back and share their real results: it's the best motivation for the next group.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C14: Speaking & test-day wrap-up

Recap on slide 18, "say more, say it better, deliver it on the day": answer + why + example · one minute of keywords, two minutes of talk · opinion → reason → other view (say more) → chunk, stress, fall at the end · the precise word, one natural idiom, paraphrase around a gap (say it better) → the timing plan · the right ID · breathe out slowly · guess, mark it, move on (deliver it on the day). Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you: use those notes when you write each learner's next step in C16.

What Practice Test 2 tells you. It's the course's second full set of evidence. Read it next to the Diagnostic (C1) and Practice Test 1 (C8): the trend is the finding, not any single band. Expect Listening and Reading to move most (strategy gains); expect Writing and Speaking to move more slowly (language gains), with Speaking often lifted by extension and fluency before accuracy catches up.

If you teach the course again: keep your three-column notes from C12–C14. The most common errors (I am agree, since two years, people is, informations) and the strategies that failed most often (the short Part 2, the reason-less Part 3) are the best warmers for the next group.
