Practice Test 2 — Full Academic Mock

The second and final full mock of the course, taken after Lesson 4.3. It has the same shape as the real IELTS Academic test: Listening (4 sections, 40 questions), Reading (3 academic passages, 40 questions), Writing (Task 1 data report + Task 2 essay) and a Speaking interview in three parts. Every passage, script, chart, prompt and cue card here is original and was written for this course. It is not taken from, or adapted from, any published IELTS test. Use the results diagnostically: set them beside Practice Test 1 to see what has moved, what hasn't yet, and what each learner should do between now and test day.

Class 15: Listening ~27 min + Reading 60 min Class 16: Writing 60 min + Speaking 11–14 min each Listening /40 · Reading /40 · Writing & Speaking band estimates Target band 6.0–7.5
How to frame the last mock

By Week 8, most learners have a real test date, and some have a university offer or a registration deadline riding on it. That is exactly why this mock should feel calm and ordinary. Say it plainly before you start: "This is a rehearsal, not a verdict. It shows you where your marks are coming from and where you can still pick them up. Whatever the number, you will leave with a clear plan." Be honest about what a mock can and can't tell you: a band from one practice test can easily be half a band above or below what the same person would get on another day, so treat it as a range, not a prediction. Remind learners that a 0.5 band gain over eight weeks is common and worth celebrating; a bigger jump happens for some people, but no course can promise it.

Access arrangements and learners with literacy gaps

The real IELTS offers arrangements (extra time, enlarged print, a scribe and others) to candidates who apply in advance with evidence; tell any learner who might qualify to ask their test center well before the booking deadline. In this mock, mirror what they would really get. If a learner writes much more slowly than they speak, don't change the tasks. Give extra time only if they will have it on the day, and write "Adapted: +__ min" on the paper so that the comparison with Practice Test 1 stays honest. For low vision, print the paper at 141% (A4 to A3) and give the Task 1 data table from the teacher pages alongside the graph. A slow writer is not a weak thinker: in the debrief, separate "I ran out of time" from "I didn't know what to write".

Personal information and opinions are always optional

The speaking cue cards ask about a place, a person and a piece of technology; Part 1 asks about everyday life. No question asks about home country, family members, immigration or status, and you should not add follow-ups that do. Tell every candidate before they start: "You can answer as yourself or invent the details. I'm listening to your English, not checking your life." The same goes for the Task 2 essay: learners may argue whichever side they can develop best.

Rebuilding the Oyster Reef

For most people, an oyster is something that arrives on a plate. For marine ecologists, it is one of the hardest-working animals on the coast. Oysters are filter feeders: they draw water across their gills, trap tiny particles of algae and sediment, and release the water cleaner than before. A single adult can filter dozens of gallons in a day, and when millions of oysters settle together on a reef, the effect on a bay can be dramatic. The water becomes clearer, sunlight reaches further down, and seagrass beds, which shelter young fish and crabs, are able to grow.

Oyster reefs were once a common feature of estuaries in many parts of the world. Early records from coastal settlements describe reefs so large that they were marked on navigation charts as hazards to shipping. Over the past two centuries, however, most of these reefs have disappeared. One widely cited global estimate puts the loss at around 85 percent, which would make oyster reefs one of the most severely reduced marine habitats on the planet. Overharvesting was the first and most obvious cause: reefs were dredged so intensively that the shell base itself was carried away. Disease, pollution, and the silt that rivers washed down from cleared land then made recovery harder.

The loss of the shell base matters more than it might seem. Young oysters, known as spat, spend their first days as free-swimming larvae. To survive, they must settle on a hard surface, and they show a strong preference for the shells of other oysters. Once a reef has been dredged flat, there is little for the larvae to land on, and those that settle on soft mud are quickly buried. In other words, a damaged reef cannot simply be left alone to recover: the habitat that once allowed it to rebuild itself has gone.

Restoration projects therefore begin by putting that hard surface back. In some bays, old shells are collected from restaurants and seafood processors, left in the open air for several months so that any disease dies off, and then returned to the water in carefully placed mounds. Where shell is scarce, engineers have experimented with alternatives, including limestone rock, recycled concrete, and specially designed ceramic units. Early trials suggested that the larvae were not particularly selective about the material, provided that the surface was rough and stood clear of the mud. Researchers have since found that the height of a structure matters more than what it is made of: reefs built only a few centimeters high tend to silt over within a season or two, while taller mounds keep their upper layers clean.

In waters where few wild larvae remain, volunteers play a surprisingly large role. In several coastal communities, homeowners with waterfront access hang cages of young oysters from their docks, where the animals grow for about a year before being moved to a protected reef. The approach, known as oyster gardening, produces relatively small numbers of animals, but organizers argue that its greatest value lies elsewhere. Participants become informed supporters of the wider restoration effort, and many go on to monitor water quality near their homes, adding to the data that scientists rely on.

Rebuilt reefs offer benefits beyond cleaner water. Because they rise from the seabed, they slow waves before those waves reach the shore, and several projects have placed reefs deliberately to reduce the erosion of salt marshes and beaches. Compared with concrete sea walls, living reefs have one notable advantage: they can grow upward as sea levels rise, at least up to a point. Researchers stress, however, that reefs are not a substitute for engineered defenses where storms are severe. Their protective effect is greatest in sheltered bays, where waves are modest.

Measuring success has proved difficult. A reef may look healthy in its first year, while the transplanted oysters are thriving, and then decline once they die if no new larvae settle. For this reason, many scientists now argue that a project should be judged successful only when the reef has shown natural reproduction for several consecutive years. Monitoring for this long is expensive, and funding is often provided for the construction phase alone, which leaves a gap between what has been built and what is actually known about it.

Despite these challenges, the outlook is more hopeful than it was two decades ago. Restoration techniques have improved, harvesting rules in many regions are stricter, and fishing communities that once saw restoration as a threat have in some cases become its partners, recognizing that a healthy reef eventually supports a more reliable harvest. Nobody expects the vast reefs of earlier centuries to return in full. The evidence increasingly suggests, however, that when oysters are given a firm place to settle and time to grow, they are capable of doing much of the rebuilding themselves.

Farming Without Fields
Can vertical farms change the way cities are fed?

APicture a warehouse on the edge of a city. Inside, there is no soil, no sunlight and no change of season: only tall racks of lettuce, basil and spinach growing in stacked trays under rows of pink-and-blue lights. The roots of the plants hang in shallow channels of water enriched with exactly the nutrients each crop requires. This is a vertical farm, and in the past fifteen years such farms have moved from research laboratories into commercial buildings in many parts of the world. Supporters describe them as the future of food. Critics call them an expensive answer to a problem that ordinary farms already solve perfectly well.

BThe case in favor begins with resources. Because the water in a vertical farm circulates in a closed system and is recycled rather than draining into the ground, operators commonly report using a small fraction of the water needed to grow the same crop outdoors. Stacking crops in layers also means that a building with a modest footprint can produce as much as many acres of open land. And because the growing rooms are sealed, insects and other pests find it very hard to get in, so chemical pesticides are rarely needed.

CA second advantage is location. A vertical farm can be built inside a city, close to the people who will eat what it grows. Leafy greens harvested in the morning can be on supermarket shelves by the afternoon, instead of spending several days in a refrigerated truck. The produce therefore tends to last longer once it reaches the customer's kitchen, and less is thrown away along the way. Retailers, for their part, value a supply that does not depend on the weather or on long-distance transport. Dr. Amina Osei, a plant scientist who has advised several growers, believes that this is where the real commercial value lies. "Companies talk a great deal about saving water," she says, "but when you ask shoppers why they buy the product, the answer is almost always that it stays fresh for longer."

DThe central difficulty is energy. Sunlight is free; electric light is not. Lighting and climate control make energy the largest single operating cost for most vertical farms, and it rises and falls with the price of electricity. Professor Martin Keller, an energy economist, has calculated the cost of growing staple foods under lights and concludes that it would be wildly uneconomic for grains such as wheat or rice. This explains why nearly every vertical farm grows the same narrow range of crops: leafy greens and herbs, which grow quickly, are mostly water, and sell for a relatively high price per kilogram. Potatoes, cereals and most fruit remain far beyond what the technology can produce at a sensible cost. Even for leafy greens, profit margins are thin: a small change in the price of electricity can decide whether a harvest makes money or loses it.

EThe industry has already had painful lessons. In recent years, a number of well-funded vertical farming companies have closed or sharply reduced their operations, most often after energy prices rose faster than they had planned for. According to Keller, the firms that came through this period in better shape tended to share one feature: they had located their farms next to a cheap and stable power supply, such as a hydroelectric plant, or had arranged to use waste heat from a neighboring factory. "In this business," he argues, "where you plug in can matter more than what you grow."

FAt the same time, the technology is improving. Lighting has become far more efficient over the last decade, and growers can now adjust the color and timing of light to suit each stage of a plant's life. Some researchers believe the next gains will come from the plants themselves. Dr. Rosa Lindqvist, a horticulturist, points out that almost every crop variety grown today was bred to cope with outdoor life, including wind, strong sun and uneven rainfall. Plants bred specifically for indoor conditions, she suggests, could be more compact, grow faster and waste less energy on features they no longer need. Lindqvist is more cautious about automation, however. Robots that move trays and harvest crops can reduce labor costs, she notes, but they bring expenses of their own, from purchase to maintenance.

GWhat, then, is the realistic future of farming without fields? Most researchers now see vertical farms as a complement to conventional agriculture rather than a replacement for it. They make the most sense in places where fresh produce is otherwise difficult to supply: desert cities that import most of their vegetables, northern regions with long, dark winters, or crowded cities where land is extremely expensive. "The question was never whether vertical farms could feed the world. They can't," says Osei. "The useful question is where they make sense, and for which crops."

The Stubborn Science of Habit
Why good intentions so often fail, and what works instead

Every January, gyms fill with new members and then, by the middle of February, quietly empty again. The pattern is so familiar that it is usually put down to weak willpower. Yet several decades of research in psychology suggest that this explanation is not only unkind but largely mistaken. The people who succeed in changing their behavior are not, on the whole, those with the most self-control. They are the people who have arranged their lives, often without realizing it, so that they need to use self-control as little as possible.

A habit, in the psychological sense, is not simply something we do often. It is a behavior that has become linked to a particular cue, such as a place, a time of day, or a preceding action, so that the cue triggers the behavior with very little conscious thought. Brushing one's teeth after breakfast is a classic example: few people stop to deliberate about it. This automatic quality is exactly what makes habits valuable. Some diary studies have suggested that roughly two in five of our everyday actions are repeated in the same setting almost every day. Because these actions run without much thought, they free up mental resources and leave more attention for the decisions that genuinely need it.

How long does it take to form a new habit? Popular advice often gives a precise answer, usually twenty-one days. The origin of this figure is obscure, and in my view it has done real harm, because people who have not "got the habit" by the third week tend to conclude that they have failed and give up. Research that has followed volunteers as they tried to build new routines paints a far more varied picture. In one well-known study, the time it took for a simple daily behavior, such as drinking a glass of water after breakfast, to feel automatic ranged from a few weeks to most of a year, with an average of around two months. Encouragingly, the same study found that missing an occasional day made little difference to progress.

If willpower is not the key, what is? Much of the answer lies in the environment. Researchers have repeatedly found that small changes in convenience have surprisingly large effects on what people do. In workplace experiments, simply moving healthier food to the front of a cafeteria counter increased how often it was chosen, even though nothing else had changed. People who have to walk past the stairs before they reach the elevator use the stairs more often. The principle works in both directions: adding a little effort to an unwanted behavior, such as keeping snacks on a high shelf, makes it less likely. To my mind, this is the most practical finding in the whole field, and also the most neglected.

Habits are also especially fragile at moments of transition. Moving to a new home, starting a new job or retiring disrupts the cues that used to trigger our routines, and for a while old habits lose some of their grip. Some researchers describe this period as a window of opportunity. In one study, people who had recently moved house were more likely than long-term residents to change the way they traveled to work after receiving information about local bus routes. I would argue that organizations hoping to encourage healthier behavior would do well to focus their efforts on such moments, rather than spreading them evenly across everyone.

Breaking a bad habit, however, is harder than forming a good one, because the link between cue and response survives even when motivation disappears. Someone who has decided to stop checking a phone during dinner may still find a hand reaching for it the moment it buzzes. Simply trying not to respond, a strategy psychologists call suppression, takes considerable effort, and it tends to fail when people are tired or under stress, which is precisely when old habits are strongest. Two approaches work better. The first is to remove the cue altogether, for instance by leaving the phone in another room. The second is to keep the cue but attach a different behavior to it, such as reaching for a glass of water instead.

Rewards matter too, but not all rewards are equal. The brain responds far more strongly to what happens now than to what might happen later. This is a serious problem for many healthy behaviors, whose benefits are real but distant: the reward for regular exercise may not be felt for months. One practical solution is to pair the behavior with something enjoyable that happens at once, for example allowing oneself to listen to a favorite podcast only while out walking.

None of this means that individuals carry no responsibility for their choices, or that environment is destiny. But I am convinced that the language of willpower, with its suggestion of personal weakness, discourages people from making the practical changes that actually work. A person who says, "I lack discipline" has diagnosed a flaw in their character, and there is little they can do about it by Monday. A person who says, "My running shoes are at the back of the closet" has identified a problem that can be solved in thirty seconds.

Section 4 · Lecture: the acoustics of concert halls (Questions 31–40)
Section 4. You will hear part of a lecture on architectural acoustics. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40. [PAUSE 45 SECONDS] Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40.
Lecturer: Good morning. In today's lecture, I'm going to look at why some concert halls sound wonderful while others, sometimes very expensive ones, disappoint both musicians and audiences. The key idea is something called reverberation. When a musician plays a note and then stops, the sound doesn't disappear at once. It continues for a moment because of reflections from the walls, the ceiling and the floor, which reach our ears slightly later than the original sound. That lingering sound is reverberation.

How much reverberation do we want? It depends on what the room is for. For orchestral music, around two seconds is generally considered ideal: it makes the sound rich and warm. For speech, though, a shorter time is far better, because long reverberation blurs one syllable into the next, and listeners struggle to follow what is being said.

For most of history, halls were designed by trial and error. Architects copied halls that seemed to work and hoped for the best. The first scientific study came at the very end of the nineteenth century, when a young physics lecturer at an American university was asked to fix a lecture room where students simply couldn't understand the speaker. He had no electronic equipment, so he played a note on an organ pipe and timed how long the sound lasted with a stopwatch. Then, late at night, he and his assistants carried in hundreds of cushions borrowed from a nearby theater and measured again. Each time more cushions were added, the reverberation became shorter, and from these experiments he worked out the first formula for predicting it.

Let's turn to shape. Many of the halls that musicians love are what we call "shoebox" halls: long, tall and relatively narrow. Their success seems to come largely from the side walls. Reflections from the side walls reach listeners from the left and right, and this gives the feeling of being surrounded by the music. A more recent design is the "vineyard" hall, where the audience sits on terraces around the stage, a little like the rows of a vineyard on a hillside. This brings everyone closer to the performers, although it is harder to get right acoustically.

Materials matter just as much. Hard surfaces, such as plaster and wood, reflect sound, while soft ones absorb it, and one of the biggest absorbers in any hall is the audience itself, with all its clothing. This created a problem: a hall that sounded perfect in rehearsal could sound dull and dry once it was full. The solution is surprisingly simple. Modern seats are designed to absorb about the same amount of sound when they are empty as when someone is sitting in them, so the hall sounds much the same at a rehearsal as at a sold-out concert.

Designers also have to avoid certain problems. Curved walls, for example, can focus sound on one part of the audience, like a mirror focusing light, and cause echoes. To prevent this, many halls use diffusers: irregular surfaces, often made of carved wood, which scatter sound in many directions so that it spreads evenly.

Finally, modern halls often host very different kinds of events, from string quartets to amplified pop concerts. So some are now built with adjustable features. The most common are heavy fabric banners that can be lowered from the ceiling to absorb sound and shorten the reverberation when a band brings its own loudspeakers, and raised again for an orchestra.

  4. Is it a good thing that machines now do so many household tasks?
  5. How do you think technology will change the way people work over the next twenty years?
  6. Are there situations where people should choose not to use technology?
What to listen for in Part 3
  • A clear answer first, then a reason, an example, and a contrast or qualification (the Lesson 4.1 extension routine)
  • Speculation and hypothesis: It's likely that…, If cities invested in…, people would…
  • General, not only personal, examples: In many cities…, For older people…
  • Paraphrase when a word is missing (Lesson 4.2), without long silences
