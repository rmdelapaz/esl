Practice Test 1 — Listening & Academic Reading

A full-length, timed Listening test (4 sections, 40 questions, teacher-read) and a full-length Academic Reading test (3 passages, 40 questions, 60 minutes), taken after Module 2. It uses every question type from Lessons 2.1–2.3. Learners self-mark it in Class 9, convert their raw scores to an approximate band, and sort their mistakes by cause. That error analysis, not the band, is the real product of this class.

Listening ~28 min · /40 Reading 60 min · /40 Self-marked in Class 9 All texts original
How to frame it

This is the first time most learners will sit a full Listening and Reading test against the clock, and the pressure is real. Name it: "Today is a rehearsal. It's supposed to feel like the real thing so that the real thing feels familiar. The score tells us which question types to work on in Weeks 5 to 7; it doesn't decide anything about you." Remind them of three rules from Module 2: read ahead and take the final version in Listening, about 20 minutes per passage in Reading, and never leave a blank, because a wrong answer costs nothing. Many learners score lower on a full test than on a single passage; tell them in advance, so the drop doesn't feel like failure.

Reading the scripts aloud well

Read at a natural conversational speed (about the speed of a radio interview), with contractions and normal rhythm. Section 4 should be a little faster and denser, like a real lecture. Never repeat a line, slow down for an answer, or look at a learner when an answer comes. Distractors are part of the test: say the wrong detail naturally, then the correction naturally. For Sections 1 and 3, change your voice or your position for each speaker, or better, read with a colleague or a confident former learner. Spell names letter by letter at a normal pace, as a receptionist would.

Learners who need adjustments

A learner with a documented need (dyslexia, a visual or hearing impairment) can have the same arrangements here that they will request from the test center: an enlarged paper, extra time for Reading, or a seat close to you for Listening. Note it on the answer sheet ("Adapted: +15 min Reading"). The official test offers special arrangements, but they must be requested well before the test date, so remind the learner privately to contact their test center early.

Pen, Keyboard and Memory

For most of the twentieth century, a student who wanted to record a lecture had one option: a pen and a notebook. Today, in many universities, the typical sound of a lecture hall is the soft tapping of keys, and many students also photograph the lecture slides with their phones. Laptops allow students to take notes faster, to store them neatly and to search them later, and few would argue that these are trivial advantages. Yet over the past two decades, a growing body of research has asked whether the speed of typing might come at a cost to learning.

The question is not simply which method records more information. Typists almost always capture more words than people writing by hand; in one classroom study conducted by the educational psychologist Mara Lindqvist, students using laptops recorded on average about 60 percent more words than those using pens. The puzzle is what happens to those words afterward. Lindqvist found that when both groups were tested a week later on the content of the lecture, the handwriters performed slightly better on questions that required them to explain or apply a concept, although the two groups did equally well on questions about simple facts.

Lindqvist's explanation centers on what she calls "processing on the way in." Because handwriting is slow, a student cannot write down everything a lecturer says. Instead, they must decide what matters, condense it and put it into their own words. Typists, by contrast, tend to transcribe the lecture almost word for word. In Lindqvist's view, it is the summarizing, not the pen itself, that strengthens memory. This distinction matters, because it suggests that a typist who deliberately summarizes might gain the same benefit.

Later studies have partly supported this view. When a team led by Tomás Arriaga instructed laptop users not to copy the lecturer's words but to paraphrase them, the gap between the two groups became smaller, though it did not disappear entirely. Arriaga suspects that the habit of word-for-word typing is hard to break: many participants, he reported, drifted back into transcription after the first ten minutes, particularly when the lecturer spoke quickly.

Other researchers have looked beyond note-taking to the physical act of forming letters. Studies of young children learning to read suggest that writing letters by hand, rather than selecting them on a keyboard, helps children to recognize those letters more quickly. Whether this effect continues into adulthood is less clear. Adults already know the alphabet thoroughly, and it is uncertain whether the movement of the hand adds anything to their memory for complex ideas. Even so, many schools continue to teach handwriting in the early years, even where pupils later move on to keyboards.

There are also practical factors that experiments in quiet laboratories tend to overlook. In a real lecture hall, a laptop offers constant temptation: messages, news and shopping sites are only a click away. In a survey of 900 undergraduates, almost two-thirds admitted to doing unrelated tasks on their laptops during lectures, and those who did so reported lower satisfaction with their courses. Critics point out, however, that a bored student with a notebook can simply doodle in the margins, and that the laptop may be blamed for a lack of interest that has other causes.

For students with certain disabilities, meanwhile, the keyboard is not a convenience but a necessity. Students who find handwriting physically painful, or whose handwriting is very difficult to read, may learn far better with a laptop, and many universities provide one as a matter of course. Any policy that banned devices from lecture halls, researchers warn, would need to take such students into account.

What, then, should a student do? The emerging consensus is less dramatic than newspaper headlines sometimes suggest. Neither method guarantees good learning, and neither condemns a student to poor results. The key appears to be active engagement with the material: selecting, summarizing and connecting ideas rather than recording them passively. For some students, the slowness of handwriting forces this engagement; others can achieve it on a keyboard with deliberate practice. Perhaps the most useful advice is also the oldest: notes are only valuable if they are reviewed, and a notebook left unopened until the night before an exam will help little, whether it was written in ink or stored in the cloud.

Farming Upward

AOn an industrial estate at the edge of a large city, a windowless warehouse produces around fifty tons of salad leaves every year. Inside, lettuce, basil and other herbs grow in shallow trays stacked twelve levels high, lit by rows of pink-purple LEDs. There is no soil. Instead, the roots hang in a thin, slowly moving film of water that carries a carefully measured mixture of nutrients, while sensors adjust the temperature, humidity and light for each crop. This is vertical farming, and its most enthusiastic supporters describe it as the biggest change in food production since the invention of the tractor.

BThe appeal of the idea is easy to understand. Because the crops are grown indoors, they are protected from drought, floods, storms and insect pests, so the farm does not need pesticides, and harvests can be planned to the day, all year round. Because conditions never change, crops can also be grown to the same size and flavor every time, which appeals to restaurants and supermarkets. Water is collected and recycled through the system, and operators claim to use as little as five percent of the water that field agriculture requires for the same crop. And because the farms can be built close to the people who will eat the food, produce can reach supermarket shelves within hours of harvest, rather than after several days in a refrigerated truck.

CThe greatest obstacle is energy. Sunlight is free; electric light is not. Although LEDs have become far more efficient over the past fifteen years, a vertical farm still needs a great deal of electricity for lighting, heating, cooling and pumping water. When energy prices rose sharply in several countries, a number of well-funded vertical-farming companies were forced to cut production, and some closed altogether. According to the agricultural economist Helen Mwangi, energy can account for between a quarter and a third of a vertical farm's running costs, a far higher share than on a conventional farm. This makes the business unusually sensitive to changes in the price of electricity. Some operators have tried to reduce their bills by running the lights at night, when power is cheaper.

DThere is also the question of what can be grown. Leafy greens and herbs are ideal: they grow quickly, are small enough to stack, are harvested whole and sell at relatively high prices. Some companies are experimenting with strawberries and tomatoes, but these fruiting crops need more light and more careful handling than leaves. Staple crops such as wheat and rice are a different matter. They need large amounts of light to produce grain, only part of the plant is eaten, and they sell cheaply. Mwangi calculates that wheat grown indoors would currently cost many times more to produce than wheat grown in fields, which is why she believes that vertical farms are unlikely ever to feed the world on their own. "They are a supplement to traditional farming, not a replacement for it," she says.

EEnvironmental comparisons are more complicated than they first appear. A vertical farm uses very little land and far less water than a field, but if its electricity is generated from coal or gas, the carbon emissions linked to each kilogram of lettuce may be higher than those of lettuce grown outdoors and transported a long distance. The engineer Sofia Brandt, who has studied the energy use of indoor farms in northern Europe, argues that the environmental case depends almost entirely on where the power comes from. Farms that run on renewable energy, or that are built beside factories and data centers so that they can use the waste heat those buildings produce, compare much more favorably.

FSome of the most interesting projects are small. Schools, hospitals and community centers have begun to install compact growing units, often no bigger than a wardrobe, which supply fresh herbs and salad to their own kitchens. These units rarely make money in a narrow financial sense, but their supporters argue that they have other kinds of value. Teachers report that children who have watched a lettuce grow from seed are more willing to eat it, and hospital staff say that patients enjoy seeing food growing nearby. In several cities, empty stores and unused parking lots have been turned into small farms that also offer training to local people who want to work in the industry.

The Uses of Boredom

Boredom has a poor reputation. It is associated with dull meetings, long journeys and wasted afternoons, and an entire industry of entertainment exists to make sure that we never have to experience it. With a smartphone in almost every pocket, it is now possible to fill every spare moment (a line at the bank, a bus ride, the minute before a meeting begins) with news, messages or games. It is worth asking, however, whether in escaping boredom so efficiently, we have lost something of value.

Psychologists have not always agreed on what boredom actually is. For much of the twentieth century, it was treated mainly as a problem of the environment: a task or a place was simply not stimulating enough. More recent work, however, treats boredom as a signal generated by the mind itself. The psychologist Hana Okafor describes it as "an alarm that tells us our current activity is not using our abilities," comparable in some ways to hunger, which tells us to find food. On this view, boredom is not a failure but a message, one that pushes us to look for something more meaningful to do.

The most widely publicized claim is that boredom encourages creativity. In a frequently cited experiment, Lucas Brenner asked one group of participants to spend fifteen minutes copying numbers from a telephone directory, a deliberately tedious task, while a second group went straight to the next stage. Both groups were then asked to think of as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups. The bored group produced more ideas, and their ideas were judged to be more original. Brenner concluded that boredom may push the mind to wander, and that mind-wandering helps people to make unusual connections.

I find this result intriguing, but I think it has been overstated. The experiment measured a short burst of creative thinking in a laboratory, immediately after a boring task; it tells us little about whether a life with more boredom produces more creative work. Moreover, attempts to repeat Brenner's study have produced mixed results. Ines Castillo, who ran a larger version with more than 400 participants, found only a small effect, and no effect at all among participants who had been allowed to keep their phones during the boring task. Castillo's interpretation is revealing: boredom seems to help only when people cannot immediately escape from it.

That last point deserves more attention than it usually receives. The problem with the smartphone is not that it prevents boredom altogether, but that it interrupts it at the earliest possible moment, before the mind has had time to wander anywhere interesting. Rafael Kim, who studies attention in adolescents, has found that teenagers who reach for their phones as soon as they feel bored report being bored more often, not less. Kim suggests that constant stimulation may raise the level at which ordinary life feels interesting enough, in the same way that a diet high in sugar makes fruit taste less sweet.

None of this means that boredom is always good. Chronic boredom, the kind that persists for weeks or months, is associated with low mood, overeating and risk-taking, and it is often a symptom of deeper problems such as unsuitable work or social isolation. Telling a person in this situation to "embrace boredom" would be unhelpful and possibly unkind. The kind of boredom that may be useful is brief and situational: the ten minutes on a train with nothing to read, not the months in a job that offers no challenge.

It also seems to me that the value of boredom lies less in the feeling itself than in what we do next. Okafor's "alarm" can be answered in many ways. We can reach for the nearest distraction, or we can take the signal seriously and ask what we would rather be doing. Some people use such moments to plan, to reflect or simply to notice their surroundings. Schools that have experimented with short, unstructured periods in the day report that, after some initial complaints, many students begin to fill the time with their own projects, although these reports come mostly from teachers and have not yet been studied systematically.

Should we, then, try to be bored more often? I would not go that far. Deliberately seeking out boredom sounds rather like deliberately seeking out hunger, and there is little evidence that it would make anyone happier. A more modest conclusion seems justified: we should be less afraid of the occasional empty moment, and less quick to fill it. The next time the bus is late, leaving the phone in a pocket for a few minutes may not produce a great idea. But it will give the mind the chance to go looking for one.

Section 4 · A lecture on urban heat islands
Section 4. You will hear part of a lecture on urban heat islands. First, you have some time to look at Questions 31 to 40. [pause 45 seconds] Now listen carefully and answer Questions 31 to 40.

Good afternoon. Today I want to look at a phenomenon that affects almost every large city in the world: the urban heat island. Put simply, an urban heat island is a city, or part of a city, that is noticeably warmer than the rural areas around it. On a summer day, the difference can be several degrees.

Now, you might expect the difference to be greatest in the middle of the day, when the sun is strongest. In fact, it's usually greatest at night. That's because city surfaces absorb heat during the day and then release it slowly after sunset, so cities cool down far more slowly than the countryside.

So what causes the effect? I'll mention four main factors. The first is the materials that cities are built from. Dark surfaces absorb much more of the sun's energy than light ones, and cities are full of them: dark roofs and, above all, the asphalt we use on roads and parking lots.

The second factor is the lack of vegetation. Plants release water through their leaves, and as that water evaporates, it cools the surrounding air. When we replace fields and trees with concrete, we lose that natural cooling.

Third, cities produce heat of their own. Vehicles, factories and air conditioners all release waste heat into the street. Air conditioners are a particularly unfortunate case, since they cool the inside of a building by pumping heat outside.

And the fourth factor is the shape of the city itself. Tall buildings close together create what are sometimes called urban canyons. These trap heat, and they also block the wind that would otherwise carry the warm air away.

What are the consequences? The most obvious is higher demand for electricity, as people turn up their air conditioning, which, as I've just said, makes the problem worse. More seriously, heat islands are a risk to health. During heat waves, the danger is greatest for elderly people, especially those who live alone and may not have anyone checking on them.

Fortunately, there are solutions, and some of them are surprisingly simple. One is the so-called "cool roof." Many cities now encourage building owners to paint their roofs white, so that they reflect sunlight rather than absorbing it. Another is to plant more trees, particularly along streets and in school playgrounds. Trees provide shade, and they cool the air through evaporation, as I described earlier.

In one study, researchers measured the temperature of pavements in the afternoon sun and compared them with pavements shaded by trees. The shaded pavements were up to twelve degrees Celsius cooler. You may see a figure of fifteen degrees quoted in some newspapers, but that was recorded on a single, exceptionally hot day, so twelve is the more reliable figure.

Of course, none of these measures is a complete answer on its own, and next week we'll look at how city planners combine them.

That is the end of Section 4. You now have two minutes to check your answers. [pause 2 minutes] That is the end of the Listening test.
