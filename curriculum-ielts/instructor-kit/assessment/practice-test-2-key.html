Scoring principles (mark as strictly as the real test)

One mark per question; no half marks; no penalty for wrong answers. In Listening and Reading completion items, the answer must be spelled correctly and must stay within the word limit: a correct answer that breaks the limit scores zero, exactly as Lessons 1.1 and 2.1 teach. Plurals count (gloves, not glove). Capital letters, lower case, or a mix are all fine. American and British spellings are both accepted (center/centre, neighbor/neighbour, upward/upwards, color/colour), as the online lessons explain. Numbers may be written as digits or words. Words already printed on the paper ($, plot, the) don't need to be repeated; if a learner repeats them and stays within the limit, accept the answer. On the Reading answer sheet, learners were told they may write T / F / NG and Y / N / NG. Writing True for a Yes/No/Not Given item scores zero in the real test; do the same here, and say why in the debrief (Lesson 2.3).

Why strict? A generous mock gives a comfortable number and an unpleasant surprise on test day. Marking strictly shows each learner exactly which marks are "free": spelling, plurals and word limits usually account for 1–3 marks, which can be half a band.

Overall band (as Lesson 1.1 teaches)

Average the four skill bands and round to the nearest half band: an average ending in .25 rounds up to .5, and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band. Example: L 6.5 · R 6.0 · W 6.0 · S 6.5 → 25 ÷ 4 = 6.25 → about 6.5.

Because Writing and Speaking are your estimates, the overall is doubly approximate. Report it as a range if one skill sits on a boundary: "about 6.0–6.5".

One mark matters most at the edges. A learner on 29 in Listening is one mark from about 7.0. Show them which of their wrong answers were spelling, plural or word-limit slips: that is their quickest half band.

Writing · Band-Estimation Sheet

The four criteria below are paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors in plain language for classroom use. They are not the official wording, and your result is a teacher's estimate. Read the whole script once for a general impression, then decide each criterion separately, starting from band 6 and moving up or down. A script usually sits within one band across the four criteria; if one criterion is two bands lower than the others, that criterion is the learner's biggest opportunity.

There is a big-picture sentence, but the choice of detail is uneven: some important points are thin while minor ones get too much space; a few figures are misread. The big picture is stated clearly and early; the most important trends and contrasts are chosen and backed up with correct figures; some points could go a little further. Nothing important is missing; the big picture is sharp; figures are chosen selectively and are accurate; any weaknesses are small and rare.

Every part is answered, and the writer's view is clear from start to finish; main points are explained and supported, though some are a little general or lose focus. The question is answered fully, and the view is clear and well argued; points are relevant and well supported, with only the occasional gap.

Plenty of complex sentences, many of them completely correct; the writer is clearly in control, with a few mistakes left. A wide variety of sentence types; the great majority are correct; the few mistakes look like slips, not patterns.
Quick caps to check first (Lessons 3.1–3.3)
  • Task 1 with no overview is unlikely to reach 6 for Task Achievement, however accurate the details are
  • Under 150 / 250 words lowers Task Achievement/Response. Count the words; don't guess
  • Task 2 that gives only one view, or no opinion, doesn't answer "discuss both views and give your own opinion"
  • Memorized chunks that don't fit the question are not credited as the candidate's own language
  • No paragraphs limits Coherence & Cohesion, however good the linking words are
Combining the two tasks

Task 2 counts for about twice as much as Task 1. The exact method is not published in full, so for this mock use: Writing ≈ (Task 1 band + 2 × Task 2 band) ÷ 3, rounded to the nearest half band. Example: Task 1 about 6.0, Task 2 about 6.5 → (6 + 13) ÷ 3 = 6.33 → about 6.5. If you are between two bands, report the lower one: a pleasant surprise on results day is kinder than a disappointing one.

Moderation. If two teachers can each estimate the same five scripts on their own and then compare, do it: aim to agree within half a band, and talk through any script where you differ by more.

Task 1 · What a Strong Answer Includes

Key features (the "must-mentions")
  • Overview: recycling rose in all three cities, but at very different rates; Westvale moved from the lowest rate to the highest
  • Easton: the highest in 2000 (25%), then almost flat (25–31% until 2020, 33% in 2025)
  • Northport: steady, uninterrupted growth from 12% to 52%, overtaking Easton around 2010
  • Westvale: slow at first (5% → 14% by 2010), then the steepest rise, level with Easton at 30% in 2015, ending at 55%, just above Northport
What to watch for
  • Year-by-year listing of all 18 figures: accurate but mechanical (Task Achievement about 5–6)
  • Invented causes ("because the government introduced new bins"): the graph gives no reasons
  • Misread crossovers, e.g. "Westvale overtook Northport in 2020" (44% vs 46%: it didn't)
  • Trend-language errors: "increased 40%" vs "increased by 40 percentage points"; "the recycling was grown"
Model answer · Task 1 · about band 7 (163 words)

The line graph compares the proportion of household waste that was recycled in three cities, Northport, Easton and Westvale, over the 25 years from 2000 to 2025.1

Overall, recycling rates increased in all three cities, but the pace of change varied considerably. Northport and Westvale saw dramatic growth, while Easton's rate rose only modestly, and by 2025 Westvale, which had started with the lowest figure, had the highest.2

In 2000, Easton recycled a quarter of its household waste, compared with 12% in Northport and only 5% for Westvale.3 Easton's figure then changed very little, fluctuating between 25% and 31% until 2020 and reaching 33% by 2025.

Northport, by contrast, grew steadily throughout the period. It overtook Easton in 2010 and had reached just over half (52%) by 2025.4 Westvale's rate rose slowly at first, to 14% in 2010, but then increased sharply. It drew level with Easton at 30% in 2015 and went on to reach 55% in 2025, slightly above Northport's figure.5

  1. Introduction paraphrases the prompt (percentage → proportion; between 2000 and 2025 → over the 25 years from…) instead of copying it (Lesson 1.2).
  2. A clear overview with no numbers, placed second so it can't be forgotten, and it names the single most striking feature (the change of rank). This is what lifts Task Achievement to 7 (Lesson 3.1).
  3. Grouping: the writer groups the "stable" city first and the two "growing" cities together, rather than going year by year. One small slip: in Northport … for Westvale mixes prepositions. It is minor and doesn't affect the meaning (Grammatical Range & Accuracy stays about 7).
  4. Approximation language (just over half) plus the exact figure in brackets: accurate and reader-friendly.
  5. Why about 7 and not 8: the comparison between Northport and Westvale could be sharper (for example, Westvale's rise of 41 percentage points in 15 years against Northport's 25), and the trend vocabulary repeats rose / increased / grew without much variety of structure (a steep rise, the sharpest increase). Coherence & Cohesion is secure (about 7); Lexical Resource is about 7 (fluctuating, drew level, dramatic growth are well chosen).

Estimate: TA 7 CC 7 LR 7 GRA 7 → Task 1 about 7.0.

Task 2 · What a Strong Answer Includes

Question analysis (Lesson 3.2)
  • Type: discussion ("Discuss both views and give your own opinion"): three jobs, not two
  • Structure it pushes: Body 1 = the view for fees · Body 2 = the view for free entry · the writer's position clear in the introduction and the conclusion (a partial position or a compromise is fine if it is clear)
  • Typical ideas, fees: running and conservation costs; less reliance on taxes or donors; people may value what they pay for; tourists could contribute
  • Typical ideas, free: education and culture for everyone; low-income families; repeat and short visits; wider benefits for society
What to watch for
  • An essay that argues only one side (Task Response about 5–6, however fluent)
  • An opinion that appears only in the last line, or that changes between the introduction and the conclusion
  • Lists of reasons with no development: each main idea needs a "why" and an example (Lesson 3.2, "Developing ideas")
  • Over-linking (Moreover… Furthermore… In addition… in every sentence), which lowers Coherence & Cohesion (Lesson 3.3)
Model answer · Task 2 · about band 7 (299 words)

Museums and galleries hold some of a society's most valuable objects, and people disagree about whether the public should pay to see them. While there are reasonable arguments for charging an entrance fee, I believe that free admission, at least to permanent collections, brings greater benefits overall.1

Those who support entrance fees point out that museums are expensive to run. Buildings must be heated, lit and secured, and delicate objects require specialist care. If visitors pay even a small amount, this money can reduce a museum's dependence on public funding or private donors, and it may allow it to open for longer hours or to improve its exhibitions.2 It is also argued that people value what they pay for, so a fee might encourage visitors to spend longer and look more carefully.

On the other hand, supporters of free entry argue that museums exist to educate everyone, not only those who can afford a ticket. A family on a low income may be unable to pay for four tickets, and yet such families are precisely the people who might benefit most from a day of learning together.3 Free entry also encourages short, repeated visits: a student can drop in for twenty minutes to look at a single painting, which would hardly be worth paying for.

In my view, the second argument is more convincing, because the main purpose of a public museum is to share knowledge as widely as possible. However, the financial concerns are real, so a sensible compromise would be to keep permanent collections free while charging for special temporary exhibitions and inviting visitors to make voluntary donations.4

In conclusion, although fees can help museums to survive financially, free access to their core collections serves the public better, provided that museums find other ways to raise income.5

  1. Introduction: a paraphrase of the question plus a clear, qualified thesis (at least to permanent collections). The reader knows the position from the start (Task Response).
  2. Body 1 is developed: claim → explanation (costs) → consequence (less dependence, longer hours). A second idea is added more briefly. A band 8 answer would develop that second idea with an example.
  3. Body 2 gives a concrete, general example (a low-income family, a student's twenty-minute visit) rather than a personal story. precisely the people who and would hardly be worth show a good range of structure (Grammatical Range & Accuracy about 7).
  4. A separate opinion paragraph with a compromise: this fully answers "give your own opinion" and keeps the position consistent with the introduction.
  5. Conclusion restates the position without repeating the introduction word for word. provided that is a precise conditional.

Why about 7 and not 8: the ideas are relevant and clear, but some are predictable and only briefly supported (the "people value what they pay for" point is asserted, not explained); the vocabulary is accurate and natural but mostly common (expensive to run, afford a ticket), with a few stronger items such as dependence on and voluntary donations; linking is correct but a little formulaic (On the other hand, In my view, In conclusion). There are no errors that affect meaning. Estimate: TR 7 CC 7 LR 7 GRA 7 → Task 2 about 7.0. Writing overall: (7 + 2 × 7) ÷ 3 = about 7.0.

Using the models with learners

Don't hand out the models before learners have seen their own estimate, or they will compare surface features ("it's longer than mine") instead of criteria. In the feedback session, give the Task 1 model with the notes removed and ask learners to find (1) the overview, (2) how the data is grouped, and (3) one sentence they could write in the same way about their own graph. Then give them the notes. For Task 2, ask them to underline the thesis and the opinion paragraph, then check that their own essay has the same "three jobs".

Each criterion counts equally; the Speaking band is the average, rounded to the nearest half band. Listen for the candidate's typical performance across the whole interview, not their best or worst moment. Part 3 usually shows the ceiling.

A wide range of structures used with ease; most sentences are correct, with only the occasional mistake.
Pronunciation Can be understood, but mispronounced words and flat or misplaced stress sometimes make the listener work hard. Understood throughout, although some sounds or words are unclear at times; stress and intonation are used, with uneven control. Easy to follow; stress, intonation and linking mostly help the message; the accent makes little difference to understanding. Easy to follow throughout; a wide range of features (stress, rhythm, intonation, chunking) with only the odd lapse; the accent makes almost no difference.
Deciding between two bands
  • Fluency 6 or 7? Ask: are the pauses about words (6) or about ideas (7)? Can they develop a Part 3 answer for 30–40 seconds without drifting?
  • Lexical 6 or 7? Count less common items or collocations that were used accurately (a steep learning curve, it's a double-edged sword). One or two memorized idioms aren't range
  • Grammar 6 or 7? Are errors mainly in complex sentences (6), or are there many completely correct complex sentences (7)?
  • Pronunciation: judge how much effort you need to follow. An accent is never a fault in itself (Lesson 4.2)
Short-form ("SF") interviews

With only about 6 minutes, Fluency & Coherence and Lexical Resource are harder to judge, because there are fewer chances to recover from a slow start. Give the estimate as a range ("about 6.0–6.5") and make the lower end the headline. If a learner's target depends on Speaking, offer a full interview before their test date.

Realistic next steps by profile
  • Listening below target: the error grid usually points to D (distractors) or S (spelling or plural). Twice a week: one full section, single play, marked strictly, with every error written in a "traps I fell for" list
  • Reading below target: check timing first. If Passage 3 was rushed, practice the 20-minute cap and the "quick types first" order (Lesson 2.2). If True/False/Not Given is the leak, practice the "point to the clashing words" test (Lesson 2.3)
  • Writing below target: find the lowest criterion. The quickest gains are an overview in Task 1, a clear position in Task 2, and developed (not listed) ideas. One timed Task 2 a week with feedback beats three without
  • Speaking below target: record a Part 2 three times a week and score it with the sheet; practice the Part 3 extension routine (answer → reason → example → contrast)
  • At or above target in every skill: keep practicing lightly, and consider booking the test while the skills are fresh
When the gap is large and the date is close

If the weakest skill is a full band or more below the requirement and the test is less than a month away, say so honestly and kindly. Outline the options: keep the date and treat it as experience; ask the test center about moving the date (fees and deadlines vary, so check the terms); or check whether the institution accepts a later result. Never say "you won't pass". Say: "Based on today, I think you'd give yourself a better chance with more time on Writing. It's your decision, and I'll help with either plan." The online course's rule of thumb (Lessons 1.1 and 4.3) is worth repeating: at higher levels a half band often takes around 12 weeks of focused study, and technique gains come faster than language gains.

Booking and retake guidance
  • When to book: when the weakest skill has reached its required band in at least two timed practice tests (this course's two, or one plus a free official practice test), or is within half a band with a clear plan and several weeks left.
  • Check the exact requirement in writing: the overall band and the minimum for each skill, how recent the result must be (results are usually valid for two years), and whether the institution will accept results combined from different sittings (many don't).
  • Already taken the real test and missed by half a band in one skill? There are usually three routes. Rules, fees and availability change, so learners must check the official IELTS website and their test center for current details: (1) a full retake after focused work on that one skill; (2) at some centers, a retake of the one skill only, within a limited period after a recent test; (3) a formal request for the paper to be re-marked, which carries a fee and a deadline and is generally worth considering only when the other skills are consistent and one is just below the cut-off.
  • Don't rebook straight away with the same preparation. The same preparation tends to produce a similar result. Change something first: the weakest criterion, the timing habit, or the amount of marked writing practice.
  • Look after the person as well as the score. Some learners carry a lot of pressure, such as a job offer, a visa timeline or family expectations. Make sure they know where to find support, and remind them that a band score describes one performance on one day, not their intelligence or their future.
Closing the course: the celebration

End Class 16 with the group, not the numbers. Ask each learner to name one thing they can do now that they couldn't do in Week 1 (writing an overview in two minutes, catching a correction, speaking for two minutes without stopping). Read out the Lesson 4.3 journal prompt and let them keep their answer: "The one change with the biggest payoff for my band is ___, and this week I will ___." Thank them for eight weeks of hard, often tiring work around jobs and families. Whatever their test date, they are now candidates who understand the test and train deliberately, and that is the part a course can give them.
