Scoring principles (score the IELTS way, and explain why)

Mark strictly, as the real test does, because a kind mark now becomes an unpleasant surprise on test day. One mark per question; no half marks. An answer earns the mark only if it is spelled correctly, is within the word limit, and has the right singular or plural form (Lesson 2.1). Words from the question that are already printed (a "$" sign, "square meters") are not needed. Numbers may be written as digits or words (25 or twenty-five). Capital letters don't matter. American and British spellings are both accepted (neighbor / neighbour, centers / centres): these papers use American English, the online lessons use British English, and the test accepts both. For "choose TWO" questions, each correct letter earns one mark, in either order. In the True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given items, the answer must use the right set of words: TRUE on a Yes/No task is marked wrong in the real test, so point this out even when you give the mark in class.

For self-marking in Class 9, tell learners: "If you're not sure whether your answer is acceptable, circle it and ask me. Don't give yourself the benefit of the doubt, and don't take it away either."

Writing sample · what to look for
  • Question type: opinion ("To what extent do you agree or disagree?", Lesson 3.2). A full answer states a position early and keeps it. "Partly agree" is fine if the writer says clearly which part and why.
  • Task Response cap: an essay that only describes university life, or only lists subjects, without a position, is unlikely to go above 5 on this criterion.
  • Useful ideas (any are fine): broader thinking, employability, discovering interests, social connection vs workload, cost, lower grades, loss of depth. Mark the development of ideas, not whether they match this list.
  • Length: it is a 20-minute sample, so don't penalize 180–250 words. Under about 150 words, note it: it suggests speed is an issue.
  • Estimate each criterion with the band criteria below; record evidence on the diagnostic note grid.
A model response (about band 7, 205 words)

It is sometimes argued that universities should require every student to complete at least one course outside their own discipline. Although this would add to already heavy workloads, I largely agree with the idea, because the benefits for students are considerable.

The main advantage is that a course in an unfamiliar field trains students to think in new ways. An engineering student who studies history, for example, has to weigh conflicting sources and build an argument from incomplete evidence, a skill that is rarely practiced in technical subjects but is valuable in any professional role. Such courses can also reveal unexpected interests, and some students discover a second career path this way.

Admittedly, critics point out that degree programs are already crowded, and that a compulsory extra course could lower students' grades in their main subject. This concern is reasonable, but it could be addressed by grading the outside course as pass or fail, or by letting students choose it from a broad list.

In conclusion, although the requirement needs careful design, I believe that a single course beyond one's specialism is a small investment that produces more flexible and better-informed graduates.

Paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors, in plain teacher language. For the official wording, see the descriptors published by the test owners.

Speaking is marked on four criteria of equal weight across the whole interview. Accent is not a criterion: learners are marked on how easy they are to understand and on their use of stress, rhythm and intonation (Lesson 4.2). Personal content is never marked: invented answers score the same as true ones.

Rounding
  • Overall band (the test's rule, Lesson 1.1): average the four skills, then round to the nearest half band. An average ending in .25 goes up to .5; one ending in .75 goes up to the next whole band. Example: 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.0 + 6.0 = 25.5 → 6.375 → 6.5.
  • Writing and Speaking criteria (this kit's convention): average the four criteria and round down to the nearest half band. It keeps teacher estimates on the cautious side; examiners are trained, and we are estimating.

  • Nothing that compares them with other learners

Returning Results Kindly

For many learners, an IELTS score feels tied to their future: a course, a job, a family plan. A number handed back carelessly can undo weeks of confidence. The aim of feedback here is to keep every learner working, with a clear next step.

Principles
  • Private, never public. No rankings, no "highest score," no reading bands aloud. Hand results back folded or in an envelope.
  • Process before number. Start with what went well and what caused the lost marks; give the band last.
  • Ranges, not verdicts. "About 6.0, and this test is only an indication." Never "You'll fail" or "You're not ready."
  • Name what is fixable. Many lost marks come from technique (distractors, word limits, spelling, time), which can change in weeks.
  • Be honest about time. Half a band in eight weeks is a common, good result. A bigger jump is possible but not guaranteed, and language growth takes longer than strategy.
Things you can say
Seven of your Listening mistakes were spelling or plurals. That's the easiest kind of mark to win back: it's a checking habit, not your English.
Your score went down from the diagnostic, and that's normal: this was a full test, with the hard sections. Let's compare like with like at Practice Test 2.
You need 7.0 and you're at about 6.0 today. That's a real gap, so let's plan it honestly: this course builds the technique, and you might want a few more weeks before you book.
You rated Reading "very hard," but you scored 29. Your English is ahead of your confidence.
When a result hits hard

Some learners will be upset, particularly if a visa, a job or a registration deadline depends on the score. Give them time and privacy; offer a short conversation after class rather than discussing it in front of others. Don't make promises about the real test, and don't give advice on immigration or admissions rules: point them to the institution or test center for official requirements, and keep the conversation on what this course can do in the weeks you have. If a learner mentions serious distress, follow your organization's support and referral procedures.
