Diagnostic — Where Are We Starting?

A short, low-stakes sample of all four IELTS skills, taken in the first class: one Listening section (10 questions), one Academic Reading passage (13 questions), a 20-minute Writing Task 2 sample and a 3-minute Speaking sample. It gives you and each learner a cautious starting band estimate, a clear picture of the gap to their target, and a first idea of which skill to prioritize. It is a map, not a verdict.

~60 min in class Listening /10 · Reading /13 Writing & Speaking: band estimate Target 6.0–7.5
How to frame it (say this before anything is handed out)

Many learners in this class have a great deal riding on IELTS: a university place, a professional license, a visa. For some, the first test of the course feels like the real thing. Lower the temperature straight away: "This is not a test you can pass or fail. It shows us where you are today, so we can spend the next eight weeks on the things that will move your score most. Everyone finds something hard today. That's the point." Tell them that the result is a rough estimate from a short sample, that it stays between you and them, and that nobody's band is announced to the class. Remind them that the real test does not penalize wrong answers, so they should answer every question.

Personal information is always optional

The Speaking questions are about study habits and a useful app or website, not about home country, family or immigration history. Even so, any learner may answer as a "new identity" (the card on the teacher pages) or invent details. Say: "You can invent anything. I'm listening to your English, not checking your life story." In the orientation pair work, learners share only what they want to share about why they need IELTS. Never ask follow-up questions about visas, legal status or family members who are absent.

Learners who read and write more slowly than they speak

At this level it is uncommon, but you may have a learner with dyslexia, a visual impairment, or a history of learning English mainly by ear. Use the same paper and change the conditions, not the content: enlarge the paper, give extra time for Reading and Writing, and note on the paper what you changed ("Adapted: +10 min Reading"). Treat the result as a separate kind of evidence rather than comparing it with others. Tell the learner, privately, that the official test offers special arrangements (for example, extra time or an enlarged paper) that must be requested from the test center well before the test date, and that you can help them find out how.

Pocket Forests

AAcross many cities, small patches of dense woodland, some no larger than a tennis court, are appearing on school grounds, beside highways and in the corners of parks, often on land that was previously covered with asphalt or hard, compacted soil. Known informally as "pocket forests," they are planted in a way that differs sharply from conventional tree planting. Instead of a few widely spaced saplings, volunteers plant three or four young trees and shrubs in every square meter, choosing only species that are native to the local area. The aim is to reproduce, within a few years, the layered structure of tall trees, smaller trees and shrubs that a natural forest might take a century to develop.

BThe method rests on an idea borrowed from natural regeneration: that crowding forces young trees to compete for light, pushing them to grow upward quickly. Supporters claim that pocket forests grow up to ten times faster than conventional plantings. Independent researchers are more cautious. A three-year study by the Northfield Urban Ecology Group found that the trees did grow faster in height during the first two years, but that the difference narrowed considerably by the third year, and that between a fifth and a quarter of the saplings died as the canopy closed above them. "Some loss is expected and even healthy," the group's lead author, Dr. Priya Raman, noted, "but it means the headline figures should be treated with care."

CWhere pocket forests appear to perform well is in attracting wildlife. Surveys at the eleven sites in the Northfield study recorded significantly more insect species than on nearby areas of mown grass of the same size, and nesting birds were found at several sites within two years of planting. Pollinators such as bees and hoverflies were especially common, and two sites attracted hedgehogs, which had not been recorded in those streets for many years. The dense planting also seems to reduce noise from nearby traffic, although the researchers point out that this effect was measured at only three of the sites and needs further investigation.

DThe social side of these projects may matter as much as the ecological one. Most pocket forests are planted in a single day by local residents, many of whom have never planted a tree before. Because the planting is so dense, a site the size of a tennis court may need several hundred young plants, so a single planting day can bring together dozens of people of all ages. Organizers report that many volunteers return to weed and water the site during the first two summers, when the young forest is most vulnerable to drought. In one neighborhood, a school turned its forest into an outdoor classroom, where students measure the growth of the trees and record the birds they see.

ECritics, however, warn against seeing pocket forests as a quick fix for urban environmental problems. A small plot of trees, they argue, cannot replace the benefits of large, connected green spaces, such as their ability to cool a whole district during a heat wave. There are also practical costs: the initial soil preparation is expensive, and the sites need regular care for at least three years. Dr. Raman believes the method is best understood as "one useful tool among many" rather than a solution in itself. Cities that plant pocket forests, she suggests, should also protect the older trees they already have, since a mature street tree provides far more shade than any newly planted sapling.

Listening Script · The Learning Commons

Read the whole script once, at a natural, unhurried pace, with normal contractions. Don't slow down or stress the answers; in the real test the answers arrive inside ordinary speech, often after a wrong detail (the distractor). Before you start, say: "You have 45 seconds to read Questions 1 to 10." The answers are in bold here so you can mark quickly; don't let your voice bold them.

You will hear a talk given to new students at a university's Learning Commons. You will hear the talk once only. [Start after the 45 seconds of reading time.]

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to the Learning Commons. My name's Dana Whitfield, and I coordinate student services here. I'll take about ten minutes to explain how things work, and then you're free to explore.

Let's start with opening hours, because that's what people ask about most. Until last year, the building closed at ten in the evening. Since September, though, we've stayed open until midnight from Monday to Friday. On weekends it's nine till six.

Now, the group study rooms on the first floor. These are very popular, so you need to book them online. We used to let you book a week in advance, but people were reserving rooms they never used, so now you can only book up to three days ahead. And each group can have a room for a maximum of two hours a day. If you need longer, you can join the waiting list at the desk, but I can't promise anything.

If you want help with your assignments, our writing tutors are available every afternoon. Some of you may have heard that the writing center is on the ground floor. That was true in the old building, but the tutors are now on the second floor, right next to the map collection.

You can also borrow a laptop for up to four hours. You don't need your passport or a letter from your department. Just bring your student card, and the desk staff will scan it.

[pause 20 seconds] Right. A few words about the silent zone, which is the whole of the top floor. Phones must be switched off completely up there, not just on silent. You're welcome to bring water, as long as it's in a bottle with a lid, but no food of any kind, and I should stress that this rule applies all year round, not only during exam periods.

People often ask me about printing. Yes, it costs ten cents a page, and you pay with your card. And some students forget to cancel room bookings, which is frustrating for everyone else. But honestly, the problem I see most often with new students is that they don't know how to find journal articles. They rely on general web searches and miss the best sources. So please come to our workshops.

Which brings me to the referencing workshop. Last year we ran it on Monday mornings, but hardly anyone came, so this year it's on Wednesday afternoons, at two o'clock.

Finally, the help desk by the main entrance. At the help desk you can borrow headphones and phone chargers, and you can book a one-to-one meeting with a subject librarian, who can show you the key databases in your field. Please note that replacement student cards are handled by Student Services in the main administration building, not by us. And there are no library fines anymore. We abolished them two years ago, so don't worry if you're a day late returning a book.

That's everything from me. Enjoy the building, and do come and say hello at the desk.

[Say:] You now have one minute to check your answers.

Speaking Sample · 3 minutes per learner

Sit beside the learner rather than opposite, if you can; it feels less like an interrogation. Keep your own talk to the questions below. Don't correct, don't teach, and don't react to mistakes; nod and move on. Time Part 2 with a phone. If a learner freezes in Part 2, one gentle prompt is fine ("Tell me more about how you use it"). Make brief notes on the grid below after the learner leaves, not while they speak.

Part 1 · about 1 minute
Thank you for coming over. I'm going to ask you a few questions about studying and learning. Real answers or invented ones are both fine.
  1. Do you prefer to study in the morning or in the evening? Why?
  2. Where do you usually study or read? What makes it a good place?
  3. Have you learned a new skill recently, outside school or work? (If "no": Is there a skill you'd like to learn?)

If a learner answers in five words or fewer, ask once: "Why?" Note it. Very short answers are the most common Part 1 habit, and they are fixed in Lesson 4.1.

Part 2 · about 2 minutes
Now I'm going to give you a topic. You have 30 seconds to think and make notes if you like, and then I'd like you to talk for about one minute. I'll tell you when to stop.

Give the cue card and a pencil. After 30 seconds: "Please start now." Stop the learner gently at about 1½ minutes: "Thank you." In the real test, preparation is 1 minute and the talk is up to 2 minutes; this is a shortened version.

Estimating a Starting Band

A diagnostic of about an hour cannot produce an official-style band. What it can do is place each learner in a range, show which skill is furthest from the target, and give you a baseline to compare with Practice Test 1 (Class 8). Be deliberately cautious: an estimate that is a little low leads to a pleasant surprise; one that is too high leads to a painful one on test day.

A full test gets harder in Listening Parts 3–4 and Reading Passage 3, where most candidates drop marks. These tables are the kit's own rough guide, not an official conversion. For full 40-question tests, use the conversion table in answer-keys.html.

  1. Listening and Reading: mark with the key and read the rough band from the tables above.
  2. Writing and Speaking: give a band for each of the four criteria on the note grid, using the paraphrased criteria in the key, and average them. For a 20-minute sample, don't penalize length heavily (180+ words is fine), but do note whether the learner stated a position.
  3. Overall: average the four skills and round it as the test does: an average ending in .25 goes up to the next half band, and one ending in .75 goes up to the next whole band (for example, 6.25 → 6.5; 6.75 → 7.0). Write it as a range: "about 6.0 (5.5–6.5)".
  4. Cross-check with the learner's own "How did it feel?" box. When a learner rates a skill "Very hard" but scores well, their confidence is the issue, not their English. Tell them.

Choosing the priority skill
  • First, any skill that is below a per-skill minimum. A strong overall score cannot rescue it (Lesson 1.1).
  • Then the skill with the largest gap.
  • When the gaps are similar, choose the one where technique can help fastest. Reading and Listening often move quickly with strategy (skimming and scanning, predicting, distractors); Writing and Speaking usually need more time and feedback.
Talking about the gap honestly and kindly
  • Be realistic: a gain of about half a band in eight weeks is common with steady work; a whole band or more is possible but not guaranteed, and language gains take longer than strategy gains.
  • If a learner's gap is more than about 1.0, don't say "you won't make it." Say: "Let's plan a realistic route. This course will build the technique; you may want to give yourself more weeks before booking the test." Then help them look at the date together.
  • If the estimate is already at or above the target, the plan is to secure it under timed conditions and protect the weakest skill.
