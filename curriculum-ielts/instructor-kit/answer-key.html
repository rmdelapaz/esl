Marking principle

These are exam-preparation answers, so mark the way IELTS marks closed items and the way a helpful teacher marks open ones. Closed items (Listening and Reading style: Exercise 5 in 1.1, Exercises 1 and 4 in 1.2, Exercise 3 in 1.3): a correct answer must be spelled correctly and must respect the word limit. British and American spellings are both accepted (center / centre), and so are numbers written as figures or words (5 p.m. / five p.m.). Open items (paraphrases, combined sentences, the upgrade): the models below are examples. Accept any version that keeps the meaning exactly (including quantities and hedges such as most, some, may), is grammatically accurate, and uses the target structure or technique. When a learner's version is correct but misses the target, accept it and point to the target: "Good. Can you also do it with a relative clause?" American English is the model.

C1 · Welcome pages (orientation)

Course contract, My target, band tracker

No answers: these belong to the learner. Check only that (1) the My target card has an overall band and a per-skill minimum (or "not sure yet": the C1 homework is to find out), (2) the test date has a week count, and (3) the band tracker is filled in privately after C2 (L and R) and C4 (W, S and error density). Expect decimal commas ("6,5") on the target card: accept them in C1, teach "six point five" in C2. If a learner's target is far above their diagnostic estimate and the test is soon, talk to them privately (see the Teacher's Guide, "High stakes, kindly").

Exercise 4 — Myth or fact?
  1. M. There is no pass or fail; you get a band for each skill and an overall band. Institutions set their own requirements.
  2. M. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a blank.
  3. F. A misspelled answer is marked wrong. British and American spellings are both accepted.
  4. M. Same content, same scoring. Choose the format you're more comfortable with and practice in it.
  5. F. Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1.
  6. F. An answer under the minimum length loses marks for task achievement / task response.
  7. M. Examiners assess how easy you are to understand, not a particular accent. A clear accent from any country is fine.
  8. M. Examiners are trained to notice rehearsed answers, and a memorized script doesn't show what you can do. Prepare ideas and phrases, not scripts.
  9. M. Vocabulary is rewarded for being precise and natural. A rare word used wrongly costs more than a plain word used well.
  10. M. Any clear, well-supported position can score well. The examiner marks how you argue, not what you believe.
  11. F. (.25 rounds up to .5; .75 rounds up to the next whole band.)
  12. F. A hyphenated word counts as one word in completion answers.

If learners ask about details that vary by test center or change over time (what you can bring, retake options, how results are sent), don't guess: point them to the official IELTS website or their test center.

Self-check
Q1
A question says "the factory increased its output." Which sentence is the paraphrased answer?
A) "The factory scaled back what it produced."
B) "The plant's production rose over the period."
C) "The manager increased his output of emails."
Why: plant = factory, production = output, rose = increased. A says the opposite; C is a trap that repeats increased and output with a different meaning.
Q2
Best academic upgrade of "Technology helps people communicate"?
A) Technology assists to communicate people.
B) Technology facilitates communication.
C) Technology aids the communicating of people.
Why: facilitate + noun is a natural collocation, and communicate → communication changes the word class. A and C are ungrammatical.
Q3 · new
"The number of visitors doubled." Which sentence matches?
A) "Visitor numbers rose considerably."
B) "Twice as many people visited as in the previous year."
C) "The visitors doubled their spending."
Why: twice as many = doubled. A is too general (a rise, not necessarily double); C shares the word doubled but is about spending, not numbers: a classic synonym trap.
Errors you will meet most (1.2)

One-word paraphrases · meaning drift with quantities and modals (some → all, should → must, may → will) · wrong member of the family ("the economy growth," "an analyze," "very benefit") · economic / economical · collocation errors with upgrades ("do a research," "a substantial adolescent," "detrimental for") · false friends (actual = current, eventual = possible, assist = attend) · choosing an answer because one word matches · stress on ANalysis, ecoNOmy. For range, praise accurate chunks: give rise to, a considerable proportion of, it is sometimes argued that.

Exercise 5 — The grammar upgrade (model; many versions are possible)

Model: "Since our neighborhood library opened a quiet study room last year, it has become far more popular with students, many of whom use the free internet there. Having seen visitor numbers rise, the library now wants to open the room on Sundays as well." Structures: subordinate clause (since) · comparative (far more popular) · non-defining relative (many of whom) · participle clause (having seen). Simpler model: "The library, which opened a quiet study room last year, now has more visitors because many students use the room, where they get free internet. If the room opened on Sundays too, even more people would visit." (Careful: that last sentence adds a prediction; accept it as a hypothetical, but point out that it is a new idea.)

Check with the tick-boxes: 4+ different structures, no new errors, same facts, no monster sentences. Watch for: "Although the room is quiet, but…" · "which students get free internet" (needs where or in which) · "Having opened the room, more people visit" (dangling participle: the people didn't open it) · "many of them use" after a comma (comma splice) instead of "many of whom."

Why: a subordinate clause with as, correctly formed. A is a comma splice; B has because of + a clause, a double connector (because… so) and less of things.

A) Although the course was short, but it was useful.
B) Although the course was short, it was useful.

Why: one clause, one connector: although already shows the contrast. A doubles the connector (very common for speakers of Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean and Japanese); C is a comma splice.

Marking principle

Mark Listening and Reading answers the way the real test does: one mark or zero, no half marks. An answer is correct only if it is (1) the right information, (2) spelled correctly (British or American spelling both accepted: color / colour, center / centre), (3) in the right form (plural if the answer is plural), and (4) within the word limit. Capital letters, lowercase, or a mix are all fine. Numbers may be written as figures or words, but figures are safer and shorter. Dates are accepted in any clear form (19 March, March 19, 19th March); an all-number date such as 19/3 is risky and best avoided in class. In Reading, words must be copied from the passage where the instructions say "from the passage." For True / False / Not Given, accept T / F / NG in class practice, but tell learners to check what the real answer sheet allows and to use the exact label the instructions give (never "Yes" on a True / False task). Be strict in marking and kind in talking about it: every lost mark is a code on the tally, and every code is fixable.

Timed task — Chart 2, bar chart: annotated band-7 model (161 words)

[Introduction: paraphrase what, who, when] The bar chart compares the average number of hours per week that first-year students at one university spent on five activities in 2014 and 2024.

[Overview: grouped, no figures, the most striking feature] Overall, time spent on paid work and online video lessons rose considerably over the decade, while the hours devoted to lectures, seminars and independent study fell. The most striking change is that paid work, which had been only the third most time-consuming activity, became the largest in 2024.

[Body A: the falling group, with figures] In 2014, lectures and seminars took up the most time, at 14 hours a week, followed by independent study at 10 hours. Both declined over the period, to 11 and 8 hours respectively.

[Body B: the rising group, comparisons] By contrast, the time students spent in paid work doubled from 6 to 12 hours, overtaking lectures and seminars. The rise in online video lessons was even more dramatic in relative terms: a fivefold increase from just one hour to five. Group projects, meanwhile, changed very little, rising only slightly from 3 to 4 hours.

Why this is about band 7 for Task Achievement

The overview is explicit, grouped (rising vs falling) and figure-free, and it names the most striking feature (paid work overtaking lectures). The body paragraphs follow the overview's grouping, not the order of the bars. Every figure is accurate. Comparison language is varied (followed by, respectively, by contrast, overtaking, even more dramatic in relative terms, meanwhile) and the trend language is precise (doubled, a fivefold increase, rising only slightly). No cause is given for any change. To reach band 8, a writer might note more precisely that paid work (12 hours) exceeded lectures (11) by only one hour, and integrate the small change in group projects into the overview's pattern.

Common problems in learners' versions: overview with numbers · going bar by bar, 2014 then 2024 · "because students need money / because of the internet" (invented causes) · "paid work increased to 6 hours" (confusing to and by) · "online video lessons increased 400%" (possible, but many learners compute it wrongly; fivefold is safer) · "In 2024 year" · under 150 words because the introduction was copied.

Timed task — P3 (sitting at work): annotated band-7 model (259 words)

[Introduction: paraphrase + thesis covering both parts] In many modern jobs, employees spend seven or eight hours a day in a chair, often in front of a screen. This lifestyle creates problems for both physical and mental health, but a combination of workplace changes and personal habits could reduce its effects considerably.

[Body 1: problems. Topic sentence → why → so what → second, linked problem] The most serious problem is the impact on the body. When people sit for long periods, they burn very little energy, and over time this can contribute to weight gain, back pain and poor circulation. These conditions are not only uncomfortable for the individual; they also lead to more sick days, which affects employers as well. Long hours at a desk can also harm concentration and mood, since workers rarely move, go outside or take a genuine break from their tasks.

[Body 2: solutions. Topic sentence → two measures (employer, individual) → example → so what] Fortunately, several practical measures could address these issues. Employers could provide adjustable desks that allow staff to stand for part of the day, and they could encourage short walking breaks by holding some meetings on foot. Such changes cost relatively little but make movement a normal part of work. At the same time, individuals can take responsibility for their own routines, for instance by setting a reminder to stand up every half hour or by cycling or walking at least part of the way to work. Small habits like these, repeated daily, add up to a meaningful difference.

[Conclusion: restates both parts, no new idea] In conclusion, a largely seated working day can damage both physical and mental well-being. However, if companies make it easier to move and workers build activity into their routines, these problems can be significantly reduced.

Why this is about band 7 or above

TR: both parts of the prompt are answered in proportion, and the thesis previews both. CC: one idea per body, clear topic sentences, and cohesion carried by referencing (This lifestyle, These conditions, these issues, Such changes, Small habits like these, these problems) rather than a stack of linkers. LR: precise collocations (burn energy, poor circulation, take a genuine break, a meaningful difference). GRA: a range of complex forms (when…, not only… but also, which affects…, by setting…) with no errors. Hedging (can contribute to, could reduce) avoids overclaiming.

3.3 · Coherence, Cohesion & the Band Criteria (C11)

Exercise 1 — Which criterion?

1 TR (no position) · 2 CC (mechanical over-linking) · 3 LR (repetition, no range) · 4 GRA (no range of structures) · 5 CC (no paragraphing) · 6 GRA (accuracy: articles) · 7 TR (a part of the task missing) · 8 LR (collocation and spelling)

Items 2 and 5 both hit CC: cohesion is about much more than "using linkers." Accept a learner's argument that 8 touches GRA (do a mistake is often discussed as grammar), but collocation and spelling belong to LR.

Exercise 2 — Fix the over-linked paragraph (model)

As more people shop online, the number of home deliveries is rising sharply. Seven signposts became two or three (As, therefore, such as), with one summary noun (This trend) and ideas combined. Watch for: versions that simply delete the linkers and leave seven disconnected sentences.

Week 1: (b) drill the weakest question type · Week 2: (d) get a Task 2 essay marked · Week 3: (a) full timed mock · Week 4: (c) light review only

Technique first, then feedback, then the full rehearsal, then taper and rest. Accept (d) in week 1 and (b) in week 2 if the learner argues that Writing is their weakest skill: the principle (build → apply → rehearse → taper) is what matters. (c) must be last and (a) must not be in week 4.

Exercise 4 — Diagnose the profile

1. Karim: average (7.0 + 7.0 + 5.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 6.5, so the overall is met, but Writing 5.5 is below the 6.0 minimum. No, he doesn't meet the requirement. Focus on Writing: it breaks the rule on its own, it's his weakest skill, and lifting it to 6.0 both fixes the rule and nudges the average up. He shouldn't put extra hours into Listening or Reading.

2. Lucía: average (7.0 + 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 6.75 → overall band 7.0 (.75 rounds up). Yes, she meets it, with every skill at 6.5 or above. The risk: there's no margin. If any one skill drops by half a band on the day, the average falls to 6.625 and the overall becomes 6.5; and Writing and Speaking are already at the minimum. She should lift Writing or Speaking to 7.0 for a safety margin while keeping Listening and Reading ticking over.

Rounding rule to teach: the overall is rounded to the nearest half band; an average ending in .25 rounds up to .5, and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band (6.25 → 6.5; 6.75 → 7.0; 6.125 → 6.0; 6.625 → 6.5).

Exercise 5 — My plan for the day (models)

A. Listening: read ahead in every pause, predict the answer type, never leave a blank, check spelling at the end. Reading: 20 minutes a passage, write on the answer sheet as I go, guess and move on. Writing: Task 1 in 20 minutes with an overview, then Task 2 for 40, keeping 3 minutes to check. Speaking: extend every answer, keywords in the prep minute, ask for repetition if needed.

B. If I miss a Listening answer, I'll move straight to the next question. · If a Reading question takes more than two minutes, I'll guess, mark it and come back later. · If my mind goes blank in Speaking, I'll say "Let me think for a second" and give an opinion and a reason. · If my hands are shaking, I'll breathe out slowly three times. · If I have 8 minutes left and no conclusion, I'll write a two-sentence conclusion immediately.

Check the form: If + present, I'll + verb. Wrong: "If I will miss…," "If I miss, I go to next."

Dialogue — the night before

If–then plans: "if you blank in Speaking? → Then I'll say…" and "if you miss an answer in Listening? → The recording moves on, so do I." Future plans: "I'm planning to arrive by 7:45"; "Just ten minutes on my toolkit notes" (a plan without a verb, fine in speech). Habits to copy: ID checked the night before, arriving early with a buffer, no cramming, a prepared filler, and a calm view of nerves.

Full speaking mock and countdown plan

No fixed answers. Mock: the examiner's two bands should come with a quote each. Countdown plan: check that it has real dates, puts the weakest skill or question type in the early weeks, includes at least one full timed mock about two weeks out, names a person for Writing/Speaking feedback, and tapers in the last week. A plan with "study hard every day" and no dates is not yet a plan.

Self-check
Q1
On test morning, which item is essential?
A) A favorite pen
B) A bottle of water
C) The ID you registered with
Why: without the same ID document you used to register, you can't take the test. Pens or pencils and water are usually provided or allowed, but they aren't essential.
Q2
Which technique appears in both Reading and Speaking?
A) Skimming for gist
B) Paraphrasing
C) Trend language
Why: in Reading you recognize paraphrases of the question in the passage; in Speaking (and Writing) you produce them to show range and to talk around a gap. Skimming is a Reading skill; trend language is for Writing Task 1.
Q3 · new
You believe your Writing score doesn't reflect your performance. What can you do?
A) Email the examiner.
B) Ask your test center for a remark (an "enquiry on results") within the allowed period.
C) Nothing.
Why: remarks go through the test center, within a limited period after the test. A fee is charged and refunded if the band score goes up. Candidates don't contact examiners.
Errors you will meet most (4.3)

"in the 14 of May" · "on 8:30" · "Tomorrow I study Reading" (for a plan) · "If I will miss…" · "I will have do two mocks" (done) · planning to study hard the night before · every row of the toolkit rated "solid" · countdown plans with no dates · confusing "overall" with "every skill" in score requirements · thinking the computer-delivered test is easier or that Speaking is done on a computer.

Practice Test 2 — Band Profile & Next Steps (C15–C16)

Guidance for the workbook page
  • Answers and bands: Listening and Reading answers and the raw score → band table are in assessment/practice-test-2-key.html. Speaking comes from your C16 speaking band slip; Writing follows after you mark the scripts.
  • Overall band: the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band (.25 → up to .5; .75 → up to the next whole band). Worked examples: 6.0 + 6.5 + 5.5 + 6.0 = 24 ÷ 4 = 6.0 · 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.0 + 6.5 = 26 ÷ 4 = 6.5 · 6.5 + 6.5 + 6.0 + 6.0 = 25 ÷ 4 = 6.25 → 6.5 · 7.0 + 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.5 = 27 ÷ 4 = 6.75 → 7.0. Until the Writing band arrives, learners can write an overall "estimate without Writing" or leave it blank.
  • "Where did my marks go?": no fixed answers. The useful finding is a starred row (an error code or question type that appears in both practice tests). T (ran out of time / left a blank) in Reading usually points to the 20-minutes-per-passage plan (2.2, 4.3); D (distractor) in Listening points to the 2.1 strategy (the speaker corrects themselves); F, K, Q and H point to 2.3; X (transfer error) to the test-day technique in 4.3. The full code-to-lesson table is in assessment/answer-keys.html.
  • Next-step plan: check that the "one change" is specific (not "improve my English"), that the three weekly actions are things the learner can actually schedule, and that a person is named for Writing/Speaking feedback.
  • Expectations: about half a band of improvement across eight weeks is common; Listening and Reading usually move first. A profile with no change is not a failure if the question-type analysis shows technique improving. Never compare learners' profiles.
