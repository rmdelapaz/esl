IELTS Academic Workbook · Module 1

The Test & Foundations

Four classes to give you a clear map and a strong start. Find out exactly how IELTS Academic works and how it is scored, set a realistic target, and build a study plan around it. Then build two foundations that help in every part of the test: paraphrasing (saying the same idea in different words) and grammar that shows range and control.

How this module works

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Write, Listen, Say, Read, Timed or With a partner. In Timed tasks, work to the clock and then mark your own answers honestly: that is how you learn where your marks go.

Your scores are private. You never have to share a score, your reasons for taking IELTS or personal information. When a task asks about you, use your real life, or a new identity card, or something you invent. All three are equally good.

IELTS Overview — Format, Bands & Strategy

Class C2
Read and say — Key vocabulary
  • a band score
  • the overall band
  • a sub-score
  • a per-skill minimum
  • a raw score (out of 40)
  • transfer time
  • a word limit
  • a cue card
  • an examiner
  • Academic / General Training
  • to round up / down
  • to prioritize

per-skill minimum = the lowest band you may have in any one skill (for example, "no band below 6.0") · transfer time = extra minutes to copy answers onto the answer sheet · to prioritize = to put first

Grammar box — The test map
Listeningabout 30 min · 4 recordings · 40 questionsYou hear each recording once.
Reading60 min · 3 long passages · 40 questionsNo extra transfer time: about 20 min per passage.
Writing60 min · Task 1: describe a graph, chart, table, map or process (150+ words) · Task 2: an essay (250+ words)Task 2 counts twice as much: about 20 + 40 min.
Speaking11–14 min · Part 1 interview · Part 2 talk from a cue card (1 min to prepare, up to 2 min to speak) · Part 3 discussionAlways with a real examiner.

The overall band = the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band.

Average ends in….0 / .5 → stays.125 → down (6.125 → 6.0).25 → up to .5 (6.25 → 6.5).375 → up (6.375 → 6.5).625 → down (6.625 → 6.5).75 → up to the next whole band (6.75 → 7.0)

Say scores like this: "six point five" or "six and a half." Not: "six comma five."

Three strategic principles: 1. Manage the clock: a hard question is worth one mark, the same as an easy one. 2. Never leave a blank: there is no penalty for a wrong answer. 3. Answer exactly what was asked: "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" means a three-word answer scores zero.

Grammar box — The paraphrase ladder

Base sentence: Young people pollute the air when they drive cars. Climb at least two rungs.

5 · General wordcars → private transport · apples and pears → fruit
4 · Structurewhen they drive → by driving · Although… → …; however, …
3 · Voicedrivers cause pollution → pollution is caused by drivers
2 · Word classpollute (verb) → pollution (noun) · decide → make a decision
1 · Synonymyoung people → adolescents · cars → vehicles

Result: Adolescents contribute to air pollution through their use of vehicles. (rungs 1, 2 and 4)

Two failures. Too little: one word swapped, same sentence. Too much: the meaning changes (some → all, may → will, affordable → cheap). Keep numbers, quantities and hedges exactly.

Word families. The sentence decides the form. Listen for the stress: it often moves.

VerbNounAdjectiveAdverb
ANalyzeaNALysisanaLYTicalanaLYTically
conTRIButecontriBUtion——
—eCONomyecoNOMic (about the economy) · ecoNOMical (cheap to run)ecoNOMically
reQUIREreQUIREmentreQUIRED—

Upgrade, with a partner word: get → acquire skills · big → a substantial increase · show → the graph illustrates · bad for → have a detrimental effect on · cause → give rise to · help → facilitate communication. Only use an academic word when you know its partners.

Read the passage

A In many dense cities, flat roofs are among the few spaces left unused. Over the past fifteen years, a growing number of building owners have covered them with soil and plants. Supporters argue that these "green roofs" can cool buildings, hold back rainwater and give insects a place to feed. Critics point out that the initial cost is high: a green roof can cost two to three times as much as a conventional one to install.

B The cooling effect is the best documented benefit. On a summer afternoon, a dark conventional roof can become far hotter than the air around it, while a planted roof usually stays close to air temperature, because water evaporating from leaves and soil carries heat away. The top floors of buildings with green roofs therefore tend to need less air conditioning, although the effect on lower floors is small.

C Rainwater is the second major benefit. A planted roof acts like a sponge, holding part of a storm's rain and releasing it slowly. In one three-year study of a city office block, the roof retained roughly half of the rain that fell on it over a year, though far less during the heaviest storms, when the soil was already full.

D The benefit for wildlife is harder to measure. Surveys have recorded bees and beetles on many roofs, but researchers caution that a roof planted with a single type of low-growing plant offers little food. Roofs with a mix of native flowering species attract a wider variety of insects. Whether green roofs can replace lost habitat at ground level remains an open question.

Read and say — Dialogue: Checking a paraphrase

Ana: Can you check my introduction? The prompt says, "Some people think that all university courses should be free."

Ben: Sure. What did you write?

Ana: "Everybody believes that university education must cost nothing."

Ben: Good changes of words, but the meaning has drifted. The prompt says "some people," not "everybody."

Ana: Oh, right. And "should" isn't "must," is it?

Ben: Exactly. "Must" is much stronger. How about "It is sometimes argued that higher education ought to be free of charge"?

Ana: That's better. "Some people think" becomes "it is sometimes argued," and "university courses" becomes "higher education."

Ben: Two rungs at least: a synonym and a change of structure. And nothing copied.

Underline the two drift errors in Ana's first version. Which rungs does Ben's version climb?

Range You Can Control — Grammar for a High Band

Class C4
Read and say — Key vocabulary
  • Grammatical Range & Accuracy
  • range
  • accuracy
  • a clause
  • a subordinate clause
  • a relative clause
  • a conditional
  • a participle clause
  • a noun clause
  • a comma splice
  • an uncountable noun
  • error density

clause = a group of words with a subject and a verb · comma splice = two sentences joined only by a comma · error density = how many of your sentences contain errors (the fewer, the better)

Grammar box — Range and accuracy

What the grammar criterion rewards (paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors):

BandRangeAccuracy
5mostly simple sentences; complex ones are rareerrors are frequent and can make reading difficult
6a mix of simple and complex sentencessome errors, but the meaning stays clear
7a variety of complex structuresmany sentences have no errors at all
8a wide range, used flexiblymost sentences are error-free; only occasional slips

From 5 to 6: add range. From 6 to 7: keep that range accurate.

The toolkit.

Subordinate clauseAlthough car use rose, bus use also increased. (Not: Although…, but…)
Relative clauseStudents who work part-time… (defining, no commas) · The bridge, which opened in 1932, … (non-defining, commas)
ConditionalsIf cities invest in cycling, congestion will fall. · If fees were lower, more people would enroll. (Not: If it will continue)
Participle clauseHaving doubled in a decade, the population then stabilized.
Noun clauseWhat is clear is that demand will keep growing. · The fact that… · One reason why… is that…
ComparisonSpending was far higher in 2020 than in any earlier year.

Tense by task. Task 1: read the dates first (2000–2015 → rose · 2012 to now → has risen · forecast → is expected to rise) · a process → present passive (is heated) · Task 2 → present simple for opinions, conditionals for possibilities.

The band-limiting seven: articles · subject–verb agreement (The number of students is) · prepositions (an increase in, responsible for, an effect on) · comma splices · uncountables (information, advice, research) · word form (affect = verb, effect = noun) · one structure on repeat.
