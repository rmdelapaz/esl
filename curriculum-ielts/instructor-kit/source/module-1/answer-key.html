Marking principle

These are exam-preparation answers, so mark the way IELTS marks closed items and the way a helpful teacher marks open ones. Closed items (Listening and Reading style: Exercise 5 in 1.1, Exercises 1 and 4 in 1.2, Exercise 3 in 1.3): a correct answer must be spelled correctly and must respect the word limit. British and American spellings are both accepted (center / centre), and so are numbers written as figures or words (5 p.m. / five p.m.). Open items (paraphrases, combined sentences, the upgrade): the models below are examples. Accept any version that keeps the meaning exactly (including quantities and hedges such as most, some, may), is grammatically accurate, and uses the target structure or technique. When a learner's version is correct but misses the target, accept it and point to the target: "Good. Can you also do it with a relative clause?" American English is the model.

C1 · Welcome pages (orientation)

Course contract, My target, band tracker

No answers: these belong to the learner. Check only that (1) the My target card has an overall band and a per-skill minimum (or "not sure yet": the C1 homework is to find out), (2) the test date has a week count, and (3) the band tracker is filled in privately after C2 (L and R) and C4 (W, S and error density). Expect decimal commas ("6,5") on the target card: accept them in C1, teach "six point five" in C2. If a learner's target is far above their diagnostic estimate and the test is soon, talk to them privately (see the Teacher's Guide, "High stakes, kindly").

Exercise 4 — Myth or fact?
  1. M. There is no pass or fail; you get a band for each skill and an overall band. Institutions set their own requirements.
  2. M. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a blank.
  3. F. A misspelled answer is marked wrong. British and American spellings are both accepted.
  4. M. Same content, same scoring. Choose the format you're more comfortable with and practice in it.
  5. F. Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1.
  6. F. An answer under the minimum length loses marks for task achievement / task response.
  7. M. Examiners assess how easy you are to understand, not a particular accent. A clear accent from any country is fine.
  8. M. Examiners are trained to notice rehearsed answers, and a memorized script doesn't show what you can do. Prepare ideas and phrases, not scripts.
  9. M. Vocabulary is rewarded for being precise and natural. A rare word used wrongly costs more than a plain word used well.
  10. M. Any clear, well-supported position can score well. The examiner marks how you argue, not what you believe.
  11. F. (.25 rounds up to .5; .75 rounds up to the next whole band.)
  12. F. A hyphenated word counts as one word in completion answers.

If learners ask about details that vary by test center or change over time (what you can bring, retake options, how results are sent), don't guess: point them to the official IELTS website or their test center.

Exercise 5 — The word-limit drill (timed)
  1. main library ("the main library" = 3 words = wrong)
  2. 5 p.m. (also 5 pm, 5:00, five p.m., 5 o'clock) ("at 5 p.m." is acceptable as a word + number; "9 p.m." = the Monday–Thursday time = wrong)
  3. information desk ("the information desk" = wrong)
  4. student card ("valid student card" repeats the given word and makes three = wrong)
  5. closed bottles (also bottles)

The point of the drill: nearly every item has a correct three-word version that scores zero. Common errors: adding the, copying a word already printed in the note ("valid"), libary, infomation.

Self-check
Q1
"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS." You write a correct three-word answer. Score?
A) Full marks
B) Half a mark
C) Zero
Why: the word limit is part of the instruction. There are no half marks; breaking the limit makes the answer wrong.
Q2
L 6.0, R 6.5, W 6.0, S 7.0. Overall?
A) 6.5
B) 6.0
C) 7.0
Why: 25.5 ÷ 4 = 6.375, which is nearer 6.5 than 6.0.
Q3 · new
Which statement about IELTS Writing is true?
A) Equal weight, 30 minutes each.
B) Task 2 carries more weight, so give it about 40 minutes.
C) Excellent grammar lets you write fewer words.
Why: Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1, so the usual split is about 20 + 40 minutes. Writing under the minimum length loses marks however good the grammar is.
Errors you will meet most (1.1)

"six comma five" / "6,5" · "I passed IELTS with 6" · "rise my score" (raise) · "the Listening have 40 questions" · thirteen / thirty mixed up in Script 1.1-B · rounding .75 down · forgetting the per-skill minimum · extra the in completion answers · plans with no numbers ("study more"). For range, praise precise test vocabulary used naturally: a per-skill minimum, my weakest skill, I'll prioritize Writing because…

Exercise 3 — Climb the ladder (models; wording may vary)
  1. The construction of more bike lanes led to a fall in the number of people driving to work. / After more bike lanes were built, fewer residents commuted by car.
  2. Children's screen time is a source of concern for many parents. / A considerable number of parents are concerned about the amount of time children spend online.
  3. Air quality was measured in twelve cities (by scientists).
  4. Fruit is less expensive at the market. / Fruit can be bought more cheaply at the market.
  5. a) The majority of students prefer to study in the evening. · b) Evening study is preferred by most students. · c) For most students, the evening is the best time to study. (Any version that says "most" or "the majority"; not "students," "many students" or "all students".)

Check each version for meaning drift: item 1 must keep both facts (more lanes, fewer drivers) and the link between them; item 2 must keep "many"; item 4 must keep "cheaper" (not "cheap"). Item 3: "Twelve cities' air was measured" is correct but awkward; accept it and offer the model.

Dialogue — Checking a paraphrase

Drift errors in Ana's first version: Everybody (the prompt says some people) and must (the prompt says should). Also "cost nothing" is fine in meaning. Ben's version climbs rung 1 (synonyms: university courses → higher education, free → free of charge, should → ought to) and rung 4 (structure: Some people think… → It is sometimes argued that…), and arguably rung 5 (university courses → higher education is a more general term).

Paraphrase Relay (models for the prompt cards)

Card 1 (cycle lanes): "It is sometimes argued that urban roads ought to devote more room to cyclists, even at the expense of drivers." Card 3 (online degrees): "There is some debate about whether a qualification earned entirely over the internet is worth as much as one from a traditional campus." Card 5 (museum fees): "Many people believe that there should be no admission charge for publicly funded galleries and exhibitions." Models for all eight cards are printed small on each card (classroom materials, set E). Accept any version that keeps the scope ("some," "all," "should") and avoids the three "don't repeat" words.

Self-check
Q1
A question says "the factory increased its output." Which sentence is the paraphrased answer?
A) "The factory scaled back what it produced."
B) "The plant's production rose over the period."
C) "The manager increased his output of emails."
Why: plant = factory, production = output, rose = increased. A says the opposite; C is a trap that repeats increased and output with a different meaning.
Q2
Best academic upgrade of "Technology helps people communicate"?
A) Technology assists to communicate people.
B) Technology facilitates communication.
C) Technology aids the communicating of people.
Why: facilitate + noun is a natural collocation, and communicate → communication changes the word class. A and C are ungrammatical.
Q3 · new
"The number of visitors doubled." Which sentence matches?
A) "Visitor numbers rose considerably."
B) "Twice as many people visited as in the previous year."
C) "The visitors doubled their spending."
Why: twice as many = doubled. A is too general (a rise, not necessarily double); C shares the word doubled but is about spending, not numbers: a classic synonym trap.
Errors you will meet most (1.2)

One-word paraphrases · meaning drift with quantities and modals (some → all, should → must, may → will) · wrong member of the family ("the economy growth," "an analyze," "very benefit") · economic / economical · collocation errors with upgrades ("do a research," "a substantial adolescent," "detrimental for") · false friends (actual = current, eventual = possible, assist = attend) · choosing an answer because one word matches · stress on ANalysis, ecoNOmy. For range, praise accurate chunks: give rise to, a considerable proportion of, it is sometimes argued that.

Exercise 2 — Combine into one sentence (models)
  1. Although public libraries are free, many people do not use them. / Many people do not use public libraries, although they are free.
  2. Employees who work from home often save time.
  3. The museum, which is in the city center, attracts two million visitors a year.
  4. If fees were lower, more students would enroll.
  5. Having doubled in a decade, car sales then fell sharply.

Wrong: "Although…, but…" (1) · "Some employees who work from home, they often save time" (2: extra subject) · "The museum which is in the city center attracts…" (3: this makes it defining, as if there were several museums; accept it only if the learner can explain that) · "If fees would be lower" / "If fees are lower, more students will enroll" (4: the second is correct grammar but a first conditional; accept it and ask for the second) · "Having doubled in a decade, the sales of cars fell then" or a dangling subject such as "Having doubled, the market saw a fall" (5).

Exercise 3 — Tense by task
  1. doubled (past simple: the period has ended)
  2. have risen (present perfect: up to now; accept have been rising)
  3. is expected to reach (a forecast; accept is projected / predicted to reach, will reach)
  4. is heated (present simple passive: a process)
  5. had … would eat (second conditional; accept had … would probably eat)

Exercise 5 — The grammar upgrade (model; many versions are possible)

Model: "Since our neighborhood library opened a quiet study room last year, it has become far more popular with students, many of whom use the free internet there. Having seen visitor numbers rise, the library now wants to open the room on Sundays as well." Structures: subordinate clause (since) · comparative (far more popular) · non-defining relative (many of whom) · participle clause (having seen). Simpler model: "The library, which opened a quiet study room last year, now has more visitors because many students use the room, where they get free internet. If the room opened on Sundays too, even more people would visit." (Careful: that last sentence adds a prediction; accept it as a hypothetical, but point out that it is a new idea.)

Check with the tick-boxes: 4+ different structures, no new errors, same facts, no monster sentences. Watch for: "Although the room is quiet, but…" · "which students get free internet" (needs where or in which) · "Having opened the room, more people visit" (dangling participle: the people didn't open it) · "many of them use" after a comma (comma splice) instead of "many of whom."

Dialogue — Range or accuracy first?

Second conditional: "If most of your sentences were error-free, your grammar band would probably go up." Relative clause: "Keep the ones (that) you can control" (a defining relative clause with the pronoun left out). Leo needs accuracy (3 of 9 sentences error-free; articles and "Although…, but"). Sara needs range (almost no errors, but one structure on repeat).

Part 3 frames and the error log

No fixed answers. For the frames task, accept any answer that uses both frames on the card accurately; the observer's "errors that blurred meaning" column should list only real breakdowns, not every slip. For the error log, check that each fix is written in the learner's own words and that the "correct sentence" really is correct: this is where learners most often copy the error again.

Self-check
Q1
Which sentence is fully correct?
A) The number of tourists is increasing, causing an increase in demand for information.
B) The number of tourists are increasing every year.
C) The number of tourists is increasing, causing an increase of informations.
Why: the number of… is (singular), an increase in, information (uncountable). B breaks agreement; C has the wrong preposition and a plural uncountable.
Q2
Which upgrade of "Prices rose. People bought less." is ranged and accurate?
A) Prices rose, people bought less.
B) Because of prices was rising, so people were buying less of things.
C) As prices rose, people bought less.
Why: a subordinate clause with as, correctly formed. A is a comma splice; B has because of + a clause, a double connector (because… so) and less of things.
Q3 · new
Which sentence is correct?
A) Although the course was short, but it was useful.
B) Although the course was short, it was useful.
C) The course was short, it was useful.
Why: one clause, one connector: although already shows the contrast. A doubles the connector (very common for speakers of Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean and Japanese); C is a comma splice.
Errors you will meet most (1.3)

"Although…, but…" and "Because…, so…" · missing or extra articles ("Government should build hospital," "The pollution is…") · "the number of X are" · "If it will…" · comma splices · "informations, advices, researches" · affect / effect · "an increase of" · past charts described in the present · dangling participle clauses · non-defining clauses with that ("The bridge, that opened in 1932, …"). For range, praise accurate, well-placed structures, and praise the learner who simplified a sentence they couldn't control.
