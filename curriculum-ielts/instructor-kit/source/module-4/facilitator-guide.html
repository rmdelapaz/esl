Module 4 — Speaking & Test Mastery

The last five classes of the course. Three workshop classes on the Speaking test and on test day, then the two-class Practice Test 2. First, the shape of the interview and the one habit that lifts most candidates: extending answers (answer + why + example), a controlled Part 2 long turn (one minute of keyword notes, two minutes of talk) and a Part 3 answer built like a small argument. Then the marking: the four Speaking criteria, the pronunciation features that move the band (chunking, stress, intonation, not accent) and lexical range used naturally, with learners estimating each other's bands. Last, full practice and a test-day plan: timing for every paper, what to bring, computer- and paper-delivered differences, nerves, results and remark options. C15 and C16 run Practice Test 2, close with an individual band debrief and next-step plan, and end with a celebration.

3 lessons + Practice Test 2 Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C12–C16 Weeks 6–8 90 min per class Target band 6.0–7.5

How to Use This Guide

  • One block = one 90-minute class. C12–C14 follow the course's workshop shape: warmer (review of the last class) → lead-in → strategy with Meaning / Form / Pronunciation and concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill → guided practice (workbook) → timed practice in trios or pairs → self- and peer-marking against paraphrased band criteria → targeted feedback with delayed error correction → homework. C15 and C16 are test classes with their own plans.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-speaking-mastery.html. Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C12. It holds the picture flashcards and word cards, the new identity cards, the Part 1 question cards, ten original Part 2 cue cards with linked Part 3 questions, the mock-examiner kit (examiner script, timekeeper card, observer sheet), the Speaking band card for peer estimation, the test-day cards, the C16 kit (interview schedule, stop-clock slips, speaking band slips, band profile card, certificates of completion), the Keep Talking! board game and the listening scripts.
  • Practice Test 2 is a separate document: assessment/practice-test-2.html (learner copy) and assessment/practice-test-2-key.html (answers, raw score → band table, paraphrased Writing and Speaking criteria). Read both before C15. The section timings printed there overrule any number in this guide.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home and copy the stress and intonation.
  • Speaking is the skill learners fear most and practice least. Every Speaking class spends at least a quarter of its time on timed, examiner-style practice in small groups: mock-examiner trios (C12), band-estimation trios (C13) and a full speaking mock in pairs (C14). Protect that time. If you're running late, shorten the controlled practice (set the rest for homework), never the timed practice.
  • Feedback in three columns. During timed practice, take notes in three columns: accuracy (errors that cost marks: I am agree, since two years, people is, informations, the most of people), range (well-chosen language to praise and spread: it's grown on me, I'm of two minds about it, a heavy workload, arguably) and strategy (test habits: the one-word answer, stopping at 50 seconds in Part 2, a memorized-sounding opening, a filler at the start of every answer). In the feedback stage, board 2–3 items from each column, without names.
  • Band estimates are estimates. When learners rate each other with the Speaking band card, they give a band per criterion with one piece of evidence ("Lexical 6: nice three times, but go-to place used well"). Never let a peer estimate become a verdict. Your own estimate is also only an estimate: examiners are trained and standardized, and we are not. Say so.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons and the classroom materials both use American English (memorize, practice (verb), analyze, favorite, traveling, apartment, mom, center). Some learners will use British forms (memorise, practise, analyse, favourite, travelling, flat, mum, centre). The test accepts either variety, consistently used. Accept British forms whenever a learner uses them, and never mark an accent as "wrong."
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Say more → say it better → deliver it on the day. In C12 learners stop giving the examiner too little: every answer grows (answer + why + example), the long turn fills two minutes, and Part 3 becomes a small argument. In C13 they learn how the examiner turns talk into a number, and they lift the two criteria candidates neglect most: pronunciation (chunking, stress, intonation) and lexical resource (precise, natural, sometimes idiomatic). In C14 they rehearse the whole test and plan the run-up to test day, including the parts no textbook practices: the check-in, the clock, the nerves, the results. C15–C16 turn that plan into evidence: a second full practice test, a band profile set beside Practice Test 1, and a personal next step.

The stakes are real: handle them kindly

Your learners need a band for a university place, a professional license or a visa. Some have already taken IELTS and missed their target; some are sitting it within weeks of C16. Speak honestly and gently about what an 8-week course can do. A gain of about half a band in 8 weeks is common; bigger jumps happen but are not guaranteed, and strategy gains (timing, question types, extending answers) come faster than language gains (range, accuracy). Never say "you'll fail" or "you're not ready." Say instead: "Here's where you are today, here's the gap, and here's the one change with the biggest payoff." Practice-test bands move up and down by about half a band from one day to the next for the same person; treat a single result as a snapshot, not a sentence. If a learner is clearly far from their target, talk to them privately about timing their test date, never in front of the group.

Trauma-informed: personal questions and official checks

Part 1 asks about home, family, work and hometown, and several cue cards invite personal memories. For some learners (refugees, people separated from family, people who have lost work or status) these questions are painful. Every personal task in this module offers three equally good options: your real life, a new identity card, or something invented. Say it every class: "In the test you can invent details. You're marked on your English, not on your life." None of our cue cards asks about a journey to a new country, a hard time, or a family member who is far away. Test day also brings ID checks, photographs and sometimes a fingerprint scan. For learners who have been through border or immigration interviews, that can feel very different from how it feels to others. In C14 describe check-in neutrally and in advance, so nothing is a surprise; never role-play an ID check, and let anyone skip that part of the discussion.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: why extend? The examiner can only mark the language you produce. A one-line answer gives no evidence of range, grammar or fluency, so it caps the score even when it's correct.

"Do you like cooking?" "Yes, I do." Is it correct? (Yes.) Is it enough? (No.) Why not? (There's nothing to mark: no vocabulary, no grammar range, no fluency.) Does a longer answer mean a speech? (No: 2–3 sentences in Part 1.) Does the examiner want the truth? (They want English. You can invent details.)

Meaning: Part 2 and the prep minute. The minute is for a plan, not a script.

You have one minute. Do you write sentences? (No: keywords.) How many clusters? (One per bullet, plus an ending.) The examiner says nothing after 1 minute 20 seconds. Do you stop? (No: keep going until they say "Thank you.") You run out of ideas at one minute. What do you add? (Another reason, an example, a comparison, a feeling.)

Meaning: Part 3. The questions become general and abstract (people, society, the future), not about you.

"Should schools teach cooking?" Is the examiner asking about your school? (No: schools in general.) Is "Yes, they should" enough? (No: give a reason and consider another view.) Must you have a strong opinion? (No: "It depends on…" is fine if you explain what it depends on.) Is there a right answer? (No: they mark the English and the reasoning, not your opinion.)

Pronunciation. An extended answer is spoken in chunks (thought groups), each with one main stress and a short pause between them: Yes, I really DO | especially on WEEKends | because it helps me switch OFF after work. Model it, then model the robot version (every word equal) and ask which is easier to follow. Fillers need a relaxed, falling tone (Well, that's an interesting question… ↘), not a panicked rise. Tomorrow's class goes deeper into chunking and stress: today just hear it.

Mock-examiner trios — how to run it
  • Roles. The examiner reads only from the examiner script and the cards: no chatting, no helping, a friendly neutral face. The timekeeper-observer runs the phone timer, says "Thank you" at 2 minutes in Part 2, and fills the observer sheet (ticks for extended answers, the Part 2 bullets covered, the Part 3 moves heard, one quote to praise, one to fix). The candidate speaks. Give dominant speakers the examiner or timekeeper job first: it asks them to listen.
  • One round (8 min): Part 1, examiner takes one Part 1 card and asks three questions (1½ min) → Part 2, examiner hands over a cue card and says "You have one minute to prepare" (1 min), then "Please start speaking now" (up to 2 min), then one rounding-off question (e.g. "Would you recommend it to others?") → Part 3, examiner asks the two Part 3 questions linked to that cue card (2 min) → feedback: observer gives one strength and one next step, both quoted (1½ min).
  • Call the parts for the whole room. Everyone moves on your timer ("Part 2, prep minute starts… now"), so no trio drifts. If a talk ends early, the timekeeper writes the time on the sheet: that's useful data, not a failure.
  • Cue cards. Deal each trio four different cue cards face down. The candidate never chooses: in the test you can't. Anyone may use a new identity card for Part 1, and invent every detail in Part 2.
  • You sit in with a different trio each round and take three-column notes. Don't interrupt a candidate. If an examiner starts helping, whisper "Just the script."
ICQs: How many roles? (Three: candidate, examiner, timekeeper.) Can the examiner help the candidate? (No: just the script.) In the prep minute, sentences or keywords? (Keywords.) Differentiation
Literacy support

Some learners speak much more confidently than they write, and the prep minute's note-taking can slow them down. Offer picture notes (a quick sketch per bullet is fine: the test only needs notes you can read) or the 4-box note frame from Workbook Ex 4 (what · who · how long · feeling). Give them the cue card read aloud as well as on paper. Fewer items: Ex 2 items 1–3, Ex 5 as a spoken task. Target: a Part 2 talk that covers all the bullets and passes 90 seconds, and one Part 1 answer with all three moves.

Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Band 7+ candidates speak at length without sounding prepared. Ask strong learners to (1) answer a Part 3 question with a concession that changes their position a little (I used to think…, but now I'd say…); (2) use two different speculative structures in one answer (I suspect… · It wouldn't surprise me if… · There's every chance that…); and (3) in Part 2, add a flashback or a comparison the bullets don't ask for, so the talk fills two minutes naturally. As examiners, they must ask one unscripted follow-up in Part 3 ("Why do you think that is?").

L1 watch-outs
  • "I am agree" / "I am not agree" (Spanish estoy de acuerdo, Portuguese estou de acordo, French je suis d'accord, Arabic and Farsi patterns): the most frequent Part 3 error. Board it every class: I agree · I don't agree · I'm not sure I agree.
  • Tense with since / for: "I live here since two years" (Spanish, French, German, Russian, Polish use a present tense). Part 1 is full of these: I've lived here for two years.
  • Dropped be and articles (Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese): "My hometown very beautiful," "I am student." In speech these errors are frequent but don't usually block meaning; target them in feedback, but praise the extension first.
  • He/she in stories about people (Tagalog siya, Chinese tā, Farsi u, Finnish, Turkish, Yoruba): long Part 2 stories about a teacher or friend expose it. Ask the observer to listen for it.
  • Very short "polite" answers: learners from cultures where talking at length to an authority figure is rude (many East and Southeast Asian classrooms, some rural or formal cultures) may give short answers out of respect, not lack of English. Name it: "In this test, talking more is the polite thing to do."
  • Memorized openings: learners from exam systems that reward memorization may arrive with a rehearsed "Let me introduce my hometown…" paragraph. Treat it kindly: keep the vocabulary, drop the script.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: Pronunciation is intelligibility. The criterion rewards being easy to understand and controlling features (stress, intonation, chunking, linking). It does not reward any particular accent.

Does the examiner want a British accent? (No.) An American one? (No.) What do they want? (To understand you easily, first time.) Can a strong accent get a high band? (Yes, if it's clear and the features are well controlled.) Should you fix every sound? (No: only the ones that cause confusion.)

Meaning: chunking and stress.

"What I love about it | is the sense of SPACE." Where's the pause? (After it.) Why there? (It's the end of one idea.) Which word is strongest? (space.) Why that one? (It's the new, important information.) · "I didn't say SHE took it." Did someone take it? (Yes.) Who? (Someone else, not her.)
"Do you live nearby?" Up or down at the end? (Up: yes/no question.) "Where do you live?" (Down: wh-question.) "I like reading, cooking and hiking." Where does the voice fall? (On hiking: the list is finished.)

Meaning: less common and idiomatic language. Higher bands use some less common words and idiomatic expressions naturally and accurately. Forced or wrongly used idioms pull the score down.

"It's not really my cup of tea." Is it about tea? (No.) Positive or negative? (Mildly negative: I don't enjoy it much.) Formal or informal? (Informal, but fine in the Speaking test.) Would you use it in a Writing Task 2 essay? (No.) · "I'm of two minds about it." Am I sure? (No: I can see both sides.) Good for Part 1 or Part 3? (Both, especially Part 3.) · A candidate says "It was raining cats and dogs, so I was over the moon to see the moon." Natural? (No: forced, and it doesn't fit.) What should they say? Band-estimation trios — how to run it
  • The band card (materials set E) has the four criteria, with short descriptions at about bands 5, 6, 7 and 8, paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors. Walk the class through one row before they use it: "On Lexical Resource, what's the difference between 6 and 7?" (At 7, some less common and idiomatic words are used well, and paraphrase is flexible.)
  • One round (7½ min): the examiner gives a cue card (1 min prep, up to 2 min talk) and asks one linked Part 3 question (1 min). The rater listens and writes quotes, not judgments, on the rating sheet: one per criterion. Then the rater gives a band per criterion with the quote as evidence, and one target ("Next time, stress the last item of your list" / "Swap good for something more precise") (2 min). The candidate re-does the first 30 seconds of the talk with the target (1 min). Rotate roles.
  • Whole bands only. Raters give a whole band per criterion (5, 6, 7 or 8). If they can't decide, they write "6/7" and the evidence for each. That's honest, not weak.
  • Calibration (last 1½ min): read aloud one answer you noted in a trio (anonymously, or a model from Script 4.2-A). Everyone rates it silently, then shows fingers for Lexical Resource. Discuss the spread: "Why a 5? Why a 7? What would move it up?" This minute makes the difference between useful peer rating and guesswork.
  • Kindness rule: the rater gives the bands and evidence to the candidate only, quietly, and starts with the strongest criterion. No bands are shared with the class.

At bands 7–8 the difference is often control: less common words used with precise collocation and register. L1 watch-outs
  • Syllable-timed rhythm (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Turkish, Tagalog, Hindi-Urdu, Cantonese, many West African languages): every syllable gets equal weight, so sentence stress and chunks disappear. Clapping the stressed syllable and "robot vs chunk" contrasts help more than explanation.
  • Extra vowels: Spanish and Portuguese speakers add e- before s + consonant (es-tudy, es-peak); Arabic and Farsi speakers insert vowels in clusters (pi-lease); Japanese speakers add final vowels (desk-u). Only fix these if they cause confusion.
  • Dropped final consonants (Vietnamese, Cantonese, Mandarin, Thai): walk / walked, like / likes merge, which also hides grammar and can cost Grammatical Range & Accuracy. Target the -ed and -s endings in the long turn.
  • Specific sounds that cause real confusion: p / b (Arabic: people / beeble), l / r (Japanese, Korean: collect / correct), v / w (Hindi-Urdu, German), th → s / t / f (many L1s). Work only on the pair that affects each learner.
  • Flat or falling intonation that sounds uninterested (German, Russian, Polish, some Chinese speakers when nervous): the examiner may hear boredom. Practice the rise-fall of enthusiasm: It was absolutely ↗ aMAzing ↘.
  • Cognate stress: Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian speakers carry their L1 stress into cognates (comforTAble, developMENT, ecoNOmy, phoTOgraph). The Ex 2 word list targets exactly these.
  • "I am very interesting in…" (-ed / -ing adjectives: Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Russian): frequent in Part 1 hobby answers.

Meaning: timing plans. The clock is part of the test. A plan decides in advance what to do when a question takes too long.

Computer-delivered and paper-delivered: the general picture. Describe differences in general terms and send learners to their test provider's official information for the details of their own booking. Content, question types, timing and difficulty are the same in both formats, and the Speaking test is face to face with an examiner in both. The practical differences: on computer, answers are typed or clicked straight in, Writing shows a word count, text can usually be highlighted, and Listening gives a short check time at the end rather than time to transfer answers; results usually arrive sooner. On paper, Listening and Reading answers go on an answer sheet (in Listening there's extra time at the end to transfer them; in Reading there isn't), Writing is handwritten so legibility matters and you estimate your own word count (count the words in three lines, average them, multiply). Practice in the format you've booked.

Is the computer test easier? (No: same content, same difficulty.) Is Speaking done with a computer? (No: with a person, in both formats.) On paper, how do you know your word count? (Estimate: words per line × lines.) Which format should you practice in? (The one you've booked.)

Results, remarks and retakes: factual. Results are released online first and then as a Test Report Form; computer-delivered results usually arrive faster than paper-based ones. Many universities and employers accept results for about two years, but each organization sets its own rules. If a candidate believes a score doesn't reflect their performance, they can ask their test center for a remark (officially an "enquiry on results") within a limited period after the test; they can usually choose which sections are re-marked, a fee is charged, and it's refunded if the band score goes up. Candidates can take the full test again whenever they like. In many locations there's also a one skill retake option for a single section. Check with the test center and with the receiving organization before relying on either. Don't quote fees, deadlines or turnaround times in class: they vary by country and change. Show learners where to find them.

Can you ask for a remark for any reason, any time? (No: within a limited period, through the test center.) Is it free? (No, but the fee is returned if your score goes up.) Does every university accept a one skill retake? (Not necessarily: check first.) Where do you find the exact rules and dates? (The test provider's official information and your test center.)

If the Listening runs longer than 26 minutes

The practice test's own timings come first. Don't shorten the Reading silently to make the class fit: five lost minutes can cost two or three answers and make the band profile misleading. Instead, ask the class at the end of C14 to arrive five minutes early so the settle-and-instructions stage happens before the official start. If you do have to shorten Reading, tell learners the new time before they start and write it on the score sheet, so the band isn't compared unfairly with Practice Test 1.

Running a fair test in a community classroom
  • Conditions matter more than the room. Quiet, spaced seats if possible, a visible clock, a sign on the door. If a learner arrives late, let them join the section in progress without catching up on missed Listening; note it on their paper so the result is read fairly.
  • Accommodations. If a learner will have special arrangements on the real test day (extra time, enlarged print, a separate room), give them the same arrangements here and note it. If you suspect a learner needs arrangements they haven't requested, mention privately after class that test providers offer them and must be asked well in advance.
  • Distress. If a learner becomes very upset, let them step out, and let them decide whether to continue or finish the section at home as untimed practice. Mark the paper "untimed" so it isn't read as a band. A practice test is never worth more than a person's wellbeing.
  • Marking. Learners swap-mark Listening and Reading with the key at the start of C16. If you have time between classes, you can mark them yourself (40 + 40 answers are quick with the key) and use those 10 minutes in C16 for a longer debrief. Either way, check spelling rules from the key: in Listening and Reading, a misspelled answer is wrong.

If you run long

Protect the Writing time (a full 60 minutes, plus every interviewee's make-up minutes) and the celebration. The next-step plan can be finished at home if it must, but the band profile debrief must happen in class: nobody should read their numbers alone for the first time.

How to run the interviews
  • Format (about 6 minutes): a short Part 1 (1½ min, three questions) → Part 2 (1 min prep + 1½–2 min talk, from the practice test's cue card; stop them politely at 2 minutes) → Part 3 (1½ min, two questions). You won't have time for the full 11–14 minutes. Tell learners so: the estimate is less certain than a full test.
  • How many fit? With 6 minutes per interview and a minute to fill in the slip, about eight interviews fit into the Writing hour. With a larger class: (1) a trained co-teacher or volunteer runs a second interview area with the same questions and band card; (2) two or three more learners are interviewed during the make-up-and-marking stage; (3) anyone left gets a 10-minute slot just before or after class, or you use their C14 full-mock bands (peer-estimated) plus their recorded Part 2 homework, clearly labeled as such. Never cut the Writing time to fit interviews in.
  • Order. Use a fixed order that doesn't signal anything about level (alphabetical by first name, or drawn at random in C15). Consider starting with a confident, relaxed learner so the room settles.
  • The stop-clock slip. The learner writes the time they leave and the time they return. At the end of the Writing hour they write for that many extra minutes during the make-up stage. Check the slips as you collect scripts.
  • Be the friendly examiner. Use the practice test's questions and a neutral, warm manner. Don't correct or teach during the interview. As soon as the learner leaves, fill in the speaking band slip: a band per criterion (whole or half), an overall estimate given as a range (for example, "about 6.0–6.5"), one strength with a quote, and one next step. Paraphrased criteria are in the practice-test key and on the band card.
  • Trauma-informed: use a new-identity name without comment if a learner gives one, and skip any Part 1 question a learner visibly doesn't want to answer ("Let's talk about something else: what do you do in your free time?").
ICQs (before Writing): How long for Writing? (60 minutes.) Which task is worth more? (Task 2.) When I call your name, what do you write on your slip? (The time I leave and the time I come back.) How to read a band profile (say this, kindly)
  • "This is a snapshot, not a sentence." The same person can score half a band higher or lower on a different day. Look at the trend across Diagnostic, Practice Test 1 and Practice Test 2, not one number.
  • "Half a band in eight weeks is a real gain." Many learners improve by about that much in a course of this length; some improve more, some hold steady while their technique improves and the band catches up later. Bigger jumps are possible but not guaranteed.
  • "Your overall band is the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band" (an average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band). Check your target: many universities and professional bodies also set a minimum for each skill. One low skill can matter more than a high average.
  • "Compare yourself with you." No one reads anyone else's profile. The only question is: what's your gap, and what's the one change with the biggest payoff?

Using the two practice tests diagnostically. Ask learners to look beyond the band. For Listening and Reading, list every wrong answer by question type (form completion, multiple choice, map or plan labeling, True/False/Not Given, matching headings…) and by error code (the Module 2 codes. Listening: D distractor · S spelling or plural · W word limit / wrong form · N number, letter or date misheard · L lost my place / froze · P missed the paraphrase · X transfer error. Reading: P · M word-match trap · S copied a word wrongly · W · O looked in the wrong place · T ran out of time / left a blank · F False vs Not Given · K own knowledge · Q missed a qualifier · H heading matched a detail · X). Compare with Practice Test 1: a question type that's wrong in both tests is the clearest target. For Writing and Speaking, compare criterion by criterion: a criterion that stays lowest across both tests is the "one change." The Workbook's Practice Test 2 page has a table for this.

Returning Writing bands. Mark the Writing scripts against the paraphrased Writing criteria in the practice-test key (Task Achievement / Task Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy; Task 2 counts for more than Task 1). Return each script with a band estimate given as a range, one strength, one next step, and one rewritten sentence of the learner's own as a model. If you promised email, send it within a week, while the next-step plan is still fresh.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Sit with these learners during marking and help with the raw score → band table (a ruler under the row helps). For the next-step plan, offer the circle-the-option version on the band profile card (three choices per box) and let them tell you their plan aloud while you or a peer write the key words. The celebration round can be spoken or shown (a thumbs-up card with a drawing is fine).

Stretch (toward band 7.5+)

Strong learners often look only at the lowest skill. Ask them to find the cheapest half band: which skill is closest to the next band boundary (raw score two or three marks short)? Ask them to set a plan that protects their strong skills (one maintenance session a week) while working on the weakest, and to write one sentence on what they'd do differently in the real test's first ten minutes.

Watch-outs
  • Comparing bands across the room: learners may ask "What did you get?" Keep profiles private; the band profile card folds in half.
  • A lower band than Practice Test 1: it happens, especially when a learner changed technique. Look at the question types together: often the new technique is working and one section went badly. Never say "you got worse."
  • "Should I cancel my test?" Don't answer in class. Talk privately, look at the gap and the test date together, and offer options (keep the date and focus on the weakest skill; move the date if the provider's rules allow). The decision is the learner's.
  • Food at the celebration: check allergies, dietary rules and fasting periods before anyone brings food. A celebration can be just words, certificates and good-luck cards.
