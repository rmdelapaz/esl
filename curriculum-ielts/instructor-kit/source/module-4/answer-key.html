Module 4 — Speaking & Test Mastery: Answers

Answers to every Module 4 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, model versions for the open speaking and planning tasks, guidance for the Practice Test 2 band profile page, and the errors you can expect to meet most. Practice Test 2 answers are in assessment/practice-test-2-key.html, not here.

Marking principle

Most Module 4 tasks are spoken or open, so the models below are examples, not the answer. For extension tasks, accept any answer that (1) actually answers the question first, (2) adds at least a reason and an example or detail, and (3) stays at Part 1 length (2–3 sentences) or Part 3 length (3–4 sentences). For vocabulary upgrades, accept any word that is precise and natural in context, not just "bigger." For pronunciation items, accept regional variation that keeps the stress pattern clear; never mark an accent as wrong. American English is the model; accept British spellings and words (memorise, practise, favourite, flat, centre, travelling) and British date forms (the fourteenth of May).

Exercise 2 — Grow the answer (models)
  1. "Yes, I really do. It's the easiest way to clear my head after a day at a desk. Most evenings, for instance, I walk to the river and back, which takes about forty minutes."
  2. "Definitely the evening. My brain seems to wake up after dinner, whereas in the morning I'm still half asleep. Last week, for example, I finished a whole practice Reading test at ten at night and got my best score."
  3. "Not really, unfortunately. There's a small square with a few benches, but it's mostly concrete. If I could change one thing about my neighborhood, I'd turn the empty parking lot into a proper park."
  4. "Pretty much every day, yes. I listen on the bus because it makes the commute go faster. At the moment I'm listening to a lot of old soul music that my uncle recommended."
  5. "Not much, to be honest. Rain makes the buses slow and crowded, so everything takes longer. Unlike some people, I don't find it cozy at all: I'd much rather have cold, sunny weather."

Check each answer for: a direct answer first, a reason, and an example or detail. Not yet: three sentences that all repeat the answer ("Yes. I like walking. Walking is good."), a Part 1 answer longer than about 30 seconds (a mini-speech), or a reason with no connection ("Yes, because."). Errors to expect: "since two years," "I am agree," "the most of people," "I like very much walking."

Any note-set is correct if it is keywords, not sentences, has one cluster per bullet, and includes a personal angle and an ending that can stretch the talk to two minutes. A learner who wrote full sentences should try again with a strict 60 seconds. Picture notes (sketches) are fine: the test only needs notes you can read.

Exercise 5 — Order the Part 3 answer

b → d → a → c → e (opinion → reason → other view → resolution → future)

Model for the follow-up question ("Should cities spend more money on parks?"): "I'd argue that they should, yes (opinion). Green spaces give people somewhere free to exercise and relax, which matters a lot in crowded neighborhoods (reason). That said, city budgets are limited, and some people would put housing or transportation first (other view). So perhaps the answer is smaller parks in more places rather than one expensive showpiece (resolution). In the long run, I imagine parks will be seen as part of a city's health system (future)." Accept any answer with an opinion, a reason and another view; the resolution and future moves are a band 7+ bonus.

Dialogue — the start of the test

Reason: "because you can see exactly what you've achieved at the end of the day." Contrast: "which sounds dull, but I find it surprisingly satisfying" (also accept "Mostly, yes… The only drawback is the noise"). Hypothetical: "If I could change one thing, I'd move that highway a mile away!" Asking for something to write with: the examiner provides paper and a pencil for Part 2 notes; asking for anything you need, or for a question to be repeated, is normal and doesn't lower the score.

Speaking — Mock-examiner trios

No fixed answers. A strong candidate: Part 1 answers of 2–3 sentences, a Part 2 talk that reaches or nearly reaches two minutes and covers every bullet, and Part 3 answers with at least an opinion, a reason and another view. Observers should quote, not judge ("You said 'That said, some people…': great"). A Part 2 time under 90 seconds is useful data for the next practice, not a failure.

Self-check
Q1
"Do you like reading?" Which response gives the best chance of a high fluency score?
A) "Yes, I do."
B) A long, rehearsed speech learned by heart.
C) "I love it, actually — it's how I switch off before bed. Lately I've been reading a lot of crime novels."
Why: C answers, gives a reason and adds a detail: extended but natural. A gives the examiner nothing to mark. B sounds memorized; examiners notice rehearsed speech and it doesn't fit the question, which hurts the score.
Q2
What's the best use of the one-minute prep time?
A) Write out the whole answer.
B) Jot keywords for each bullet plus a personal angle, and plan how to fill two minutes.
C) Sit quietly without writing.
Why: you can't write a full answer in 60 seconds, and reading one aloud would sound unnatural. Keywords give you a map; the personal angle gives you more to say. C wastes the only planning help the test gives you.
Q3 · new
Part 3: "Why do some people prefer to shop online?" Which answer is strongest?
A) "Because it's easy."
B) "I'd say convenience is the main reason… That said, some people still prefer stores… so I suspect both will survive."
C) "I shop online every week. Yesterday I bought some shoes."
Why: B gives an opinion, a reason with a detail, another view and a prediction: a small argument. A is a one-line reason. C answers a Part 1 question about the candidate, not the general Part 3 question about people.
Errors you will meet most (4.1)

"I am agree" · "I live here since two years" · "people is" · "the most of people" · "I like very much…" · "My hometown very beautiful" (missing be) · he/she mix-ups in Part 2 stories · Part 1 mini-speeches of a minute or more · Part 2 talks that stop at 50–70 seconds · Part 3 answers that give an opinion with no reason · the same filler at the start of every answer ("Well, it's a good question…") · memorized openings ("Let me introduce my hometown"). For range, praise That said,…, I suppose it's possible that…, Unlike…, I… and any natural hypothetical.

Higher version: the second one (about band 7); the first is about band 5. Reasons to accept: precise, less common words (bustling, vividly, recharged), collocations (catch up on the week, a wide range of spices), an idiomatic expression (my go-to place), a paraphrase instead of silence, varied sentences. The first version repeats nice, good, a lot of, things and uses only simple sentences. The paraphrase: "that thing — I don't know the exact word — where you argue a bit about the price and both sides end up happy." The missing word: haggling (accept bargaining).

Lost syllables in natural speech: comfortable (COMF-ta-ble), interesting (IN-tres-ting), vegetable (VEJ-ta-ble). Accept necessary said with three syllables as a fourth answer. For economic, the first vowel may be "ee" or "eh": both are standard. Wrong stress to expect: comFORtable, developMENT, ecoNOmy (for the noun), phoTOgraph, TECHnology, enviRONment. Note: necessary is NEcessary in American English; British speakers often say NE-cess-ry.

Exercise 3 — Chunks and arrows

A. (Suggested; accept other sensible chunking with one main stress per chunk.)

  1. What I really enJOY about my job / is the vaRIety / because NO two days / are ever the SAME.
  2. When I was YOUNGer / I HATed vegetables / but these DAYS / I actually grow my OWN.
  3. PERsonally / I think public transporTAtion / should be FREE / for STUdents and OLDer people.

B. 4 ↗ (yes/no question) · 5 ↘ (wh- question) · 6 bread ↗, milk ↗ and eggs ↘ (the list is finished) · 7 tea ↗ or coffee ↘ (a closed choice: one or the other). If the speaker means "any drink, for example tea or coffee?", a rise at the end is also possible; accept it if the learner can explain it.

Exercise 4 — Upgrade and collocate

A. Models; many good versions exist.

  1. My new job is genuinely rewarding, but the workload is pretty heavy. / …but I have a heavy workload.
  2. The park near my apartment is really pleasant, and there's a wide range of activities: tennis courts, a pond, even outdoor yoga.
  3. The movie was pretty disappointing, and the ending was bizarre / baffling / completely unexpected.
  4. Online learning has some real benefits, such as flexibility, but also a few drawbacks, like the lack of face-to-face contact.

Judge by precision, not size: scenic for a park is fine; scenic for a job is not. Not yet: "very very good," "extremely nice," or a "big word" in the wrong place ("The park is prestigious").

B. 5 balance · 6 workload · 7 attention · 8 knit · 9 daunting · 10 vividly · 11 make (British English also take a decision; accept it)

C. 12 N · 13 F (a game-changer is something that changes a whole situation, like a new technology; it doesn't describe a person's cooking) · 14 N · 15 F (two idioms forced together, and they contradict each other: over the moon = very happy, under the weather = slightly ill)

Exercise 5 — Paraphrase around a gap (models)
  1. receipt: "It's the little piece of paper the store gives you after you pay, which shows what you bought and how much it cost."
  2. commute: "It's the trip you make every day from your home to work or school and back again."
  3. landlord: "It's the person who owns the apartment or house you live in and who you pay rent to."
  4. microwave: "It's a kind of small oven in the kitchen that heats food really quickly, in a minute or two."

Any description a listener understands scores the point. The skill is to keep talking with a frame; perfection isn't the goal.

Dialogue — after the rating

Mina's evidence: Fluency: "You kept going for the whole two minutes and you used 'That said' really naturally." Lexical Resource: "You said 'nice' three times, but 'a heavy workload' was great." Pronunciation: "Clear, easy to follow… Your lists were a bit flat." (Grammatical Range & Accuracy isn't discussed: ask learners what she should have added.) She starts with the strongest criterion, gives a band with a quote each time, and ends with one target.

Speaking — Band-estimation trios

No fixed answers. A good rating sheet has a quote (not an opinion) for each criterion, a whole band or "6/7" for each, and one concrete target. Peer bands are estimates; differences of one band between raters are normal. What matters is that the evidence matches the band card.

Self-check
Q1
What does the Pronunciation criterion reward?
A) Being clearly understood, with good use of stress, intonation and connected speech
B) A British or American native accent
C) Speaking as fast as possible
Why: the criterion is about intelligibility and control of pronunciation features. Accent isn't marked, and speed isn't the goal: fast, unclear speech lowers the score.
Q2
You can't remember a word. The best move for Lexical Resource?
A) Paraphrase: describe the word so the examiner understands
B) Stop and stay silent
C) Use a memorized idiom, even if it doesn't fit
Why: paraphrase shows flexible vocabulary and keeps you fluent. Silence costs twice (Fluency and Lexical Resource). A forced idiom that doesn't fit shows poor control of vocabulary.
Q3 · new
Which sentence uses idiomatic language naturally?
A) "My boss is a piece of cake, so the meeting was over the moon."
B) "The questions were raining cats and dogs in my exam."
C) "I used to find jazz a bit chaotic, but it's really grown on me."
Why: it's grown on me (= I like it more now than at first) is used with the right meaning in a natural context. A and B force idioms into places where they don't make sense.
Errors you will meet most (4.2)

Stress: comFORtable, developMENT, ecoNOmy, PHOtography · flat lists with no final fall · every syllable equal (no chunks) · dropped -ed / -s endings in the long turn · "I am very interesting in…" (interested) · "an informations," "advices," "a good news" · forced idioms ("It's raining cats and dogs of problems") · very + extreme adjective ("very exhausted," "very fascinating") · "make a party / do a decision." For range, praise precise collocation (a heavy workload, strike a balance), a natural idiom used once, and a calm paraphrase frame.

4.3 · Full Practice & Test-Day Strategy (C14)

My toolkit

No right answers. Look for honesty: a learner who rates every row "solid" may need a private conversation about their Practice Test 1 results. The "not yet" rows should appear in the learner's countdown plan.

Accept "used to register," "booked with" for 1; "transparent" for 3; "storage area" for 4.

Exercise 2 — Spot the test-day error
  1. Poor time management. A hard question is worth one mark like any other: guess, mark it, move on, and come back only if there's time (about 20 minutes per passage).
  2. A memorized script. Examiners recognize rehearsed speech, and it doesn't fit the card. Use the prep minute for keywords and talk naturally to the bullets.
  3. Letting one miss cascade. The recording moves on. Let question 14 go, read ahead and re-anchor at the next question.
  4. Over-writing Task 1. Task 2 is worth more and needs about 40 minutes. Aim for about 20 minutes and 150–200 words on Task 1 (overview + key features), then move on.
  5. Silence. Ask: "Sorry, could you repeat that?" or "Could you put that another way?" In Part 3 the examiner can rephrase the question. Asking doesn't lower the score; silence does.
Exercise 3 — Order the countdown

Week 1: (b) drill the weakest question type · Week 2: (d) get a Task 2 essay marked · Week 3: (a) full timed mock · Week 4: (c) light review only

Technique first, then feedback, then the full rehearsal, then taper and rest. Accept (d) in week 1 and (b) in week 2 if the learner argues that Writing is their weakest skill: the principle (build → apply → rehearse → taper) is what matters. (c) must be last and (a) must not be in week 4.

Exercise 4 — Diagnose the profile

1. Karim: average (7.0 + 7.0 + 5.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 6.5, so the overall is met, but Writing 5.5 is below the 6.0 minimum. No, he doesn't meet the requirement. Focus on Writing: it breaks the rule on its own, it's his weakest skill, and lifting it to 6.0 both fixes the rule and nudges the average up. He shouldn't put extra hours into Listening or Reading.

2. Lucía: average (7.0 + 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 6.75 → overall band 7.0 (.75 rounds up). Yes, she meets it, with every skill at 6.5 or above. The risk: there's no margin. If any one skill drops by half a band on the day, the average falls to 6.625 and the overall becomes 6.5; and Writing and Speaking are already at the minimum. She should lift Writing or Speaking to 7.0 for a safety margin while keeping Listening and Reading ticking over.

Rounding rule to teach: the overall is rounded to the nearest half band; an average ending in .25 rounds up to .5, and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band (6.25 → 6.5; 6.75 → 7.0; 6.125 → 6.0; 6.625 → 6.5).

Exercise 5 — My plan for the day (models)

A. Listening: read ahead in every pause, predict the answer type, never leave a blank, check spelling at the end. Reading: 20 minutes a passage, write on the answer sheet as I go, guess and move on. Writing: Task 1 in 20 minutes with an overview, then Task 2 for 40, keeping 3 minutes to check. Speaking: extend every answer, keywords in the prep minute, ask for repetition if needed.

B. If I miss a Listening answer, I'll move straight to the next question. · If a Reading question takes more than two minutes, I'll guess, mark it and come back later. · If my mind goes blank in Speaking, I'll say "Let me think for a second" and give an opinion and a reason. · If my hands are shaking, I'll breathe out slowly three times. · If I have 8 minutes left and no conclusion, I'll write a two-sentence conclusion immediately.

Check the form: If + present, I'll + verb. Wrong: "If I will miss…," "If I miss, I go to next."

Dialogue — the night before

If–then plans: "if you blank in Speaking? → Then I'll say…" and "if you miss an answer in Listening? → The recording moves on, so do I." Future plans: "I'm planning to arrive by 7:45"; "Just ten minutes on my toolkit notes" (a plan without a verb, fine in speech). Habits to copy: ID checked the night before, arriving early with a buffer, no cramming, a prepared filler, and a calm view of nerves.

Full speaking mock and countdown plan

No fixed answers. Mock: the examiner's two bands should come with a quote each. Countdown plan: check that it has real dates, puts the weakest skill or question type in the early weeks, includes at least one full timed mock about two weeks out, names a person for Writing/Speaking feedback, and tapers in the last week. A plan with "study hard every day" and no dates is not yet a plan.

Self-check
Q1
On test morning, which item is essential?
A) A favorite pen
B) A bottle of water
C) The ID you registered with
Why: without the same ID document you used to register, you can't take the test. Pens or pencils and water are usually provided or allowed, but they aren't essential.
Q2
Which technique appears in both Reading and Speaking?
A) Skimming for gist
B) Paraphrasing
C) Trend language
Why: in Reading you recognize paraphrases of the question in the passage; in Speaking (and Writing) you produce them to show range and to talk around a gap. Skimming is a Reading skill; trend language is for Writing Task 1.
Q3 · new
You believe your Writing score doesn't reflect your performance. What can you do?
A) Email the examiner.
B) Ask your test center for a remark (an "enquiry on results") within the allowed period.
C) Nothing.
Why: remarks go through the test center, within a limited period after the test. A fee is charged and refunded if the band score goes up. Candidates don't contact examiners.
Errors you will meet most (4.3)

"in the 14 of May" · "on 8:30" · "Tomorrow I study Reading" (for a plan) · "If I will miss…" · "I will have do two mocks" (done) · planning to study hard the night before · every row of the toolkit rated "solid" · countdown plans with no dates · confusing "overall" with "every skill" in score requirements · thinking the computer-delivered test is easier or that Speaking is done on a computer.

Practice Test 2 — Band Profile & Next Steps (C15–C16)

Guidance for the workbook page
  • Answers and bands: Listening and Reading answers and the raw score → band table are in assessment/practice-test-2-key.html. Speaking comes from your C16 speaking band slip; Writing follows after you mark the scripts.
  • Overall band: the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band (.25 → up to .5; .75 → up to the next whole band). Worked examples: 6.0 + 6.5 + 5.5 + 6.0 = 24 ÷ 4 = 6.0 · 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.0 + 6.5 = 26 ÷ 4 = 6.5 · 6.5 + 6.5 + 6.0 + 6.0 = 25 ÷ 4 = 6.25 → 6.5 · 7.0 + 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.5 = 27 ÷ 4 = 6.75 → 7.0. Until the Writing band arrives, learners can write an overall "estimate without Writing" or leave it blank.
  • "Where did my marks go?": no fixed answers. The useful finding is a starred row (an error code or question type that appears in both practice tests). T (ran out of time / left a blank) in Reading usually points to the 20-minutes-per-passage plan (2.2, 4.3); D (distractor) in Listening points to the 2.1 strategy (the speaker corrects themselves); F, K, Q and H point to 2.3; X (transfer error) to the test-day technique in 4.3. The full code-to-lesson table is in assessment/answer-keys.html.
  • Next-step plan: check that the "one change" is specific (not "improve my English"), that the three weekly actions are things the learner can actually schedule, and that a person is named for Writing/Speaking feedback.
  • Expectations: about half a band of improvement across eight weeks is common; Listening and Reading usually move first. A profile with no change is not a failure if the question-type analysis shows technique improving. Never compare learners' profiles.
