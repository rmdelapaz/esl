IELTS Academic Preparation Workbook · Module 3

Academic Writing

Three lessons, three jobs. Task 1: report a chart clearly and objectively, with an overview the examiner can find in three seconds. Task 2: argue by answering exactly the question that was asked, with two ideas developed properly. Then judge: read writing the way an examiner does, criterion by criterion, starting with other people's scripts and ending with your own.

Lessons 3.1–3.3 Classes C9–C11 Target band 6.0–7.5 Name: ____________________
How this module works

Each exercise heading tells you what to do: Write, Listen, Say, Read, With a partner or Timed. Timed tasks are practice under exam conditions: it is normal to feel rushed, and it is normal not to finish the first time. What you learn from the self-marking afterward matters more than the result.

When a task asks for your own ideas or examples, you decide what to share. Use your real life, or a new identity card, or an example you invent. In IELTS, an example must be relevant and specific; it does not have to be true.

Read and say — New words and chunks
  • an overview — the big picture: the one or two main features, no figures
  • a key feature — a highest or lowest value, a big change, a crossover, a stable figure
  • to group — to put related data together instead of describing it item by item
  • to paraphrase — to say the same thing in different words
  • a trend — the general direction of change over time
  • a proportion / a share — a part of a whole (a third, 12%)
  • to overtake — to go from below something to above it
  • to level off / to plateau — to stop rising or falling and stay the same
  • to fluctuate — to go up and down with no clear direction
  • to peak at — to reach the highest point
  • respectively — in the same order as the items just mentioned
  • approximately / roughly — not exactly, but close

Read — Chart 1 and a model report

The task: The line graph shows the percentage of commuters who cycled to work in three cities between 2004 and 2024. Summarize the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. (All cities and figures are invented.)

(A) The line graph compares the proportion of commuters who cycled to work in three cities, Northport, Eastvale and Riverton, at four-year intervals between 2004 and 2024.

(B) Overall, cycling to work became far more common in Northport and Riverton, whereas it declined slightly in Eastvale. As a result, Eastvale, which had the highest rate at the start of the period, had the lowest by the end.

(C) In 2004, 14% of commuters in Eastvale cycled, compared with just 6% in Northport and 3% in Riverton. Northport's figure then rose steadily, drawing level with Eastvale's at 12% in 2012 and overtaking it soon afterward, before climbing steeply to reach 31% in 2024, the highest rate shown.

(D) Riverton followed a similar but later pattern. After remaining very low until 2012, its rate increased sharply, drawing level with Eastvale at 11% in 2016 and ending the period at 22%. Eastvale, by contrast, saw a gradual fall to 10% in 2020, after which the figure remained stable.

Read and say — Dialogue: checking the overview

Lina: Can you check my overview? "Overall, paid work increased from 6 to 12 hours, and lectures fell from 14 to 11."

Ravi: It's accurate, but it's full of numbers. That sounds like body paragraph detail.

Lina: So what should the overview say, if not the figures?

Ravi: The shape of the story. Which activities grew, which ones shrank, and anything surprising.

Writing Task 2 — Essay Structure & Argument

Class C10
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • a prompt — the question you must answer
  • instruction words — the words that tell you what to do (discuss both views, to what extent)
  • a position / a stance — what you think, stated clearly
  • a thesis — the sentence in the introduction that gives your answer to the whole question
  • a topic sentence — the first sentence of a body paragraph; it states the paragraph's one idea
  • to develop — to explain why, show the consequence and give an example
  • a verdict — a final judgment (which side is stronger)
  • to outweigh — to be more important than
  • relevant — directly connected to the question
  • a concession — admitting that the other side has a point
It is often argued that…While…, I believe…The most compelling reason is…This is because……, which means that…For example, …Admittedly, …In conclusion, …

Build a paragraph: one idea, developed.

Topic sentenceThe most serious problem is the effect on physical health.
Why?This is because the body burns very little energy when we sit.
So what?Over time, this can lead to back pain, which means more sick days.
ExampleA call-center worker who sits for eight hours, for example, may rarely walk more than a few hundred steps.

Register: no contractions, no slang, no "you" talking to the reader, no memorized openers ("In this modern era…"). Use can, may, tends to, many to avoid overclaiming.

Read — Exercise 5: Model essay A (P2, discussion)

(1) A large share of the food produced in many countries is never eaten, and there is disagreement about who should take the lead in reducing this waste. While educating households has clear value, I believe that the greater responsibility lies with supermarkets and restaurants, because their decisions shape what everyone else buys and throws away.

(2) Those who favor educating households argue that most waste happens at home. When families plan meals, store fresh produce correctly and learn to cook with leftovers, they buy less and discard less, which also saves them money. A short community cooking course, for example, can show parents how to turn vegetables that are past their best into soups or sauces, a habit that may last for years. Such skills also tend to spread, since children who grow up in these households often learn the same habits.

(3) However, individual effort can only go so far when businesses encourage over-buying. Supermarkets often sell fruit in large multipacks and run "buy one, get one free" offers, so shoppers take home more than they can use. Restaurants, meanwhile, frequently serve portions that are too large for one person. If these businesses were required to offer smaller packs, donate unsold food and measure their own waste, the effect would reach millions of customers at once, rather than depending on each family changing its habits. Where large stores have already been required to donate edible surplus food to charities, considerable amounts of waste have been prevented.

(4) In conclusion, teaching households to manage food better is worthwhile, but it treats the symptoms rather than the cause. I am convinced that making supermarkets and restaurants responsible would reduce waste more quickly and on a much larger scale.

Read and say — Dialogue: two ways to plan

Ana: I've got my plan for the question about adults going back to school. I'm going to write about why it's good.

Ben: Let me see the prompt. "Why is this happening? Is it a positive development?" That's two questions.

Ana: Right, but they're connected, aren't they?

Ben: They are, but if you only answer the second one, half the task is missing. I'd give one body paragraph to the reasons and one to whether it's positive.

Ana: OK. Reasons: jobs are changing, so people need new skills. And online courses make it easier.

Ben: Good. Can you pick one and push it? Why do jobs change? So what?

Ana: Because technology replaces some tasks, which means people in their forties may need a second career. For example, a bank clerk retraining as a nurse.

Ben: That's a developed point. And your answer to the second question?

Ana: Mostly positive, as long as courses are affordable. That's my thesis.

What did Ana forget at first? What does "push it" mean? Underline Ana's thesis.

Writing — Coherence, Cohesion & the Band Criteria

Class C11
Read and say — New words and chunks
  • a criterion (plural criteria) — one of the four things the examiner judges
  • coherence — ideas in a logical order; one idea per paragraph
  • cohesion — the links between sentences: linkers, reference, substitution
  • a linker / a cohesive device — a word that shows a relationship (however, as a result)
  • reference — pointing back (it, this measure, these pressures)
  • a summary noun — a noun that names a whole idea (trend, problem, approach)
  • mechanical — done by habit, without thinking about meaning
  • a band-limiter — a fault that caps your band (no overview, off topic, under length)
  • to estimate — to make a careful guess
The four criteria (paraphrased from the public IELTS band descriptors)

Each criterion counts 25% of the task band. This is a guide for estimating, in our own words, not the official wording. Task 1 uses Task Achievement (overview, key features, accurate data) instead of Task Response.

Repetitive: Governments could raise fuel taxes. Raising fuel taxes would reduce car use.
Referenced: Governments could raise fuel taxes. This measure would reduce car use.

The over-linking trap: a linker on every sentence sounds mechanical and is marked down. Link only a real relationship (a real contrast, a real result). A new paragraph and a summary noun often do the job better.
