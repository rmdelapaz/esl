Marking principle

Mark Listening and Reading answers the way the real test does: one mark or zero, no half marks. An answer is correct only if it is (1) the right information, (2) spelled correctly (British or American spelling both accepted: color / colour, center / centre), (3) in the right form (plural if the answer is plural), and (4) within the word limit. Capital letters, lowercase, or a mix are all fine. Numbers may be written as figures or words, but figures are safer and shorter. Dates are accepted in any clear form (19 March, March 19, 19th March); an all-number date such as 19/3 is risky and best avoided in class. In Reading, words must be copied from the passage where the instructions say "from the passage." For True / False / Not Given, accept T / F / NG in class practice, but tell learners to check what the real answer sheet allows and to use the exact label the instructions give (never "Yes" on a True / False task). Be strict in marking and kind in talking about it: every lost mark is a code on the tally, and every code is fixable.

Exercise 5 — Spot the correction
  1. 10:30 / half past ten (the correction "sorry")
  2. 25 / twenty-five ("Actually, make that…")
  3. Station Road ("Well, it was, until…")
  4. (the) main hall ("not the library as it says in the leaflet")
  5. eight weeks / 8 weeks: a trick. The course lasts eight weeks; most people finish in seven. The answer depends on the question.
  6. (a) notebook and (a) pen: "notebook and pen" fits three words; "a notebook and pen" is four words (over the limit). The laptop is the distractor.
Dialogue — a Part 1 call

Correction: "Just the morning. Oh, actually, could we make it the whole day?" → whole day. Distractor: "Eight thirty on weekdays, but on Saturdays we open at nine." → 9:00. Possible form questions: Surname (NGUYEN) · Phone (07700 900163) · Number of bikes (2) · Rental period (whole day / full day) · Price per bike ($35) · Saturday opening time (9:00 / 9 a.m.).

Information gap — Build the trap

The answers are on each caller's identity card (materials, Set B). A form is correct when every detail matches the card, including the final version after the correction. Common mismatches: the first date instead of the corrected one, -teen / -ty house numbers, a doubled letter missed ("double S"), a phone number written with one group missing.

Self-check
Q1
"The trip's on the fourth of June — oh, sorry, that clashes with the exam, so it's been moved to the eleventh." Date of the trip?
A) 11 June
B) 4 June
C) the exam date
Why: "sorry" signals a correction, and the final version wins. B is the distractor; C isn't asked for.
Q2
"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER." You hear "student identification cards."
A) student identification cards
B) identification cards
C) card
Why: A is three words (zero marks even though the meaning is right). C drops the plural and changes the answer. ID cards is also acceptable.
Q3 · new
"The talk is in the Garrick Building — that's G-A-double R-I-C-K." Gap: "__________ Building" (ONE WORD ONLY).
A) Garick
B) GARRICK
C) Garrick Building
Why: capitals are accepted, and the spelling follows the letters exactly ("double R"). A misses one R; C is two words and repeats a word already printed.
Errors you will meet most (2.1)

Writing the first answer heard instead of the correction (D) · -teen / -ty confusion (14 / 40, 15 / 50) · E and I, A and E, G and J swapped in spelled names · dropped plurals (battery for batteries) · repeating a printed word or unit ($70, spare batteries, Garrick Building) and breaking the limit · large numbers off by a factor of ten · dates as numbers only (3/5) · left and right reversed on the plan because the learner never fixed the starting point · freezing after one missed gap and leaving the next two blank (L) · blanks at the end (in the real test, fill every one). For range, praise learners who predict precisely ("a plural noun, probably equipment"), who write the paraphrase pair beside a Part 4 answer, and who build natural corrections in the pair task ("Oh, did I say Tuesday? I meant Wednesday").

Exercise 5 — My time budget (models)

a) Guess the three, mark them with a small dot to return to if time allows, and start Passage 2 now. b) Bank the time: start Passage 2 at once, and don't re-check Passage 1 now; Passage 3 is harder and needs it. c) Write a sensible guess in every blank (there's no penalty); for T/F/NG guess one label, for completion a word near the keyword. Plan: any plan that gives Passage 3 at least as long as Passage 1 is fine (for example 17 · 20 · 23 minutes, with the last 2 minutes for blanks).

Dialogue — how did you find it?

Paraphrase pairs: without permission ↔ "with users' consent" (and the opposite meaning makes A a trap) · all groups of people ↔ "older residents" (people the data may miss). The option that is the opposite of the text is A (and B, too: "more expensive" vs "cheaper").

Explain your route (materials, Set C)

Card A: 1 steps (B: "saves more than a few steps") · 2 B, once the main routes had become clear (D: "Only after the main desire lines had become clear were they paved") · 3 F ("people are not obstacles to good design but participants in it"). Card B: 1 younger people (E) · 2 B, a visible line attracts more walkers (C: "a visible line invites the next walker to follow it") · 3 A ("a free and remarkably honest source of data").

Self-check
Q1
You've just opened a new passage. What should you do first?
A) Read every sentence carefully from the top.
B) Start with the matching-information questions.
C) Skim the whole passage quickly for gist and structure.
Why: a 2–3 minute skim builds the map that makes every later scan faster. A is the biggest time-sink; B is the slowest question type, so it comes last.
Q2
"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS." The passage says "aging equipment"; you write "the aging equipment."
A) Full marks
B) Half a mark
C) Zero: three words break the limit
Why: the limit is part of the marking, and there are no half marks. (British spelling ageing would also be accepted.)
Q3 · new
26 minutes on Passage 1, three questions left. Best move?
A) Keep working until you are sure.
B) Guess the three, mark them, and move to Passage 2 now.
C) Skip Passage 2 and go to Passage 3.
Why: Passage 1 is already six minutes over budget, and every question is worth one mark. A risks losing many easy marks later; C throws away Passage 2, which is usually easier than Passage 3.
Errors you will meet most (2.2)

Reading the whole passage slowly before looking at the questions (T) · choosing the option that repeats the text's words (M: Q5 C is the classic) · scanning for the question's exact words and never finding them (P) · breaking the limit with an extra word (Q1: "a" is already printed, so the maintenance problem is three words and wrong) · singular for plural (aerial photograph) · writing answers in the booklet and not on the answer sheet · doing matching information first and running out of time · spending 25+ minutes on Passage 1 · blanks. For range, praise learners who can say "The question says X; the text says Y," who eliminate options with a reason, and who stick to their checkpoint times.

2.3 · True / False / Not Given & Matching — The Tricky Types (C7)

Exercise 1 — True, False or Not Given? Prove it. (Passage A)
#LabelSentenceEvidence (model)
1TRUE3"Children who move to a new country usually develop a more native-like accent than their parents, particularly if they arrive before the age of about ten." (arrive in a new country = "move to a new country")
2FALSE4Clash: "adults and teenagers often make faster progress than young children" vs "young children make faster progress."
3NOT GIVEN4The text talks about note-taking as one strategy, but not about which strategy is most effective. (Qualifier trap: "the most.")
4FALSE5Clash: "followed 400 adult learners for three years" vs "more than three years."
5NOT GIVEN5The text talks about the number of learners (400 adults), but not about their gender.
6TRUE6"practiced speaking with others at least twice a week were almost twice as likely to reach an intermediate level" (regularly = "at least twice a week"; other people = "others").
7FALSE7Clash: "Age … made surprisingly little difference to progress among learners between thirty and sixty" vs "the older ones made much slower progress."
8TRUE9"learners who chose to join conversation groups may already have been more confident or more motivated" (accepts = "points out"; from the start = "already").
9NOT GIVEN10The text says the study did not include learners over sixty, but says nothing about any plans for a future study.

Evidence sentences in the learners' own words are fine, as long as they quote or point to the right sentence and name the relationship (same meaning · clash · silent on).

Exercise 2 — What went wrong?
  1. K (used own knowledge) · correct label NOT GIVEN. "I've seen it myself" is not evidence from the text.
  2. F (confused False and Not Given) · correct label FALSE. The text doesn't need exact speeds: "made surprisingly little difference" already clashes with "much slower."
  3. Q (missed a qualifier) · correct label NOT GIVEN. Sentence 4 mentions note-taking, but "the most effective" is a ranking the text never makes. (Accept P / M if the learner explains it as a word-match: the word "note-taking" appears, but the idea doesn't.)

Exercise 5 — Qualifiers
  1. F · qualifier every (the text says "some cities").
  2. T · qualifier usually: the statement is weaker than the text ("several times longer"), so the text supports it.
  3. NG · qualifier never. The text says bats "may avoid lit routes," but nothing about whether they ever hunt near lights. An over-claim against a hedged text is not automatically False: look for a clash.
  4. NG · qualifier sharply. "Did not rise" could mean it stayed the same or fell; the text doesn't say it fell, let alone sharply.

Items 3 and 4 are the discussion items: they show that "too strong" can be False or Not Given, and the deciding question is still "Can I point to the clash?"

Dialogue — False or Not Given?

Evidence language: "Can you point to the words that clash?" · "It doesn't say anything about…" · "It just doesn't tell us." · "False needs a clash." · "The text says 'three years,' whereas the statement says…" · "The text talks about…, but not about…" The deciding sentence: "False needs a clash."

Self-check
Q1
"The museum opened in 1980." Statement: "The museum was the first of its kind in the country."
A) Not Given
B) False
C) True
Why: the text gives only a date. Nothing confirms "first of its kind," and nothing says it wasn't. No clash → Not Given.
Q2
A paragraph mainly about how solar panels are manufactured mentions once that they are expensive. Which heading is the trap?
A) "How solar panels are made"
B) "The high cost of solar panels"
C) "The production process of solar technology"
Why: B matches a single detail, not the main idea. A and C both describe manufacturing (in a real task only one of them would be offered).
Q3 · new
"Only a few species of frog can survive being frozen." Statement: "Most frogs can survive freezing."
A) True
B) False
C) Not Given
Why: "only a few" and "most" cannot both be true: you can point to the clash. The qualifier decides the answer.
Errors you will meet most (2.3)

False for Not Given ("It doesn't say that, so it's false") (F) · True or False from general knowledge (K) · missing qualifiers (all, only, the most, much slower, sharply) (Q) · "True" because the statement repeats the text's words even though the meaning differs (M) · headings matched to a detail or a word in the paragraph (H) · matching features by paragraph instead of by person · writing "Yes" on a True / False task, or "T" when the answer sheet requires a full word · in speaking: "The text don't say," "It is not say about," "Yes, it doesn't." For range, praise whereas, there's no mention of, is a paraphrase of, the word "only" makes it false, and learners who defend Not Given by naming what the text does talk about.
