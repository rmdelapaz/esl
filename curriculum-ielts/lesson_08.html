✍️ Lesson 3.2: Writing Task 2 — Essay Structure & Argument

Task 2 is the single most valuable thing you write on test day — it carries roughly two-thirds of your Writing score. The good news is that a high band here is largely a matter of doing the right things in the right order: read the question type correctly, plan for five minutes, and build a clear, well-supported essay of at least 250 words in 40 minutes. This lesson gives you that repeatable method.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the Task 2 question type and answer it fully — the core of the Task Response criterion
  • Plan an essay quickly — decode the task, choose a position, and outline in about five minutes
  • Structure a clear four/five-paragraph essay — intro with a thesis, body paragraphs with topic sentences, and a conclusion
  • Develop and support ideas with reasons, consequences, and specific examples

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~65 minutes

🎯 Task: Plan and write a full ~250-word Task 2 essay in 40 minutes on a given prompt, then self-mark it against a Task Response checklist.

Analyse the Question Type First

Before you plan a single idea, work out what the question is actually asking you to do. This is the most important 30 seconds of the whole task, because answering the wrong question type is the biggest Task Response failure there is — and it caps your band no matter how good your English is.

⚠️ The number-one Task Response mistake

Candidates see a familiar topic, write everything they know about it, and never notice the question said "discuss both views" or "to what extent." A beautifully written essay that answers a different question than the one asked is capped around Band 5 for Task Response. Read the prompt twice; underline the instruction words.

There are a handful of common Task 2 question types. Each demands a slightly different job — and a slightly different structure:

TypeTypical wordingWhat it demandsStructure it pushes
Opinion (agree/disagree)"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"State one clear position and defend it throughoutThesis (your stance) + 2 bodies supporting it
Discussion"Discuss both views and give your own opinion."Cover both sides fairly and give your viewBody 1 = view A, Body 2 = view B; your opinion clear in intro + conclusion
Problem–Solution (or Causes–Solutions)"What problems does this cause and how can they be solved?"Explain the problems/causes and propose solutionsBody 1 = problem(s)/cause(s), Body 2 = solution(s)
Advantages–Disadvantages"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"Cover both, and (if asked) weigh them into a verdictBody 1 = advantages, Body 2 = disadvantages; verdict in conclusion
Two-part / direct question"Why is this happening? What can be done?"Answer every part directlyOne body per question part

💡 Match the answer to the verb

The instruction verb is your instruction manual. "Agree or disagree" asks for your position. "Discuss both views" asks you to present both — writing only one side here is an automatic Task Response cap. "Do the advantages outweigh…" asks for a judgement, not just a list. Give the task exactly the kind of answer its wording requests.

Planning in Five Minutes

Five minutes of planning feels like a luxury when the clock is running — but it is the fastest route to a coherent, on-task essay. Unplanned essays wander, repeat themselves, and forget to answer part of the question. Spend the five minutes like this:

  1. Decode the task (1 min): identify the question type, underline the instruction words, and note the topic and any scope ("in developing countries," "young people").
  2. Decide your position & main points (2 min): choose your stance, then pick two strong ideas to build the two body paragraphs around. Two well-developed ideas beat four thin ones.
  3. Jot topic sentences + an example each (2 min): for each body paragraph, write a one-line topic sentence and note a specific example you can develop.

💡 A plan template you can reuse

Scribble this skeleton on your question paper before you write a word:

  • Type: ____ (opinion / discussion / problem–solution / adv–disadv / two-part)
  • My position / answer: ____
  • Body 1 idea → because → example: ____
  • Body 2 idea → because → example: ____
  • Conclusion = restate position, no new idea

🧠 Mindset

Task 2 is a trainable skill, not a verdict on how clever you are. You are not being marked on brilliant, original ideas — examiners reward a clear, relevant, well-organised answer to the exact question asked. That is a formula you can practise until it is automatic. Two candidates with the same English can land a band apart purely because one planned and answered the task and the other didn't. Train the method, and the band follows. 🌱

The Four/Five-Paragraph Structure

A reliable Task 2 essay has four or five paragraphs: an introduction, two (or three) body paragraphs, and a conclusion. This shape is not a gimmick — it is the clearest way to show the examiner a logical progression and to make sure you address every part of the prompt.

ParagraphJobContains
IntroductionFrame the topic and state your answerParaphrase of the prompt + a clear thesis (your position / what the essay will do)
Body 1Develop your first main ideaTopic sentence → explanation → a specific example / support
Body 2Develop your second main ideaTopic sentence → explanation → a specific example / support
ConclusionClose the argumentRestate the position / summarise the two ideas — no new information

Optionally you can add a third body paragraph if you have time and a strong third idea — but a tight two-body essay, fully developed, usually scores higher than a rushed three-body one. Whatever you choose, one rule is non-negotiable:

⚠️ Address ALL parts of the prompt

A two-part question ("Why is this happening? What can be done?") requires both parts. A discussion question requires both views plus your opinion. If the prompt has two jobs and you do one, Task Response is capped — the essay simply hasn't answered the question. When you finish your plan, check it against the prompt: is every part covered?

Developing Ideas (Band 6 → 7)

Here is the difference that separates a Band 6 from a Band 7: development. A weaker essay lists points; a stronger one extends them. To extend a point, do at least one of these: explain why it is true, give its consequences, or add a specific example.

❌ Thin point (lists, doesn't develop):

"Public transport is good for cities. It reduces traffic and pollution. Many people use it. Therefore governments should invest in it."

✅ Developed point (topic sentence → why → consequence → example):

"Investing in public transport is one of the most effective ways to ease urban congestion. [topic sentence] When reliable metro and bus networks exist, commuters have a genuine alternative to driving, so fewer private cars enter the city each morning. [why] This not only shortens journey times for everyone but also cuts the exhaust emissions that damage public health. [consequence] In Copenhagen, for instance, sustained investment in integrated transit and cycling has kept car use low despite a growing population, demonstrating that the effect is real and lasting. [specific example]"

Notice the developed version is one idea, thoroughly explored — not four ideas mentioned in passing. That depth, repeated across two body paragraphs, is what lifts Task Response and Coherence & Cohesion together.

💡 The "so what?" test

After each sentence in a body paragraph, silently ask "so what?" or "why?". If your point stops after one sentence, you've listed, not developed. Keep pushing — because… which means… for example… — until the idea is fully unpacked.

Full Annotated Model Essay

Here is a complete, band-7+ opinion essay of about 260 words, with each part labelled. Read it once for the argument, then again for the structure.

Sample prompt (Opinion type): Some people believe that schools should focus mainly on academic subjects such as mathematics and science, while others think that practical life skills are just as important. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[INTRODUCTION — paraphrase + thesis]
It is often argued that education should concentrate on traditional academic disciplines like mathematics and science, though others maintain that practical, everyday skills deserve equal attention. While academic knowledge remains essential, I firmly believe that schools should give practical life skills a comparable place in the curriculum. ← thesis states a clear position

[BODY 1 — topic sentence → explanation → example]
The most compelling reason to teach life skills is that they prepare young people for the realities of adult independence. ← topic sentence. Knowledge of algebra or chemistry, however valuable, does not help a school-leaver manage a budget, cook a healthy meal, or understand a rental contract. When these competencies are missing, young adults often struggle with basic decisions and rely heavily on others. In many countries, for example, schools now run compulsory financial-literacy classes precisely because so many graduates left education unable to handle debt or savings responsibly. ← specific example develops the point.

[BODY 2 — topic sentence → explanation → example]
Furthermore, practical skills and academic learning are not in competition; they reinforce one another. ← topic sentence. A student who applies mathematics to a real cooking or budgeting task understands the concept more deeply than one who only memorises formulas. This means that time spent on life skills can actually strengthen academic performance rather than dilute it. Schools that run project-based programmes, where teenagers plan events or build simple products, consistently report higher engagement and better retention of the underlying theory. ← consequence + example.

[CONCLUSION — restate position, no new ideas]
In conclusion, although academic subjects will always form the backbone of education, I strongly agree that practical life skills belong alongside them. Equipping students for real life is not a distraction from learning but an essential part of it. ← restates stance, adds no new argument.

✅ What makes this a band-7+ answer

  • Task Response: takes a clear position in the thesis and holds it consistently to the conclusion.
  • Coherence: one idea per paragraph, each opened by a topic sentence and closed with support.
  • Development: every point is extended with a why and a specific example — no listing.
  • Register: formal, impersonal-to-measured academic tone; no slang, no memorised filler.

Register & Relevance

Task 2 rewards a formal-ish academic register — the same tone we built in Lesson 1.3. Write full sentences, avoid contractions and slang, and use hedging where appropriate ("it is often argued," "this can lead to"). But register is worth nothing if the content drifts: stay on topic and answer the actual question from the first line to the last.

Useful functional language — but only when it fits your argument, never bolted on for show:

Stating your opinion

I firmly believe that… In my view,… It seems to me that… I am convinced that…

Introducing arguments

The main reason is that… One key advantage is… On the one hand… / On the other hand… Furthermore,… A further consideration is…

Giving examples

For instance,… A clear example of this is… This is illustrated by… Take … as an example.

Concluding

In conclusion,… To sum up,… Weighing both sides,… Overall, the evidence suggests that…

⚠️ What costs you Task Response marks

  • Memorised template phrases stuffed in wrongly: examiners recognise "In this globalised era of modern technology…" openers instantly, and they add nothing. Say something relevant instead.
  • Off-topic examples: a vivid example that doesn't actually support your point drags relevance down. The example must earn its place.
  • Not answering all parts: covering one view in a "discuss both views" prompt, or forgetting the "how can it be solved?" half, caps your score.
  • Under 250 words: a short essay is penalised for Task Response — aim for roughly 260–280 to be safely over the minimum.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These sentence frames map onto the parts of a Task 2 essay — introduction, body, and conclusion.

  • It is often argued that technology has made our lives more convenient than ever before.
  • While there are clear benefits to this approach, it also raises serious concerns.
  • This essay will discuss both sides of the argument before giving my own opinion.
  • One of the strongest arguments in favour of this view is its long-term impact.
  • In conclusion, although both positions have merit, I firmly believe the advantages outweigh the drawbacks.

Practice & Your Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Identify the question type

Objective: Name the type and the structure each prompt requires.

  1. "Many people work long hours and have little leisure time. Why is this the case, and what can be done about it?"
  2. "Some argue that governments should fund the arts; others believe money is better spent elsewhere. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."
  3. "Nowadays more people are choosing to live alone. Do the advantages of this trend outweigh the disadvantages?"
  4. "University education should be free for all students. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"
✅ Answer

1. Two-part / causes–solutions — one body on the causes, one on the solutions; answer both halves. · 2. Discussion — Body 1 = fund the arts, Body 2 = spend elsewhere; state your own opinion in the intro and conclusion. · 3. Advantages–disadvantages — cover both, then deliver a clear verdict on which outweighs which in the conclusion. · 4. Opinion (agree/disagree) — state one position in the thesis and defend it across both bodies.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Thesis + two topic sentences

Objective: Turn a prompt into a plan skeleton.

Prompt: "Some people think that children should start school at a very young age, while others believe they should begin later. Discuss both views and give your opinion." Write (a) a thesis, and (b) one topic sentence for each body paragraph.

✅ Answer (model — judge yours by the structure, not the exact wording)

(a) Thesis: "Although an early start can accelerate learning, I believe children benefit more from beginning formal schooling a little later, once they are developmentally ready."

(b) Topic sentence — Body 1 (early-start view): "Those who favour an early start point to the fact that young children absorb language and social routines with remarkable speed."

Topic sentence — Body 2 (later-start view + my leaning): "However, there is strong evidence that delaying formal instruction protects children's wellbeing and long-term motivation."

Note how the thesis signals a position, and each topic sentence flags which view its paragraph handles — that is what makes a discussion essay easy to follow.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Develop a thin point

Objective: Extend a listed point into a full body-paragraph point (add why + an example).

Thin point to develop: "Working from home is convenient. It saves time. People like it."

✅ Answer (model)

"The clearest benefit of remote work is that it removes the daily commute, giving employees back hours they can spend more productively. [topic sentence + why] Instead of sitting in traffic for two hours, a worker can begin the day rested and use that reclaimed time for focused tasks or family. [consequence] A software company that shifted to home working, for example, reported both higher output and lower staff turnover once employees no longer faced long journeys. [specific example]" — one idea, fully unpacked, rather than three fragments.

🎯 Your Task: Write a Full Task 2 Essay Under Timing

Use this prompt (Opinion type): "In many countries, the amount of time people spend using digital devices is rising sharply. Some believe this is a positive development, while others see it as harmful. To what extent do you think the benefits outweigh the drawbacks?"

  1. (5 min) Plan. Identify the type, decide your position, and jot two topic sentences with an example each, using the plan template.
  2. (30 min) Write a full essay of at least 250 words: intro with thesis, two developed body paragraphs, and a conclusion.
  3. (5 min) Check & self-mark. Count your words, then run the Task Response checklist below.

✅ Task Completion Checklist (Task Response focus)

  • ☐ I identified the question type correctly and answered that question
  • ☐ My introduction paraphrases the prompt and states a clear thesis/position
  • ☐ I addressed all parts of the prompt
  • ☐ Each body paragraph has a topic sentence and is developed (why + example), not just listed
  • ☐ My conclusion restates the position and adds no new ideas
  • ☐ The essay is 250+ words in a formal register, on topic throughout

Solo option: write it by hand under a 40-minute timer and self-mark against the checklist. Partner/tutor option: swap essays and mark each other's using only the checklist — can a reader instantly see your position and find a developed example in each body paragraph?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Read the prompt twice and name the type before you plan — it determines your whole structure.
  • Spend five minutes planning: two strong ideas, topic sentences, and an example each.
  • Develop, don't list: apply the "so what?" test until each point is fully unpacked.
  • Leave three minutes to check the word count and that every part of the prompt is answered.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Answering the wrong question type: the single biggest Task Response failure — write for the exact instruction given.
  • Too many thin ideas: four half-explained points score lower than two fully developed ones.
  • Memorised filler and off-topic examples: they add words but not marks, and can lower relevance.
  • No clear position: if the examiner can't tell what you think, Task Response suffers — put your stance in the thesis and the conclusion.
  • Running short: under 250 words is penalised; plan for roughly 260–280.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Identify the question type first — opinion, discussion, problem–solution, advantages–disadvantages, or two-part — and answer that question fully. This is the heart of Task Response.
  • Plan for five minutes: decode the task, choose a position, and outline two strong ideas with topic sentences and examples.
  • Use a clear four/five-paragraph structure: intro (paraphrase + thesis), body 1 and 2 (topic sentence → explain → example), conclusion (restate, no new ideas).
  • Develop, don't list — extending each point with a why, a consequence, and a specific example is what lifts band 6 to band 7.
  • Stay formal and on topic, address every part, and write 250+ words.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How many paragraphs and words should I aim for?

Four or five paragraphs — an introduction, two (occasionally three) body paragraphs, and a conclusion — and roughly 260–280 words. That keeps you comfortably over the 250-word minimum without padding, and gives you room to develop two ideas fully.

Do I have to pick a side, or can I sit on the fence?

You need a clear position. For an opinion prompt, you can agree, disagree, or take a balanced "partly agree" stance — but say so explicitly and defend it consistently. An essay where the examiner can't tell what you think is penalised on Task Response.

Do my examples have to be real facts?

They should be specific and plausible, not necessarily verifiable statistics. A concrete, relevant example ("a city that invested in transit," "a company that shifted to remote work") develops your point far better than a vague generalisation. Don't invent precise fake data — you're marked on argument, not accuracy of figures.
