🎧 Lesson 2.1: IELTS Listening — Strategies & Question Types

Listening is the section where marks are won or lost in seconds. The audio plays once, moves relentlessly forward, and hides answers behind casual corrections and clever paraphrase. But it's also the most trainable part of IELTS: with the right habits — predicting, reading ahead, following signposts, and catching distractors — you can hear the answer coming before it arrives. This lesson gives you those habits and a plan for every question type.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the four parts of IELTS Listening and what each one contains
  • Apply the core strategies — predicting answers, reading ahead, following signposts, and catching distractors
  • Handle every question type — completion, multiple choice, matching, and map/plan/diagram labelling
  • Avoid the spelling and word-limit penalties that turn right answers into zeros

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Build and run a short "predict-then-listen" practice set from a transcript, then self-mark it and note the strategy you used for each question type.

The Four Parts of Listening

IELTS Listening lasts about 30 minutes (plus transfer time on paper) and contains 40 questions across four recordings. The parts move from everyday to academic, and from easy to hard, in a deliberate ramp:

PartRegisterSpeakersTypical content
Part 1Everyday / social2 speakers (a conversation)A booking or enquiry — e.g. reserving a venue, joining a club. Often form-filling: names, numbers, dates.
Part 2Everyday / social1 speaker (a monologue)An informative talk — e.g. a guide describing a place, event, or facility.
Part 3Academic / educational2–4 speakers (a discussion)Students and a tutor discussing an assignment, project, or study problem.
Part 4Academic / educational1 speaker (a monologue)A university-style lecture on a single academic topic.

Each part has 10 questions. The difficulty rises as you go: Part 1 is a gentle warm-up with clear, concrete answers, while Part 4 is dense, fast, and rarely gives you a mid-recording pause to read ahead.

⚠️ You hear each recording ONCE — only once

Unlike some other English tests, IELTS plays every recording a single time. There is no replay. This one fact drives every strategy in this lesson: you can't rewind, so you must read ahead, predict, and stay with the flow. If you miss an answer, you let it go instantly and lock onto the next one — freezing over question 14 will cost you 15, 16, and 17 as well.

The Core Strategies

Four habits turn a passive listener into an examiner-savvy one. Practise them until they're automatic.

💡 1. Read ahead and predict

Before each part, the recording gives you time to read the questions. Use every second of it. Read the gaps and ask: what kind of answer fits here? A number? A name? A place? A plural noun? A time? Predicting the word class and type means that when the answer arrives, your ear is already primed to catch it.

💡 Predict the answer type from the gap

Look at: "The tour leaves from the main __________ at 9:30." Before you hear a word, you know the answer is probably a place / noun (entrance? car park? reception?). Now you're listening for a location, not for everything at once. Prediction shrinks the search.

💡 2. Follow the signposting language

Speakers announce their answers with signposts — small phrases that say "the important bit is coming." Train your ear to prick up at them: "Let's move on to…", "The main problem was…", "What you really need to remember is…", "Firstly… secondly…". When you hear a signpost, the answer is usually one breath away.

💡 3. Beware distractors — the answer is the correction

IELTS loves to give a plausible wrong answer first, then correct it. The correction is the real answer.

💡 The correction is the answer

"So the meeting's on Tuesday — oh, no, sorry, they moved it to Thursday." A rushed listener writes Tuesday and loses the mark. Listen for the reversal words — actually, sorry, no wait, in fact, on second thoughts, I mean — and always take the final version.

💡 4. Keep up — never freeze

The recording will not wait for you. If you miss an answer, let it go and catch the next one immediately. A single missed gap is one mark; freezing to agonise over it can cost you a whole run of questions. Mark it, move on, and — during transfer time on paper — come back with a sensible guess. Never leave a blank on the answer sheet.

🧠 Mindset

Listening feels like a test of your ears, but it's really a test of attention management — and that is a trainable skill, not a verdict. The best listeners aren't the ones who hear every word; they're the ones who know where to point their attention and when. Every practice recording sharpens that aim. You will miss answers while you learn — that's the training working, not proof you can't do it. Stay in the flow, and let each mistake teach you a signpost you'll catch next time. 🌱

Every Question Type

Listening reuses a fixed menu of question types. Each has a reliable strategy — learn the strategy once and it works on every test.

Question typeWhat it looks likeWinning strategy
Form / Note / Table / Flow-chart / Sentence completion Fill a gap with words from the audio (a form, a set of notes, a table, a summary sentence). Predict the word class (noun? number? adjective?) and count words — the word limit applies! Answers are usually exact words you hear.
Multiple choice Choose A/B/C (or several from a longer list). Read the options first so you know what to listen for. Watch for paraphrase (the audio won't use the option's exact words) and distractors (all options get mentioned; only one is confirmed).
Matching Match items to a list of options — e.g. match opinions to the people who hold them, or features to categories. Track who says what. Note each speaker's name and listen for the idea attached to them; options are often reused or ruled out.
Plan / Map / Diagram labelling Label locations on a map, rooms on a plan, or parts of a diagram. Follow the directions — left / right, opposite, next to, beyond, in the corner. Fix your starting point and orientation first, then move with the speaker.

💡 The golden rule for every type: read before you listen

Whatever the type, the time before the audio is your most valuable resource. Underline key words in the questions, predict answers, and decide what you're hunting for. Candidates who read ahead consistently outscore equally good listeners who don't.

Worked Samples

Here are two realistic samples — written for this lesson, not from a real exam — with mini transcripts and the strategy made visible.

🎧 Sample A — Note completion (with transcript)

The questions (as you'd see them):

Community Sports Centre — Membership Notes

Complete the notes. Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer.

  • Membership type wanted: (1) __________
  • Monthly fee: £ (2) __________
  • Free class included: (3) __________
  • Best day to visit: (4) __________

The transcript (what you hear once):

Clerk: Welcome! Are you after the standard membership or the premium one?

Caller: I think the premium — I want the pool access.

Clerk: Good choice. That's normally forty pounds a month, but there's an offer on at the moment, so it's just thirty-five pounds.

Caller: Great. And does it include any classes?

Clerk: Yes — you get one free class. Most people pick yoga, but the included one is actually pilates.

Caller: Perfect. When's it quietest?

Clerk: Weekends are packed. I'd come on a Wednesday — Mondays are busy too.

✅ Answers & why:

  • (1) premium — the caller states it directly; "standard" was the distractor.
  • (2) 35 (or "thirty-five") — the correction after "forty"; the offer price is the real fee. The £ is already printed, so don't repeat it.
  • (3) pilates — "most people pick yoga" is a distractor; the signpost "the included one is actually…" gives the answer.
  • (4) Wednesday — "weekends" and "Mondays" are ruled out; "I'd come on a…" signposts the answer.

Notice: three of the four gaps hid a distractor or correction. That is normal — expect it.

🗺️ Sample B — Map / plan labelling

The task: A guide describes a small park. Label the map — match each place (A–E) to a feature: café, playground, pond, toilets, information point.

The map (described): You enter at the south gate at the bottom. A straight main path runs north. A is just inside the gate on your left; B is on your right, opposite A; C is halfway up the path on the left; D is at the far end on the right; E is in the centre where the path forks.

The transcript (what you hear once):

Guide: As you come through the south gate, the information point is immediately on your left, so do grab a leaflet. Directly opposite it, on your right, you'll find the toilets. Head up the main path, and about halfway along on your left is the café — a lovely spot. Keep going to the very end, and on your right you'll see the children's playground. Finally, right in the middle where the path splits, there's the ornamental pond.

✅ Answers: A = information point · B = toilets · C = café · D = playground · E = pond.

Strategy shown: Before the audio, fix your orientation (you enter at the bottom, path goes up) and note each label's position in words ("A = inside gate, left"). Then just walk the route with the speaker, catching direction words — on your left, opposite, halfway along, at the very end, in the middle. The speaker moves in a logical order; follow it and don't jump ahead.

📣 Phrase bank — Listening signposts & correction cues

Answer-is-coming signposts:

Let's move on to… The main point is… The main problem was… What you need to remember is… Firstly… / Secondly… One thing to note is… The key difference is… I'd recommend…

Correction / distractor cues (take the FINAL version):

actually… sorry, I mean… no, wait… in fact… on second thoughts… or rather… let me correct that… it's not X, it's Y

Direction words (map/plan):

on your left / right opposite next to / beside halfway along at the far end in the corner where the path forks just past

Spelling, Numbers & Word Limits

In Listening, you often hear the right answer but write the wrong one. Two silent killers cause most of these lost marks.

⚠️ A misspelt answer is WRONG. Over the word limit is WRONG.

The marker doesn't award "nearly right." If the answer is accommodation and you write acommodation, you score zero. If the limit is "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" and you write three, you score zero — even with the meaning perfect. Classic traps:

  • Spelling: names and tricky words are often spelled out in the audio — listen for the letters and write them exactly.
  • Plurals: did you hear an -s? "Book" and "books" are different answers; missing the plural loses the mark.
  • Word count: re-read the instruction for every completion task — the limit can change between sections.

🔢 Hearing numbers, dates & spellings accurately

TrapHow to catch it
thirteen vs thirty (and -teen vs -ty)Listen to the stress: thirTEEN stresses the end; THIRty stresses the start. When unsure, the "-teen" numbers sound longer.
DatesFormats vary — "the third of May" = 3 May. Write a clear form (e.g. "3 May" or "May 3"); note whether the day or month comes first in the audio.
Spelled-out lettersWhen a name is spelled, watch confusable pairs: M/N, B/P/V, G/J, A/E/I. "Double L" means ll. Write letters as you hear them, then read back.
Phone numbers / codes"Oh" is usually zero; "double three" = 33. Group digits as spoken.
Currency & unitsIf "£" or "km" is already printed on the form, don't repeat it — just write the number, or you may exceed the word limit.

✅ Transfer-time habit (paper test)

On paper, you get about 10 minutes at the end to copy answers onto the answer sheet. Use it to check spelling and plurals, fill every blank with a guess, and confirm you've stayed within each word limit. On computer, your answers are recorded as you type — so proofread as you go.

Practice & Your Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Predict the answer type

Objective: Practise reading a gap and predicting the word class / type before any audio.

For each gapped question, say what kind of answer must fit (e.g. number, plural noun, place, name, time):

  1. "The library closes at __________ on weekdays."
  2. "Bring two forms of __________ to register."
  3. "The workshop is held in the __________ Building."
  4. "There are __________ parking spaces for visitors."
✅ Answer

1. A time (e.g. "6 pm"). · 2. A plural noun (e.g. "identification" / "documents" — listen for the -s). · 3. A name / adjective describing the building (e.g. "Science", "Hartley"). · 4. A number. Predicting the type narrows what your ear hunts for.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Spot the correction / distractor

Objective: Find the real answer when the speaker offers a wrong one first.

  1. "The deposit is fifty pounds — sorry, they've just put it up to sixty."
  2. "We'll meet in Room 12 — no, actually, Room 12 is booked, so it's Room 20."
  3. "You'll need a blue folder — well, any colour's fine really, but I'd bring a red one to match the course."
  4. "The talk starts at half past nine. Get there by nine fifteen to get a seat."
✅ Answer

1. £60 / sixty (correction after "sorry"). · 2. Room 20 ("no, actually" reverses it). · 3. red (the speaker's final recommendation; "blue" and "any colour" are distractors). · 4. Depends on the question! If asked when the talk starts → 9:30 / half past nine; if asked when to arrive → 9:15. Always match the answer to what was asked.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Map / plan labelling from a route

Objective: Follow directions to place labels.

A guide describes a museum floor. You enter at the bottom; a corridor runs straight ahead (north). Label rooms P, Q, R with: gift shop, gallery, cloakroom.

Transcript: "As you come in, the cloakroom is the first door on your right. Carry on down the corridor, and the main gallery is at the far end straight ahead. The gift shop is on your left, opposite the cloakroom."

Positions: P = first door on the right · Q = first door on the left · R = far end, straight ahead.

✅ Answer

P = cloakroom (first door, right) · Q = gift shop ("on your left, opposite the cloakroom") · R = gallery ("at the far end straight ahead"). Fixing orientation first — enter at bottom, corridor goes up — makes "right / left / far end" unambiguous.

🎯 Your Task: A predict-then-listen practice set

Simulate a real Listening item using a transcript, so you can practise the strategies without needing audio.

  1. (10 min) Find or write a short transcript (a booking call, a tour talk, or a study discussion — 150–250 words). Podcasts, videos with transcripts, or your own writing all work.
  2. (8 min) Turn it into a practice set: cover the transcript and write 5–6 questions of different types — a couple of note-completion gaps, one multiple choice, and one matching or map item. Deliberately build in one distractor (a wrong answer stated then corrected).
  3. (6 min) Now predict: for each gap, write the expected answer type (number? plural? place?) before you look back.
  4. (6 min) Reveal the transcript, "listen" by reading it once at a steady pace, and answer under the word limit you set.
  5. (5 min) Self-mark. Check spelling and plurals strictly. Beside each question, note the strategy that got (or would have got) the answer — predict, signpost, correction, or directions.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My set covers at least three different question types
  • ☐ I built in at least one distractor / correction
  • ☐ I predicted an answer type for every gap before answering
  • ☐ I self-marked strictly — spelling and plurals count
  • ☐ I labelled the strategy used for each question

Partner / tutor option: Swap practice sets with a study partner and mark each other's. Reading someone else's distractors trains your ear far faster than your own.

🎯 Quick Quiz

Question 1: You hear: "The trip's on the fourth of June — oh, sorry, that clashes with the exam, so it's been moved to the eleventh." The question asks the date of the trip. What do you write?

Question 2: The instruction says "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER." The answer you hear is "student identification cards." What should you write to be safe?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Read ahead relentlessly — use every pause to preview questions and predict answers.
  • Listen for the correction — when a speaker changes their mind, the second answer wins.
  • Check the word limit for every completion task — it can differ between sections.
  • Write while you listen, then use transfer time to fix spelling, plurals, and blanks.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Freezing on a missed answer: agonising over one gap loses you the next three. Let it go and re-join the flow.
  • Writing the distractor: catching the first-stated answer instead of the correction is the most common Listening error.
  • Spelling and plural slips: "accommodation", "-s" endings, and spelled-out names turn heard answers into lost marks.
  • Ignoring the word limit: a correct three-word answer under a two-word limit scores zero.
  • Not reading the options first in multiple choice — you then hunt blindly and miss the paraphrase.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Listening has four parts — everyday conversation, everyday monologue, academic discussion, academic lecture — rising in difficulty, with 40 questions and one play only.
  • The four core strategies: read ahead & predict, follow signposts, catch the correction (distractors), and keep up — never freeze.
  • Each question type has a fixed strategy: predict & count words for completion; read options & watch paraphrase for multiple choice; track who-says-what for matching; follow directions & orientation for maps.
  • Spelling, plurals, and word limits turn right answers into zeros — write exactly, and stay within the limit.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can I write answers in CAPITAL LETTERS to avoid spelling doubts?

Yes — many candidates write Listening and Reading answers in capitals, which sidesteps unclear handwriting and capitalisation worries. It's accepted and won't cost you marks. The spelling itself must still be correct, though.

American or British spelling — does it matter?

Both are accepted (e.g. "colour" or "color", "organise" or "organize"). Just be consistent and spell the word correctly in whichever variety you choose.

What if I genuinely can't hear whether it's singular or plural?

Use the grammar of the sentence as a clue: "There are three ___" needs a plural; "each ___" needs a singular. When still unsure, make your best judgement and move on — but train your ear for -s endings, as they're a frequent trap.
