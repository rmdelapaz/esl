🔤 Lesson 1.2: Academic Vocabulary & Paraphrasing

If IELTS had a single master skill, it would be this one. In Listening and Reading, the answer is almost never worded the way the question is — it hides behind synonyms and reworded phrasing, and spotting that is the test. In Writing and Speaking, saying the same idea in varied, precise, academic language is exactly what lifts your Lexical Resource band. Learn to paraphrase, and every section of the test gets easier at once.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Paraphrase a sentence by changing synonyms, word class, and structure
  • Build topic and academic vocabulary, including the idea behind the Academic Word List (AWL)
  • Recognise paraphrase in Listening and Reading, where answers hide behind reworded questions
  • Produce paraphrase in Writing and Speaking to lift your Lexical Resource

Why Paraphrasing Is the Master Skill

Paraphrasing means saying the same idea in different words. It sounds like a small trick, but it runs through every part of IELTS — which is why we teach it before anything else.

SectionWhere paraphrase decides your score
ListeningThe recording says the answer using different words from the question. If the question mentions "put off the meeting," the speaker may say "postpone it." You must hear the match.
ReadingThe passage rewords the question. A "True/False" statement or a heading is almost always a paraphrase of a sentence in the text — never a copy of it.
WritingYou must paraphrase the task prompt in your introduction (copying it earns no credit), and varied wording throughout lifts Lexical Resource.
SpeakingRepeating the examiner's words sounds mechanical; rephrasing them shows range and directly raises the Lexical Resource band.

💡 The one insight to carry all week

In Listening and Reading, stop hunting for the exact words of the question. Hunt for the meaning, wearing different clothes. The test-writers deliberately swap the words — that swap is the skill they are measuring. Once you expect it, half the difficulty disappears.

Five Paraphrase Techniques

Strong paraphrase isn't one swapped word — it's a combination of moves. Here are the five that matter, each with a worked example. Our base sentence: "Young people pollute the air when they drive cars."

#TechniqueExample
1Synonyms — swap words for words of similar meaning"young people" → adolescents / the youth; "cars" → vehicles / automobiles
2Change word class — turn a verb into a noun (nominalise) or vice versa"pollute the air" → air pollution; "decide" → make a decision
3Change voice — active ↔ passive"drivers cause pollution" → pollution is caused by drivers
4Change structure / word order — reorder clauses, change the connector"They pollute the air when they drive" → By driving cars, they contribute to air pollution
5Superordinate / general word — replace a specific term with a broader one"cars" → a form of transport; "roses and tulips" → certain flowers

Combine them and one plain sentence becomes many. From "Young people pollute the air when they drive cars" we can reach:

Original: Young people pollute the air when they drive cars.

Paraphrase A (synonym + nominalise): Adolescents contribute to air pollution through their use of vehicles.

Paraphrase B (passive + structure): Air pollution is partly caused by the driving habits of the youth.

Paraphrase C (superordinate + reorder): When younger generations rely on private transport, air quality suffers.

⚠️ A good paraphrase changes STRUCTURE + WORDS — but never the meaning

Two failures cap your band:

  • Too little change: swapping a single word ("big" → "large") while keeping the whole sentence is not a paraphrase — examiners see it, and it doesn't help. Change words and grammar.
  • Too much change: don't paraphrase so hard that you alter the meaning, or grab a "synonym" that doesn't fit. "Cheap" and "affordable" seem alike, but "affordable housing" ≠ "cheap housing" — and "do a mistake" is a wrong collocation for "make a mistake." Accuracy first; cleverness second.

Everyday vs Academic Register (and the AWL)

Higher-band vocabulary tends to be more precise, formal, and academic. There is even a well-known reference list — the Academic Word List (AWL) — of around 570 word families that appear again and again in university texts (words like significant, factor, concept, sufficient, consequently). You don't need to memorise all of it, but the idea is useful: learn to upgrade everyday words to their academic equivalents.

⚠️ Don't overuse rare words wrongly

A single misused "big word" hurts more than a well-chosen plain one. Examiners reward vocabulary that is accurate and natural in collocation, not showy. "A substantial amount of pollution" is strong; "a substantial adolescent" is nonsense. Accuracy beats showing off — only use an academic word once you know how it collocates.

Topic Vocabulary Banks

IELTS recycles a predictable set of topics: environment, education, technology, health, work, urbanisation, crime, globalisation, media. Because they repeat, you can prepare. The trick is to collect collocations (word partnerships), not single words — examiners reward vocabulary used the way native speakers actually chunk it.

💡 Build banks, don't hoard words

For each topic, keep a running page in your journal with three columns: the collocation, a paraphrase of it, and one sentence you wrote using it. A phrase you've used is a phrase you'll recall under pressure. Aim for quality chunks over long lists of isolated words.

Recognising Paraphrase Under Pressure

This is where paraphrase wins you Listening and Reading marks. The answer is in the text or recording — but reworded. Train your eye and ear to map the disguise.

Reading — question: "The museum's income fell after the entry fee was removed."  (True / False / Not Given?)

Sentence in the passage: "Once admission became free, the gallery saw its revenue decline."

The mapping:

  • "income" ↔ revenue (synonym)
  • "fell" ↔ decline (synonym, verb → noun)
  • "the entry fee was removed" ↔ admission became free (structure + meaning restated)
  • "museum" ↔ gallery (near-synonym / superordinate)

Answer: True. Every element matches — through paraphrase, not matching words. A candidate scanning for "income" or "museum" would miss it entirely.

💡 Listening works the same way

If the question sheet reads "the class was cancelled," expect to hear "the session won't be going ahead" or "we've had to call it off." Underline the key words in each question before the recording starts, then listen for their meanings, not their exact sounds.

The Writing intro move: paraphrase the prompt

Task 2 (and Task 1) begin by restating the prompt in your own words. Copying it word-for-word earns no credit and signals a weak Lexical Resource. Here's the difference between a weak and a strong opening.

Prompt: "Some people believe that children should start learning a foreign language at primary school rather than secondary school. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

❌ Weak (near-copy): "Some people believe that children should start learning a foreign language at primary school rather than secondary school. I will discuss this." — This copies the prompt almost verbatim; it demonstrates no language of your own.

✅ Strong (paraphrased): "It is sometimes argued that young learners ought to be introduced to a second language during their early years of schooling instead of waiting until adolescence. This essay will argue that an early start brings clear benefits." — Synonyms (children → young learners; foreign → second; primary school → early years of schooling), a word-class shift (start learning → be introduced), and a restructured sentence. Same meaning, all your own words.

Practice & Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match the question to its paraphrased answer

Objective: Train paraphrase-recognition for Reading/Listening. Match each question (1–3) to the text sentence (A–C) that answers it.

Questions:

  1. The new policy reduced traffic in the city centre.
  2. Most staff welcomed the change.
  3. The scheme was expensive to run.

Text sentences:

  • A. "The initiative proved costly to maintain."
  • B. "After the measure was introduced, the number of vehicles downtown dropped sharply."
  • C. "The majority of employees responded to the reform with enthusiasm."
✅ Answer

1 → B ("reduced traffic" ↔ "number of vehicles… dropped"; "policy" ↔ "measure"). 2 → C ("most staff" ↔ "the majority of employees"; "welcomed" ↔ "responded… with enthusiasm"). 3 → A ("expensive to run" ↔ "costly to maintain"; "scheme" ↔ "initiative"). Notice not a single key word was repeated — each answer is pure paraphrase.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Upgrade the everyday word (with a collocation)

Objective: Practise academic register. Replace the bold word with an academic upgrade and keep the collocation natural.

  1. Pollution is very bad for public health.
  2. The report shows a steady rise in temperatures.
  3. Universities want students to get practical skills.
  4. Poverty can cause a range of social problems.
✅ Answer (models — other good choices exist)

1. Pollution has a detrimental / adverse effect on public health. · 2. The report demonstrates / illustrates a steady rise in temperatures. · 3. Universities want students to acquire practical skills. · 4. Poverty can give rise to / contribute to a range of social problems. Judge yourself on collocation: does the academic word partner naturally with the words around it?

🏋️ Exercise 3: Paraphrase three ways each

Objective: Combine techniques. Rewrite each sentence three different ways, using at least two techniques each time (synonym, word class, voice, structure, superordinate).

  1. The government should spend more money on public transport.
  2. Many students find exams stressful.
✅ Answer (models — yours will differ)

Sentence 1:

  • Greater investment in public transport should be made by the authorities. (nominalise "spend money" → "investment"; passive voice)
  • Authorities ought to allocate additional funding to mass-transit systems. (synonyms; superordinate "public transport" → "mass-transit systems")
  • Increasing state spending on buses, trains and trams would be worthwhile. (restructure; specific examples; word-class shift)

Sentence 2:

  • A large number of learners experience anxiety during examinations. (synonyms; "stressful" → "experience anxiety")
  • Examinations are a source of stress for many young people. (structure flipped; noun form)
  • For a considerable proportion of students, tests can be highly stressful. (reorder; "many" → "a considerable proportion"; superordinate "exams" → "tests")

Check each version: same meaning, changed structure AND words, natural collocation.

🎯 Your Task: Build a Paraphrase Bank

  1. (15 min) Choose three common IELTS topics (e.g. environment, education, technology). For each, list 8–10 collocations (not single words) plus one paraphrase for each — building the three-column bank described above.
  2. (15 min) Write five plain sentences about any of your topics, then rewrite each one three ways, using at least two techniques per version. That's 15 paraphrases in total.
  3. (10 min) Take one Task 2-style prompt (invent one, e.g. "Do the benefits of remote work outweigh the drawbacks?") and write a paraphrased one-sentence introduction that restates it entirely in your own words.
  4. (5 min) Re-read everything and check for the two traps: a wrong-collocation synonym, and any version where the meaning has drifted.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I built paraphrase banks for three topics (collocations, not single words)
  • ☐ I rewrote five sentences three ways each (15 paraphrases)
  • ☐ Each paraphrase changes both words and structure
  • ☐ I wrote a paraphrased Task 2 introduction with no copying
  • ☐ I checked for wrong collocations and meaning drift

Partner / tutor option: swap paraphrases with a study partner and mark each other's — can they identify which techniques you used, and does any version accidentally change the meaning?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Underline key words in Listening/Reading questions and hunt for their meanings, not their exact wording.
  • Change both words and structure — a real paraphrase does at least two things at once.
  • Collect vocabulary as collocations and topic banks, and use each new phrase in a sentence you write.
  • Paraphrase the prompt in your Writing introduction — always in your own words.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Single-word swaps: changing one word and keeping the sentence is not paraphrasing — and examiners aren't fooled.
  • Wrong-collocation "synonyms": "affordable" ≠ "cheap," "make a mistake" not "do a mistake." A dictionary synonym can still be wrong in context.
  • Meaning drift: paraphrasing so aggressively that the sense changes — accuracy always comes before variety.
  • Copying the task prompt: a copied introduction earns no Lexical Resource credit and flags a weak candidate.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Paraphrasing is the master IELTS skill — it decides answers in Listening and Reading and lifts your band in Writing and Speaking.
  • Real paraphrase combines techniques: synonyms, word-class change, voice, structure, and superordinates — changing words and grammar.
  • Upgrade everyday words to academic register (the AWL idea), but keep collocations natural — accuracy beats showing off.
  • Build topic vocabulary banks of collocations for the recycled IELTS topics, and train yourself to recognise reworded answers under time pressure.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How many synonyms do I really need to memorise?

Fewer than you think — but you need to use them accurately. A modest set of well-understood collocations across the common topics beats a huge list of half-known words. Depth (knowing how a word combines) matters more than breadth for your Lexical Resource band.

What if I can't think of a synonym during the test?

Don't force a risky "big word." You can also paraphrase by changing structure (active↔passive, verb↔noun) or using a more general word — those moves need no rare vocabulary and still show range. A clear, accurate sentence always beats an impressive but wrong one.

Is it bad to repeat a word from the passage in Reading?

For finding answers, expect the opposite of the passage's words. But be careful: sometimes a word that matches exactly is a deliberate trap pointing to the wrong sentence. Confirm the whole meaning matches, not just one familiar word.
