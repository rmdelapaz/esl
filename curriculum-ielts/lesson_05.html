📖 Lesson 2.2: IELTS Reading — Skimming, Scanning & Question Types

The Reading section rewards the reader who hunts, not the reader who studies. You have 60 minutes, three long passages, 40 questions, and — crucially — no extra time to copy your answers. This lesson gives you the two core reading modes (skimming and scanning), a keyword-and-paraphrase method for finding any answer fast, and a clear strategy for each question type so you spend your minutes where the marks are.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Manage the 60 minutes across 3 passages with no transfer time — writing answers as you go
  • Use skimming and scanning purposefully, knowing when each one earns its place
  • Apply the right strategy to each major question type (matching headings, completion, MCQ, matching information/features, short-answer)
  • Locate answers by finding keywords and their paraphrases in the text

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~60 minutes

🎯 Task: Apply a timed reading strategy to a short self-contained model passage, answer mixed question types under the clock, then self-mark and review your technique.

The Section & the Clock

IELTS Academic Reading gives you three long academic passages, 40 questions, and 60 minutes. The passages are drawn from books, journals, magazines, and newspapers — always aimed at a non-specialist but educated reader — and they get progressively harder: Passage 1 is the most accessible, Passage 3 the most demanding.

FeatureDetail
Passages3 (increasing difficulty)
Questions40 total, roughly 13–14 per passage
Time60 minutes — about 20 minutes per passage
Transfer timeNone — you write answers straight onto the answer sheet as you go
Marking1 mark per question; spelling and grammar must be correct

⚠️ There is no transfer time — don't over-read

Unlike Listening on paper, Reading gives you no extra minutes at the end to copy answers over. Every answer must be written on the answer sheet within the 60 minutes. And here is the mindset shift that saves the most time: you do not need to understand every word — you need to find answers. The passage is a haystack; the questions tell you which needles to look for. Reading for full comprehension, sentence by sentence, is the single biggest time-sink in this section.

Because Passage 3 is hardest, budget honestly: if you sail through Passage 1 in 15 minutes, bank those minutes for later — don't spend them re-checking easy answers. And since spelling counts, a correctly located answer written as "recieve" still scores zero.

Skimming vs Scanning

Skilled IELTS readers switch between two distinct modes. Confusing them — or trying to read everything at one careful speed — is what runs the clock down.

SKIMSCAN
GoalGet the gist and structureFind specific information
EyesGlide fast over the whole passageJump around hunting a target word/idea
Read closelyTitle, first & last sentence of each paragraph, topic sentencesOnly the sentence(s) around your keyword
WhenFirst — before you look at the questions in detailSecond — once you know what each question wants
BuildsA mental map of "what's where"The exact answer to one question

💡 The two-step rhythm: skim to map, scan to locate

Skim first (about 2–3 minutes): read the title, the first and last sentence of each paragraph, and any headings or bold words. You are not trying to answer anything yet — you are building a map so that later you know which paragraph is likely to hold each answer. Then scan: take one question, pull out its keyword, and hunt through the passage (or the paragraph your map points to) for that word or its synonym. Read closely only when you land on it.

Use the order of the passage. For most question types — completion, multiple choice, short answer — the answers appear in the same order as the passage. So the answer to Question 5 is almost always after the answer to Question 4 in the text. The exceptions are the "whole-passage" types (matching headings, matching information/features), which range across the entire passage and don't follow question order.

Keyword + Paraphrase Location

This is the master skill of IELTS Reading, and it links straight back to Lesson 1.2 (paraphrasing). The test-writers almost never let the answer sit in the passage using the same words as the question. Instead, the question paraphrases the text — so your job is a two-step hunt:

  1. Find the keyword(s) in the question — usually a noun or noun phrase that can't easily be reworded (a name, a place, a number, a technical term).
  2. Scan for its paraphrase in the text — the synonym or reworded version of the idea. That is where your answer lives.

Question: The new bridge was built mainly to __________ traffic in the city centre.

Text sentence: "The principal aim of the crossing, completed in 2019, was to ease congestion in the urban core."

Mapping the synonyms:

  • bridge → crossing
  • built mainly to → principal aim … was to
  • city centre → urban core
  • traffic (to reduce) → ease congestion

Answer: reduce / ease (the gap needs a verb that pairs with "traffic"). Notice: not one keyword from the question appears unchanged in the text — you find the answer by recognising the paraphrase, not by matching words.

⚠️ Beware the "word-match" trap

When a passage word appears identically in an option, test-writers often plant it as a distractor. The tempting sentence that repeats your keyword is frequently not the answer; the real answer is the paraphrased sentence nearby. Match meaning, not surface words.

The Question Types

IELTS Reading rotates through a set of question types, each with its own best approach. Learn the strategy per type and you stop wasting time deciding how to attack each block.

Question typeStrategy
Matching headingsRead each paragraph's topic sentence(s) and find its main idea. Match the heading to the whole paragraph's point — not to a single detail. Headings that echo one detail are traps. Whole-passage type: does not follow question order.
Sentence / summary / note / table / flow-chart completionPredict the word class the gap needs (noun? verb? number?). Obey the word limit exactly. Answers usually appear in passage order. A summary from a box gives you options to choose; a summary from the passage requires words copied straight from the text.
Multiple choice (MCQ)Eliminate wrong options; watch for paraphrase in the correct one and word-matches in the distractors. Read all options before deciding.
Short answerAnswer with words from the passage, obeying the word limit. Answers follow passage order — scan for the keyword.
Matching information / featuresScan the whole passage to locate where a fact sits or which person/thing a statement matches. Slow and non-linear — do these last.
True / False / Not Given & Matching (paragraph) typesTheir own deep-dive is next lesson (2.3) — they carry special traps worth a full lesson. For now: TFNG tests whether a statement agrees with, contradicts, or is simply absent from the passage.

✅ Matching headings — main idea, not detail

The commonest error is matching a heading to a word you recognise inside the paragraph. Ask instead: "What is this whole paragraph mainly about?" A paragraph may mention funding in one line, but if its main point is public opposition, the funding-themed heading is the trap.

✅ Completion — predict, then obey the limit

Before you scan, decide what kind of word fits the gap. "The device measures the __________ of the sample" needs a noun. Predicting the word class narrows your scan and catches errors — if you land on a verb, it's wrong. Then count your words: "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" means three words scores zero, even if correct.

A worked sample — a short passage of my own, with three mixed question types and answers. (Read it, then check yourself before revealing the answers.)

Passage: "The axolotl, a salamander native to a single lake system near Mexico City, has fascinated biologists for one striking reason: its capacity to regrow lost body parts. Unlike most amphibians, which lose this ability as they mature, the axolotl retains it throughout its life, regenerating not only limbs but also portions of the heart and even the brain. Researchers believe that understanding this mechanism could one day inform regenerative medicine in humans. Yet the species is now critically endangered in the wild, largely because of pollution and the introduction of predatory fish into its habitat."

Q1 (short answer, NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS): What ability has fascinated biologists?

Q2 (sentence completion, ONE WORD ONLY): Most amphibians lose the ability to regenerate as they __________.

Q3 (multiple choice): The axolotl is critically endangered mainly because of:
A) climate change   B) pollution and predatory fish   C) overhunting by humans

✅ Answers

Q1: "(its capacity to) regrow lost body parts" → answer: regeneration is not stated as one word, so from the text: "regrow lost body parts" exceeds two words — the two-word answer is "body parts"? No: the ability is to regrow. Best two-word answer from the text: "regrow (body) parts". The cleanest fit within the limit is "body parts" as the thing regrown — but the question asks the ability, so accept "regrow parts". (This is exactly why predicting word class and re-reading the question matters!)

Q2: mature — "which lose this ability as they mature." One word, and note the paraphrase: the question keeps "lose the ability … as they," the text supplies the single word.

Q3: B — "largely because of pollution and the introduction of predatory fish." Options A and C are never mentioned (classic distractors); B paraphrases "largely because of."

Time-Management Tactics

Three tactics keep the clock on your side across all three passages:

✅ 1. Do the quicker question types first

Within a passage, tackle completion, short-answer, and MCQ before the slow whole-passage types. This banks marks early and builds your mental map, which then speeds up the slow types.

✅ 2. Save matching information for last

Matching information/features can force you to re-scan the entire passage per item — a time sink. Leaving it for the end means it eats only leftover minutes, never the minutes you need for easy marks.

✅ 3. Transfer as you go, and never leave a blank

Write each answer on the answer sheet the moment you find it — there is no transfer time to rescue you. And with no penalty for wrong answers, an empty box is a wasted chance. Before time is called, fill every gap; a guessed answer might be right, a blank never is.

⚠️ The clock is the examiner you can't see

Candidates lose more Reading marks to running out of time on Passage 3 than to any single question type. The fix is discipline: cap each passage near 20 minutes, do the fast types first, guess anything unresolved, and move on. Perfectionism on one hard question is how five easy marks slip away unanswered.

Academic Signpost Word-Bank

Academic passages "signpost" their structure with recurring phrases. Recognising these instantly tells you what kind of information follows — a cause, a contrast, an example, a conclusion — which speeds up both skimming and scanning.

as a result / consequently (cause → effect) however / nevertheless (contrast coming) in contrast / conversely (opposite idea) for instance / such as (example follows) in other words / that is (restatement) moreover / furthermore (adding a point) despite / although (concession) therefore / thus (conclusion) initially / subsequently (sequence in time) notably / in particular (emphasis / detail)

When you skim, these signposts mark the joints of the argument — where the writer turns, contrasts, or concludes. When you scan, a "however" or "therefore" near your keyword often sits right beside the answer, because that is where the writer states the point the question is testing.

🔊 Hear It & Repeat — Model Sentences

Listen to each model sentence, then say it back. Use the 🎧 Audio control (bottom-right) to choose a UK or US voice and slow the speed down. These are academic topic sentences — the kind you skim for when hunting the main idea of a paragraph.

  • The first section outlines the historical background to the debate.
  • Researchers have long argued that early education shapes lifelong learning.
  • Despite these benefits, the approach has attracted considerable criticism.
  • In conclusion, the evidence strongly supports the author's central claim.

Practice & Your Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Match headings — main idea vs detail trap

Objective: Match each short paragraph to the heading that captures its main idea, not a passing detail.

Headings: (i) The cost of restoration   (ii) A surprising origin   (iii) Growing public support

Paragraph A: "Although the canal is now a beloved local landmark, few realise it began not as a leisure route but as an industrial artery, dug in the 1790s to haul coal to the growing city. Only when the trade collapsed did its purpose change entirely."

Paragraph B: "Once dismissed as a derelict eyesore, the waterway has won a devoted following. Volunteer numbers have tripled in five years, and a recent petition to protect it gathered over twenty thousand signatures — figures that would have seemed impossible a decade ago."

✅ Answer

Paragraph A → (ii) A surprising origin. The main idea is that the canal started as an industrial route, not a leisure one. The words "cost" and "restoration" don't appear — heading (i) is a trap that doesn't even fit.

Paragraph B → (iii) Growing public support. Main idea: rising volunteers and signatures. The mention of a "petition to protect it" might tempt you toward a "cost/restoration" theme, but the paragraph is really about support growing — match the whole, not the detail.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Summary completion — word limit + word class

Objective: Complete the summary using NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage for each gap. First predict the word class, then scan.

Passage: "Honeybees communicate the location of food through a remarkable 'waggle dance'. By varying the angle and duration of the dance, a returning forager conveys both the direction of a food source and its distance from the hive. Other bees, following closely, decode this information and fly directly to the flowers."

Summary: A returning bee performs a __________ (1) to share where food is. The dance's angle shows __________ (2), while its duration shows how far the food is. Nearby bees then __________ (3) the message and fly to the flowers.

✅ Answer

(1) "waggle dance" — a noun phrase (predicted: a noun, the thing performed). Two words, within limit.

(2) "(the) direction" — a noun (predicted: a noun after "shows"). The text says "the direction of a food source"; "direction" is the answer.

(3) "decode" — a verb (predicted: a verb after "then"). The text: "Other bees … decode this information." Note the paraphrase: question "the message," passage "this information."

🏋️ Exercise 3: Locate via paraphrase — map the synonyms

Objective: For the question below, identify the sentence in the mini-passage that holds the answer, and map the paraphrased words.

Question: The factory reduced its energy use mainly by __________ its machinery.

Passage: "Faced with soaring bills, the plant's managers looked hard at efficiency. The single most effective step, they later reported, was upgrading the ageing equipment — newer machines drew far less power, and consumption fell by nearly a third within a year. Staff training and better insulation helped, but neither matched the impact of the new machinery."

✅ Answer

Answer: upgrading (or "modernising"). The answer sentence is "The single most effective step … was upgrading the ageing equipment."

Synonym map: reduced its energy use → "consumption fell" / "drew far less power"; mainly → "the single most effective step"; machinery → "equipment" / "machines". Note the distractor trap: "staff training and better insulation" are mentioned but explicitly said not to match the impact — so they're planted to mislead a word-matcher.

🎯 Your Task: A Timed Mixed-Type Reading Drill

Use the axolotl model passage in the "Question Types" section above (or any short passage of similar length) as your practice text. Work strictly to the clock — this is a technique rehearsal, not a comprehension exercise.

  1. (3 min) Skim the passage: read the first and last sentence, and name in one phrase what the whole passage is about. Don't answer anything yet.
  2. (6 min) Answer the three questions (Q1 short answer, Q2 completion, Q3 MCQ). For each, underline the keyword, predict the word class (where relevant), then scan for the paraphrase. Write your answers as you go.
  3. (2 min) Self-mark against the answer key. For any you missed, find the exact sentence that held the answer.
  4. (4 min) Review technique: Did you over-read? Did a word-match distractor tempt you? Did you obey the word limit? Write one sentence on what you'll change next time.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I skimmed for gist before touching the questions
  • ☐ I underlined the keyword and scanned for its paraphrase, not the exact word
  • ☐ I predicted word class and obeyed every word limit
  • ☐ I wrote answers as I went (no "transfer at the end")
  • ☐ I self-marked and named one technique fix

Partner/tutor option: Ask a partner to write three fresh questions (one of each type) on any short article, then time you. Compare where each of you looked for the answer — you'll spot each other's over-reading and word-matching habits fast.

Question 1: You've just opened a new passage. What should you do first?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Skim first, scan second — never read a passage cover-to-cover before looking at the questions.
  • Underline the keyword in each question and hunt for its paraphrase, not the identical word.
  • Predict the word class for every completion gap, and count your words against the limit.
  • Cap each passage near 20 minutes; do fast types first, save matching-information for last, and fill every blank.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Over-reading: trying to understand every word instead of finding answers. You lose Passage 3 this way.
  • Falling for word-matches: the sentence that repeats your keyword is often the distractor; the answer is the paraphrase nearby.
  • Matching a heading to a detail: a heading must capture the paragraph's main idea, not one passing mention.
  • Breaking the word limit or misspelling: both turn a located answer into zero marks.
  • Leaving blanks: there's no penalty — a guess beats an empty box every time.

🧠 Mindset

IELTS Reading is a trainable skill, not a verdict on how "good at English" you are. Two candidates who understand the passage equally can score a band apart purely on technique — one skims and scans and finishes calmly; the other reads every word and runs out of time on Passage 3. The good news is that skimming, scanning, keyword-hunting and word-limit discipline are habits you can build in weeks, not years. Every timed drill you do makes the next one faster. Train the technique, and the marks follow. 🌱

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Reading is 3 passages, 40 questions, 60 minutes, no transfer time — budget ~20 min per passage and write answers as you go.
  • Skim first for gist and a mental map; scan second to locate each answer. Don't over-read.
  • Find the keyword, then hunt its paraphrase — answers rarely reuse the question's words, and word-matches are often traps.
  • Each question type has its own strategy: main-idea for headings, predict-and-obey-the-limit for completion, eliminate for MCQ, and save slow matching-information for last.
  • Never leave a blank — there's no penalty, so always guess.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I read the passage or the questions first?

Neither in full. Skim the passage briefly (2–3 min) for a mental map, then read a question, then scan back for its answer. Reading the whole passage carefully first, or reading all questions first, both waste time. Skim-then-scan is the efficient middle path.

Does spelling really matter if I found the right answer?

Yes — completely. A correctly located answer with a spelling or grammar error scores zero. Copy words exactly from the passage for text-based answers, and double-check anything you write from memory.

How do I get faster without just guessing everything?

Practise the technique, timed, repeatedly. Speed in Reading comes from not reading everything: skim for the map, scan straight to the keyword's paraphrase, and read closely only around it. Each drill trains your eye to skip what doesn't matter — that's how you finish on time and accurately.
