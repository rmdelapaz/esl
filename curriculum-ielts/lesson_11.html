🎤 Lesson 4.2: Speaking — Pronunciation, Lexical Range & Band Descriptors

You already know how to keep talking (Lesson 4.1). Now let's understand exactly how the examiner turns your two-way chat into a number. Speaking is marked on four criteria — and two of them, Pronunciation and Lexical Resource, are where careful candidates leave the most marks on the table. In this lesson we demystify the mark scheme and give you concrete, trainable ways to lift both.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Name the four Speaking marking criteria and explain what each one rewards
  • Improve the pronunciation features that move the band — stress, intonation, connected speech, chunking, clarity — without chasing a "native accent"
  • Widen your lexical range with topic vocabulary, collocation, natural idiom, and paraphrase when a word won't come
  • Self-assess a recording of your own speaking against simplified band descriptors

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Task: Record a Part 2 long turn, self-assess it against all four criteria, set one target per criterion, then re-record to improve at least one.

The Four Marking Criteria

Your examiner isn't grading a conversation on "how good your English sounds" overall. They score you against four separate criteria, each worth 25% of your Speaking band, then average them. Knowing what each one rewards lets you aim your practice — and stops you pouring effort into the wrong thing.

Criterion (25% each)What it measuresHow to lift it
Fluency & CoherenceSpeaking at length without long pauses or constant self-correction; connecting ideas logicallyExtend answers, use linkers and discourse markers, don't over-correct (see Lesson 4.1)
Lexical ResourceRange of vocabulary and how precisely and naturally you use it, including less common and idiomatic languageTopic vocab, collocation, some natural idiom, and paraphrase around gaps (this lesson)
Grammatical Range & AccuracyVariety of sentence structures (simple + complex) and how error-free they areMix tenses and clause types; control errors that block meaning (see Lesson 1.3)
PronunciationHow easily you're understood, and how well you use stress, intonation and connected speechWord/sentence stress, intonation, linking, chunking — intelligibility, not accent (this lesson)

💡 Two criteria, one focus each

This lesson zooms into Pronunciation and Lexical Resource because they're the two most candidates neglect — and both are highly trainable with feedback. Fluency was Lesson 4.1; grammatical range links back to Lesson 1.3. Nothing here works in isolation: a precise word (Lexical) said with clear stress (Pronunciation) inside a well-formed sentence (Grammar) delivered without a stumble (Fluency) is what a Band 7 turn actually sounds like.

Pronunciation: Clarity, Not Accent

⚠️ The biggest Pronunciation myth

You do NOT need a British, American or "native" accent. The descriptor rewards being clearly understood and using pronunciation features well. Examiners hear dozens of accents daily and are trained to score intelligibility, not to prefer any one accent. Keep your accent — sharpen the features below.

Pronunciation is not one skill but a bundle of features. You lift the band by controlling these, not by imitating a nationality:

FeatureWhat it isWhy it moves the band
Word stressStressing the right syllable in a wordWrong stress can make a common word unrecognisable, reducing clarity
Sentence / content stressEmphasising the important (content) words in a sentenceGuides the listener to your meaning; flat, even stress muddies it
IntonationThe rise and fall of your voiceSignals questions, lists, contrast and attitude; a monotone hides all of that
Connected speechLinking words so they flow naturallySounds fluent and natural; word-by-word delivery sounds robotic and effortful
Chunking / pausingPausing at sense groups, not mid-ideaMakes meaning easy to follow; random pauses fragment it
Individual soundsSpecific consonants/vowels you produceOnly worth fixing the ones that actually cause confusion

🔊 Word stress: put it on the right syllable

Capitals show the stressed syllable. Say these aloud and feel the beat land in the right place:

PHO·to·graph → pho·TO·gra·phy → pho·to·GRA·phic

de·VEL·op · com·FOR·ta·ble · e·CO·no·my · e·co·NO·mic

Notice how stress moves as a word changes form. Stressing the wrong syllable (say, PHO·to·gra·phy) can leave a listener briefly lost — a small clarity cost that examiners hear.

🔊 Sentence stress: highlight the content words

Stress the words that carry the meaning (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs); glide over the grammar words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries). The same sentence can shift meaning depending on which word you stress:

I NEVER said she took the money. (someone else did — but not me)

I never said SHE took the money. (I meant someone else took it)

I never said she took the MONEY. (she took something else)

🔊 Intonation: don't speak in a flat line

Let your voice rise and fall to carry attitude and structure. Arrows show the pitch movement:

Do you enjoy it? ↗ (yes/no question — rising)

Where do you live? ↘ (wh-question — falling)

I like reading ↗, cooking ↗, and hiking ↘. (list — rise, rise, fall to close)

It was absolutely ↗ amazing ↘! (attitude / enthusiasm)

🔊 Connected speech: link the words

Natural English glues words together. Aim for smooth linking, not textbook word-by-word delivery:

an apple → "a·napple" (consonant links to the next vowel)

turn it off → "tur·ni·toff"

a lot of → "a·lo·tof" · going to → "gonna" · want to → "wanna" (natural in relaxed speech)

💡 Intelligibility over accent

The one test that matters: can a patient stranger understand you first time, without leaning in? If yes, your accent is fine — polish stress, intonation and linking to climb higher. If a specific sound (say, /θ/ in "think", or word-final consonants you drop) keeps causing genuine confusion, target that one thing. Don't try to erase your accent; that isn't the goal and it isn't scored.

Lexical Resource: Range & Precision

Lexical Resource rewards two things together: range (how much vocabulary you can draw on) and precision (using the right word, naturally). Band 7 and above specifically rewards less common and idiomatic vocabulary used appropriately. Four moves lift this criterion:

1. Deploy topic vocabulary

Speaking topics recur — work, study, technology, environment, hometown, health, travel. Build and use a topic bank (link back to your Lesson 1.2 banks). Instead of "I use my phone a lot," reach for "I'm fairly reliant on my smartphone for pretty much everything — navigation, banking, staying in touch."

2. Use collocations (words that live together)

Natural English runs on collocation. Say the pairing a native speaker expects:

  • make a decision
  • a heavy workload
  • strike a balance
  • a close-knit family
  • bitterly disappointed
  • a wide range of
  • pay close attention
  • a daunting task
  • vividly remember

3. Sprinkle in natural idiom

A little idiomatic language, used correctly and in the right register, signals a higher band. Keep it conversational, not proverb-heavy:

it's not really my cup of tea I'm in two minds about it off the top of my head it grew on me a bit of a game-changer time flies to be honest it depends, really

4. Paraphrase when a word won't come (the key band-saver)

When you can't retrieve a word, describe around it instead of freezing or going silent. This keeps you fluent and shows lexical flexibility — examiners reward the strategy, not penalise the gap. Keep these fillers ready:

it's a kind of… it's the thing you use to… it's a bit like a… I don't know the exact word, but… it's what you'd call… you know, the sort of…

Example: forgotten "corkscrew"? "It's that little thing you use to open a bottle of wine — you twist it in and pull the cork out." The examiner understood you perfectly, and you never froze.

Upgrade over-used basic words

Repeating good, nice, thing, very, a lot caps your Lexical band. Keep sharper alternatives on call:

Over-usedSharper upgrades (choose by context)
goodexcellent, impressive, worthwhile, enjoyable, rewarding, beneficial
nicelovely, pleasant, welcoming, charming, delightful, scenic
badpoor, disappointing, harmful, unpleasant, dreadful, off-putting
thingaspect, factor, issue, feature, element, benefit, drawback
very + adjuse a stronger single word: very tired → exhausted; very big → enormous; very interesting → fascinating
a lot ofa great deal of, plenty of, a wide range of, countless, numerous

⚠️ Don't force "big words" or idioms

Cramming memorised idioms or fancy vocabulary in the wrong place hurts you — it sounds unnatural and shows you don't really control the word. A single idiom used correctly beats three used awkwardly. The descriptor rewards natural and accurate over show-off. Rule of thumb: if you're not confident it's right and it fits, use a simpler word you do control, or paraphrase.

Band 5 vs Band 7: Same Idea

Here's the same content — a Part 2 answer about a place the candidate likes — delivered at roughly Band 5 and roughly Band 7. Watch the lexical upgrades, the stress/intonation notes, and one paraphrase-around-a-gap.

Cue card: Describe a place you enjoy visiting. Say where it is, what you do there, and why you like it.

~ Band 5 version:

"I like to go to a park near my house. It is a very nice place. There are a lot of trees and it is good. I go there with my friends and we talk and it is nice. I like it because it is a good place to relax and it is not bad."

Why it caps at ~5: basic, repeated words (nice, good, a lot, thing); no collocation or idiom; likely flat, even stress with little intonation. The meaning is clear but the range is thin.

~ Band 7 version (same idea):

"There's a lovely little park just around the corner from my flat, and it's honestly become my go-to spot whenever I need to switch off. It's a really PEACE·ful ↘ place — tree-lined paths, a small lake, that sort of thing. I usually head there with a couple of close friends; we'll grab a coffee, stroll around, and just CATCH up ↗. What I love about it is that it's a proper escape from the hustle and bustle of the city — you know, that constant… I don't know the exact word, but that background noise and pressure — and after half an hour there I feel completely recharged."

Why it reaches ~7: less common and idiomatic language (go-to spot, switch off, hustle and bustle, recharged); collocations (tree-lined paths, catch up, a proper escape); a natural paraphrase ("I don't know the exact word, but…") instead of freezing on "bustle"; and marked stress and intonation (rise on "catch up", fall to close "peaceful"). Same idea — far more range and control.

Self-Assessment Table

Record yourself, listen back, and place each criterion on this simplified band-descriptor grid. Be honest — the band that best describes what you actually did, not what you meant to do.

Criterion~ Band 5~ Band 6~ Band 7
Fluency & Coherence Noticeable pauses; often self-corrects or repeats; simple links only Speaks at length but with some hesitation; a range of linkers, sometimes overused Speaks at length easily; hesitation is content-related, not language-related; connects ideas smoothly
Lexical Resource Limited, repeated basic words; struggles to paraphrase Enough vocabulary to discuss topics; attempts less common words with some inaccuracy Flexible range; some less common and idiomatic vocabulary used well; paraphrases effectively
Grammatical Range & Accuracy Mostly simple sentences; frequent errors, some blocking meaning Mix of simple and complex forms; errors present but meaning usually clear Range of complex structures; frequent error-free sentences; errors rarely impede
Pronunciation Often clear but mispronunciations or flat delivery reduce clarity at times Generally understood; some control of features, with lapses Easy to understand throughout; good use of stress, intonation and connected speech

💡 Record yourself regularly

Recording and listening back is the single most effective speaking practice there is. You cannot hear your own flat intonation, dropped word endings, or over-used "good" while you're talking — but you hear them instantly on playback. One recording a day, self-assessed against this table, beats hours of unrecorded chat.

Exercises & Your Task

🏋️ Exercise 1: Upgrade the basic words

Objective: Replace over-used words with precise, topic-appropriate vocabulary.

Rewrite each sentence, upgrading the bold word(s):

  1. The museum was very good and I saw a lot of nice paintings.
  2. My job has a lot of good things but also some bad things.
  3. The food there was very nice and the staff were good.
✅ Answer (models — many good versions exist)

1. "The museum was fascinating, and I saw a whole range of striking, beautifully preserved paintings." · 2. "My job has plenty of rewarding aspects, but also a few real drawbacks — the workload can be overwhelming." · 3. "The food was absolutely delicious, and the staff were friendly and attentive." Judge yours by the descriptor: precise, natural, not forced.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Mark the stress / choose the intonation

Objective: Control stress and intonation deliberately.

(a) Which syllable is stressed? photography · comfortable · economic · development

(b) Rising ↗ or falling ↘? "Do you live nearby?" · "Where are you from?" · "I love reading, cooking and travelling."

✅ Answer

(a) pho·TO·gra·phy · COM·for·ta·ble · e·co·NO·mic · de·VEL·op·ment.

(b) "Do you live nearby?" → rising ↗ (yes/no question). · "Where are you from?" → falling ↘ (wh-question). · "I love reading ↗, cooking ↗ and travelling ↘." (rise through the list, fall on the last item to close it).

🏋️ Exercise 3: Paraphrase around a gap

Objective: Describe a word instead of freezing on it.

Pretend you can't remember these three words. Describe each in one sentence so a listener understands, using a filler like "it's a kind of…" or "it's the thing you use to…":

  1. umbrella
  2. librarian
  3. recycling
✅ Answer (models)

1. "It's the thing you use to keep the rain off you — you hold it above your head and it opens up." · 2. "It's the person who works in a place full of books and helps you find and borrow them." · 3. "It's a kind of process where you don't throw things away — you collect paper, glass and plastic so they can be used again." Any version a listener understands scores the point; the goal is to keep talking, not to be perfect.

🧠 Mindset

IELTS Speaking is a trainable skill, not a verdict on who you are. Pronunciation features and vocabulary range respond fast to deliberate practice with feedback — far faster than people expect. Your accent is part of your identity and it is not a barrier; clarity is. Every recording you make and listen back to is a rep. Do the reps, and the band climbs. 🌱

🎯 Your Task: Record, Self-Assess, Re-record

Use a real Part 2 cue card and a phone recorder. Total ~20 minutes.

  1. (1 min) Take a cue card, e.g. "Describe a skill you would like to learn. Say what it is, why you want to learn it, and how you would go about it." Note a few points.
  2. (2 min) Record your long turn — speak for 1½–2 minutes without stopping.
  3. (5 min) Listen back and score yourself on the self-assessment table — one band per criterion. Be honest.
  4. (3 min) Set one target per criterion (e.g. Lexical: "swap 'good' for a precise word 3 times"; Pronunciation: "add a falling tone to end statements").
  5. (2 min) Re-record the same turn, focusing on lifting at least one criterion.
  6. (2 min) Compare the two recordings. What actually improved?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Record and listen back regularly — it's the fastest way to hear your own flat intonation and repeated words.
  • Keep paraphrase fillers ready ("it's a kind of…") so a forgotten word never becomes a silence.
  • Upgrade one or two over-used words per topic (good → rewarding, thing → aspect) rather than memorising word lists you can't deploy.
  • End statements with a falling tone and questions/lists with a rise — practise it consciously until it's automatic.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Chasing a "native accent": it isn't scored and wastes practice time — target clarity and features instead.
  • Forcing show-off vocabulary: a wrongly used "big word" or idiom lowers Lexical Resource; natural and accurate wins.
  • Speaking in a monotone: flat delivery hides your meaning and caps Pronunciation even when every sound is correct.
  • Freezing on an unknown word: silence costs you twice (Fluency + Lexical); paraphrase and keep moving.
  • Over-repeating good / nice / thing / very: the surest sign of a limited lexical range to an examiner's ear.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Speaking is scored on four criteria, each 25%: Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, and Pronunciation.
  • Pronunciation = intelligibility, not accent: lift the band through word/sentence stress, intonation, connected speech and chunking.
  • Lexical Resource rewards range and precision — topic vocab, collocation, some natural idiom, and paraphrase when a word won't come.
  • Don't force show-off vocabulary or chase a native accent; natural, accurate and clear beats both.
  • Record and self-assess against the descriptors — the fastest feedback loop in speaking practice.

That self-assessment loop is what turns practice into progress. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Will my accent limit my Speaking band?

No. The Pronunciation criterion is about being understood and using features well, not about sounding native. Many Band 8 and 9 speakers have clearly identifiable accents. Only work on individual sounds that genuinely cause a listener confusion.

How many idioms should I use to hit Band 7?

There's no quota. A couple used naturally and correctly is plenty; the descriptor rewards range and precision, not density. One well-placed idiom beats five forced ones, which actually lower the band.

Is it bad to pause to think?

Short, natural thinking pauses are fine — even Band 9 speakers pause for content. What hurts is long, language-related hesitation and freezing on a word. Fill those with a paraphrase or a discourse marker ("that's an interesting question…") and keep going.
