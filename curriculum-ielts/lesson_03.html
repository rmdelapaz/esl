🧱 Lesson 1.3: Grammar for a High Band

One of the four criteria in both Writing and Speaking is Grammatical Range & Accuracy — a full quarter of your mark. It rewards two things at once: a variety of complex sentence structures and the accuracy to control them. This lesson is a practical toolkit for both. You already know the grammar; here you'll learn which structures IELTS pays for, which tenses each task demands, and the handful of errors that quietly cap thousands of scripts at Band 6.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Deploy a range of complex structures — subordination, relative clauses, conditionals, participle and noun clauses — as the band descriptors require
  • Use the tenses IELTS demands accurately, especially for Task 1 trends and Task 2 argument
  • Fix the high-frequency errors that cap bands — articles, subject–verb agreement, prepositions, and run-ons
  • Balance range against accuracy so ambition never costs you control

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Task: Upgrade a simple, error-free paragraph for range without losing accuracy, then error-correct a flawed paragraph — the two halves of the GRA criterion.

What GRA Actually Rewards

Grammatical Range & Accuracy is scored on a simple idea: the higher the band, the more your grammar shows both variety and control. Neither alone is enough. A script full of flawless short sentences lacks range; a script full of ambitious sentences riddled with mistakes lacks accuracy. Examiners reward the writer who does both.

💡 What the bands roughly say

BandRangeAccuracy
5Mostly simple sentences; few complex formsFrequent errors that can strain the reader
6A mix of simple and complex formsSome errors, but meaning stays clear
7A variety of complex structuresFrequent error-free sentences; good control
8+A wide range, used flexiblyMostly error-free; only occasional slips

Notice the pattern: moving from 5 to 6 is mostly about adding range; moving from 6 to 7+ is about keeping that range accurate. Know which gap is yours.

The two big levers, then, are the structures you can produce and the errors you can avoid. The rest of this lesson equips you with both — a toolkit of complex forms, a map of which tenses each task needs, and a hit-list of the errors that most often hold candidates at Band 6.

The Complex-Structure Toolkit

"Complex" doesn't mean long or convoluted — it means a sentence with more than one clause joined in a meaningful way. You already know these forms from your CEFR study; the job now is to deploy them on purpose. Here is the practical toolkit, with a worked example of each.

StructureSignalsExample
Subordinate (adverbial) clausebecause, although, while, whereas, since, ifAlthough car ownership rose, public-transport use also increased.
Relative clause — definingwho, which, that (no commas)Students who study abroad often become more independent.
Relative clause — non-definingwho, which (with commas)Renewable energy, which is now cheaper, is expanding fast.
First conditionalIf + present, will…If governments invest in cycling, congestion will fall.
Second conditional (hypothetical)If + past, would…If fees were lower, more people would attend university.
Participle clause-ing / -ed openerHaving risen sharply, prices then levelled off.
Noun clauseThe fact that…, What is clear is…The fact that emissions keep rising is deeply worrying.
Comparative / superlativemore…than, the most…, far higherSales were far higher in 2020 than in any earlier year.

💡 Match the structure to the task

  • Task 1 (data): comparatives and superlatives are non-negotiable — you're constantly ranking and contrasting figures. Participle clauses ("peaking in June, before falling") pack trends efficiently.
  • Task 2 (argument): conditionals carry hypotheticals ("if this continued…"); subordination with although / whereas concedes and contrasts; noun clauses ("What critics overlook is…") frame ideas with sophistication.
  • Speaking: the same forms, spoken — "I'd say the main reason is…", "If I had more time, I would…" Range counts here too.

🔑 A phrase-bank of reliable frames

Memorise a small set of dependable complex-sentence frames. Slotting your content into a frame you control is far safer than improvising a structure under pressure.

Although X, Y… While X is true, Y… Whereas A rose, B fell… One reason why … is that … This suggests that … The fact that … means that … What is clear is that … If … were …, … would … Not only … but also … Having … , … then …

📈 Model: simple → ranged (same meaning, higher band)

Simple (accurate but flat — Band 5–6 range): Many people move to cities. They want jobs. Cities become crowded. This causes problems. Governments must build more housing.

Ranged (Band 7+ range, still accurate): Because so many people move to cities in search of work, urban areas are becoming increasingly crowded, which creates a range of problems. If this trend continues, governments will have to build far more housing than they currently plan.

Structures added: subordinate clause (because), participle-style phrase (in search of), non-defining relative (which), first conditional (if…will), comparative (far more…than) — and not a single error introduced. That is the whole game.

The Tenses That Matter

IELTS doesn't test every tense equally. A few carry most of the work, and each task leans on particular ones. Choosing the right tense for the context is itself part of accuracy.

ContextTypical tensesWhy
Task 2 — general truths & opinionsPresent simpleTechnology plays a central role in modern life. Facts and stances are stated in the present.
Task 1 — trends over timePast simple, present perfect, future formsData with dates: sales rose (past), have risen (up to now), are projected to rise (forecast).
Task 1 — processes / how things workPresent simple passiveThe beans are roasted, then they are ground. The passive keeps the focus on the process, not the doer.
Task 2 — hypotheticalsConditionals (1st / 2nd)If cities banned cars, air quality would improve. Argue about possible futures.
Speaking Part 1/2Mixed — present habits, past experiencesI usually… (present), Last year I… (past). Accurate tense-shifting shows range.

⚠️ The Task 1 tense trap

A chart's dates decide the tense — read them first. If the data ends in the past (e.g. "1990–2010"), describe it in the past simple. If it runs up to the present, the present perfect often fits ("numbers have grown"). If it shows a forecast ("2025–2040"), use future forms ("is expected to reach"). Writing a past chart in the present simple is one of the most common Task 1 errors — and it repeats in every sentence.

Errors That Cap Your Band

This is where marks quietly leak away. Most candidates stuck at Band 6 don't lack ideas or vocabulary — they repeat a small set of high-frequency errors often enough that accuracy pulls the score down. Learn to catch these and you protect every complex sentence you attempt.

⚠️ The band-limiting seven

Error type❌ Incorrect✅ Correct
Articles (a/an/the/zero) — the #1 IELTS errorGovernment should build hospital in every city.The government should build a hospital in every city.
Subject–verb agreementThe number of students are rising.The number of students is rising.
Prepositionsan increase of prices; responsible of; effect toan increase in prices; responsible for; an effect on
Run-on / comma splicePrices rose, people bought less.Prices rose, so people bought less. (or a full stop)
Uncountable nounsinformations, advices, researchesinformation, advice, research (no plural -s)
Word formPollution effects health. / a positive affectPollution affects health. / a positive effect
Over-using one structureTen sentences all beginning "There are many…"Vary openings: "Many…", "One factor…", "Although…"

💡 Quick fixes worth memorising

  • "The number of X is" (singular) but "A number of X are" (plural). The subject is number vs X.
  • increase / decrease / rise / fall + IN · responsible / famous / known FOR · an effect / impact ON · depend / rely ON.
  • affect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (a result). "Smoking affects you and has a bad effect."
  • Uncountables take no plural and no a/an: much information, some advice, a piece of research.

🩹 Model: flawed → corrected

Flawed (six errors — accuracy well below Band 6): Nowadays, the number of students who study abroad are increasing. This have many benefits, but it also effect local universities. Government should give more informations to students, they can make better choice. This lead to increase of competition.

Corrected: Nowadays, the number of students who study abroad is increasing. This has many benefits, but it also affects local universities. The government should give more information to students, so they can make a better choice. This leads to an increase in competition.

Fixes: agreement (is, has, leads), word form (affects), uncountable (information), articles (The, a, an), comma splice (, so), preposition (increase in). None of these are advanced — they are cheap marks left on the table.

Range vs Accuracy

Here is the strategic heart of GRA. Range and accuracy pull against each other: the more ambitious the structure, the easier it is to break. The examiner weighs both — so the winning move is not "attempt the hardest sentences," but "attempt structures you can control."

graph LR
    A["Simple & correct"] -->|add complex forms| B["Ranged & correct — the target"]
    A -->|reach too far| C["Ambitious & broken"]
    C -->|master a few frames| B

⚠️ Ambition without control scores worse

A long, tangled sentence with three grammar mistakes damages your accuracy mark more than a clean simple sentence would — and it can even blur your meaning, which risks the Coherence mark too. If you can't yet control a structure reliably, a correct simpler version beats a broken sophisticated one. Range you can't control is not range; it's noise.

🧠 Mindset

Grammar for IELTS is a trainable skill, not a verdict on your talent. You don't need to master every structure in English — you need a reliable handful: two or three subordinators, both relative clauses, first and second conditionals, comparatives, and one noun-clause frame. Drill those until they're automatic and error-free, and you have all the range a Band 7 demands. Depth of control beats breadth of ambition every time. 🌱

Practice

🏋️ Exercise 1: Combine into complex sentences

Objective: Turn simple ideas into ranged sentences using subordination and relative clauses.

  1. Online learning is convenient. It lacks face-to-face interaction. (use "although" or "while")
  2. Some students take a gap year. These students often feel more motivated. (use a defining relative clause)
  3. Traffic is increasing. The government may introduce a congestion charge. (use a first conditional)
✅ Answer (models — other correct versions exist)

1. Although online learning is convenient, it lacks face-to-face interaction. (or: While it is convenient, online learning lacks…)
2. Students who take a gap year often feel more motivated.
3. If traffic keeps increasing, the government may introduce a congestion charge.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Correct the high-frequency errors

Objective: Spot and fix articles, agreement, and prepositions. There are five errors below.

In many countries, the number of overweight children are rising. This is largely caused by increase of fast food and a lack of exercise. Government should provide more informations about healthy eating, and schools must take responsibility of physical education. Such measures would have positive affect on public health.

✅ Answer

1. are → is ("the number of… is").
2. increase of → an increase in (article + correct preposition).
3. informations → information (uncountable).
4. responsibility of → responsibility for.
5. positive affect → a positive effect (noun form + article).
Corrected: …the number of overweight children is rising. This is largely caused by an increase in fast food… provide more information… take responsibility for physical education. Such measures would have a positive effect on public health.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Choose the right tense

Objective: Match tense to the task context.

  1. Task 1, a chart labelled 1980–2010: "Car ownership (rise) steadily over the period."
  2. Task 1, a diagram of how paper is made: "First, the wood (chop) into small chips."
  3. Task 2, a general opinion: "Education (be) the key to reducing poverty."
  4. Task 2, a hypothetical: "If public transport (be) free, fewer people (drive)."
✅ Answer

1. rose (past simple — the period has ended).
2. is chopped (present simple passive — describing a process).
3. is (present simple — a general truth/opinion).
4. were … would drive (second conditional — a hypothetical situation).

🎯 Your Task: Upgrade & Correct (25 min)

Two halves — one for range, one for accuracy. Work to the clock.

  1. (10 min) Upgrade for range. Take this simple, error-free paragraph and rewrite it using at least four complex structures (subordination, relative, conditional, comparative, noun clause) — without introducing any error:
    "Cities are growing. Many people move there for work. Housing is expensive. Some people cannot afford a home. Governments should act."
  2. (10 min) Error-correct. Find and fix every error in this flawed paragraph (there are at least seven):
    "Nowadays, technology have changed how we work. The number of remote workers are growing every year, this give people more freedom. However, it also effect team communication. Company should provide better tools and more informations, so employees can stay connected. If this trend will continue, offices may disappear."
  3. (5 min) Reflect. Count the complex structures in your upgraded paragraph and the errors you fixed. Which was harder — adding range, or catching errors? That tells you your priority.

✅ Task Completion Checklist

  • ☐ My upgraded paragraph uses at least four distinct complex structures
  • ☐ I introduced no new errors while adding range (re-read to confirm)
  • ☐ I fixed the agreement, article, preposition, word-form, run-on, and uncountable errors
  • ☐ I corrected the conditional error (if this trend continues, not will continue)
  • ☐ I decided whether range or accuracy is my priority to drill next
✅ Sample answers (models — judge yours against the descriptors)

1. Upgraded for range: As cities grow, many people move to them in search of work, which drives up the cost of housing. Because homes have become so expensive, some residents can no longer afford one — a problem that governments will have to address if the trend continues. (Structures: subordination as/because, relative which/that, comparative so expensive, first conditional if…continues.)

2. Error-corrected: Nowadays, technology has changed how we work. The number of remote workers is growing every year, which gives people more freedom. However, it also affects team communication. Companies should provide better tools and more information, so employees can stay connected. If this trend continues, offices may disappear. (Fixes: have→has, are→is, comma splice→, which, effect→affects, Company→Companies [or add A], informations→information, will continue→continues.)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Vary your sentence openings — start some with a subordinate clause (Although…), a participle (Having…), or a noun clause (What is clear…). Range is partly visible in how sentences begin.
  • Leave two minutes to proofread specifically for the band-limiting seven: articles, agreement, prepositions, run-ons, uncountables, word form, and tense. A dedicated error-hunt catches more than a general re-read.
  • Drill a reliable handful of complex frames until they're automatic and error-free — that is enough range for Band 7.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • Chasing range at the cost of control: a broken complex sentence scores worse than a correct simple one — and can blur your meaning.
  • Ignoring the "small" errors: articles and prepositions feel minor, but they are so frequent that they cap accuracy across a whole script.
  • Using one structure on repeat: ten sentences starting "There are many…" shows control but no range — the descriptor asks for variety.
  • Wrong Task 1 tense: describing past data in the present simple repeats the same error in every sentence.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • GRA rewards both range and accuracy — variety of complex structures and the control to keep them error-free.
  • Build a toolkit: subordination, relative clauses, conditionals, participle and noun clauses, comparatives — matched to the task.
  • Choose the right tense: present simple for opinions, past/perfect/future for Task 1 trends, present passive for processes, conditionals for hypotheticals.
  • Hunt the band-limiting seven: articles, agreement, prepositions, run-ons, uncountables, word form, and structure repetition.
  • When in doubt, accuracy beats ambition — master a reliable handful of forms rather than reaching for structures you can't control.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How many complex sentences do I actually need for Band 7?

There's no magic count. The descriptor asks for a variety of complex structures used with good control. A useful rule of thumb: most of your sentences should be complex (not a string of short simple ones), drawing on several different forms, with frequent error-free sentences. Quality and variety matter more than sheer number.

Are articles really that important? They feel tiny.

Yes — precisely because they're everywhere. A single article slip is minor, but article errors recur in almost every sentence for many candidates, so they weigh heavily on the accuracy mark across a whole script. Fixing your article habit is often the single highest-value accuracy gain available.

Will one big grammar mistake ruin my band?

No — examiners score the overall pattern, not one slip. The question is frequency: occasional errors are fine at Band 7; frequent errors that strain the reader pull you toward Band 5–6. Aim for frequent error-free sentences, not perfection.
