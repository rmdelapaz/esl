🎯 Lesson 2.3: Adverbs of Frequency & Manner

How often do you do things? And how do you do them? In this lesson you'll learn two groups of adverbs: frequency adverbs (always, usually, sometimes, never) tell us how often, and manner adverbs (quickly, well, carefully) tell us how. Together, they help you describe your real habits in English.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use adverbs of frequency and put them in the correct position in a sentence
  • Ask and answer "How often…?" with expressions like once a week and every day
  • Form adverbs of manner from adjectives (adjective + -ly) and spell them correctly
  • Use common irregular adverbs — especially well, fast, and hard

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe your habits and how you do things — speak (or record) 6+ sentences using both frequency adverbs and manner adverbs.

How Often & How

Imagine you're telling a new friend about your daily life. You want to say two kinds of things. First, how often something happens — "I always drink coffee in the morning". Second, how you do it — "I speak English slowly, but I speak it clearly."

Both of these small describing words are called adverbs. An adverb tells us more about a verb (an action). In this lesson we meet the two most useful groups for everyday talk: adverbs of frequency (how often) and adverbs of manner (how). Learn where to put them, and your English will sound much more natural. 🌟

🧠 Mindset

You already know many adjectives — quick, careful, quiet, happy. Here's the good news: most of them turn into adverbs with just one small ending: -ly. You don't learn a whole new list. You use words you already know and give them a new job. If a form feels hard, remember: you don't have it wrong — you just don't have it yet. 🌱

Adverbs of Frequency

Adverbs of frequency tell us how often something happens. Think of them as a line from 100% (all the time) down to 0% (never).

AdverbRoughly how oftenExample
always~100%I always brush my teeth.
usually~90%She usually walks to work.
often~70%We often eat out on Fridays.
sometimes~50%They sometimes play football.
hardly ever~10%He hardly ever watches TV.
never~0%I never drink coffee at night.
 
graph LR
    A["100% always"] --> B["usually"]
    B --> C["often"]
    C --> D["sometimes"]
    D --> E["hardly ever"]
    E --> F["0% never"]

⚠️ Watch Out: "never" is already negative

Because never means "0% of the time," we don't add another not. Say "I never eat meat." ✅ — never "I don't never eat meat." ❌ One negative is enough in English.

Where Frequency Adverbs Go (the KEY rule)

This is the most important part of the lesson. There is one simple rule with two parts:

Sentence has…Adverb goes…Example
a main verb (drink, eat, work)BEFORE the verbI always drink coffee.
the verb to be (am, is, are, was, were)AFTER the verbI am always tired.

⚠️ The One Rule to Remember

Frequency adverb BEFORE the main verb, but AFTER the verb to be.

  • ✅ She usually gets up early. (before "gets" — main verb)
  • ✅ She is usually late. (after "is" — verb to be)
  • ❌ She gets up usually early.   ❌ She usually is late.

If there's a helper verb like can or do, the frequency adverb goes right after the first helper: "I can never remember his name." / "Do you usually walk?"

💡 A flexible bonus

Sometimes, usually and often can also start a sentence to make them stronger: "Sometimes I work from home." "Usually we have dinner at seven." But always and never stay in the middle — we don't say "Never I eat meat." ❌

Asking "How often…?"

To ask about frequency, use How often…? The answer can be a frequency adverb, or a more exact phrase with numbers.

A: How often do you exercise?

B: Three times a week. And you?

A: I hardly ever exercise — maybe once a month!

To say how many times in a period, use these expressions with a / every:

Number word+ a + periodFull example
once (1×)once a weekI clean my room once a week.
twice (2×)twice a monthWe meet twice a month.
three times (3×)three times a dayShe takes the pills three times a day.
everyevery day / weekHe checks email every morning.

⚠️ once & twice — not "one time / two times"

For 1 and 2, English prefers once and twice: "once a day," "twice a week." From 3 up, use the number + times: three times, four times, ten times a week. We don't usually say "one time a day" or "two times a day."

Unlike frequency adverbs, these longer expressions normally go at the end of the sentence: "I go to the gym twice a week."

Adverbs of Manner (how you do something)

An adverb of manner tells us how an action happens — quickly, slowly, carefully. Most of the time you make one by adding -ly to an adjective.

Adjective→ AdverbExample
quickquicklyHe eats quickly.
slowslowlyPlease speak slowly.
carefulcarefullyShe drives carefully.
quietquietlyThe baby is sleeping — walk quietly.
badbadlyI sing badly!

A few adjectives change their spelling before -ly. Here are the two you'll meet most:

Spelling ruleAdjective→ Adverb
ends in -y → change y to -ilyeasyeasily
ends in -y → -ilyhappyhappily
ends in -le → change to -lysimplesimply
ends in -le → -lyterribleterribly

💡 Position of manner adverbs

Manner adverbs usually go after the verb, or after the verb + object. So we say "He speaks English well" (after the object "English"), not "He speaks well English." And "She drives carefully." Never put the manner adverb between the verb and its object.

Irregular Adverbs: well, fast, hard

A small group of adverbs don't follow the -ly pattern. The most important one is well — the adverb of good.

AdjectiveAdverbNote
goodwellcompletely different word
fastfastsame form (no -ly)
hardhardsame form (no -ly)

⚠️ "good" vs "well" — the big one

good is an adjective — it describes a noun. well is an adverb — it describes a verb (how you do it).

  • ✅ She is a good singer. (good + noun "singer")
  • ✅ She sings well. (well describes the verb "sings")
  • ❌ She sings good.   ❌ She is a well singer.

Be careful with fast and hard: the adverb looks exactly like the adjective — "a fast car" (adjective) and "he runs fast" (adverb). There is no "fastly." ❌ Also, hardly is a real word, but it means "almost not" ("I hardly ever go out"). It is not the adverb of hard. To say you work with a lot of effort, say "I work hard." ✅

My brother works hard and learns fast. He speaks three languages well, and he always explains things simply and patiently.

Phrase Bank & Dialogue

Keep these ready-made phrases for talking about routines and habits:

How often do you…? I usually… I hardly ever… once a week twice a month every day I never… She does it well.

Now listen to two friends comparing their weekly habits:

A: How often do you cook at home?

B: Almost every day. I usually cook quickly after work — but on Sundays I cook slowly and enjoy it. What about you?

A: I hardly ever cook, to be honest. I'm a bad cook! I order food twice a week.

B: Come on, you're not that bad. Do you exercise?

A: Yes! I go running three times a week. I run quite fast, but I don't swim well at all.

B: Same here — I always start well and then get tired. We should train together sometimes!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Put the frequency adverb in the right place

Objective: Rewrite each sentence with the adverb in brackets in the correct position.

  1. I drink tea in the morning. (always)
  2. She is late for class. (never)  ⚠️ this one has the verb to be!
  3. We go to the cinema. (sometimes)
  4. He checks his phone. (often)
  5. They are tired on Mondays. (usually)
✅ Answer

1. I always drink tea in the morning. (before main verb)
2. She is never late for class. (after "is" — verb to be)
3. We sometimes go to the cinema. (or: Sometimes we go…)
4. He often checks his phone.
5. They are usually tired on Mondays. (after "are")

🏋️ Exercise 2: Make the adverb of manner (spelling drill)

Objective: Change each adjective into its adverb.

  1. quick →
  2. easy →
  3. good →
  4. careful →
  5. hard →
✅ Answer

1. quickly · 2. easily (y → ily) · 3. well (irregular!) · 4. carefully · 5. hard (same form — no "hardly" for effort)

🏋️ Exercise 3: good or well?

Objective: Choose the correct word.

  1. She is a teacher. (good / well)
  2. He plays the guitar . (good / well)
  3. They cook very . (good / well)
  4. That's a idea! (good / well)
✅ Answer

1. good (before the noun "teacher") · 2. well (describes "plays") · 3. well (describes "cook") · 4. good (before "idea")

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Put frequency adverbs before the main verb but after to be: "I usually walk" / "I am usually tired."
  • Make manner adverbs with -ly, and remember the spelling changes: easy → easily, simple → simply.
  • Use once and twice for 1 and 2 times, then three times, four times…

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "She sings good." ❌ → "She sings well." ✅ (after a verb, use the adverb well).
  • "I don't never…" ❌ → "I never…" ✅ (never is already negative).
  • "He runs fastly." ❌ → "He runs fast." ✅ (fast and hard don't take -ly).
  • "Never I eat meat." ❌ → "I never eat meat." ✅ (always/never stay in the middle).

🌍 Register note

In very casual speech, you'll sometimes hear people say "It's going good." It's common, but it's not standard. In class, at work, and in writing, use well with verbs: "It's going well." ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Frequency adverbs run from always (100%) to never (0%): always, usually, often, sometimes, hardly ever, never.
  • Position: before the main verb, but after the verb to be ("I always drink" / "I am always tired").
  • Answer "How often…?" with adverbs or number expressions: once a week, twice a month, every day.
  • Manner adverbs = adjective + -ly (quick→quickly; easy→easily; simple→simply). Irregular: good→well, fast→fast, hard→hard.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now describe not just what you do, but how often and how well you do it. That's a big step forward — it's the difference between "I cook" and "I usually cook quickly, but I don't bake very well." Adverbs make your English start to sound like you. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is it "good" or "well"?

Use good before a noun ("a good film") and well after a verb to say how you do something ("She writes well"). One exception you'll meet later: after feeling verbs like look/feel, good is fine — "I feel good."

Can I start a sentence with a frequency adverb?

With sometimes, usually, and often, yes — to make them stronger ("Sometimes I stay late"). But keep always and never in the middle of the sentence.

What's the difference between "hard" and "hardly"?

They're not related in meaning! Hard = with a lot of effort ("I work hard"). Hardly = almost not ("I hardly ate anything," "I hardly ever go out"). Don't use "hardly" to mean effort.
