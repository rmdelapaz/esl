🤝 Lesson 3.3: Future Arrangements & Making Suggestions

You already know the present continuous for what's happening right now — "I'm reading." Now you'll use that same tense for a very useful new job: talking about fixed plans in the future. Then you'll learn the everyday phrases that make social life happen — how to suggest an activity, invite a friend, and say yes or a polite no.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the present continuous for future arrangements (with a time or place)
  • Make and respond to suggestions (Let's…, Why don't we…, How about…?, Shall we…?)
  • Make, accept, and politely decline invitations
  • Talk about your diary and schedule for the days ahead

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Role-play making plans with a friend — suggest an activity, agree on a day and time, and confirm the arrangement. (Solo option: write and say both sides of the conversation.)

Plans, Not Predictions

In the last lesson you used will and might to talk about the future — predictions, decisions, and maybe's. But there's another, very natural way English speakers talk about the future, and it uses a tense you already know: the present continuous (am/is/are + verb-ing).

We use it for fixed arrangements — plans that are already decided, usually with another person, usually with a time or place set. Think of your calendar or diary: "I'm meeting Sara on Friday." "We're leaving at six." These aren't guesses about the future — they're things you've already agreed. 📅

🧠 Mindset

Here's the good news: you're not learning a new tense today — you're giving a tense you already own a second job. The shape (I'm meeting, we're going) is exactly the same as A1; only the meaning shifts into the future, and a small time word does the pointing. You already have 90% of this. Let's add the last 10%. 🌱

Present Continuous for the Future

To talk about a fixed future arrangement, use the same form you know: am / is / are + verb-ing. Add a future time (or a place) so your listener knows you mean the future, not now.

PersonFormExample (with a future time)
Iam (I'm) + -ingI'm meeting Sara on Friday.
he / she / itis (he's) + -ingShe's starting her new job next week.
you / we / theyare (we're) + -ingWe're leaving at 6 o'clock.
negativeam/is/are + not + -ingI'm not working tomorrow.
questionAm/Is/Are + subject + -ing?Are you doing anything tonight?

Use it when the plan is fixed — you've decided the day, the time, or the people. It often answers "What are you doing this weekend?"

I'm seeing the dentist on Monday. We're having dinner with Tom on Saturday. My sister's flying to Rome next month. Are you coming to the party?

💡 Present continuous vs going to

Both talk about the future, and often either is fine. The small difference: the present continuous feels like a fixed arrangement in your diary — a specific plan, usually with a time and often with other people ("I'm meeting Sara at 7"). Going to is more about an intention or plan in your head ("I'm going to learn to cook") — it doesn't need a set time. Don't worry about the border too much; for definite plans with a time, the present continuous sounds very natural. 👍

Now or Future? The Time Word Decides

The same sentence can mean now or the future — the time expression tells your listener which one you mean.

SentenceTime wordMeaning
I'm working now.nowHappening at this moment (present)
I'm working tomorrow.tomorrowA fixed plan (future)
She's cooking at the moment.at the momentRight now (present)
She's cooking for us on Sunday.on SundayAn arrangement (future)
⚠️ Important Note: Without a time word, context decides. If it's not clear, add a future time expression — tomorrow, tonight, this evening, next week, on Friday, at 8 — so no one thinks you mean right now. "I'm meeting Sara" is unclear; "I'm meeting Sara at 5" is a clear future plan.

Making Suggestions

Once you can talk about plans, the next step is making them. Here are the four everyday patterns English speakers use to suggest an activity. Notice which ones take a base verb and which one takes -ing.

PatternFollowed byExample
Let's …base verbLet's go to the cinema.
Why don't we …?base verbWhy don't we eat out?
How about …?-ing verb / nounHow about watching a film? / How about Saturday?
Shall we …?base verbShall we meet at 7?

✅ Pro Tip

Three of these (Let's, Why don't we, Shall we) are followed by the plain base verb — no to, no -ing: Let's go, Shall we meet. Only How about takes -ing (or a noun): How about going? / How about Saturday? Remember: "How about" → add the "-ing".

Let's is short for let us — you use it to suggest doing something together (you and the listener). The negative is Let's not: "Let's not stay late."

    ING["+ -ing / noun"]
    L["Let's"] --> BASE
    W["Why don't we...?"] --> BASE
    S["Shall we...?"] --> BASE
    H["How about...?"] --> ING

Saying Yes & Saying No

A suggestion needs a reply. Here's a phrase bank for accepting warmly and declining politely. In English, a soft "no" almost always comes with a short reason or a friendly "another time" — a bare "No" can sound rude.

👍 Accepting:

Good idea! Sure, that sounds great. OK, let's do it. Yes, I'd love to. Perfect, see you then!

🙏 Declining politely:

Sorry, I can't. I'd love to, but I'm busy. Maybe another time. Thanks, but I already have plans. That's kind, but I can't make it.

💡 Register note

Starting a refusal with "I'd love to, but…" shows you want to say yes — it keeps the friendship warm even when the answer is no. Adding "Maybe another time" leaves the door open for the future. These little softeners are how polite English works. 🌷

Invitations

An invitation is a special kind of suggestion — you're inviting someone to something. The classic polite pattern is Would you like to + base verb?

InvitationAcceptDecline (politely)
Would you like to come to dinner?Yes, I'd love to!Thanks, but I'm busy tonight.
Would you like to join us on Saturday?That'd be lovely, thanks.I'd love to, but I can't make it.
Do you want to grab a coffee?Sure, sounds good!Maybe another time — I'm tired.

💡 Formal vs friendly

Would you like to…? is polite and works everywhere. Do you want to…? is a little more casual, great between friends. Both take a base verb: Would you like to come? / Do you want to go?

Putting It Together

Here's a full conversation. Watch how the friends suggest, respond, agree a day and time, and finally confirm the arrangement with the present continuous.

Mia: Are you doing anything this weekend?

Ben: Not really. Why?

Mia: How about seeing a film? There's a new comedy on.

Ben: Good idea! Shall we go on Saturday?

Mia: Saturday's tricky — I'm meeting my sister in the afternoon. Why don't we go on Sunday instead?

Ben: Sure, that sounds great. What time?

Mia: Let's meet at 6, and we can eat first.

Ben: Perfect. So we're meeting at 6 on Sunday. See you then!

✅ Notice the moves

  • Suggestion: "How about seeing…?" · "Shall we go…?" · "Why don't we…?" · "Let's meet…"
  • Response: "Good idea!" · "Sure, that sounds great."
  • A reason to change the plan: "I'm meeting my sister" (a fixed future arrangement).
  • Confirm: "So we're meeting at 6 on Sunday." — present continuous seals the plan. 🔒

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Future arrangements (gap-fill)

Objective: Use the present continuous for the future. Make a sentence from the prompt.

Example: (I / see / the dentist / on Monday) → I'm seeing the dentist on Monday.

  1. (We / leave / at 6 o'clock) → We at 6 o'clock.
  2. (She / start / her new job / next week) → She her new job next week.
  3. (I / not work / tomorrow) → I tomorrow.
  4. (you / do / anything / tonight?) → anything tonight?
  5. (They / fly / to Rome / on Friday) → They to Rome on Friday.
✅ Answer

1. 're leaving (are leaving) · 2. 's starting (is starting) · 3. 'm not working (am not working) · 4. Are you doing · 5. 're flying (are flying)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Suggest it three ways

Objective: Rewrite the idea as a suggestion using Let's, Why don't we, and How about. Watch the verb form!

Idea: go to the beach

  1. Let's
  2. Why don't we
  3. How about
✅ Answer

1. Let's go to the beach. (base verb)
2. Why don't we go to the beach? (base verb)
3. How about going to the beach? (-ing) — remember, "How about" takes the "-ing" form.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Match the response

Objective: Match each invitation (1–4) to the best reply (a–d). One reply accepts, the others decline politely.

Invitations:

  1. Would you like to come to dinner tonight?
  2. Shall we play tennis on Saturday?
  3. Do you want to grab a coffee now?
  4. Let's go dancing on Friday!

Replies:

  • a. Yes, I'd love to — what time?
  • b. Sorry, I can't. I'm working late tonight.
  • c. I'd love to, but I'm playing football on Saturday.
  • d. Maybe another time — I'm a bit busy right now.
✅ Answer

1 → b (declines: a reason for tonight) · 2 → c (declines: already has a Saturday plan) · 3 → d (declines: busy right now) · 4 → a (accepts, asks the time).
Note: other matches can work too — the key is that each reply either accepts warmly or declines with a polite reason.

🎯 Your Project: Make Plans With a Friend

Role-play arranging to meet. Do it with a partner or in class; if you're studying alone, write and say both sides (Person A and Person B) and record yourself.

  1. (3 min) Suggest an activity two different ways (e.g. "How about…?" and "Why don't we…?").
  2. (3 min) Respond: accept one idea warmly, or decline politely and suggest a change ("I'd love to, but… Let's… instead.").
  3. (3 min) Agree a day and time — use Shall we…? and a real time ("Shall we meet at 7?").
  4. (2 min) Confirm the plan with the present continuous: "So we're meeting at 7 on Friday. See you then!"

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ I made a suggestion in at least two ways (Let's / Why don't we / How about / Shall we)
  • ☐ I accepted OR politely declined with a reason
  • ☐ I agreed a specific day and time
  • ☐ I confirmed the plan using the present continuous (e.g. "We're meeting at…")

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Add a future time word (tonight, on Friday, at 7) to make a present-continuous sentence point to the future.
  • Use the base verb after Let's / Why don't we / Shall we, and -ing after How about.
  • Soften a "no": start with "I'd love to, but…" and add "Maybe another time."

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "Let's to go…" ❌ → "Let's go…" ✅ (no to after Let's).
  • "How about go…?" ❌ → "How about going…?" ✅ (How about takes -ing).
  • "I meet Sara tomorrow." (for a plan) ❌ → "I'm meeting Sara tomorrow." ✅ (use the present continuous for a fixed arrangement).
  • Don't just say "No." to an invitation ❌ — add a reason: "Sorry, I can't — I'm busy." ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • The present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) talks about fixed future arrangements: "I'm meeting Sara on Friday."
  • A future time word shows it means the future, not now — tomorrow, tonight, at 7.
  • Suggest with Let's / Why don't we / Shall we + base verb and How about + -ing.
  • Accept warmly ("Good idea!") or decline politely with a reason ("I'd love to, but…").
  • Invite with Would you like to + base verb? → "Yes, I'd love to."

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now do something that real friendships run on: make plans. You can put an arrangement in your diary and say it out loud, suggest an activity four different ways, invite someone, and say yes or a graceful no. That's the language of a whole social weekend — and you've got it. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the difference between "I'm meeting Sara" and "I'm going to meet Sara"?

Very little in everyday use! The present continuous ("I'm meeting Sara at 5") sounds like a fixed appointment in your diary. "Going to" can be a looser intention. For a definite plan with a time, the present continuous is natural and common.

Is "Shall we…?" old-fashioned?

Not at all for suggestions! "Shall we meet at 7?" and "Shall we go?" are alive and well, especially in British English. It's a friendly, polite way to suggest doing something together.

Can I say "Let's not"?

Yes — the negative of Let's is Let's not: "Let's not stay too late." It's a gentle way to suggest not doing something.
