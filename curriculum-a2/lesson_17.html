🔗 Lesson 6.2: Relative Clauses — who, which, that, where

Which man? The man who lives next door. Which book? The book that I bought. Which café? The café where we met. Relative clauses let you add exactly the right detail — and join two short sentences into one smooth sentence. This is how your English starts to flow. 🌊

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use who for people, which for things, that for both, and where for places
  • Add information to a noun with a defining relative clause
  • Join two short sentences into one longer, natural sentence
  • Know when you can leave out who / which / that

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe three people, places, or things in your life using relative clauses — 6+ sentences, spoken to a partner or recorded on your phone.

Adding the Right Detail

Imagine you say, "I saw a man." Your friend asks, "Which man?" You answer: "The man who lives next door." That little group of words — who lives next door — is a relative clause. It tells us exactly which man you mean.

Relative clauses answer the question "Which one?" They point to the exact person, thing, or place. Without them, we would need lots of short, separate sentences. With them, we join ideas into one clear sentence. 🔑

🧠 Mindset

You already describe things with adjectives — a tall man, a red book. A relative clause is just a longer, sentence-shaped description: a man who is tall, a book that is red. If it feels like a lot at first, remember: you're not learning a new idea, only a new way to add detail you already know how to give. 🌱

who, which, that, where

A relative clause usually starts with a special linking word called a relative pronoun. Which one you choose depends on what kind of noun you are describing:

WordUse forExample
whopeoplethe man who lives next door
whichthings & animalsthe book which I bought
thatpeople or things (informal)the film that we watched
whereplacesthe café where we met
 
graph LR
    N["a noun"] --> P["people: who"]
    N --> T["things: which"]
    N --> B["both: that"]
    N --> PL["places: where"]

the doctor who helped me · the phone which broke · the song that I like · the shop where I work

💡 A quick memory hook

Who has an h, like human — it's for people. Which is for things. Where has the word here/there hidden in it — it's for places. And that is the easy one: it works for both people and things in informal English.

Joining Two Sentences

The real power of relative clauses is joining. Look at these two short sentences:

I know a woman. + She speaks five languages.

The word She and the word woman mean the same person. So we can replace She with who and join the sentences:

I know a woman who speaks five languages.

Here is the same trick with things and places:

Two short sentencesJoined with a relative clause
This is the book. It changed my life.This is the book which changed my life.
I met a man. He works at the bank.I met a man who works at the bank.
That's the café. We met there.That's the café where we met.

⚠️ Watch Out: don't repeat the subject

When the relative pronoun replaces the subject, don't say the pronoun and the word. ✅ "the woman who speaks…" · ❌ "the woman who she speaks…" The word who already does the job of she, so you only need one.

that for People & Things

In everyday, informal English, that is a friendly all-rounder. It can replace who (for people) and which (for things):

With who / whichWith that (informal)
the man who calledthe man that called
the bag which I lostthe bag that I lost
the people who live herethe people that live here

💡 One small exception

that works for people and things, but not for places. For places, keep where: ✅ "the town where I grew up." In speaking and everyday writing, many people prefer that for people and things because it's short and easy. Both are correct.

⚠️ Important Note: This lesson teaches defining relative clauses — the ones that tell us which one, with no commas. You'll meet a second kind (with commas) later, at B1. For now, keep it comma-free: "the man who lives next door" — no comma.

When You Can Drop the Pronoun

Sometimes you can leave out who / which / that completely — and the sentence still sounds perfect. This happens when the relative pronoun is the object (there's a new subject right after it).

Full sentenceShorter (pronoun dropped)
the book that I boughtthe book I bought
the film which we watchedthe film we watched
the person who you metthe person you met

How can you tell? Look at the word right after the pronoun. If it's a new subject (I, we, you, she…), you can drop the pronoun. If it's a verb, you must keep it:

Keep it: the man who lives here (verb after — keep who). · Drop it: the man (who) I met here (new subject after — you can drop it).

✅ Good News

This is optional. You never have to drop the pronoun — keeping it is always correct. It's just useful to recognise, because you'll hear native speakers do it all the time: "That's the song I love," "Here's the photo I took." 🎶

Describing People & Places

Relative clauses make it easy to describe the people and places in your life. Here's a phrase bank to get you started:

a person who … the friend who … a thing which … the app that … the place where … a city where …

Now let's hear two friends describe people and places they know:

A: Who's the woman who waved at you?

B: That's Nadia, the neighbour who helped me move house. She's the person who knows everyone here.

A: Nice. And is this the café that you told me about?

B: Yes! This is the place where I study every morning. They make a coffee that I really love.

A: Is that the book you were reading last week?

B: It is. It's a story which starts slowly, but the ending is amazing. 📖

💡 Did you spot it?

In "the book you were reading," B dropped that — because a new subject (you) comes right after. But in "the person who knows everyone," B kept who — because a verb (knows) comes right after. Same rule you just learned! 🎉

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Match the pronoun to the noun: who (people), which (things), where (places).
  • Use that for people or things when you want a short, informal word.
  • Put the relative clause right after the noun it describes.
  • Keep defining clauses comma-free (they tell us which one).

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "the man which lives here" ❌ → "the man who lives here" ✅ (use who for people).
  • "the woman who she works here" ❌ → "the woman who works here" ✅ (don't repeat the subject).
  • "the town that I grew up" ❌ → "the town where I grew up" ✅ (use where for places).
  • "the book what I bought" ❌ → "the book that I bought" ✅ (never use what as a relative pronoun).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • who = people · which = things & animals · where = places.
  • that can replace who or which in informal English (but not where).
  • Relative clauses join two short sentences into one and answer "Which one?"
  • Don't repeat the subject: "the woman who works…" — not "who she works".
  • You can drop who/which/that when a new subject follows: "the book (that) I bought."

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You just learned to add exactly the right detail and to join ideas smoothly. Instead of lots of short, choppy sentences, you can now say "That's the friend who lives in the flat where I stayed." This is a big step toward fluent, natural English — the kind that flows. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I use "who" or "that" for people?

Both are correct. who is a little more careful and common in writing; that is very common in everyday speech: "the man who called" = "the man that called." Use whichever feels natural — you can't go wrong.

Why is "where" different from "which"?

where already means "in/at that place", so you don't need an extra word: "the café where we met." If you used which, you'd need a preposition: "the café which we met in." At A2, where is the simple, natural choice for places.

Do I always have to include who/which/that?

Not always. When a new subject comes right after (I, you, she…), you can leave the pronoun out: "the film (that) we watched," "the person (who) you met." But keeping it is never wrong, so include it whenever you're unsure.
