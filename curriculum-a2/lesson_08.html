🔮 Lesson 3.2: 'Will' & 'Might' — Predictions & Decisions

In the last lesson you learned going to for plans you've already decided. Now we look into the future. Will lets you predict the future, make a decision on the spot, and offer to help. And might is your word for "maybe" — when you're just not sure yet. Let's look ahead! 🔭

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use will / won't + base verb for predictions, instant decisions, offers and promises
  • Use might to talk about a future possibility (when you're not sure)
  • Feel the difference between will and going to (a light contrast)
  • Ask questions with will: "Will you be there?"

Looking Into the Future

English has more than one way to talk about the future, and each one feels a little different. In Lesson 3.1 you met going to — the future you've already planned: "I'm going to visit my aunt on Sunday."

This lesson adds two more tools:

  • will — for what you think will happen (a prediction), for a decision you make right now, and for offers and promises.
  • might — for something that is only possible. You're not sure, so you leave the door open.

Both are modal verbs, and that's great news: they never change their shape. The same will works for I, you, he, she, we, and they. No -s, no to, no problems. 🎉

🧠 Mindset

You can't see the future — and that's exactly why English gives you might. You don't have to be sure to speak! Real speakers guess and predict all the time. If a sentence feels risky, make it softer with might or I think. Being able to say "maybe" in English is a real skill, not a weakness. 🌱

The Form of 'Will'

Here is the pattern in one line: subject + will + base verb. The base verb is the plain form — go, rain, help, be — with no -s and no to. And it's the same for every person.

Subjectwill + base verbContraction ('ll)
II will goI'll go
youyou will goyou'll go
he / she / itshe will goshe'll go
wewe will gowe'll go
theythey will gothey'll go

The negative is won't ( = will not) — again, the same for everyone:

PositiveNegative (won't)
It will rain tomorrow.It won't rain tomorrow.
They will be late.They won't be late.
I will forget.I won't forget.

✅ Pro Tip

After will, always use the base verb. Say "She will come," never "She will comes" or "She will to come." Modal verbs like will keep the next verb plain and simple.

Read these aloud to feel the natural 'll in speech:

I think it 'll be sunny tomorrow. You 'll love the film. We 'll be there at eight. Don't worry — I won't forget.

Three Uses of 'Will'

Why do we choose will? There are three everyday reasons. Learn them as three small stories.

UseMeaningExamples
1. Prediction / opinion What you think will happen in the future I think it will rain later.
You 'll love this restaurant.
He won't pass the exam.
2. Instant decision A decision made at the moment of speaking I'm tired — I 'll go to bed.
The phone's ringing. I 'll get it!
OK, I 'll have the pizza.
3. Offer / promise Offering to help, or promising to do something That bag looks heavy — I 'll carry it.
I 'll call you tomorrow, I promise.
Don't worry, we 'll help you.

💡 Notice the "right now" feeling

Uses 2 and 3 both happen in the moment. You didn't plan to answer the phone — you decide as it rings. That "I decide now" feeling is the heart of will. If a plan already existed before you spoke, you'd use going to instead (more on that below).

Here's a short scene where all three uses appear naturally:

A: The film starts in ten minutes and I can't find my phone!

B: Don't panic — I'll help you look for it.

A: Thanks. Do you think we'll be late?

B: No, I think it'll be fine. There it is! I'll get it for you.

A: You're a lifesaver. I'll buy the popcorn, then.

B: Deal! I might get a lemonade too — I'm not sure yet.

'Might' — Maybe, Not Sure

Use might + base verb when something is possible but not certain. It's your polite, easy way to say "maybe." Like will, it's the same for every person and takes the base verb.

FormExampleIdea
subject + might + base verbI might go to the party.Maybe yes, maybe no — I'm not sure.
same for all personsShe might be late.It's possible she'll be late.
negative: might notWe might not come.Maybe we won't come.

✅ Pro Tip

Might works just like will: no -s, no to. Say "He might come," not "He might comes" or "He might to come." For the negative we usually keep the full form might not (we don't often contract it in everyday speech).

Compare how sure the speaker is:

SentenceHow sure?
It will rain tomorrow.Sure — a confident prediction. ☔
It might rain tomorrow.Not sure — it's just possible. 🌤️
It won't rain tomorrow.Sure it won't. ☀️

Say these possibilities out loud — notice how relaxed and honest might sounds:

I might visit my cousin this weekend — I haven't decided. We might not have time. She might call later, or she might text. Who knows? 🤷

Questions with 'Will'

To make a question, put will at the front, before the subject: Will + subject + base verb? Short answers use just will or won't.

QuestionShort answer (yes)Short answer (no)
Will you be there?Yes, I will.No, I won't.
Will it work?Yes, it will.No, it won't.
Will they come?Yes, they will.No, they won't.

For more detail, add a Wh- word before will:

What will you do? Where will you go? When will they arrive? Who will help us? How will you get there?
⚠️ Important Note: In a short answer, don't shorten the yes form. Say "Yes, I will." — never "Yes, I'll." But the negative is contracted: "No, I won't."

'Will' vs 'Going To'

Learners often ask: "When do I use will, and when do I use going to?" Keep it simple with this one idea.

💡 The quick rule

  • going to = a plan you already decided before now (or clear evidence you can see): "I'm going to paint my room this weekend." (I decided yesterday.) · "Look at those clouds — it's going to rain." (evidence)
  • will = a prediction or a decision made at the moment of speaking: "I think it 'll be a good year." (opinion) · "You've dropped your pen — I'll pick it up." (deciding now)

Don't worry too much about this — in everyday talk both are often fine. Just remember: plan → going to, prediction or on-the-spot decision → will.

Practice & Project

Before the exercises, here's a phrase bank of useful words for predictions — add them to your sentences:

I think… I'm sure… probably maybe in the future one day

🏋️ Exercise 1: will or won't?

Objective: Fill the gap with will or won't to match the meaning.

  1. Don't worry, I forget your birthday. (promise — I'm sure I won't)
  2. I think our team win — they're the best! (positive prediction)
  3. It's very cold, so I'm sure it be sunny tomorrow. (negative prediction)
  4. That's heavy — I carry it for you. (offer)
  5. He's always late, so he probably be on time. (negative prediction)
✅ Answer

1. won't · 2. will · 3. won't · 4. will · 5. won't

🏋️ Exercise 2: will (decision/offer) or going to (plan)?

Objective: Read each situation and choose will or going to.

  1. You already decided last week to visit Rome in July. → "I ___ visit Rome in July."
  2. The phone rings. You decide to answer it now. → "I ___ answer it."
  3. Your friend's bags are heavy. You offer to help right now. → "I ___ help you."
  4. You planned this morning to cook pasta tonight. → "I ___ cook pasta tonight."
  5. You look at the dark sky and predict rain. → "It ___ rain." (on-the-spot prediction)
✅ Answer

1. am going to (a plan already decided) · 2. will ('ll) (instant decision) · 3. will ('ll) (offer) · 4. am going to (a plan already decided) · 5. will ('ll) (prediction at the moment — going to is also acceptable here because of the evidence, but "will" fits an on-the-spot opinion)

🏋️ Exercise 3: make it less certain with 'might'

Objective: Rewrite each sure sentence as a possibility using might.

Example: I will go to the party. → I might go to the party.

  1. We will travel next summer.
  2. She will be at the meeting.
  3. They will move to a new city.
  4. It won't rain. (make it "possibly won't")
✅ Answer

1. We might travel next summer. · 2. She might be at the meeting. · 3. They might move to a new city. · 4. It might not rain.

  1. (3 min) Make 2 confident predictions with will (e.g. "In the future, more people will work from home.").
  2. (3 min) Make 2 predictions with won't (e.g. "I don't think cars won't need drivers… I mean, one day cars won't need drivers.").

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use the base verb after will and might: "She will come," "He might be late."
  • Use 'll and won't when you speak — they sound natural and native.
  • Reach for might when you're honestly not sure — it's better than guessing wrongly with will.
  • Remember: plan → going to, prediction or on-the-spot decision → will.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "She will comes." ❌ → "She will come." ✅ (no -s after a modal).
  • "I will to help." ❌ → "I will help." ✅ (no to after will).
  • Don't drop 'll and won't in speech — they carry the whole future meaning. won't is pronounced /woʊnt/ (rhymes with "don't"), not "wont" like "want".
  • "I might to go." ❌ → "I might go." ✅ (might also takes the base verb).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • will + base verb — same for every person; contraction 'll, negative won't.
  • Use will for predictions, instant decisions, and offers/promises.
  • Use might + base verb for a possibility when you're not sure (negative might not).
  • Questions put will at the front: "Will you be there?" → "Yes, I will / No, I won't."
  • Plan → going to; prediction or on-the-spot decision → will.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is there really no difference between "will" for I, you, he, she, we, they?

Correct — will never changes. That's one of the nice things about modal verbs. The same goes for might. Learn one form and you know them all.

What's the difference between "might" and "maybe … will"?

They mean almost the same thing! "I might come" ≈ "Maybe I'll come." Both show it's possible but not sure. Use whichever feels easy — just don't use both at once in a way that sounds odd.

Do I really have to choose perfectly between "will" and "going to"?

No — in everyday talk, both are often fine, and native speakers mix them freely. Follow the simple rule (plan → going to, prediction/decision → will), and people will understand you every time. Don't let it slow down your speaking.
