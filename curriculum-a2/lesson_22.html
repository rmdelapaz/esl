⌛ Lesson 8.1: Present Perfect — for, since, just, already, yet

In Lesson 4.1 you used the present perfect to talk about your life experiences — "I've been to Spain," "I've never eaten sushi." Now we go deeper. We'll learn how to say how long something has continued (with for and since), and how to add the small but powerful words just, already, and yet. These are the words that connect your past to your right now. ⏳

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use for (a length of time) and since (a start point) correctly
  • Ask and answer "How long have you…?" questions
  • Add just, already, and yet in the right position
  • See when to choose the present perfect and when to choose the past simple

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

A Quick Look Back

Remember the shape of the present perfect: have / has + past participle. "I have lived here." "She has worked here." In A1 and in Lesson 4.1 you used it for experiences — things that happened at some time in your life, with words like ever and never.

Today we use the same tense for a new job: to talk about something that started in the past and is still true now. "I've lived here for five years" means I moved here five years ago and I still live here. The present perfect is a bridge — one end is in the past, the other end is in the present. 🌉

🧠 Mindset

You already know the hardest part — the form. Everything new today is about the little words around it: for, since, just, already, yet. Learn them one at a time. If a sentence still feels tricky, add the magic word: "I can't use yet perfectly… yet." 🌱

for & since

Both for and since answer the question "How long?" The difference is what comes after them.

  • for + a length of time — how long the whole period is.
  • since + a start point — the moment it began.
for + length of timesince + start point
for three yearssince 2020
for two weekssince Monday
for ten minutessince 9 o'clock
for a long timesince I was a child
for six monthssince last summer

"I've lived in this city for five years." · "I've lived in this city since 2020." · "We've known each other for a long time." · "We've known each other since school."

See how they can describe the same situation? Both sentences below are true at the same moment:

    START -.->|since 2020| NOW

💡 A Quick Test

Ask: Is this a piece of time or a point in time? If you can count it (three days, two years, an hour) → use for. If it's a date, a day, or an event that says when it began (Monday, 2020, breakfast) → use since. A handy trick: since often works with a calendar or a clock. 🗓️

How long have you…?

To ask about duration, use How long + have/has + subject + past participle? The answer usually uses for or since.

QuestionAnswer with forAnswer with since
How long have you lived here?For six years.Since 2018.
How long has she had that job?For a few months.Since March.
How long have they been married?For ten years.Since 2014.
How long have you lived here? How long have you studied English? How long have you known him? For about two years. Since last summer.

just & already

Now two words that add feeling to the present perfect. Both go between have/has and the main verb (the past participle).

just — a very short time ago

just means "a moment ago." The action finished only seconds or minutes before now:

"I've just finished my homework." · "She's just arrived." · "We've just had lunch."

already — sooner than expected

already means the action is done — and often earlier than we thought. It is used in positive sentences:

"I've already seen that film." · "He's already eaten." · "They've already left."

💡 Word Order

Put just and already before the main verb: have + just / already + past participle. So it's "I've already eaten," not "I've eaten already" (although native speakers do say that too, in a relaxed way). For A2, the safe place is in the middle. 🎯

graph LR
    H["have / has"] --> J["just / already"]
    J --> V["past participle"]

yet

yet means "up to now." We use it in questions and negatives, and it goes at the end of the sentence.

  • Question: asking if something has happened by now — "Have you finished yet?"
  • Negative: it hasn't happened, but we expect it will — "I haven't eaten yet."

"Have you called her yet?" — "No, I haven't called her yet." · "Has the bus arrived yet?" — "It hasn't arrived yet."

✅ already vs yet

They are a natural pair. Use already when the thing is done (positive): "I've already done it." Use yet when it isn't done, or you're asking (question/negative): "Have you done it yet?" "I haven't done it yet." One says "done!", the other says "not done… so far."

⚠️ Watch Out: where the word goes

just and already go in the middle (before the main verb). yet goes at the end. ✅ "I've just arrived." · ✅ "She's already gone." · ✅ "Have you eaten yet?" · ❌ "I've yet arrived." · ❌ "Have you yet eaten?"

Present Perfect vs Past Simple

You know both tenses now, so here is the key idea in one line: use the present perfect when the time is unfinished or connected to now, and the past simple when the time is finished and clear.

Present perfect (link to now)Past simple (finished time)
I've lived here for five years. (still here)I lived in Paris in 2019. (not now)
I've just eaten. (a moment ago)I ate at seven o'clock. (a finished time)
Have you seen it yet?Did you see it yesterday?

💡 The Time-Word Clue

Finished time words — yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago, at seven o'clock — call for the past simple. "Up to now" words — for, since, just, already, yet, ever, never — call for the present perfect. Let the time word guide you. 🧭

Putting It Together

Here is a small phrase bank to help you start:

I've lived here for… I've known her since… I've just… I've already… Have you … yet? I haven't … yet.

Now let's listen to two friends catching up:

A: Hi! How long have you lived in this neighbourhood?

B: For about three years — since I started my new job. And you?

A: Since last summer. I've just moved into the flat on the corner.

B: Nice! Have you found a good café yet?

A: Not yet. I haven't explored much. Have you had breakfast?

B: I've already eaten, thanks — but I'd love a coffee. There's a lovely place I've known for years.

A: Perfect. Let's go — I haven't tried it yet! ☕

✅ Notice the Mix

Look again at the dialogue: for three years, since last summer, just moved, already eaten, and two uses of yet. All five words in one short chat — that's exactly how real conversation sounds. 🎉

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use for with a length of time (for two weeks) and since with a start point (since Monday).
  • Put just and already before the main verb: "I've just seen it."
  • Put yet at the end, and use it in questions and negatives: "Have you eaten yet?"
  • Use the present perfect for time that reaches "now," and the past simple for a finished time.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "since three years" ❌ → "for three years" ✅ (a length of time takes for).
  • "for Monday" ❌ → "since Monday" ✅ (a start point takes since).
  • "I've eaten yet." ❌ → "I've already eaten." / "I haven't eaten yet." ✅ (yet is for questions/negatives).
  • "I've lived here for five years ago." ❌ → "I've lived here for five years." ✅ (ago goes with the past simple, not the present perfect).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • for + a length of time (for two years); since + a start point (since 2020).
  • Ask duration with How long have/has + subject + past participle?
  • just = a moment ago; already = sooner than expected — both go before the main verb.
  • yet = up to now — used in questions and negatives, at the end of the sentence.
  • Present perfect = link to now; past simple = a finished, clear time.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can I use "for" and "since" with the past simple too?

You can use for with the past simple for a finished period: "I lived in Paris for two years" (but not now). since normally pairs with the present perfect, because it looks from a start point up to now. For A2, keep since with the present perfect.

What's the difference between "already" and "yet"?

already says the thing is done (positive): "I've already paid." yet is for questions and negatives — the thing is not done, or you're checking: "Have you paid yet?" "I haven't paid yet."

Why is "I've just eaten" better than "I just ate" for something that happened a minute ago?

Both are heard, and American English often uses the past simple ("I just ate"). But the classic, safe form for "a moment ago, and it matters now" is the present perfect with just: "I've just eaten." Use that in your writing and you'll always be correct.
