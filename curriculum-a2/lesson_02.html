📖 Lesson 1.2: Past Simple — Regular Verbs (-ed)

In Lesson 1.1 you put the verb to be into the past. Now we do the same for action verbs — the things you did. Most English verbs make the past in one easy way: they add -ed. This lesson shows you how to spell that ending. It also shows the hard part: how to say it three different ways.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the past simple of regular verbs by adding -ed (the same form for every person)
  • Apply the four spelling rules for the -ed ending
  • Pronounce -ed correctly in its three sounds: /t/, /d/, and /ɪd/
  • Talk about a past day or event using regular verbs

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe what you did yesterday (or last weekend) in 6 or more sentences using regular past verbs — spoken aloud or recorded.

One Ending for Everyone

Here is the good news. The past simple is easier than the present. Regular verbs use the same form for every person. No extra -s for he/she/it. No changes at all. You learn one past form and it works for everyone.

PersonPresent (A1)Past (A2)
IworkI worked
youworkyou worked
he / she / itworkshe/she/it worked
weworkwe worked
theyworkthey worked

Look at that past column: worked, worked, worked — the same word every time. Say it out loud. It's easy:

I worked yesterday. She worked too. We all worked hard. They worked late.

⚠️ Important Note: This lesson is about affirmative (positive) sentences only — the plain "I worked" form. To make negatives and questions, English uses a helper: didn't and Did…? (for example, "I didn't work" / "Did you work?"). That is the next lesson (1.3), so don't worry about it yet — just enjoy the -ed.

🧠 Mindset

Two thousand English verbs are regular. So when you learn this one small rule — add -ed — you can talk about the past with thousands of verbs. You are not learning two thousand words. You are learning one easy habit. If the sounds feel hard at first, don't worry. Native speakers don't think about it either. Their mouths just learned the pattern, and yours will too. 🌱

Spelling Rules for -ed

Adding -ed is usually easy, but four small spelling rules cover every case. Here they all are in one table:

RuleHowExamples
Most verbsadd -edwork → worked, play → played, watch → watched
Ends in -eadd only -dlike → liked, live → lived, arrive → arrived
Consonant + ychange y to -iedstudy → studied, try → tried
Short vowel + single consonant (1 syllable)double the consonant + -edstop → stopped, plan → planned

✅ Pro Tip

The "-y" rule only works after a consonant. If a vowel comes before the y, keep the y and just add -ed: play → played, stay → stayed (not "plaied"). So it's study → studied but play → played.

⚠️ Watch the doubling rule

We only double the last consonant when the word is one syllable and ends in one vowel + one consonant: stop → stopped, plan → planned. We do not double after two vowels (rain → rained) or after two consonants (work → worked). Never double -w, -x, or -y.

The Three Sounds of -ed

This is the most important part of the lesson. It surprises many learners. We spell the -ed ending the same way, but we say it three different ways. The sound you use depends on the sound just before the -ed.

SoundWhenExamples
/ɪd/ (extra syllable) after a t or d sound wanted /ˈwɒntɪd/, needed /ˈniːdɪd/, decided /dɪˈsaɪdɪd/
/t/ after a voiceless sound (k, p, s, sh, ch, f) worked /wɜːkt/, watched /wɒtʃt/, liked /laɪkt/, stopped /stɒpt/
/d/ after a voiced sound (vowels, b, g, l, n, r, v…) played /pleɪd/, lived /lɪvd/, called /kɔːld/, arrived /əˈraɪvd/

💡 One syllable — except after t/d

The /t/ and /d/ endings do not add a syllable. Worked and played are still one syllable — the ending is just a quick t or d sound on the end. Only after a t or d do we add the extra /ɪd/ syllable, because "wantt" is very hard to say. So wanted = two syllables, but worked = one.

A quick way to feel the difference: put your hand on your throat. Voiced sounds (like the end of play, live, call) make your throat buzz, so the ending buzzes too → /d/. Voiceless sounds (like the end of work, watch, like) have no buzz, so the ending is a soft /t/.

Regular Verb Wordlist

Here are sixteen useful regular verbs in their past form. These are everyday words. You'll use them to describe your own days. Say each one out loud and notice which of the three endings it takes.

  • worked /t/
  • played /d/
  • watched /t/
  • studied /d/
  • cooked /t/
  • cleaned /d/
  • walked /t/
  • talked /t/
  • listened /d/
  • arrived /d/
  • started /ɪd/
  • finished /t/
  • wanted /ɪd/
  • visited /ɪd/
  • stayed /d/
  • called /d/

Take a few of these and make sentences with a partner or in class. Here are some phrases to help you start:

I worked from home. We watched a film. She studied all evening. They arrived late. I called my mother. He cleaned the kitchen. We visited a museum. The lesson started at nine.

Using It: Talk About a Past Day

Regular verbs are great for telling the story of a day. Put a few together with time words, and you have a little story. Here is one about yesterday:

Yesterday I started work at eight. In the morning I cooked breakfast and cleaned the kitchen. At lunchtime I called a friend and we talked for an hour. In the evening I studied English, then I watched a film and listened to some music. It was a good day!

Now listen to two people using regular past verbs in a natural conversation:

A: What did you do yesterday?

B: I worked in the morning, and then I cleaned my flat.

A: Nice. Did you relax in the evening?

B: Yes! I cooked dinner, watched a film, and called my sister. We talked for ages.

A: Sounds lovely. I just studied all day — my exam started this morning!

B: Oh! How was it?

A: It finished an hour ago, and I think I passed. I'm so relieved.

💡 Register note

Notice how B joins verbs together with and and then: "I cooked dinner, watched a film, and called my sister." This is how English speakers tell everyday stories. They join past verbs with small words like and and then. Try to copy this in your project.

  1. stop →
  2. study →
  3. live →
  4. play →
  5. watch →
✅ Answer

1. stopped (double the p) · 2. studied (consonant + y → ied) · 3. lived (ends in -e, add -d) · 4. played (vowel + y, just add -ed) · 5. watched (most verbs, add -ed)

🏋️ Exercise 2: Sort by pronunciation

Objective: Put each verb in the right sound group: /t/, /d/, or /ɪd/.

Verbs: wanted, played, cooked, arrived, visited, walked, cleaned, needed, liked

✅ Answer

/t/ (voiceless): cooked, walked, liked
/d/ (voiced): played, arrived, cleaned
/ɪd/ (after t/d, extra syllable): wanted, visited, needed

🎯 Your Project: What I Did Yesterday

Speak out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder. Your goal: 6 or more sentences about yesterday or last weekend, all with regular past verbs.

  1. (3 min) Plan. Choose 6+ regular verbs from the wordlist that describe your day (e.g. worked, cooked, called, watched, studied, cleaned).
  2. (3 min) Speak your story in order — morning, afternoon, evening — joining sentences with and and then. Add a time word or two (yesterday, in the evening, an hour ago).
  3. (2 min) Focus on pronunciation: say each -ed the right way. Is it a quick /t/, a soft /d/, or an extra /ɪd/ syllable?
  4. (2 min) If you recorded yourself, listen back. Count your verbs and check your endings.

Partner / class option: Tell your day to a partner, then swap and retell their day back to them ("You worked, then you cooked dinner…"). Retelling in the past is great practice.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use the same -ed form for every person — no extra -s for he/she/it.
  • Check the spelling first, then the sound — they're two separate steps.
  • Only add the /ɪd/ syllable after a t or d sound (wanted, needed, visited).

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "He workeds…" ❌ → "He worked" ✅ — the past never takes an extra -s.
  • Adding a syllable to every verb: "work-ed" (two syllables) ❌ → say "workt" ✅ (one syllable). The extra syllable is only for t/d verbs.
  • "studyed / plaied" ❌ → study → studied (consonant + y), but play → played (vowel + y). Don't mix them up.
  • Don't use didn't or did yet — those are for negatives and questions in Lesson 1.3. Here, just: subject + verb-ed.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Regular verbs make the past simple by adding -ed — the same form for every person.
  • Spelling: most verbs +ed; -e → +d; consonant + y → -ied; short vowel + single consonant → double it.
  • Pronounce -ed three ways: /ɪd/ after t/d, /t/ after voiceless sounds, /d/ after voiced sounds.
  • The -ed adds a syllable only after t/d; otherwise it's just a quick sound on the end.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now put thousands of action verbs into the past — and you know how to say them too. Telling someone what you did yesterday is one of the most common things we do in conversation, and you can do it now. That's real, everyday English. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Why is "worked" one syllable but "wanted" is two?

It is hard to join a t sound to another t or d sound. So after t/d we add a small /ɪd/ syllable to make it easy to say (want-ed). After other sounds, the ending is just a light /t/ or /d/ with no new syllable (workt, playd).

How do I know if a sound is "voiced" or "voiceless"?

Put your hand on your throat and say the sound. If it buzzes (like b, g, l, n, v and all vowels), it's voiced → use /d/. If there's no buzz (like k, p, s, sh, ch, f), it's voiceless → use /t/.

Are all English verbs regular?

No — many common verbs are irregular and don't take -ed (go → went, see → saw, have → had). You'll meet those in the next lesson. But regular verbs are the biggest group, so what you learned here covers most verbs you'll ever use.
