🛒 Lesson 2.2: Quantifiers — some, any, much, many

How much rice? How many eggs? In A1 you learned food words and there is / there are. Now we learn how to talk about quantity — the words for how much and how many. We use these small words every day. We use them when we shop, cook, order in a café, and check the fridge.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • See the difference between countable and uncountable nouns
  • Use some and any correctly in positives, negatives, and questions
  • Use much, many, and a lot of — and choose a few or a little
  • Ask "How much…?" and "How many…?"

⏱️ Estimated Time: ~50 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe what's in your kitchen or fridge (or make a shopping list) using quantifiers — 6+ sentences.

Counting Food

Imagine you open your fridge. You see three eggs, two bottles of water, and some cheese. You can count the eggs: one, two, three. But you can't count cheese in the same way. We don't say "three cheeses" for a piece of cheese. So English uses two kinds of nouns, and it uses them in different ways. Today we learn how.

This is important. The quantity words — some, any, much, many — change with the noun. They are different for countable and uncountable nouns. When you see the pattern, it is easy. 🔑

🧠 Mindset

You don't need to learn a hundred rules today. Just learn one question to ask yourself: "Can I count this noun — one, two, three?" If yes, it is countable. If no, it is uncountable. Almost all of this lesson comes from that one question. You already know the food words from A1. Now you just add the quantity words. 🌱

Countable vs Uncountable

Countable nouns are things you can count. They can be plural, and you can use a / an or a number in front of them.

Uncountable nouns are things you don't count one by one. Think of liquids, powders, materials, and ideas. They have no plural form. You can't use a / an or a number with them. Instead we use some, or a container word (a bottle of water, a kilo of rice).

CountableUncountable
Plural?Yes — apple → applesNo — rice (never "rices")
a / an?Yes — an eggNo — not "a rice" ❌
Numbers?Yes — three eggsNo — use some rice
Examplesan apple, three eggs, two bottles, five bananas, tomatoesrice, water, milk, bread, money, sugar, cheese, coffee

Here are the food words from this lesson, sorted for you:

Countable (can be plural)Uncountable (no plural)
apples, eggs, bananas, bottles, tomatoesrice, bread, water, milk, sugar, cheese, money, coffee

💡 A Handy Trick

To count an uncountable noun, add a container or unit: a bottle of water, a cup of coffee, a slice of bread, a bag of rice, a piece of cheese. Now you can count them — two bottles, three cups!

⚠️ Watch Out

Some nouns are uncountable in English, even if your language counts them. Common ones: bread, money, advice, information, furniture, homework. We say "some bread", not "a bread". We say "some money", not "moneys".

some & any

some and any both mean "not an exact amount." Which one you use depends on the kind of sentence:

  • Use some in positive sentences.
  • Use any in negatives and questions.

Both work with plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns.

Sentence typeWordExample
Positive (+)someI have some money. We bought some eggs.
Negative (−)anyI don't have any money. There aren't any eggs.
Question (?)anyDo you have any eggs? Is there any milk?

✅ One Friendly Exception

Use some in questions when you offer or ask for something. It sounds warmer and more polite: "Would you like some tea?" "Can I have some water, please?" Here you expect the answer "yes", so we use some, not any.

Read these aloud to feel the difference:

There's some cheese in the fridge, but there isn't any bread. Do we have any tomatoes? — Would you like some coffee?

much, many & a lot of

These words are for big amounts, or they ask about them. The rule is about countable and uncountable again:

WordUse withBest inExample
manycountable pluralquestions & negativesHow many eggs? Not many people came.
muchuncountablequestions & negativesHow much water? I don't have much time.
a lot ofbothpositives (and all types)a lot of friends · a lot of water

💡 The Simple Version

In positive sentences, a lot of is the easy choice for everything: "We have a lot of tomatoes." "There's a lot of sugar in it." Use much and many mostly for questions and negatives. People almost never say "I have much time." They say "a lot of time" or "not much time."

Compare:

How many bananas do we need? — We have a lot of bananas, so not many. How much milk is left? — Not much, only a little.

a few & a little

These mean a small amount — "not a lot, but enough." Again, one is for countable and one is for uncountable:

WordUse withExample
a fewcountable plurala few eggs · a few friends · a few tomatoes
a littleuncountablea little milk · a little sugar · a little time

Here's an easy way to remember: a few goes with things you can count on your fingers (few = countable). a little goes with a little bit of something you pour or scoop.

I take a little milk and a little sugar in my coffee. Let's buy a few apples and a few bananas.

How much? / How many?

To ask about quantity, use:

  • How many + countable plural? → How many eggs do we have?
  • How much + uncountable? → How much rice do we need?
How many apples? How many people? How much water? How much time? How much sugar?

💡 Back to A1: "How much is it?"

You already met How much in A1 for prices: "How much is it?" "How much are the tomatoes?" Money is uncountable, so prices always use how much — even for countable things. "How much are these bananas?" asks about the price, not the number. 💷

Now let's hear it all in a conversation at the shop:

Shopper: Excuse me, do you have any fresh bread?

Seller: Yes, we have some over here. How many loaves would you like?

Shopper: Just one, please. And I need a few tomatoes and some rice.

Seller: No problem. How much rice — a small bag or a big one?

Shopper: A small bag is fine. We don't need much. How much is it all?

Seller: That's five pounds. Would you like some coffee too? It's on offer today.

Shopper: No thanks, we still have a lot of coffee at home!

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Countable or Uncountable?

Objective: Sort each noun into C (countable) or U (uncountable).

  1. egg
  2. water
  3. banana
  4. rice
  5. bottle
  6. money
  7. tomato
  8. cheese
✅ Answer

1. egg — C · 2. water — U · 3. banana — C · 4. rice — U · 5. bottle — C · 6. money — U · 7. tomato — C · 8. cheese — U

🏋️ Exercise 2: some or any?

Objective: Fill each gap with some or any.

  1. There's milk in the fridge.
  2. We don't have eggs.
  3. Do you have bananas?
  4. I bought bread this morning.
  5. Would you like tea? (offer)
✅ Answer

1. some · 2. any · 3. any · 4. some · 5. some (offers use "some")

🏋️ Exercise 3: much, many, or a lot of?

Objective: Choose the best word. More than one can work, but pick the most natural one.

  1. How eggs do we need?
  2. I don't have time today.
  3. There are people at the market. (positive)
  4. How water is in the bottle?
  5. We have sugar, so don't buy more. (positive)
✅ Answer

1. many · 2. much · 3. a lot of · 4. much · 5. a lot of

🎯 Your Project: What's in My Kitchen?

Describe what's in your kitchen or fridge — or make a shopping list. Speak out loud (to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder), then write your sentences down.

  1. (3 min) Look in your fridge or cupboard (or imagine one). List what you see.
  2. (4 min) Say 6 or more sentences using quantifiers. Include at least one of each: some, any, a lot of, a few, and a little. Example: "There's some cheese and a few eggs, but there isn't any milk. We have a lot of rice and a little sugar."
  3. (2 min) Turn it into a shopping list question: "How much rice do we need? How many tomatoes?"

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Ask yourself first: Can I count it? That answer chooses your quantifier.
  • Use some for positives, any for negatives and questions (but some for offers).
  • In positive sentences, reach for a lot of — it's natural for both countable and uncountable.
  • Match the pairs: many / a few / How many (countable) · much / a little / How much (uncountable).

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "a rice / two rices" ❌ → "some rice / two bags of rice" ✅ (uncountable nouns take no a/an and no plural).
  • "How much eggs?" ❌ → "How many eggs?" ✅ (eggs are countable).
  • "Do you have some money?" — usually ❌ in a neutral question → "Do you have any money?" ✅ (use some only for offers/requests).
  • "I have much friends" ❌ → "I have a lot of friends" ✅ (avoid much/many in plain positives).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Countable nouns can be plural and take numbers (eggs, bottles); uncountable nouns can't (rice, water, money).
  • some = positives; any = negatives & questions; some for offers/requests.
  • many + countable, much + uncountable (mostly questions/negatives); a lot of for both in positives.
  • a few + countable, a little + uncountable — a small amount.
  • Ask How many? (countable) and How much? (uncountable / price).

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now talk about quantity like a real shopper and cook — how much, how many, some, any, a little, a few. We use these words when we talk about food, money, and daily life. Put them together with your A1 food words and there is / there are. Now you are ready for the market, the café, and the kitchen. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is "some" ever used in a question?

Yes — when you offer or ask for something: "Would you like some cake?" "Can I have some water?" For normal questions, when you don't know the answer, use any: "Do you have any cake?"

Can I always just say "a lot of" instead of much/many?

In positive sentences, yes — "a lot of" works for both and sounds natural. In questions and negatives, it is better to use much (uncountable) and many (countable): "How much time?", "not many people."

Why is "bread" uncountable when I can hold one loaf?

English sees bread as a material, so it has no plural and no a/an. To count it, add a unit: a loaf of bread, two slices of bread. It is the same with cheese, rice, and coffee.
