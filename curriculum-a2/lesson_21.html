🩺 Lesson 7.3: Health & Appointments

When you're not feeling well, you need to explain the problem — and quickly. In A1 you learned to say "I have a headache." Now let's go further: book an appointment on the phone, describe your symptoms clearly, ask for help at the pharmacy, and understand the advice people give you. Looking after your health in English is one of the most important skills you can have. 💊

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Make an appointment on the phone — "I'd like to make an appointment", "Have you got anything on Monday morning?"
  • Describe symptoms in more detail — "I've had a headache since yesterday", "It hurts when I…", "I feel dizzy/sick"
  • Ask for help at the pharmacy — "Have you got something for a cold?", "How often should I take it?"
  • Understand and give advice — You should…, You'd better…, Why don't you…?

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Role-play phoning a clinic to book an appointment and describe a health problem — spoken with a partner or recorded on your phone.

Looking After Yourself

Getting sick in your own language is hard enough. In a new language, it can feel scary. But the conversations at a clinic or a pharmacy are simpler than you think — they follow the same friendly patterns every time. When you know them, you can get the help you need without stress.

Today we build on A1's basic doctor's visit. You'll phone to book an appointment, explain your symptoms in more detail, and understand the advice you get. We'll also gently reuse should from A2 Lesson 4.2, because should is the heart of health advice. Let's take care of ourselves in English. 🌿

🧠 Mindset

You don't need medical words to explain how you feel. Simple language works best: "I feel sick," "My head hurts," "I've had a cold since Monday." Doctors and pharmacists are used to helping people who are learning the language — they will slow down and ask questions. Speak simply, and it's enough. 🌱

Note: this lesson teaches everyday language, not medical advice. For real health problems, always talk to a doctor or pharmacist.

The Body & Symptoms

First, a quick refresh of body words and the common problems (symptoms) that go with them.

  • head → a headache
  • throat → a sore throat
  • stomach → a stomach ache
  • tooth → toothache
  • back → backache
  • temperature / a fever
  • a cold / a cough
  • the flu

There are three common ways to talk about a symptom. Learn all three patterns:

PatternExample
I've got / I have + a problemI've got a headache. I have a sore throat.
I feel + adjectiveI feel sick. I feel dizzy. I feel tired.
My … hurts / It hurts when I…My back hurts. It hurts when I swallow.

💡 "hurt", "ache", and "sore"

hurt is a verb: "My arm hurts." ache can be a noun in fixed words (headache, backache, toothache). sore is an adjective meaning painful: "a sore throat," "sore eyes." You don't need to be perfect — "My throat hurts" and "I have a sore throat" both work.

Making an Appointment

To see a doctor, you usually make an appointment — you book a time to visit. This often happens on the phone. The key opener is "I'd like to make an appointment."

I'd like to make an appointment, please. Have you got anything on Monday morning? Is there anything earlier? Can I see a doctor today? What time is free? Is it urgent? — Yes, a little.

The receptionist will ask for your name, the problem (briefly), and check the times. Listen for questions like "What's it about?" and "Does Tuesday at ten work for you?"

I'd like to make an appointment. — Have you got anything on Monday morning? — Is there anything earlier?

✅ Phone phrases

On the phone, a few small phrases help a lot: "Hello, this is Ana speaking," "Could you speak more slowly, please?" and "Let me check… yes, that works." If you don't catch a time, repeat it back: "So, Tuesday at ten — thank you." 📞

Describing How You Feel

At the appointment, the doctor asks "What's the matter?" or "How can I help?" Now you describe your symptoms. Two useful tools here:

1. How long? Use since and for

To say how long a problem has lasted, we use the present perfect (a taste of Lesson 7.4!): I've had… + since (a starting point) or for (a length of time).

I've had a headache since yesterday. · I've had a cough for three days. · I haven't slept well for a week.

2. When does it hurt? Use It hurts when I…

It hurts when I swallow. · It hurts when I walk. · My arm hurts when I lift it.

I feel dizzy. I feel sick. I've had a temperature since last night. I've got a bad cough. It hurts when I swallow. I can't sleep.

⚠️ Watch Out: "sick" and "ill"

In British English, ill usually means generally unwell, and be sick often means to vomit (throw up). "I feel sick" = I feel like I might vomit. In American English, sick just means unwell. Both are common — if in doubt, say "I don't feel well" or "I feel unwell," which is always clear.

At the Pharmacy

For small problems — a cold, a cough, a headache — you can go straight to the pharmacy (chemist's) instead of the doctor. Ask for "something for…" your problem.

Have you got something for a cold? I need something for a cough. Do you have anything for a headache? How often should I take it? How many should I take? Are there any side effects?

Now let's put it together. Listen to Ana phone the clinic, then talk to the pharmacist. Notice the appointment language, the symptoms with since/for, and the advice at the end:

Ana: Hello, I'd like to make an appointment, please.

Receptionist: Of course. What's it about?

Ana: I've had a bad sore throat and a temperature since Monday.

Receptionist: I'm sorry to hear that. Have you got a cough too?

Ana: Yes, a little. And it hurts when I swallow.

Receptionist: Have you got anything on Monday morning? We have ten o'clock free.

Ana: Is there anything earlier? I feel quite unwell.

Receptionist: Let me check… Yes, a doctor can see you today at four. Your name?

Ana: Ana Costa. Thank you! (later, at the pharmacy) Excuse me, have you got something for a sore throat?

Pharmacist: Yes, these lozenges help. You should take one every four hours.

Ana: How often should I take them?

Pharmacist: Up to four a day. And you'd better rest and drink plenty of water. If it's not better in a few days, see the doctor.

Ana: Thank you very much!

Understanding Advice

When you're unwell, people give you advice — ideas about what's best to do. English has three friendly ways to give advice. You met should back in Lesson 4.2 — here it is again, with two new friends.

PhraseHow strong?Example
You should…normal advice (a good idea)You should rest. You shouldn't go to work.
You'd better…stronger advice (important!)You'd better see a doctor. You'd better not wait.
Why don't you…?a friendly suggestionWhy don't you take a day off?

All three are followed by the base verb (no to): should rest, better see, why don't you take.

You should drink lots of water. · You'd better stay in bed today. · Why don't you take some medicine?

graph LR
    A["Giving advice"] --> B["You should + verb = good idea"]
    A --> C["You'd better + verb = stronger, important"]
    A --> D["Why don't you + verb? = friendly suggestion"]

💡 "had better" is really strong

You'd better = you had better. It sounds like normal advice but it's stronger — it hints there could be a problem if you don't. "You'd better see a doctor" means "this is important, please do it." For a gentle idea, use should or why don't you…? instead.

🎯 Your Project: Phone the Clinic

Role-play a phone call to a doctor's clinic, then a short chat with the pharmacist. Speak out loud with a partner, or record both parts on your phone.

  1. (2 min) Book the appointment: start with "Hello, I'd like to make an appointment, please." Ask about a day/time ("Have you got anything on Monday morning?").
  2. (3 min) Describe your problem: say 2–3 symptoms, and use since or for to say how long ("I've had a sore throat since Tuesday"). Add "It hurts when I…" or "I feel…"
  3. (2 min) At the pharmacy: ask for "something for…" your problem, and ask "How often should I take it?"
  4. (2 min) Swap roles and be the receptionist / pharmacist. Give advice using should, you'd better, and why don't you…?
  5. (1 min) If you recorded yourself, listen back. Did you say how long the problem lasted with since or for?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Open politely: "I'd like to make an appointment, please."
  • Say how long with since (a point in time) or for (a length of time).
  • Describe symptoms simply: "I feel…," "I've got…," "It hurts when I…"
  • At the pharmacy, ask for "something for [problem]" and "How often should I take it?"
  • Use the base verb after should, 'd better, and why don't you…?

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I've had a cold since three days." ❌ → "…for three days." ✅ (for + a length of time).
  • "You should to rest." ❌ → "You should rest." ✅ (base verb after should).
  • "I have pain my head." ❌ → "I have a headache" / "My head hurts." ✅
  • "Why you don't rest?" ❌ → "Why don't you rest?" ✅ (question word order).
  • "I want an appointment." — abrupt → "I'd like to make an appointment, please." ✅

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When do I use "since" and when do I use "for"?

Use since with a starting point — a day, a date, a time: "since Monday," "since 9 o'clock," "since last week." Use for with a length of time: "for three days," "for an hour," "for a week." You'll practise this a lot more in the next lesson, present perfect.

What's the difference between "you should" and "you'd better"?

You should is normal, friendly advice — a good idea. You'd better (had better) is stronger — it means "this is important, and there may be a problem if you don't." For a gentle suggestion, use should or Why don't you…? Save you'd better for more serious things.

Do I need to go to the doctor for a cold?

For small problems like a cold or a cough, many people go to the pharmacy first — it's quicker and the pharmacist can help. Ask for "something for a cold." For anything serious, or if it doesn't get better, book a doctor's appointment. (Remember: this lesson teaches language, not medical advice.)
