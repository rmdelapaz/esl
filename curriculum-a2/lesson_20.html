🍽️ Lesson 7.2: Eating Out & Shopping — Ordering, Booking & Problems

In A1 you learned to order a coffee. Now let's step up. Today you'll book a table, order a full meal, ask for the bill, and go shopping for clothes. And when something goes wrong — a shirt that doesn't fit, a dish that's too salty — you'll know exactly what to say. This is real, everyday English for eating out and spending your money well. 🛍️

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

A Meal Out, A Day Shopping

Some of the most useful English happens in restaurants and shops. You book a table, you order food, you try on a jacket, you ask for a refund. These moments repeat all over the world, and they follow polite patterns. When you know the patterns, you feel calm and confident — even when something goes wrong.

This lesson gives you two toolkits. The first is for eating out: booking, ordering, paying. The second is for shopping: sizes, colours, trying on, and returns. Along the way we'll learn two small but powerful words — too and enough — that let you say exactly what's wrong. 🔧

🧠 Mindset

You already know more than you think. From A1 you can say "I'd like…" and "How much is it?" Today we add polite, grown-up phrases on top of that base. You don't need perfect grammar to order a meal — you need a few good phrases and a friendly voice. If a word doesn't come, point and smile. Communication first, perfection later. 🌱

Booking a Table

Before a meal at a busy restaurant, you often book (reserve) a table. You can do this on the phone or in person. The key phrase is "I'd like to book a table for [number] at [time]."

I'd like to book a table for two at eight (o'clock). · Have you got a table for four tonight?

I'd like to book a table for two. A table for four at seven thirty, please. Have you got a table for tonight? It's under the name Silva. Could we sit by the window? Do you take reservations?

The restaurant may ask for your name and the number of people. Listen for "For how many?" and "What time?" and "What name is it under?"

💡 "I'd like" is your polite friend

I'd like is short for I would like. It's softer and more polite than "I want." Use it everywhere: "I'd like to book a table," "I'd like the soup," "I'd like to pay." Follow it with a noun (I'd like the fish) or with to + verb (I'd like to book).

Ordering a Meal

A full meal often has three parts. Knowing these words helps you read any menu:

CourseMeaningExamples
starterthe small first dishsoup, salad, garlic bread
main (course)the big central dishchicken, pasta, fish and chips
dessertthe sweet last dishice cream, cake, fruit

To order, use these polite openers. They all work — pick the one you like:

Could I have the soup, please? I'll have the chicken. I'd like the pasta, please. What do you recommend? What's in the salad? Is this dish spicy? Can we see the menu, please?

For a starter, could I have the soup? Then, for my main, I'll have the fish, please. — What do you recommend?

Now let's listen to a family at a restaurant. Notice how they book, order each course, and ask a question about the menu:

Waiter: Good evening. Have you got a reservation?

Marco: Yes, a table for two at eight, under the name Silva.

Waiter: Perfect, this way please. Here are the menus. Can I get you any drinks?

Marco: Two waters, please. Could we have a few minutes?

Waiter: Of course. (later) Are you ready to order?

Marco: Yes. For a starter, could I have the soup? And what's in the green salad?

Waiter: It's lettuce, tomato, and cheese, with an olive oil dressing.

Marco: Lovely. Then I'll have the salad and the grilled chicken for my main.

Waiter: And to follow? We have ice cream and apple cake for dessert.

Marco: The apple cake, please. Thank you!

Paying the Bill

At the end, you ask for the bill (in the US, the check). If you're with friends, you might split it — divide the cost between you.

Could we have the bill, please? Can I pay by card? Shall we split the bill? Let's split it three ways. Is service included? Keep the change.

✅ Splitting the bill

Split the bill = share the total. "Let's split it" usually means share it equally. "Split it three ways" means divide it between three people. If everyone pays only for what they ate, some people say "separate bills, please." Asking is normal and polite. 💳

Shopping for Clothes

Now let's go shopping. In a clothes shop, you ask about sizes and colours, and you try things on before you buy them.

Have you got this in a medium? Do you have this in blue? Can I try this on? Where are the fitting rooms? Have you got a bigger size? How much is this? I'm just looking, thanks.
You want to…Say
ask for a sizeHave you got this in a small / medium / large?
ask for a colourDo you have this in black / red / a lighter colour?
try it onCan I try this on? — Yes, the fitting rooms are over there.
say it's right / wrongIt fits / It doesn't fit. It suits me.
💬 Word note: Fit and suit are different. Fit = the right size for your body ("These jeans don't fit — they're too small."). Suit = the right style or colour for you ("That colour really suits you!").

too & enough

When something is wrong, two little words do the work: too and enough. They are opposites in meaning and they sit in different places.

too = more than you want (a problem)

too goes before an adjective. It always means something negative — too much of a quality.

It's too expensive. · This coffee is too hot. · These shoes are too small.

With nouns, use too much (uncountable) and too many (countable) — remember these from Lesson 2.2!

There's too much salt in the soup. · There are too many people in here.

enough = as much as you need

enough goes after an adjective, but before a noun.

PatternExample
adjective + enoughIt's not big enough. This is warm enough.
enough + nounWe don't have enough chairs. Is there enough time?
 
graph LR
    A["too + adjective"] --> B["too small / too expensive = a problem"]
    C["adjective + enough"] --> D["big enough / warm enough = OK"]
    E["not ... enough"] --> F["not big enough = still a problem"]

⚠️ Watch Out: too vs very

very just makes the adjective stronger — it's neutral. too means it's a problem. "It's very hot" = it's really hot (maybe nice!). "It's too hot" = it's so hot I can't drink it. Don't mix them up: "This tea is too good" sounds strange — say "very good."

Returns & Problems

Sometimes you buy something and it's wrong — it doesn't fit, or it's faulty (broken, not working). Then you take it back to the shop for a refund (your money back) or an exchange (a different one).

I'd like to return this, please. It doesn't fit. It's faulty — the zip is broken. Can I get a refund? Could I exchange it for a bigger size? Here's the receipt.

Let's listen to a shopper returning a faulty jacket. Notice how she stays polite, explains the problem, and uses too and enough:

Shopper: Hi. I'd like to return this jacket, please.

Assistant: No problem. Is there something wrong with it?

Shopper: Yes — it's faulty. The zip is broken. And it's a bit too small.

Assistant: I'm sorry about that. Have you got the receipt?

Shopper: Here it is. Can I get a refund, or exchange it for a bigger size?

Assistant: We can do either. Would you like to try the large? This one isn't big enough for you.

Shopper: Yes, please. But if the zip is broken again, I'd like a refund.

Assistant: Of course. Let me get the large for you to try on.

💡 Stay calm and polite

Even when you complain, English stays polite. Start softly: "I'm sorry, but…" or "Excuse me, there's a small problem…" Explain the problem clearly, then say what you want: "Could I get a refund?" A calm, friendly tone gets the best result. 😊

🎯 Your Project: A Meal Out or a Return

Choose one role-play (or do both!). Speak out loud with a partner, or record both parts on your phone.

Option A — Booking & ordering:

  1. (2 min) Book a table: say how many people and what time. Give a name.
  2. (3 min) Order a full meal — a starter, a main, and a dessert — using "Could I have…?" and "I'll have…" Ask one question about the menu ("What's in the…?" or "What do you recommend?").
  3. (1 min) Ask for the bill and offer to split it.

Option B — Returning a faulty item:

  1. (2 min) Take an item back to a shop. Explain the problem — "It's faulty," "It doesn't fit," or "It's too small."
  2. (2 min) Ask for a refund or an exchange. Use too and enough at least once each ("It's too tight," "It's not long enough.").
  3. (1 min) Swap roles and be the shop assistant. Stay polite!

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use polite openers: "I'd like…", "Could I have…?", "Can I…?" — always add please.
  • Learn the three courses: starter, main, dessert — they help you read any menu.
  • Put too before the adjective (too small); put enough after it (big enough).
  • When you return something, explain the problem first, then say what you want (refund or exchange).
  • Keep your receipt — shops usually need it for a refund.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I want a table." — sounds abrupt → "I'd like a table, please." ✅ (softer and more polite).
  • "It's enough big." ❌ → "It's big enough." ✅ (enough comes after the adjective).
  • "There are too much people." ❌ → "There are too many people." ✅ (people are countable).
  • "This tea is too good." ❌ (for a compliment) → "This tea is very good." ✅ (too = a problem).
  • "Can I try it?" for clothes → better: "Can I try it on?" ✅ (try on = wear to test).

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the difference between "I'll have" and "Could I have"?

Both are polite and both are fine for ordering food. "I'll have the fish" is a friendly, confident choice. "Could I have the fish, please?" is a little softer, like a request. Use whichever feels comfortable — they mean the same thing at a restaurant.

Where exactly does "enough" go?

After an adjective or adverb: big enough, quickly enough. Before a noun: enough time, enough chairs. A quick check: if the next word describes a thing (a noun), enough comes first; if it describes a quality (an adjective), enough comes second.

Is "faulty" the same as "broken"?

Close. Faulty means something doesn't work properly or has a defect — it may be broken, or just not working right. You use it a lot when returning products: "The phone is faulty," "a faulty zip." Broken is more specific — a part is physically damaged.
