🏔️ Lesson 2.1: Comparatives & Superlatives

Which city is bigger? Which phone is the cheapest? Which restaurant is the best? Every day we compare things — and English has two easy tools for this. In this lesson you'll learn how to compare two things (comparatives) and how to pick the top of three or more (superlatives).

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form comparative adjectives with -er and more (cheaper, more expensive)
  • Form superlative adjectives with -est and the most (the cheapest, the most expensive)
  • Use than after comparatives and the before superlatives
  • Use common irregular forms: good/better/best and bad/worse/worst

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Compare three things you know well (three cities, three foods, or three phones) in 6+ spoken sentences, using both comparatives and superlatives.

Why We Compare

Every day you make choices, and choices mean comparisons. "This bag is cheaper." "That road is faster." "She's the best teacher in the school." When you can compare things in English, you can shop, give opinions, suggest places, and explain your choices. 🛍️

There are just two jobs to learn:

  • Comparative — compares TWO things: A is bigger than B.
  • Superlative — finds the top of THREE or more: A is the biggest.

🧠 Mindset

You already know many adjectives from A1 — big, small, cheap, fast, good. This lesson does not ask you to learn new words. It teaches you two small changes you can add to words you already know. Learn the pattern once, and it works on almost every adjective in English. A little work gives you a lot. 🌱

Comparatives: Comparing Two

To compare two things, we change the adjective in one of two ways — and the choice depends on how long the adjective is.

Short adjectives (one syllable) → add -er, then than:

AdjectiveComparativeExample
cheapcheaperThe bus is cheaper than the train.
bigbiggerTokyo is bigger than my town.
oldolderMy brother is older than me.
fastfasterA plane is faster than a car.

Long adjectives (two or more syllables) → put more in front, then than:

AdjectiveComparativeExample
expensivemore expensiveThe hotel is more expensive than the hostel.
interestingmore interestingThis book is more interesting than the film.
beautifulmore beautifulSpring is more beautiful than winter here.
difficultmore difficultChinese is more difficult than Spanish for me.

✅ Pro Tip

A quick test: count the syllables. One syllable (cheap, big, fast) → -er. Two or more (ex-pen-sive, in-ter-est-ing) → more. Two-syllable words ending in -y are a special case — they take -er too (happy → happier). You'll see why in the spelling rules below.

Superlatives: The Top of the Group

When you compare three or more things and point to the number one, use a superlative. The pattern is like the comparative — and it almost always starts with the.

Short adjectives → the + adjective + -est:

AdjectiveSuperlativeExample
cheapthe cheapestThis is the cheapest phone in the shop.
bigthe biggestIt's the biggest city in the country.
oldthe oldestShe's the oldest student in the class.
fastthe fastestThis is the fastest route home.

Long adjectives → the most + adjective:

AdjectiveSuperlativeExample
expensivethe most expensiveIt's the most expensive restaurant in town.
interestingthe most interestingThat was the most interesting class this year.
beautifulthe most beautifulIt's the most beautiful beach I know.

Of the three phones, the first is the cheapest, the second is the most popular, and the third is the best — but also the most expensive!

Spelling Rules

When you add -er or -est, four small spelling rules keep the words looking right. Learn these and your writing will be correct.

RuleAdjectiveComparativeSuperlative
Normal → just add -er / -estcoldcolderthe coldest
Ends in -e → add -r / -stnicenicerthe nicest
Consonant + y → -ier / -iesthappyhappierthe happiest
Consonant + y → -ier / -iesteasyeasierthe easiest
Short vowel + consonant → double itbigbiggerthe biggest
Short vowel + consonant → double ithothotterthe hottest

💡 The doubling rule, simply

Only double the last letter when the word ends in one vowel + one consonant and you say the stress on that part: big → bigger, hot → hotter, thin → thinner. You don't double after two vowels (cheap → cheaper, not "cheapper") or after two consonants (fast → faster).

Irregular Forms

A few very common adjectives don't follow the rules at all. You just have to learn them — the good news is there are only a few, and you'll use them all the time.

AdjectiveComparativeSuperlative
goodbetterthe best
badworsethe worst
farfurther / fartherthe furthest / farthest

This café is good, that one is better, but the one by the river is the best. My cooking is bad, my brother's is worse, but our uncle's is the worst! 😄

✅ Note on "far"

Both further and farther are correct for distance ("The station is further/farther than the shop"). Further is a little more common and also means "more" or "extra" ("further information"). Either is fine to use.

Than, The & a Warning

Two little words hold these patterns together. Get them right and your comparisons sound natural.

  • than comes after a comparative: "A is bigger than B." "Coffee is more expensive than tea."
  • the comes before a superlative: "the best," "the cheapest," "the most beautiful."
cheaper than bigger than more expensive than better than the best the cheapest the most popular the worst

⚠️ Watch Out — use ONE form only

Never use two comparative forms or two superlative forms together. Pick either -er or more — never both.

  • more cheaper ❌ → cheaper ✅
  • the most biggest ❌ → the biggest ✅
  • more better ❌ → better ✅
  • the most fastest ❌ → the fastest ✅

Now listen to it all in a real conversation — two friends choosing where to eat:

A: Where should we eat? There are three places near here.

B: Well, the pizza place is the cheapest, but the food isn't great.

A: The Thai restaurant is more expensive than the pizza place, right?

B: Yes, but it's much better. Honestly, it's the best food in the area.

A: And the sushi bar? Is it more expensive than the Thai place?

B: It's the most expensive of the three, and it's further away too. Let's go Thai — better than sushi and cheaper!

Bonus: (not) as … as

💡 Here is one more useful pattern. To say two things are equal, use as … as. To say they are not equal, use not as … as.

MeaningPatternExample
Equalas + adjective + asTom is as tall as his father.
Not equalnot as + adjective + asThe bus is not as expensive as the taxi.

"The bus is not as expensive as the taxi" means the same as "The bus is cheaper than the taxi." Two ways to say one idea — good to know. 🧰

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Spelling drill

Objective: Write the comparative and superlative of each adjective.

  1. big
  2. happy
  3. expensive
  4. good
  5. nice
✅ Answer

1. bigger / the biggest
2. happier / the happiest
3. more expensive / the most expensive
4. better / the best
5. nicer / the nicest

🏋️ Exercise 2: than or the?

Objective: Complete each sentence with than or the.

  1. My phone is cheaper yours.
  2. This is best café in town.
  3. Winter is colder autumn.
  4. She is tallest player on the team.
  5. A taxi is more expensive a bus.
✅ Answer

1. than · 2. the · 3. than · 4. the · 5. than

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the mistake

Objective: Each sentence has one error. Rewrite it correctly.

  1. This bag is more cheaper than that one.
  2. The plane is the most fast way to travel.
  3. My new job is more better than my old one.
  4. Mount Everest is the most biggest mountain in the world.
✅ Answer

1. This bag is cheaper than that one.
2. The plane is the fastest way to travel.
3. My new job is better than my old one.
4. Mount Everest is the biggest (or highest) mountain in the world.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Count syllables: one syllable → -er / -est; two or more → more / the most.
  • Put than after every comparative and the before every superlative.
  • Memorize the three irregulars: good/better/best, bad/worse/worst, far/further/furthest.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "more cheaper" ❌ → "cheaper" ✅ — use one form only.
  • "the most biggest" ❌ → "the biggest" ✅.
  • "more good" ❌ → "better" ✅ — good is irregular.
  • "bigger then" ❌ → "bigger than" ✅ — than compares; then means "next".
  • "biger / hoter" ❌ → "bigger / hotter" ✅ — double the final consonant.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Comparative compares two: short adj + -er + than; long adj + more … than.
  • Superlative is the top of three or more: the + short adj + -est; the most + long adj.
  • Spelling: nice→nicer, happy→happier, big→bigger (double the consonant).
  • Irregulars: good→better→best, bad→worse→worst, far→further→furthest.
  • Never mix forms: not "more cheaper," not "the most biggest."

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now compare almost anything in English — prices, places, people, phones. Two small patterns (-er/more and -est/the most) plus a few irregulars give you endless everyday sentences: shopping choices, suggestions, opinions, and decisions. That is a really useful new skill. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know if an adjective is "short" or "long"?

Count the syllables when you say it. One syllable (big, cheap, fast) is short → use -er/-est. Two or more (ex-pen-sive) is long → use more/the most. Two-syllable words ending in -y (happy, easy) are a special case — they take -er/-est.

Do I always need "the" with a superlative?

Almost always, yes: "the biggest," "the best," "the most expensive." The superlative points to one top item, and the shows there is only one.

What's the difference between "than" and "then"?

Than is for comparing: "bigger than." Then is about time or order: "First we ate, then we left." They sound similar but mean different things — comparisons always use than.
