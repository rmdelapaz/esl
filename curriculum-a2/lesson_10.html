🌟 Lesson 4.1: Present Perfect — Life Experiences

This is one of the most useful tenses in English — and one that many learners worry about. Don't! The present perfect is just have/has + a past participle, and you already know most of the participles. In this lesson you'll use it to talk about the experiences of your whole life: the places you've been, the food you've tried, the things you've done.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the present perfect (have/has + past participle) and its contractions
  • Talk about life experiences using ever and never, and ask "Have you ever…?"
  • Understand the difference between been and gone
  • Choose between present perfect (experience, no time) and past simple (finished time)

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55–65 minutes

🎯 Project: Interview a partner (or yourself) with "Have you ever…?" questions about life experiences — at least 6 exchanges, moving to the past simple for the details.

A New Way to Talk About Your Life

So far in A2 you've told stories about finished moments — "I visited Paris last year," "We were at the coast." Those sentences point to a clear time in the past.

But sometimes we don't care when something happened — we only want to say that it happened at some point in our life. Look at the difference:

"I have visited Paris." → at some time in my life — the date isn't important.
"I visited Paris last year." → a finished, specific time.

That first sentence is the present perfect. It connects your whole life up to now into one big "experience box." This is exactly how English speakers talk about travel, food, films, jobs, and adventures. 🌍

🧠 Mindset

The present perfect feels tricky because it's new — but it's just have + a past participle. You already know most of those participles (seen, done, eaten, been). You're not learning a hundred new words; you're learning one small pattern and clicking your old words into it. If it feels strange today, add the magic word: "I haven't mastered this… yet." 🌱

The Form: have/has + Past Participle

The recipe never changes: subject + have/has + past participle. Use have for I/you/we/they, and has for he/she/it.

💡 About "she's"

The contraction she's can mean she is OR she has. Here it means she has: "She's seen it" = "She has seen it." The past participle after it (seen) tells you it's the present perfect, not present tense.

Negatives add not → haven't / hasn't:

I haven't been to Japan. · He hasn't tried it. · We haven't met the new teacher.

Past Participles

The past participle is the "third form" of a verb. Here's the good news:

  • For regular verbs, the participle is exactly the same as the -ed past simple you already learned: work → worked, visit → visited, try → tried, play → played.
  • For irregular verbs, you learn one extra form — but many of them are ones you already know, and some are identical to the past simple.

✅ Good News

Notice how many participles you already own: met, made, had, read, tried are the same as the past simple. Learn the tricky handful (been, gone, seen, done, eaten, written, broken) and you can build hundreds of present-perfect sentences. Keep a little list in your journal and add one new participle a day.

Experience vs Finished Time

Here is the heart of the lesson. Use the present perfect for an experience in your life up to now, when the exact time is not important and not said. Use the past simple when you say when it happened.

Present Perfect (experience)Past Simple (finished time)
I have visited Rome.I visited Rome in 2019.
She has seen that film.She saw it last night.
We have eaten Thai food.We ate it on Friday.

⚠️ The Golden Rule

The present perfect has NO specific past time word. If you say when — yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago, on Monday — you must use the past simple. ❌ "I have visited Paris last year." → ✅ "I visited Paris last year." OR ✅ "I have visited Paris." (no time word).

💡 Why the two tenses work together

In real conversation we often start with the present perfect to open a topic, then switch to the past simple to give the details: "I've been to Egypt. (experience) I went there in 2015 and I saw the pyramids." (details). This "open, then explain" pattern is your project today.

Ever & Never

Two little words make the present perfect come alive.

  • ever = "at any time in your life" — we use it in questions. It goes before the participle: "Have you ever eaten sushi?"
  • never = "at no time in your life" — it gives a negative meaning, so we don't also use not: "I have never been to Japan."
TypePatternExample
QuestionHave/Has + subject + ever + participle?Have you ever ridden a horse?
Negative (never)subject + have/has + never + participleI have never broken a bone.
Negative (not)subject + haven't/hasn't + participleShe hasn't tried it.
Positivesubject + have/has + participleWe have met before.

⚠️ Don't double the negative

never already means "not ever," so say "I have never been…" ✅ — never "I haven't never been." ❌

Been vs Gone

The verb go has two participles in the present perfect, and they mean different things. This is a favourite exam point — get it once and you'll never forget it.

WordMeaningExample
beenwent and came back — an experience, now here againHe has been to Italy. (He's home now — he visited.)
gonewent and is still there / on the way — not here nowHe has gone to Italy. (He's in Italy now.)

💡 Quick test

If the person is back with you, use been. If they're away right now, use gone. "I've been to the shop" = I went and I'm back (here's the milk!). "She's gone to the shop" = she's out at the shop now. For life-experience sentences, you almost always want been.

Asking & Answering

Here's the natural pattern for a "Have you ever…?" conversation. The question is present perfect; the short answer is present perfect; then the details move into the past simple.

Have you ever…? Yes, I have. No, I haven't. No, never. I've never… I've already… Really? When did you…?
QuestionYesNo
Have you ever been to Spain?Yes, I have.No, I haven't. / No, never.
Has she ever tried skiing?Yes, she has.No, she hasn't.

Now hear it all in a real conversation. Notice the "open in present perfect, explain in past simple" move:

A: Have you ever been to another country?

B: Yes, I have! I've been to Thailand.

A: Wow! When did you go?

B: I went there in 2018. I ate amazing street food and I saw beautiful temples. Have you ever tried Thai food?

A: No, I haven't. I've never eaten it. But I've been to Italy — I love the pizza there!

B: Nice! Where's your brother today? I haven't seen him.

A: Oh, he's gone to the airport — he's flying to Italy tonight!

🎯 Your Project: The "Have You Ever…?" Interview

Interview a partner — or yourself, using your phone's voice recorder and imagining the answers.

  1. (3 min) Write 6 "Have you ever…?" questions about life experiences (travel, food, sport, films, adventures). Example: "Have you ever ridden a horse?"
  2. (5 min) Ask your 6 questions. For each, your partner answers with a short answer (Yes, I have / No, I haven't / No, never), then adds one detail in the past simple. Example: "Yes, I have. I rode a horse in 2020."
  3. (3 min) Swap roles and answer your partner's questions the same way.
  4. (1 min) If you recorded yourself, listen back: did you keep the questions in present perfect and put the times in the past simple?

Aim for at least 6 full exchanges (question → short answer → detail).

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use the present perfect for life experience with no specific time: "I've been to Brazil."
  • Switch to the past simple the moment you add a time word: "I went there in 2017."
  • Put ever in questions and never in negatives — both go before the participle.
  • Remember been = went and came back; gone = still away.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I have visited Paris last year." ❌ → "I visited Paris last year." ✅ (specific time → past simple).
  • "She has went." ❌ → "She has gone / been." ✅ (use the participle, not the past simple, after has).
  • "I haven't never been." ❌ → "I have never been." ✅ (don't double the negative).
  • "He has gone to Italy" when he's sitting right next to you ❌ → "He has been to Italy." ✅

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Present perfect = have/has + past participle (I've been, she's seen).
  • Use it for life experience when the time is not said; use the past simple when you give a time.
  • ever in questions, never in negatives — both before the participle; never double the negative.
  • been = went and came back; gone = still away.
  • Answer: Yes, I have. / No, I haven't. / No, never. — then add details in the past simple.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How is the present perfect different from the past simple, really?

Think of a time line. The past simple points to one finished moment ("I saw it yesterday"). The present perfect covers your whole life up to now with no specific point ("I've seen it"). If a specific time word appears (yesterday, in 2019, last week), you must use the past simple.

Why do we say "been" for the verb "go"?

When you talk about visiting and returning, English uses been as the participle of go: "I've been to Rome." Keep gone for someone who is still away: "She's gone to Rome." For life experience, you almost always want been.

Do I really never use a time word with the present perfect?

Not a specific one (yesterday, in 2019). But words that don't fix an exact moment — ever, never, already — are fine, because they still leave the time open: "I've never been," "Have you ever tried…?", "I've already eaten."
