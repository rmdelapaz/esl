🕰️ Lesson 5.3: used to — Life in the Past

Some things in our lives change. Maybe you used to live in another city. Maybe your grandmother used to tell you stories. English has one beautiful little phrase for all these past habits and old realities that are gone now: used to. In this lesson you'll learn to talk about how life used to be — and compare it to how it is today. Let's look back. 📷

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use used to + base verb for past habits and past states that are no longer true
  • Make the negative (didn't use to) — with the important spelling change
  • Ask questions (Did you use to…?)
  • Compare then vs now to describe how life has changed

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe how your life (or your hometown) used to be different in 6+ sentences using used to, comparing then and now — spoken to a partner or recorded on your phone.

Looking Back

Think about your childhood. What did you do again and again? Maybe you walked to school every day, or you played in the street with friends, or you had long hair. All of these were true then, but they are not true now. English has a special phrase for exactly this feeling: used to. 🌳

When you say "I used to walk to school," you tell the listener two things at once: it happened regularly in the past, and it doesn't happen anymore. That little phrase carries a whole "before and after" inside it. It's one of the most useful tools for sharing memories.

🧠 Mindset

You already know the past simple ("I walked to school"). So why learn used to? Because it adds a warm extra meaning: this was normal for me, and now it has changed. You don't have to be perfect from the first try. Learning to look back and compare is a lovely, human thing to do in a new language. If it feels new, add the magic word: "I can't talk about my old habits easily… yet." 🌱

The Form: used to + base verb

The affirmative form is beautifully simple and never changes for the person. Whether it's I, you, she, or they, you always say used to + base verb.

I used to live in Madrid. · She used to smoke. · They used to play football every weekend.

Notice: no -s for he/she, no change at all. The base verb is the plain dictionary form — live, smoke, play, have, be.

PersonAffirmativeExample
Iused to + base verbI used to live in Madrid.
youused to + base verbYou used to have long hair.
he / she / itused to + base verbShe used to smoke.
weused to + base verbWe used to walk to school.
theyused to + base verbThey used to play outside.

⚠️ Watch Out: use the BASE verb

After used to, always use the plain base verb — not -ing and not past tense. ✅ "I used to play tennis." · ❌ "I used to playing tennis." · ❌ "I used to played tennis." The used to part already shows the past, so the verb stays plain.

Habits & States

We use used to for two kinds of past things — both true "again and again" or "for a long time", and both no longer true now.

1 — Past habits (repeated actions)

Things you did regularly, over and over, in the past:

"I used to walk to work every day." · "We used to visit our grandparents every summer." · "He used to smoke ten cigarettes a day."

2 — Past states (things that were true for a long time)

Situations or facts that were true in the past, but changed:

"I used to live in a small village." · "She used to have long hair." · "There used to be a cinema here."

⚠️ Important Note: Used to is only for repeated habits or long states — not for a single action at one moment. Say "Yesterday I went to the doctor" (one time, past simple), not "I used to go to the doctor yesterday." For a one-time event, use the past simple.

💡 A quick test

Ask yourself: "Was this true many times or for a long time in the past — and is it different now?" If yes to both, used to fits perfectly. If it was just one action at one moment, use the past simple instead.

Negatives & Questions

Here comes the one tricky part — but it's easy once you see it. In negatives and questions, we use did, and the phrase changes to use to (no -d). Why? Because did already carries the past, so used drops its -d.

TypeFormExample
Affirmative (+)used to + base verbI used to play football.
Negative (−)didn't use to + base verbI didn't use to like coffee.
Question (?)Did … use to + base verb?Did you use to play football?
⚠️ Important Note: After did / didn't, write use to — no -d. ✅ "Did you use to…?" · ✅ "I didn't use to…" · ❌ "Did you used to…?" · ❌ "I didn't used to…" (In fast speech use to and used to sound the same, so this only matters in writing — but it matters!)

Here are short answers and Wh- questions:

Did you use to live here? Yes, I did. No, I didn't. Where did you use to work? What did you use to do?

Then vs Now

The real power of used to shines when you compare the past and the present. Say what used to be true, then say what is true now — often with the word but.

"I used to walk to work, but now I drive." · "She used to have short hair, but now it's long." · "We used to live in a flat, but now we have a house."

graph LR
    THEN["Then: used to walk"] --> BUT["but now"]
    BUT --> NOW["Now: I drive"]

✅ A natural pattern

The "used to …, but now …" pattern is one of the most natural ways to tell someone about change — in your city, your job, your hobbies, or yourself. It works for happy changes and sad ones. It's how we share the story of our lives. 🌟

Comparing Lives

Here is a small vocabulary set for talking about life changes and childhood:

  • childhood
  • hometown
  • a habit
  • to move (house)
  • to change
  • years ago
  • when I was young
  • these days
  • nowadays

Now let's hear two old friends comparing how life used to be:

A: This street has changed so much! Do you remember the old bakery?

B: Of course! There used to be a lovely little shop right on the corner. Now it's a coffee bar.

A: I know. We used to buy fresh bread there every morning. Did you use to walk here as a child?

B: Yes, I did! I used to play in this park with my brother. We didn't use to have phones, so we played outside all day.

A: Life used to be slower, didn't it? These days everyone is so busy.

B: True. But some things are better now. I didn't use to enjoy coffee — and now I love it! Shall we try that new place?

A: Ha! Yes, let's. ☕

💡 "used to" in speech

When we speak, used to is usually said quickly, more like "yoosta". That's normal and natural to hear. But when you write, spell it fully: used to (affirmative) and use to (after did/didn't). Keep the fast sound for speaking only.

  2. (have short hair / have long hair)

3. I used to play football, but now I watch football.

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use used to + base verb for past habits and states that are no longer true.
  • The affirmative is the same for every person — no -s for he/she.
  • After did / didn't, write use to (drop the -d).
  • Compare then and now with "used to …, but now …".

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I didn't used to smoke." ❌ → "I didn't use to smoke." ✅ (no -d after did/didn't).
  • "Did you used to play?" ❌ → "Did you use to play?" ✅ (drop the -d in questions).
  • "I used to lived there." ❌ → "I used to live there." ✅ (base verb after used to).
  • "Yesterday I used to see a film." ❌ → "Yesterday I saw a film." ✅ (one time = past simple, not used to).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • used to + base verb = a past habit or state that is no longer true.
  • The affirmative never changes for the person: I / she / they used to…
  • Negative: didn't use to; question: Did … use to? — drop the -d after did.
  • Use it for repeated habits or long states, not single past events.
  • Compare eras with "used to …, but now …".

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the difference between "I used to live there" and "I lived there"?

Both are correct! "I lived there for two years" simply states a finished fact. "I used to live there" adds the feeling: it was true for a long time, and now it's different. Use used to when you want to highlight that the situation has changed.

Why do we drop the "-d" in "didn't use to"?

Because did and didn't already carry the past tense. English doesn't like to mark the past twice, so used loses its -d and becomes use. It's the same idea as "I didn't go" (not "didn't went"). In speech you can't hear the difference, but in writing it matters.

Can I use "used to" for the future or the present?

No — used to is only for the past. For present habits, use the present simple with an adverb: "I usually walk to work." (Careful: "be used to" — with am/is/are — is a different phrase meaning "familiar with". You'll meet that at a higher level.)
