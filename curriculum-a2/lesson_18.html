🧵 Lesson 6.3: Linking Words — and, but, so, because, although

Short sentences are fine. But real, natural English joins them together: "I was tired, so I went to bed." "I like tea, but I prefer coffee." "We stayed home because it rained." Linking words are the thread that sews your ideas into smooth, grown-up sentences. Let's pick up the needle. ✨

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use and (add), but (contrast), and so (result)
  • Give reasons with because and see the because / so pair
  • Show contrast with although / though and compare it to but
  • Punctuate linkers correctly and combine short sentences into fluent ones

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Retell your day (or a small story) in 6+ joined sentences using and, but, so, because, and although — spoken to a partner or recorded on your phone.

Sewing Ideas Together

When we speak like beginners, our sentences are short and separate: "I was tired. I went to bed. It was late." That's clear, but it feels choppy. Linking words join these small ideas into one flowing sentence: "I was tired, so I went to bed because it was late."

Each linker has a job. and adds. but shows a difference. so shows a result. because gives a reason. although shows a surprise or contrast. Once you know the jobs, you can join almost anything. 🔑

🧠 Mindset

You already use these words a little — everyone knows and and but. Today you're just adding a few more threads (so, because, although) and learning where the commas go. You don't need long, perfect sentences right away. Start by joining two short ideas. Then three. Longer sentences come with practice, so be patient with yourself. 🌱

The Five Linkers

Here is the whole lesson in one table. Keep it close — it shows each linker, its job, and an example:

LinkerJobExample
andadd another ideaI made tea and sat down.
butshow a contrast / differenceI like tea, but I prefer coffee.
soshow a resultI was tired, so I went to bed.
becausegive a reasonI went to bed because I was tired.
although / thoughcontrast (a surprise)Although it rained, we went out.

💡 A note on "though"

though means the same as although, but it's a little more casual. "Although it rained, we went out" = "Though it rained, we went out." You can even put though at the very end in speech: "It rained. We went out, though." At A2, just remember: although and though do the same job as a stronger, more formal cousin of but.

and, but & so

These three are the everyday joiners. They sit between two ideas and glue them together:

  • and adds a second idea that fits: "We cooked dinner and watched a film."
  • but shows the second idea is different or surprising: "It was cold, but sunny."
  • so shows the second idea is the result of the first: "It was cold, so I wore a coat."

She studied hard and passed the exam. · The film was long, but good. · The shop was closed, so we went home.

✅ From short to smooth

Watch three short sentences become one: "I was hungry. I made a sandwich. It was tasty." → "I was hungry, so I made a sandwich, and it was tasty." Same ideas — but now they flow. 🥪

because vs so

This is the most useful pair in the lesson. because and so describe the same situation, but from opposite ends. One gives the reason; the other gives the result.

LinkerWhat follows itExample
becausethe reason (the cause)I went to bed because I was tired.
sothe result (what happened)I was tired, so I went to bed.

Look closely — both sentences describe the same night. Only the order and the linker change:

I was tired, so I went to bed. (tired = reason first, then result) · I went to bed because I was tired. (result first, then reason)

💡 Which one first?

After so comes the result. After because comes the reason. A quick test: you can answer a "Why?" question with because. "Why did you go to bed?" — "Because I was tired." You cannot answer "Why?" with so.

although vs but

Both although and but show a contrast — two ideas that don't seem to fit together. The difference is where they go:

  • but joins two ideas in the middle: "It rained, but we went out."
  • although usually starts the sentence (or the second half): "Although it rained, we went out."
With butWith although
It rained, but we went out.Although it rained, we went out.
He's rich, but he isn't happy.Although he's rich, he isn't happy.
The test was hard, but I passed.Although the test was hard, I passed.

⚠️ Watch Out: don't use both together

English uses one contrast word per pair of ideas — not two. ✅ "Although it rained, we went out." · ✅ "It rained, but we went out." · ❌ "Although it rained, but we went out." Pick although or but, never both.

Punctuation & Combining

A few simple comma habits will make your longer sentences look right:

  • Put a comma before but and so when they join two full ideas: "I was tired, so I rested."
  • because and and usually take no comma: "I rested because I was tired." "I rested and slept."
  • When although starts the sentence, put a comma after the first half: "Although it rained, we went out."
⚠️ Important Note: because can start a sentence or join in the middle. In writing, a full sentence should have both halves: "Because I was tired, I rested" ✅ or "I rested because I was tired" ✅. Avoid a lonely half-sentence like "Because I was tired." on its own.

Now let's hear two friends use reasons and contrasts as they chat about the weekend:

A: Did you go to the market on Saturday?

B: No, I didn't, because it was raining. So I stayed home and cooked.

A: Nice! Although the weather was bad, I went for a run.

B: Really? I wanted to run too, but I felt lazy!

A: Ha! I was cold, so I ran fast and finished quickly.

B: Good idea. Let's meet on Sunday, though — the forecast is sunny, so we can walk in the park. ☀️

💡 Combining is a skill

Try this: take any two short sentences and ask, "What's the link between them?" If the second adds → use and. If it's different → but/although. If it's a result → so. If it's a reason → because. Naming the link tells you the word. 🎯

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Name the link first: add → and, contrast → but/although, result → so, reason → because.
  • Put a comma before but and so when they join two full ideas.
  • Put a comma after the although half when it starts the sentence.
  • Combine short sentences into longer ones — it sounds more natural and fluent.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "Although it rained, but we went out." ❌ → "Although it rained, we went out." ✅ (not both).
  • "I was tired, because I slept." ❌ (wrong logic) → "I slept because I was tired." ✅ (reason after because).
  • "I was tired so tired I slept." ❌ → "I was tired, so I slept." ✅ (comma before so; one idea each side).
  • "Because I was tired." (alone) ❌ → "I rested because I was tired." ✅ (finish the sentence).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • and adds · but contrasts · so gives a result · because gives a reason · although contrasts.
  • because + reason, so + result — the same event from opposite ends.
  • although and but both show contrast — use one, never both together.
  • Comma before but and so; comma after the although half; usually no comma with and or because.
  • Combining short sentences makes your English longer, smoother, and more natural.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the real difference between "so" and "because"?

They describe the same link but from opposite directions. because introduces the reason: "I stayed home because it rained." so introduces the result: "It rained, so I stayed home." A quick test: only because can answer a "Why?" question.

Is it okay to start a sentence with "and" or "but"?

In friendly, everyday writing and speech, yes — people do it all the time: "But that's another story." In careful, formal writing, it's safer to keep and and but in the middle, joining two ideas. At A2, focus on joining within a sentence first.

When do I use "although" instead of "but"?

Both show contrast. Use although when you want to start with the surprising idea: "Although it was late, she kept working." Use but to join in the middle: "It was late, but she kept working." Just don't use both in the same sentence.
