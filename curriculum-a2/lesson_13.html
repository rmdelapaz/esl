⏳ Lesson 5.1: Past Continuous — What Was Happening

You already know how to say what happened yesterday with the past simple: "I cooked dinner." But sometimes we want to freeze a moment and describe the action in progress — what was happening at 8 o'clock, right in the middle. For that, English uses the past continuous. In this lesson you'll learn to paint the picture of a past moment, so your stories come alive. 🎥

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form the past continuous with was / were + verb-ing
  • Make negatives (wasn't / weren't + -ing) and questions (What were you doing?)
  • Set a scene and describe an action in progress at a past time
  • Describe two actions happening at the same time with while

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe what you were doing at three different times yesterday in 6+ sentences using the past continuous — spoken to a partner or recorded on your phone.

Freezing a Past Moment

Imagine someone asks you: "What were you doing at eight o'clock last night?" You don't want to say only "I cooked dinner" — that sounds finished. Instead, you want to point to that exact moment and describe the action in the middle of happening: "At eight o'clock I was cooking dinner." The cooking wasn't finished — it was in progress. That is exactly the job of the past continuous. 🍳

Think of a photograph of a past moment. The past simple says what happened, start to end. The past continuous holds the picture still and shows you the action mid-way through. Both are useful, and soon you'll use them together.

🧠 Mindset

Good news: you already know both parts of this tense. In A2 Lesson 1.1 you learned was and were. And you've been using the -ing form since the present continuous ("I'm cooking"). The past continuous simply joins them: was/were + -ing. You're not learning something brand new — you're combining two things you already have. If it feels tricky at first, that's normal. Add the magic word: "I can't use the past continuous smoothly… yet." 🌱

The Form: was/were + -ing

The pattern is simple and never changes: subject + was / were + verb-ing. You only need to choose was or were for the person — and the choice is exactly the same as with was/were from Lesson 1.1.

I was cooking dinner. · She was reading a book. · They were watching TV.

Here is the verb for every person. Notice the two groups: I / he / she / it use was, and you / we / they use were.

Personwas / wereExample
Iwas + -ingI was working at 9 p.m.
youwere + -ingYou were sleeping.
he / she / itwas + -ingShe was studying English.
wewere + -ingWe were having lunch.
theywere + -ingThey were playing football.

    BE --> V["verb + ing"]

💡 A time word usually points to the moment

The past continuous loves a moment in time. Add words like at 8 p.m., at that time, this morning, yesterday afternoon, or then: "At midnight we were sleeping." These words show when the action was in progress.

-ing Spelling Recap

The -ing spelling is the same one you learned for the present continuous. Here is a quick reminder so your writing stays correct:

RuleVerb-ing form
Most verbs: just add -ingwatch, play, readwatching, playing, reading
Ends in -e: drop the e, add -ingmake, write, havemaking, writing, having
Short verb, one vowel + one consonant: double itrun, sit, swimrunning, sitting, swimming
Ends in -ie: change to -yinglie, dielying, dying
⚠️ Important Note: Some verbs describe states, not actions, and we usually don't use them in the continuous: know, like, want, need, love. Say "I knew the answer," not "I was knowing the answer." Keep the continuous for real actions you can picture happening.

Negatives & Questions

To make a negative, add not after was/were — just like the negative of was/were in Lesson 1.1. The short forms are wasn't and weren't.

PersonNegative (short form)Example
I / he / she / itwasn't + -ingHe wasn't listening to me.
you / we / theyweren't + -ingThey weren't working yesterday.

To make a question, put was/were at the front, then the subject, then the -ing verb.

QuestionShort answer (yes)Short answer (no)
Were you sleeping?Yes, I was.No, I wasn't.
Was she working?Yes, she was.No, she wasn't.
Were they waiting?Yes, they were.No, they weren't.
⚠️ Important Note: In the short yes answer, don't drop the verb: say "Yes, I was." The action verb (-ing) is not repeated — was or were is enough.

You can also ask for information with Wh- words:

What were you doing at 9? Where was she going? Who were they talking to? Why was he crying? What were we watching?

Setting the Scene

One of the best jobs of the past continuous is to set a scene — to describe the background of a story. Before you tell what happened, you paint the picture: what was going on all around.

"It was a quiet evening. The rain was falling softly. My family was watching TV, and I was reading a book by the window."

Can you see the picture? Nothing is finished — everything is happening at once, in the middle. That is the feeling of the past continuous: a living moment, not a list of finished events.

✅ Why this matters

Good stories almost always begin with a scene. When you can set the scene with the past continuous, your English sounds richer and more natural. You are no longer only saying what happened — you are showing what it was like. That's a big step up from A1. 🌟

Two Actions at Once

We also use the past continuous for two actions happening at the same time in the past. We often join them with the word while. Both verbs are in the past continuous, because both were in progress together.

"While I was reading, she was cooking." · "He was studying while the children were playing."

graph LR
    A["I was reading"] --- W["at the same time"]
    W --- B["she was cooking"]

💡 while = at the same time

The word while tells the listener: these two actions overlapped. You can put while at the start or in the middle: "While I was reading, she was cooking" = "She was cooking while I was reading." The meaning is the same. If while comes first, remember the comma in the middle.

Now let's hear two friends remember a past moment:

A: I called you at nine last night, but you didn't answer. What were you doing?

B: Sorry! I was taking a shower at nine. What were you doing so late?

A: I was cooking a big dinner. My brother was helping me in the kitchen.

B: Nice! And what was your sister doing? Was she helping too?

A: No, she wasn't. She was watching a film while we were cooking. She only came for the food!

B: Ha! That sounds like her. It was raining here all evening, so I was just relaxing at home.

⚠️ Watch Out: don't forget the verb

The past continuous always needs two parts: was/were and the -ing verb. Don't drop either one. ❌ "I cooking dinner." · ❌ "I was cook dinner." · ✅ "I was cooking dinner."

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use both parts every time: was/were and the -ing verb.
  • Match the person: I / he / she / it → was; you / we / they → were.
  • Add a time word (at 8 p.m., yesterday morning, at that time) to show the moment.
  • Use while to join two actions that were happening at the same time.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I cooking dinner." ❌ → "I was cooking dinner." ✅ (don't drop was/were).
  • "They was watching TV." ❌ → "They were watching TV." ✅ (they takes were).
  • "I was knowing the answer." ❌ → "I knew the answer." ✅ (state verbs don't take the continuous).
  • "She was cook." ❌ → "She was cooking." ✅ (use the -ing form after was/were).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Form: subject + was/were + verb-ing for an action in progress in the past.
  • Use was with I / he / she / it, and were with you / we / they.
  • Negatives: wasn't / weren't + -ing; questions put was/were at the front.
  • The past continuous sets the scene and shows a moment in the middle of happening.
  • Use while for two actions happening at the same time.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the difference between "I cooked" and "I was cooking"?

"I cooked dinner" is the past simple — it tells you the action is finished, complete. "I was cooking dinner" is the past continuous — it shows the action in progress at a moment, not necessarily finished. In the next lesson we'll use both together in stories.

When do I use "was" and when do I use "were"?

It's the same rule you learned in Lesson 1.1. Use was with I, he, she, it. Use were with you, we, they. The -ing verb after it never changes.

Can I use "when" instead of "while"?

You can, but they feel a little different. While is the natural choice for two long actions happening together ("While I was reading, she was cooking"). When is often used for a short action that interrupts a longer one — and that's exactly what we'll explore in the next lesson!
