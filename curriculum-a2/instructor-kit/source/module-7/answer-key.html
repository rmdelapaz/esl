Module 7 — Getting Things Done: Answers

Answers to every Module 7 workbook exercise and self-check question, with the "why," acceptable alternatives, the classroom-materials answers (Riverside map directions, ticket office, restaurant bookings, store policy, clinic appointments, pharmacy), and the errors you can expect to hear most.

Lessons 7.1–7.3Classes C20–C229 self-check items15 exercises
Marking principle

Module 7 is functional: mark whether the task gets done, politely and clearly. A direction with a small grammar slip ("Turn to left") that gets the partner to the right box is a success with a note. A grammatically perfect order that sounds rude ("I want the soup.") is a register point to coach, not a wrong answer. American English is the model; British forms from the online lessons (single, return, straight on, first turning, starter, bill, zip, fitting room, Have you got…?, chemist's) are always acceptable.

7.1 · Travel & Directions (C20)

Exercise 1 — Listen and follow the map (Script 7.1-A)
  1. the park (straight, cross Main Street at the lights, on your right)
  2. the hotel (right at the lights onto Main Street, past Lake Avenue, on your right)
  3. the café (straight to Hill Street, left, past Oak Avenue, on your right)
  4. the bank (straight, cross River Road, first building on your left)

Common wrong answers: post office for 1 (left/right mix-up: it is opposite the park) and hospital for 2 (left/right again). Walk the route on slide 5 with the learner who chose it.

Exercise 2 — Read and find

a) pharmacy · b) museum · c) school · d) movie theater

Distractors: a) bank (opposite, on the left) · b) supermarket (on the right) · c) library (opposite, on the right) · d) hospital (it is also past Lake Avenue, but on your right).

Exercise 3 — The missing word

1 get · 2 straight · 3 left · 4 opposite · 5 platform · 6 round-trip · 7 off · 8 change

Also correct: 4 across from is a good synonym, but it is two words, and the box word is opposite. 6 return (UK). 5 track is what many U.S. stations say; accept it. Wrong: "go to straight," "get out the bus."

Exercise 5 — At the ticket office (Script 7.1-B)

round-trip · to Lakeview · $17.50 · leaves at 9:45 · platform 3 · change? no (it's direct; it arrives at 10:35)

Accept "seventeen fifty" in words. Learners often write $70.50 (seventy/seventeen): drill the stress, sevenTEEN vs SEVenty.

Speaking — write directions (open)

Check: 4+ steps, imperatives with no subject, one position phrase at the end. Model (★ to the hospital): Go straight to the traffic lights. Turn right onto Main Street. Go past Lake Avenue. The hospital is on your left, opposite the hotel. You can't miss it! Learners must not be asked to write directions to their home.

Self-check
Q1
You need help to find the bank. What is the most polite start?
A) Where bank?
B) Excuse me, how do I get to the bank?
C) You must tell me the bank.
Why: open with Excuse me and use a full question with do. A is too short and direct; C sounds like an order.
Q2
You want to go to Chicago and come back the same day.
A) A one-way to Chicago.
B) A round-trip to Chicago.
C) A platform to Chicago.
Why: round-trip = there and back (UK return). A one-way ticket doesn't bring you home; a platform is where you wait.
Q3 · new
You are on a bus. Your stop is next. What do you do?
A) I get off the bus.
B) I get out the bus.
C) I get down the bus.
Why: buses, trains and planes take on/off. Get out of is for cars and taxis (and B is missing of). C translates Spanish bajar / Tagalog bumaba.
Errors you will hear most (7.1)

"How I get to the station?" · "Where is bank?" · "Go to straight." · "Turn to left." / "in the left" · "get in the bus" · "a ticket for return" · "opposite of the park" (no of) · "lef" / "reft" (pronunciation). Correct the first four; they block communication or sound abrupt.

7.2 · Eating Out & Shopping (C21)

Exercise 1 — Book a table (Script 7.2-A)

Name Silva · 4 people · Saturday · 8:00 (7:30 is fully booked) · special request a table by the window · phone 555-0142

Learners often write 7:30 because they hear it first. That's the point of the task: the first time isn't the final time.

Exercise 2 — too, too much, too many or enough?

1 too · 2 too much (salt can't be counted) · 3 too many (people = plural) · 4 enough (adjective + enough) · 5 enough (enough + noun) · 6 too

Also correct: 2 a lot of / so much fit the grammar but not the meaning (a problem). 6 very is grammatical, but "I can't drink it" shows it is a problem, so too.

Exercise 3 — Complete the polite phrases

1 like · 2 have · 3 check (UK bill) · 4 try · 5 refund · 6 recommend

Exercise 4 — Make it polite (model answers)
  1. I'd like a table, please. / I'd like to book a table, please. / Do you have a table for two?
  2. Could we have the check, please? / Can I have the check, please?
  3. Excuse me. I'm afraid there's a problem with my soup. It's cold. Could I have a hot one, please?
  4. I'm so sorry about that. I'll bring you a hot one right away. / I'm sorry. Let me take it back to the kitchen.

Any answer with a polite opener (I'd like / Could I / Excuse me / I'm sorry) and please is correct. For 3, all three steps (soften → explain → ask) earn full credit; two steps is a success at A2.

Self-check
Q1
The soup has much more salt than you want.
A) There's too many salt.
B) There's too much salt.
C) There's enough salt.
Why: salt is uncountable, so too much. C means the amount is OK, which is the opposite of the problem.
Q2
A jacket is too small for you.
A) It's not enough big.
B) It's not big enough.
C) It's too big enough.
Why: enough goes after an adjective: big enough. C mixes too and enough.
Q3 · new
Your soup is cold. What is the best thing to say to the server?
A) This soup is bad. Take it.
B) Excuse me, I'm afraid there's a problem with my soup. It's cold.
C) I want new soup now.
Why: B softens (Excuse me, I'm afraid…) and explains the problem clearly. A and C are understandable but sound angry, and they don't say what is wrong.
Errors you will hear most (7.2)

"I want a table / Give me…" (register) · "It's enough big." · "too much people" · "This tea is too good." (as praise) · "The account, please." · "Can I try it?" (for clothes: try it on) · "I'm afraid" pronounced as a real fear, with a worried face. Coach the tone as much as the grammar.

7.3 · Health & Appointments (C22)

Exercise 1 — What's the problem?

1 headache · 2 sore throat · 3 stomachache · 4 toothache · 5 backache

Also correct: stomach ache (two words, common in the UK). I have toothache / backache (no a) is also normal. Wrong: "a head pain," "a pain of stomach."

Exercise 2 — since or for?

1 since Monday · 2 for three days · 3 since last night · 4 for a week · 5 since nine o'clock · 6 for two hours

Rule: since + a point in time, for + a length of time. "since three days" is the most common error (French depuis, Spanish desde hace, German seit).

Exercise 3 — Complete the sentences

1 appointment · 2 since · 3 for · 4 should · 5 hurts

Exercise 4 — Match the problem and the advice

1 c · 2 b · 3 d · 4 e · 5 a

Other sensible matches are fine if learners can explain them (e.g. 5 d, 3 a). The point is pairing a problem with helpful advice.

Exercise 5 — Ana calls the clinic (Scripts 7.3-A and 7.3-B)

a) Patient Ana Costa · a sore throat and a fever since Monday · Dr. Lee · today (Wednesday) · 4:15 · 10 minutes early

Friday at 10:30 with Dr. Patel is offered first, but Ana asks for something earlier. Learners who write "Friday" missed the change.

b) One every 3 hours · not more than 6 a day · You should drink lots of water, and you'd better rest. (See the doctor again if it isn't better in a few days.)

Self-check
Q1
Leo started to feel sick on Monday. Now it's Thursday.
A) I've felt sick since Monday.
B) I've felt sick for Monday.
C) I've felt sick since three days.
Why: Monday is a starting point, so since. B needs a length (for three days); C mixes since with a length. (UK: I've felt ill, the online lesson's version, is also correct.)
Q2
Which sentence gives advice correctly?
A) You should to rest.
B) You should see a doctor.
C) You should drinking water.
Why: should + base verb. No to, no -ing.
Q3 · new
Which is the strongest advice?
A) Why don't you rest?
B) You should rest.
C) You'd better see a doctor today.
Why: 'd better is stronger than should: it says "this is important; there may be a problem if you don't." Why don't you…? is the softest, a friendly idea.
Errors you will hear most (7.3)

"I have pain my head." · "I have fever." (no article) · "I'm sick since Monday." / "since three days" · "You should to rest." · "Why you don't rest?" · "You had better to…" · "head-ache" with /tʃ/ (like chair) · "cow" for cough. Correct since/for and should to; recast the rest.
