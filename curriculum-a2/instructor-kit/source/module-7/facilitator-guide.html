Module 7 — Getting Things Done

Three practical classes for the situations adults meet every week. Learners ask the way and buy a ticket, book a table by phone and solve a problem politely in a restaurant or store, then make a clinic appointment and ask a pharmacist for help. The grammar is small (imperatives, too / enough, should / 'd better / Why don't you…?). The real target is getting the job done in English: role-play, role-play, role-play.

3 lessons Lessons 7.1–7.3 Classes C20–C22 Weeks 10–11 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (role-play) → feedback → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-7-getting-things-done.html (19 slides). Print materials/module-7/classroom-materials.html before C20. It holds the flashcards, the Riverside map information gap (A/B), the ticket-office cards with the train timetable, the restaurant booking phone cards, the problem & return role-play cards, the clinic appointment and pharmacy cards, the listening scripts and the Around Town board game.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, and this module starts the course's Listening & Speaking strand, so the listening is the heart of the homework.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.

The through-line

Get there → get what you want → get help. Each class has one repeating shape: learn the phrases → hear a real exchange → do it with a card. In C20 learners find places and buy a ticket. In C21 they book, order and complain politely: "I'm afraid there's a problem with…". In C22 they phone a clinic and talk to a pharmacist. Every class also has a staff side. Many learners work as clerks, servers, cashiers or care workers, so being the person who helps is as useful as being the customer. Always swap roles.

Trauma-informed: places, money and health are personal

Directions: never ask learners to give directions to their own home. Use the Riverside map, the school building, or any public place (a store, a park, the library). Money: restaurants may be a rare treat for some learners. The role-plays use pretend money, and "I'd like the soup, it's the cheapest" is a perfectly good order. Health (C22): learners never talk about their own real health. Every patient in this module is a pretend patient on a card, with a pretend name, birthday and symptoms. Some learners have had frightening medical experiences, have no health insurance, or have survived violence. Do not ask "Are you sick?" or "What's wrong with you?" as a class question. If a learner tells you about a real health problem, thank them, talk privately after class, and point to local help (a community health center; in the U.S., dial 211). You teach language, not medicine.

Before the Module

  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board). Card stock helps.
  • C20: print one Riverside map A/B page per pair (A and B on separate pages, so partners cannot see each other's map) and one ticket-office set per pair (clerk card with timetable + traveler cards).
  • C21: one restaurant booking set per pair (reservation book + caller cards), and one problem & return set per group of 4 (6 customer cards, 2 staff cards). Bring 2–3 real or paper receipts and an old menu if you have one.
  • C22: one clinic set per pair (receptionist calendar + patient cards), one pharmacy set per pair. Optional realia: an empty cough-syrup box or a toy phone. Never bring real medicine to class.
  • Two old phones or two chairs back-to-back make the phone calls real: partners cannot see each other, just like a real call.
  • Print the Around Town board game (one per group of 3–4), plus one die per group and a coin for each player. It is the Module 7 review (C22 fast finishers, or the C23 warmer).

Travel & Directions — Getting Around

Objectives — learners can…

If you run long

Cut the ticket role-play to one traveler card per learner, or move it to the C21 warmer. Never cut the map information gap: it is where learners must listen to real directions and act on them.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning — asking. "How do I get to…?" asks for the route. "Is there a… near here?" asks if the place exists nearby (the speaker doesn't know). "Which way is…?" asks only for the direction.

"Is there a pharmacy near here?" Do I know there is a pharmacy? (No, maybe not.) Can the answer be "No, sorry"? (Yes.)
"How do I get to the bank?" Do I want one word or the whole way? (The whole way.) What's the first word I say to a stranger? ("Excuse me.")

Meaning — giving. Directions use the imperative: the base verb, no subject. It is not rude here. It is normal and friendly for directions and instructions.

"Take the first left." Is it the first street or the second street? (The first.) Do I turn? (Yes, left.) "Go past the bank." Do I go into the bank? (No. I walk by it.)
"The bank is opposite the pharmacy." Is it next to the pharmacy? (No.) Is the street between them? (Yes. They face each other.) Another way to say opposite? (across from.)

Meaning — tickets.

"A round-trip to Lakeview." Do I come back? (Yes.) "A one-way ticket." Do I come back on this ticket? (No.) "It's direct." Do I need to change trains? (No.)

Form.

AskExcuse me, how do I get to + place?Excuse me, how do I get to the station?
Exists?Is there a / an + place + near here?Is there a pharmacy near here?
Givebase verb (no subject): go · turn · take · crossGo straight. Turn left at the traffic lights.
Where?It's + on your left / right · opposite · next to · between… and… · on the cornerIt's on your right, opposite the park.
Vehiclesget on / off a bus, train, plane · get in / out of a car, taxiGet off at the next stop.
TicketA + one-way / round-trip + to + place, please.Two round-trips to Center City, please.

Pronunciation. The polite rise-fall: "ExCUSE me ↗" (small pause), then a friendly falling question. Linking: turn_left, get_off /ɡɛˈdɔf/, next_to /nɛks tə/ (the t of next disappears). Stress: OPposite /ˈɑpəzɪt/, TIMEtable, PLATform, round-TRIP. Straight = /streɪt/ (the gh is silent). In a direction, the key word is stressed: "Turn LEFT at the LIGHTS."

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the question: "the station?" → "get to the station?" → "how do I get to the station?" → "Excuse me, how do I get to the station?" Choral ×3, halves, then individuals.
  2. Substitution (hold up a place flashcard): T: "bank" → S: "Excuse me, how do I get to the bank?" T: "pharmacy… near here" → S: "Is there a pharmacy near here?"
  3. TPR chain: the class says the command together as you do it, then a learner gives the commands and you follow them. Make a deliberate mistake (turn right on "left"); learners correct you.
  4. Ticket lines, open pairs: S1: "A round-trip to Center City, please." S2: "That's eight dollars." S1: "Which platform?" S2: "Platform two." Across the room, then closed pairs, 1 minute.
Map information gap — how to run it
  • Pairs sit face to face with the map cards held up (or a folder standing between them). Both maps show the same streets, the star (You are here, walking north) and the traffic lights.
  • A knows the bank, park, museum, hospital, café and school. B knows the pharmacy, post office, supermarket, library, hotel and movie theater. The other six boxes on each map are blank and numbered.
  • A asks for one of B's places. B gives directions from the star every time. A follows with a finger and writes the name in the box where they arrive. Then B asks. Six places each.
  • At the end, pairs put the maps side by side and check. Wrong box? Walk the route again together and find where the directions went wrong. That talk is great practice too.

Boxes: 1 bank · 2 pharmacy · 3 post office · 4 park · 5 museum · 6 supermarket · 7 library · 8 school · 9 café · 10 hospital · 11 hotel · 12 movie theater. Model directions for all twelve are in the Answer Key.

(The name of the place.) · Workbooks open or closed? (Closed!) · What do you say if it's too fast? ("Sorry, could you say that again, please?")
Differentiation
Literacy support

The arrows on the board and flashcards carry the meaning: learners can point to ⬅ ➡ ⬆ as they listen. In the map task they may draw a line and write only the first letters (B for bank). Give a strip with the six place names to copy into the boxes. In Ex 3 give the word choices on a card. Success = 3 places found and one full question: "Excuse me, how do I get to the…?"

Stretch

Give directions with no finger on the map. Use take the second right, go past, cross, at the end of the street, You can't miss it! In the ticket role-play, the clerk offers a choice ("The 8:30 is cheaper but you change at Center City") and the traveler explains a preference with because / although (recycling C19). Early finishers write directions from the school entrance to a public place nearby.

L1 watch-outs
  • "How I get to the station?" (no do): Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, Vietnamese and Arabic speakers make questions with intonation or a question word only. Point at the do on the board. Accept it for meaning in the map task; recast once.
  • "Turn to left" / "Turn to the left" / "in the left": Spanish a la izquierda, Portuguese à esquerda, French à gauche, Russian налево. Drill "turn left" and "on your left" as fixed chunks. "Go to straight" is the same problem.
  • "Get in the bus" / "get down the bus": Spanish subir / bajar, Portuguese subir / descer, Tagalog sumakay / bumaba, German einsteigen use one verb for all vehicles. Rule of thumb: if you can stand up and walk inside, it's on/off.
  • left / right: Japanese and Korean speakers may merge /l/ and /r/, and right vs light matters here ("turn right at the light"). Show the tongue: l touches behind the teeth, r touches nothing. Hold up the correct hand as a backup.
  • Final consonants: Vietnamese, Thai and Cantonese speakers may say "lef" for left and "strai" for straight. Tap the table on the final sound.
  • /p/ and /b/: Arabic speakers may say "blatform" and "bass the bank" (for pass). Paper in front of the mouth: /p/ makes it move.

Eating Out & Shopping — Booking, Ordering & Problems

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • book a table on the phone: I'd like to book a table for four at seven thirty. · It's under the name…
  • order a meal politely: Could I have…? · I'll have… · What do you recommend? · Could we have the check, please?
  • use too + adjective, too much / too many + noun, adjective + enough, enough + noun

If you run long

This class has two freer tasks. If you must cut, shorten the ordering stage (the dialogue is in the workbook for homework) and give the reservation info gap one round only. Keep the problems & returns carousel: polite problem-solving is the skill learners need most at work and in stores.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

too — meaning. too = more than is OK: a problem. very = strong, but not a problem.

"This jacket is too expensive." Can I buy it? (No.) Is it a problem? (Yes.) "This jacket is very expensive." Can I maybe buy it? (Yes, maybe.)
"This tea is too good." Is that a compliment? (No! It sounds strange.) So we say…? ("very good.")

enough — meaning. enough = the amount you need: OK. not… enough = less than you need: a problem.

"The jeans aren't long enough." Are they too long or too short? (Too short.) Is it a problem? (Yes.) "Is there enough time?" Do we need more time? (Maybe. That's the question.)

Complaint language — meaning. I'm afraid… here does not mean "I'm scared." It is a soft way to start bad news.

"I'm afraid there's a problem with my soup." Am I scared? (No.) Am I angry? (Maybe a little, but I'm polite.) Is it good news or bad news? (Bad news, said softly.)

Form.

too + adjectivea problem: more than OKIt's too expensive. These shoes are too small.
too much / too many + nounmuch = can't count · many = can counttoo much salt · too many people
adjective + enoughOK: as much as you needIt's big enough. It isn't long enough.
enough + nounOK / not OK amountWe don't have enough chairs.
ComplainExcuse me. I'm afraid there's a problem with + noun. + Could I / Could you…?I'm afraid there's a problem with the check. Could you check it, please?
Staff answerI'm (so) sorry about that. + I'll + verbI'm so sorry. I'll bring you a new one right away.

Pronunciation. enough /ɪˈnʌf/: the gh sounds like f, stress on the second syllable. too is stressed and long in a complaint: "It's TOO small." Contractions sound friendly: I'd like /aɪd/, I'll have /aɪl/, I'm afraid. Intonation matters more than grammar here: say the complaint twice, once flat and angry, once warm with a small smile. Learners vote: which one gets a new soup faster? Stress in reFUND (noun and verb), exCHANGE, reCEIPT /rɪˈsiːt/ (the p is silent).

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the booking: "at seven thirty" → "a table for four at seven thirty" → "I'd like to book a table for four at seven thirty." Choral, halves, individuals.
  2. too / enough transformation (hold up a flashcard): T: "The shoes are small." → S: "They're too small." / "They aren't big enough." T: "The coffee is hot." → S: "It's too hot." T: "chairs… not" → S: "We don't have enough chairs."
  3. Soften it: T (flat, rude): "Soup cold." → S (warm): "Excuse me. I'm afraid there's a problem with my soup. It's cold." Then T: "Check wrong." → S: "I'm afraid there's a problem with the check."
  4. Staff reply chain: S1 complains; S2 answers "I'm so sorry about that. I'll…"; S2 turns to S3 with a new problem card. Around the room.

Differentiation
Literacy support

Pictures do the work: the restaurant and clothes flashcards, and the icons on the role-play cards (🥣❄️ = cold soup). Give a sentence-starter strip: "Excuse me. I'm afraid there's a problem with my ___." In the booking task, the host only ticks the time and writes the number of people. Success = one full polite complaint and one staff answer.

Stretch

Customers add a second problem ("…and it's too salty"). Staff offer two choices ("Would you like a refund or an exchange?") and a small extra ("Can I get you a free dessert?"). In the booking, add a special request (a high chair, a quiet table, a vegetarian menu). Write a short, polite complaint email to a store for homework.

Cultural note: tipping and "the check"

In U.S. sit-down restaurants the tip is usually not included; people add about 15–20% for the server. The online lesson's "Is service included?" is more British/European. At a counter or fast-food place, a tip is optional. Present this as information, not a rule to judge: tipping customs differ a lot around the world, and many learners find them confusing. Servers in the class can explain it from their own experience if they want to.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I want…" / "Give me the soup": direct requests are polite in Russian (Дайте…), Arabic, Polish, Chinese and many other languages, but sound rude in English. Treat it as a register issue, not a grammar mistake: "Good English, but in a restaurant we say 'Could I have…?'"
  • "It's too good / too beautiful!" as a compliment: Chinese 太好了 (tài hǎo le), Spanish demasiado (informal), and Tagalog speakers sometimes use "too" to mean "very." The CCQ "Is it a problem?" fixes it.
  • "It's enough big": Spanish bastante grande, Portuguese grande o suficiente/bastante grande, French assez grand, Russian достаточно большой put the word before the adjective. Clap the order: big — ENOUGH.
  • "too much people": Spanish mucha gente and Portuguese muita gente are singular/uncountable. People is plural in English: too many people.
  • "The account, please": Spanish la cuenta, Portuguese a conta, Italian il conto. Teach the check (US) and the bill (UK, also understood in the U.S.).
  • "I'm afraid" taken literally ("Why are you afraid?"). Explain once with the CCQ; it is a very common softener at work.

7.3 · C22

Health & Appointments

90 min
Before you teach C22: pretend patients only

Say it at the start of class and again before each pair task: "Today everyone is a pretend patient. We use the cards. We don't talk about our own health." The journal prompt is about an imagined friend. If a learner chooses to mention something real, don't follow up in front of the group. Say "Thank you. I hope you feel better," and move on. You do not give medical advice. The pharmacy products in the materials are invented.

Objectives — learners can…
  • name body parts and symptoms: a headache, a sore throat, a stomachache, a backache, a toothache, a fever, a cough, a cold, the flu
  • describe symptoms three ways: I've got / I have…, I feel dizzy / sick / tired, My back hurts / It hurts when I…
  • say how long with the chunk I've had… since Monday / for three days (the full present perfect comes in 8.1)
  • make an appointment by phone: I'd like to make an appointment. · Do you have anything on…? · Is there anything earlier?
  • ask a pharmacist: Do you have something for a cough? · How often should I take it?
  • give advice with You should… · You'd better… · Why don't you…? (recycling should from 4.2)

  • Slides 14–18 · flashcards (body, symptoms, clinic) · Clinic appointment set (receptionist calendar + 4 pretend patient cards, 1 set per pair) · Pharmacy set (pharmacist shelf card, 1 per pair)
  • Listening scripts 7.3-A (phoning the clinic) and 7.3-B (at the pharmacy) · Around Town board game (fast finishers)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Symptoms — meaning. I've got and I have mean the same here (both are fine in the U.S.). hurt is a verb; ache lives inside nouns (headache); sore is an adjective (a sore throat).

"It hurts when I swallow." Does it hurt all the time? (No, only when I swallow.) Show me "swallow." (Learners mime.)

How long — meaning. Draw a time line: Monday ● — — — ● today.

"I've had a cough since Monday." When did it start? (On Monday.) Do I have it now? (Yes.) Is "Monday" a point or a length of time? (A point.) So: since or for? (Since.)
"…for three days." Is "three days" a point or a length? (A length.) Can I say "since three days"? (No!)

Advice — meaning. All three are advice. The difference is strength.

"You should rest." Is it a good idea? (Yes.) Is it an order? (No.) "You'd better see a doctor." Stronger or softer? (Stronger.) Is there a problem if you don't? (Maybe, yes.) "Why don't you drink some tea?" Is it really a question about "why"? (No. It's a friendly idea.)

Form.

I've got / I have+ a / an + problemI've got a headache. I have a sore throat.
I feel+ adjectiveI feel dizzy. I feel sick. I don't feel well.
hurtsMy + body part + hurts · It hurts when I + verbMy back hurts. It hurts when I walk.
How long?I've had + problem + since + point / for + lengthsince Monday · since 9 o'clock · for three days · for a week
AppointmentI'd like to make an appointment. · Do you have anything on + day?Do you have anything on Monday morning?
Adviceshould / 'd better / Why don't you + base verbYou should rest. You'd better not drive. Why don't you take a day off?

Pronunciation. ch = /k/: headache /ˈhɛdeɪk/, stomachache /ˈstʌməkeɪk/. gh = /f/: cough /kɔf/ (like enough last class). appointment /əˈpɔɪntmənt/: stress on POINT. You'd better is fast: /jʊd ˈbɛtər/; in American speech the tt is a quick d-like tap ("bedder"). Many Americans drop the 'd in speech ("You better…"); accept it, but write You'd better. Why don't you links to /waɪ doʊntʃu/ ("why-don-chu"). I've had → /aɪv hæd/: make learners hear the 've.

Drill script
  1. Mime drill: you mime (hand on head); the class says "I've got a headache." Hand on throat + swallow → "It hurts when I swallow." Wobbly walk → "I feel dizzy."
  2. since / for sort: T says a time; learners call "since!" or "for!": Monday · three days · last night · a week · 9 o'clock · two hours. Then full sentences: T: "a cough… Tuesday" → S: "I've had a cough since Tuesday."
  3. Advice chain: S1 (with a patient card): "I've got a bad cough." S2: "You should…" S3: "You'd better…" S4: "Why don't you…?" Then S4 reads a new problem.
  4. Phone lines, open pairs: S1: "I'd like to make an appointment, please." S2: "We have Thursday at ten." S1: "Is there anything earlier?" S2: "Today at four." S1: "Today at four. Thank you."
Clinic information gap — how to run it
  • The receptionist's calendar shows next week at "Riverside Health Center": free times, booked times, and one urgent slot today.
  • Each pretend-patient card has a name, a birthday, 2–3 symptoms with since/for, and when the patient is free (e.g. "You work mornings Monday to Wednesday"). The patient must find a time that fits. The receptionist asks: "What's it about?" "How long have you had it?" "What's your date of birth?"
  • Only a patient whose card says "feel very unwell" should ask for the urgent slot today. If two pairs both want it, that's real life: "I'm sorry, today is full now."
  • Step 2 (Freer 2): the same patient goes to the pharmacy with the "Pharmacy" line on their card.

Possible bookings are in the Answer Key. Any time that fits the card is correct.

ICQs before the clinic and pharmacy tasks
Clinic: Is this your real health or a card? (A card. Pretend!) · Can you see your partner? (No, it's a phone call.) · What's your first sentence? ("I'd like to make an appointment, please.") · The time is wrong for you. What do you ask? ("Do you have anything on…? / Is there anything earlier?") · Receptionist, what do you write? (The name and the time.)
Pharmacy: What do you ask for? ("something for" + the problem) · What's the second question? ("How often should I take it?") · Pharmacist, how many kinds of advice do you give? (Three: should, 'd better, why don't you.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Symptom pictures on the patient cards; learners point and say "I've got a…". Teach one "how long" chunk only: "I've had it since Monday." In the clinic task, the receptionist circles a time instead of writing the name. In Ex 2, give a since card and a for card to hold up. Success = book one appointment and ask "How often should I take it?"

Stretch

Patients add "It started when…" and "It's worse at night." Receptionists offer two choices and confirm everything back ("So that's Tuesday at 4:45 with Dr. Lee."). Pharmacists warn with You'd better not… ("You'd better not drive"). Write a short message to a manager: "I'm sorry, I can't come to work today. I've had… since…" (a pretend person).

L1 watch-outs
  • "I have pain my head" / "My head is hurting me": Spanish me duele la cabeza / tengo dolor de cabeza, Portuguese estou com dor de cabeça, Arabic عندي ألم في رأسي. Give the chunks: I have a headache. My head hurts.
  • "I'm sick since Monday" / "since three days": French depuis, Spanish desde hace, German seit, Russian уже три дня use the present tense and one word for both. Don't teach the full present perfect today; drill the chunk I've had… since / for….
  • "I have fever / I have cold" (no article): Spanish tengo fiebre, Portuguese, Russian, Chinese, Vietnamese. Recast: "a fever," "a cold." But: the flu, and toothache with or without a.
  • "You should to rest" / "You must to…": Spanish deberías, Portuguese deveria and Arabic learners generalize to from have to. Point at the "base verb" on the board.
  • "Why you don't rest?": Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic and Spanish speakers keep statement word order. Clap the order: WHY — DON'T — YOU.
  • sick vs ill: in British English I feel sick can mean "I might vomit"; in the U.S. sick = unwell. The online lesson explains this. "I don't feel well" is always safe.

Closing the Module

End of C22: Module 7 wrap-up

Recap on slide 19: Excuse me, how do I get to…? → I'm afraid there's a problem with… → I'd like to make an appointment. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you.

Around Town board game: use it for C22 fast finishers or as a 10-minute C23 warmer. It revisits every Module 7 skill and makes a good review before the final assessment (C26). It works well again in the C26 catch-up time.

Watch for in Module 8: 7.3's I've had… since / for… is the doorway to 8.1 (present perfect with for, since, just, already, yet). Remind learners in C23: "You already know this! 'I've had a cough since Monday.'" The polite phrases from C21 (I'd like to…, Could I…?) link to 8.2's verb patterns (would like to + verb).

Final assessment (C26): functional role-plays from this module (ask the way, buy a ticket, a polite complaint, making an appointment) make excellent speaking-test tasks, always with pretend cards for the health one.
