Module 6 — Joining Ideas Together

Three classes where learners stop speaking in short, separate sentences and start joining them. First they join a condition to a result with the first conditional (If it rains, I'll stay home). Then they join a detail to a noun with relative clauses (the man who lives next door). Last, they join whole ideas with and, but, so, because and although, and write their first connected paragraph.

3 lessons Lessons 6.1–6.3 Classes C17–C19 Weeks 9–10 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs and groups) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-6-joining-ideas.html. Print materials/module-6/classroom-materials.html before C17. It holds the flashcards, the If-chain game cards, the "make a deal" role cards, the Define it! word cards, the apartment-building information gap, the sentence-builder strips and idea cards, and the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons sometimes use British forms (watch a film, neighbour, move house, flat, recognise). Teach watch a movie, neighbor, move, apartment, recognize, and accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • A2 learners know the present simple, will / might (Module 3) and the past simple (Modules 1, 4, 5). This module re-uses all of them. It adds no new tense, only new ways to join. Say this to the class: it lowers anxiety.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Join a result → join a detail → join a story. In C17 learners join a condition to a result, and the If-chain game makes one result grow into the next condition. In C18 they join a detail to a noun, and the Define it! game makes them describe a word without saying it. In C19 they join whole ideas and finish with a short connected paragraph about their job, their town or their weekend. Each class ends with longer speaking or writing than the class before. Tell learners at the start of C17: "In three classes, you will write a real paragraph."

Trauma-informed: plans, people and places can be personal

"If…" sentences are about the future, and for some adults the future is uncertain (immigration papers, work permits, a family far away). Keep the model topics light: weather, weekends, food, the bus. Never ask "What will you do if you can't stay?" In C18, "a person who…" can be a neighbor, a TV character, a coworker or a famous person, not only family. In C19, "my job" can be a past job, a job at home, or a dream job; "my town" can be this town, a hometown, or an imagined town. For every personal task, say the three choices out loud: your real life, a "new identity" card, or an imagined life. Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The first conditional talks about a real possibility in the present or future and its result. The condition may happen or may not. It is not a dream (that is the second conditional, B1).

"If it rains, we'll stay home." Is it raining now? (No / we don't know.) Is rain possible? (Yes.) Past, or future? (Future.) If it rains, what happens? (We stay home.)
"If it rains, we might stay home." Are we sure we'll stay home? (No. Maybe.) "If you don't hurry, you won't catch the bus." Will you catch it? (No.)
"When I get home, I'll call you." Will I get home? (Yes, of course.) "If I get home early, I'll call you." Will I get home early? (Maybe. We don't know.)

Form. Two boxes on the board; the if-box is a "no will zone."

If-clause (condition)If + present simpleIf it rains · If she calls · If we don't hurry
Result clausewill / 'll / won't / might + base verbwe'll stay home · I'll answer · we won't catch the bus
If firstcomma after the if-clauseIf you study, you'll pass.
Result firstno commaYou'll pass if you study.
QuestionWhat will you do if…?What will you do if it rains? — If it rains, I'll stay home.
if / whensame form; when = sureWhen the movie ends, we'll go home.

If a learner says a general truth like "If you heat ice, it melts" (present in both halves), accept it, but don't teach it as a new structure today: it is the zero conditional (B1).

Pronunciation. Use the contractions in speech: I'll /aɪl/, you'll /jʊl/, we'll /wil/ (like "wheel"), it'll /ˈɪtəl/. Many learners drop the /l/ completely, so "I'll call" becomes "I call." Make the "dark l" clear: tongue up, a little "ul" sound. won't /woʊnt/ vs want /wɑnt/: this pair confuses listeners. Drill a minimal pair: "I won't go" (no) / "I want to go" (yes). Intonation: the if-clause rises and pauses at the comma, and the result falls: "If it RAINS ↗ | we'll stay HOME ↘." The comma in writing = the pause in speech.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the model: "stay home" → "we'll stay home" → "If it rains, | we'll stay home." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Show the rise-pause-fall with your hand.
  2. Picture substitution: hold up a flashcard (☀️, 🌧️, 🚌) and a result card. T: "sunny… park" → S: "If it's sunny, I'll go to the park." T: "late… miss the bus" → S: "If I'm late, I'll miss the bus."
  3. Certainty drill: T says the sentence and shows a gesture: thumbs up = will, shrug = might, thumbs down = won't. T: "If I have time… cook dinner" (shrug) → S: "If I have time, I might cook dinner."
  4. Clause flip: T: "If you study, you'll pass." → S: "You'll pass if you study." Write one flip on the board and ask: "Comma?" (No.)
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 asks across the room: "What will you do if it rains on Saturday?" S2: "If it rains, I'll…" Then all pairs for 1 minute with new weather words (cold, sunny, snowy).
The If-chain game — how to run it
  • Groups of 4–5 sit in a circle. Each group gets the starter cards face down and the twist cards face down in a second pile.
  • Round 1 (model): read Script 6.1-C. Show the change on the board: "we'll stay home" → "If we stay home, …" The result loses its 'll and moves into the if-box.
  • Round 2 (play): S1 turns a starter card and says it: "If it rains, I'll stay home." S2 takes the result and makes a new condition: "If I stay home, I'll watch a movie." S3: "If I watch a movie, I'll eat popcorn…" Go around twice.
  • Beep! If anyone says will in the if-half ("If I will stay home…"), the group says "Beep!" and the speaker says it again. That's the only rule the group polices.
  • Twists: every third turn, the speaker takes a twist card first: use might, use won't, start with when, put if in the middle, make it a warning, make it a promise.
  • Finish: when a chain gets back to the start ("…so I'll go outside, and it's raining!"), the group claps and turns a new starter. Stronger groups try to make a chain of 8 without a Beep.
  • Walk around with a notebook: write errors for the feedback stage. Don't stop the game to correct.
ICQs before "Make a deal" and the If-chain
Make a deal: Do you show your card? (No. Just talk.) · How many "If…" sentences? (Three or more.) · When do you stop? (When you agree.)
If-chain: Where does your if-clause come from? (The last person's result.) · "If I will stay home" — OK? (No! Beep!) · When do you take a twist card? (Every third turn.) · Do you write? (No. Only speak.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give the two-box frame on a card: If ___ , I'll ___ . Learners point to the flashcards and say the sentence; the aim is speaking. In Ex 2, do items 1–4 only. In the If-chain, they can use picture flashcards as prompts. A 4-sentence plan in the project is a success.

Stretch

Use unless for "if… not" (receptive: "Unless you hurry, you'll miss the bus"). Add a time and a reason: "If it's sunny on Saturday morning, I'll go hiking, because I need some fresh air." In the If-chain, end the chain with a when sentence. Early finishers write a warning sign for the classroom: "If you leave your phone on, …"

L1 watch-outs
  • "If it will rain…" / "When I will get home…": Russian, Polish, Ukrainian and Turkish use a future form in the condition. French, Spanish and Portuguese use the present after si / if, but a future or subjunctive after quand / cuando / quando, so the when sentence is where these learners slip. Point at the "no will zone" box every time.
  • "If it rain, we stay home" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian): no tense marking in L1, so both the -s and the will disappear. Pull the 'll out with your fingers, and accept progress, not perfection.
  • "If it rained, we will stay" (Arabic): idhā is often followed by a past form in Arabic. Recast: "If it rains…"
  • Clause order (Japanese, Korean, Turkish): the condition always comes first in L1. These learners avoid the result-first order. Practice the flip drill, but don't insist in speech.
  • "I'll to help you" and "I will helps" (many L1s): base verb after will. Point at the form box.
  • Dropped /l/ in I'll, we'll (Vietnamese, Cantonese and other speakers who drop final consonants; also Japanese). The sentence becomes present: "I call you." Model it slowly and let learners say the full I will if the contraction is hard.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A defining relative clause tells us which person, thing or place we mean. It works like a long adjective: a tall man → a man who is tall. Without it, the listener asks "Which one?"

"The man who lives next door is a doctor." Which man? (The man next door.) How many ideas are in the sentence? (Two: he lives next door; he's a doctor.) Is who for a person or a thing? (A person.)
"This is the phone which I bought yesterday." Is which for a person? (No, a thing.) "That's the café where we met." Is the café a person, a thing or a place? (A place.) So which word? (where.)
"the woman who she works at the bank" — OK? (No.) Why not? (who = she. We need only one.)
"That's the book I bought." Is a word missing? (Yes: that / which.) Is the sentence OK? (Yes.) "That's the man lives next door." OK? (No! A verb comes after, so keep who.)

Form.

whopeoplethe doctor who helped me
whichthings & animalsthe phone which broke
thatpeople or things (everyday English), not placesthe song that I like · the man that called
whereplacesthe shop where I work
Joiningreplace he / she / it / there with the pronounI met a man. He works at the bank. → I met a man who works at the bank.
Drop it?only when a new subject followsthe movie (that) we watched · the man lives here ❌

Pronunciation. A defining clause is one tone unit, with no pause and no comma: "the-man-who-lives-next-DOOR." Drill it as one chunk. who /hu/ has a clear /h/ (French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and Russian speakers often drop or change it to /x/). which /wɪtʃ/ sounds the same as witch; don't worry about the "wh." where /wɛr/ vs were /wɜr/: many learners mix them up, so drill the pair once. that in a relative clause is weak: /ðət/.

Drill script
  1. Speed choice: T holds up a Define it! card; the class calls out the pronoun only: (nurse) "who!" · (airport) "where!" · (key) "which!" Get faster.
  2. Joining drill: T says two sentences; S joins. T: "I have a friend. She lives in Texas." → S: "I have a friend who lives in Texas." T: "This is the bus. It goes downtown." → S: "This is the bus which goes downtown." T: "That's the school. I studied there." → S: "That's the school where I studied."
  3. Back-chain the chunk: "next door" → "who lives next door" → "the man who lives next door" → "That's the man who lives next door." One breath, no pause.
  4. Which one? in open pairs: S1 points at something in the room: "Which one is your bag?" S2: "It's the bag which is under the chair." S2 → S3: "Who is Maria?" S3: "She's the woman who is sitting next to the door."
Define it! — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4. One set of word cards, face down. Learners take turns as the describer.
  • The describer takes the top card and looks at it privately. They start with the frame from the card's icon: 👤 "It's a person who…", 📍 "It's a place where…", 🧰 "It's a thing which / that…"
  • They must not say the word, point at the object, or mime. (Literacy-support learners may mime after one sentence.)
  • The first person to guess says the full sentence: "Is it a nurse? A nurse is a person who helps doctors." Then they keep the card. Most cards wins.
  • Stuck after 1 minute? The describer adds a second clue: "It's a thing that you use when it rains." If still stuck, the card goes to the bottom.
  • Stretch round: learners write their own card for a word from their life or job (a forklift, a food bank, a nail salon) and the group guesses.
ICQs before the information gap and Define it!
Information gap: Can you show your card? (No.) · What is the question? ("Who is…? / What is…?") · Short answer or full sentence? (Full: "She's the woman who…")
Define it!: Can you say the word on the card? (No!) · "It's a place…" + who or where? (Where.) · Can you point or mime? (No.) · Who keeps the card? (The person who guesses.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

In Ex 2, do items 1–3. Three spoken definitions is a success.

Stretch

Use a preposition + which: "the café which we met in" (and compare with where). Drop the pronoun in natural speech: "That's the song I love." Describe a person with two clauses: "She's the friend who lives in Chicago and who calls me every Sunday." Tell the class about something they bought or found, using that.

L1 watch-outs
  • "the man who he lives next door" (Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Urdu): these languages often keep a pronoun inside the relative clause, and Arabic needs one for objects. Cross out the extra word on the board every time.
  • Clause before the noun (Chinese, Japanese, Korean): "the lives-next-door man." Build the sentence left to right on the board: noun first, then the clause.
  • "the man which…" / "the book what I bought": Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian use one word (que / che) for people and things, and Russian kotoryj works for both too. Learners pick which or what for everything. Offer that as the safe choice for people and things.
  • "the town that I grew up" / "the town where I grew up in" (Spanish, Portuguese, Tagalog, Russian): mixing that and where. Rule of thumb: place + new subject → where, and no extra preposition.
  • One linker for everything (Tagalog na / -ng, Indonesian yang, Chinese de): learners may use that everywhere, even for places. Accept that for people and things; correct it for places.
  • A comma before the clause ("the man, who lives next door"): German, Russian and Polish always put a comma before a relative clause. At A2, defining clauses have no comma.

6.3 · C19

Linking Words — and, but, so, because, although

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • join ideas with and (add), but (contrast), so (result)
  • give a reason with because, and say the same event with because and with so
  • show a surprising contrast with although / though, and never use although + but together
  • put commas in the right place: before but and so, after the although half
  • turn short, choppy sentences into a connected paragraph (5–7 sentences) about their job, their town or their weekend
Materials
  • Workbook 6.3 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–5, dialogue, "My paragraph" planner)
  • Slides 14–18 · linker flashcards (and, but, so, because, although) · Sentence-builder envelopes: sentence-half strips, linker cards, topic idea cards (1 per group of 3) · one clean sheet of paper per learner
  • Listening scripts 6.3-A (Carlos's strange day) and 6.3-B (optional dictogloss: "My weekend", or use it as the C20 warmer)

Left: the choppy story: I was tired. I went to bed. It was late. → I was tired, so I went to bed because it was late.

Center: five linker cards with a job: and ➕ add · but ↔️ contrast · so ➡️ result · because ❓ reason ("Why?") · although 😮 surprise. Under because / so: I went to bed because I was tired. = I was tired, so I went to bed.

Right: commas: ✅ It rained, but we went out. · ✅ Although it rained, we went out. · ❌ Although it rained, but we went out. · paragraph checklist: topic sentence → 4–5 joined sentences → an ending.

If you run long

Skip step 1 of the sentence builder (the halves) and go straight to the topic envelopes. If the paragraph isn't finished, the learner finishes it at home; collect it at C20. Keep the 5-minute "Share": reading a paragraph to a real listener is the reward.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Each linker has one job. Teach the job, not a translation. The key pair is because (reason) and so (result): the same event from opposite ends.

"I was tired, so I went to bed." What came first? (I was tired.) What's the result? (I went to bed.) "I went to bed because I was tired." Why did I go to bed? (Because I was tired.) Same night, same story? (Yes.)
"Why did you stay home?" Can you answer "So it was raining"? (No.) "Because it was raining"? (Yes.) Which word answers "Why?" (because.)
"Although it rained, we went out." Did it rain? (Yes.) Did we go out? (Yes.) Is that a little surprising? (Yes.) "Although it rained, but we went out." OK? (No. One contrast word.)
"I like tea, but I prefer coffee." Two ideas the same, or different? (Different.) "I made tea and sat down." Different, or one more idea? (One more idea.)

Form.

andadd · usually no commaI made tea and sat down.
butcontrast · comma beforeI like tea, but I prefer coffee.
soresult · comma beforeI was tired, so I went to bed.
becausereason · usually no commaI went to bed because I was tired. / Because I was tired, I went to bed.
although / thoughsurprise contrast · comma after the first halfAlthough it rained, we went out.
✗never two linkers for one linkAlthough… but · Because… so

Pronunciation. because is usually weak: /bɪˈkəz/, stress on the second syllable. In fast speech learners will hear 'cause /kəz/; accept it in speaking, not in writing. although /ɔlˈðoʊ/: stress on -THOUGH, and the th is voiced. Learners mix though / through / thought, so write all three once and say them. Pausing: pause at the comma before but and so, and after the although half: "Although it RAINED ↗ | we went OUT ↘." and is weak /ən/: "tea-n-toast."

Drill script
  1. Linker cue: T says a first half and holds up a linker card; S finishes. T: "I was hungry…" (SO) → S: "I was hungry, so I made a sandwich." T: "I was hungry…" (BUT) → S: "…but I didn't eat." T: "I was hungry…" (BECAUSE) → S: "…because I didn't have breakfast."
  2. because ↔ so flip: T: "I stayed home because it was raining." → S: "It was raining, so I stayed home." Then reverse. Choral, then individuals.
  3. but → although: T: "The test was hard, but I passed." → S: "Although the test was hard, I passed." Tap your palm at the comma.
  4. Grow a sentence: start with "I went to the market." Each learner adds one linker + idea: "…and I bought some fruit" → "…but it was expensive" → "…so I bought only apples" → "…because I love apples!" The class repeats the whole sentence at the end.
Sentence builder → paragraph — how to run it
  • Step 1 · halves (4 min). Each group gets 12 sentence-half strips. The first half ends with a comma or nothing; the second half starts with the linker (except Although, which starts a first half). Groups match them and read the pairs aloud. There is only one perfect match per strip (answers in the Answer Key).
  • Step 2 · join the ideas (8 min). Groups open one topic envelope: My job (Rosa, a hospital cleaner), My town (Emil's small town) or My weekend (Amal's weekend). They lay out 6–8 short idea cards in a sensible order, then put linker cards between them. Rule: use every linker at least once, and one comma card where it belongs.
  • Step 3 · read it. One learner reads the group's version aloud; others listen for the five linkers and hold up a finger for each one.
  • Step 4 · my paragraph (writing stage). Each learner writes their own paragraph on the same topic or a different one. They can copy the group's version, change it for their own life, or use the idea cards as a "new identity."
  • Target: 5–7 sentences, at least 4 different linkers, one because and one although or but. Paragraph shape: a first sentence that says the topic, the middle, an ending ("I like my town because…").
ICQs before the sentence builder and the paragraph
Sentence builder: How many linkers do you use? (All five.) · Do you write, or move the cards? (Move the cards.) · Who reads it at the end? (One person from the group.)
Paragraph: How many sentences? (Five to seven.) · Must it be about your real job? (No. Real, a card, or imagined.) · "Although… but" OK? (No!) · What's in the first sentence? (The topic.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

In the sentence builder, they move and read the cards; that is the task. For the paragraph, give them the frame in the workbook ("My town is ___ . It is ___ , but ___ . I like it because ___ .") and let them copy idea cards. Three joined sentences with and, but, because is a success. They can say it to a partner instead of writing all of it.

Stretch

Use though at the end of a sentence ("It was cold. We had fun, though."). Add a first conditional or a relative clause from C17–C18: "My town is a place where everyone knows you, so…" Write two paragraphs (my town + my weekend). Peer-edit a partner's commas with a pencil.

L1 watch-outs
  • "Although it rained, but we went out" / "Because I was tired, so I slept": Chinese (suīrán… dànshì, yīnwèi… suǒyǐ), Vietnamese (tuy… nhưng, vì… nên) and Korean use a pair of linkers. This is the #1 error of the lesson for these learners. Show two cards in one hand, then drop one.
  • "and… and… and…" chains (Arabic wa, and many learners with oral traditions): long sentences joined only with and. Praise the fluency, then ask "What's the link here: result or reason?"
  • "Because it was raining." alone as a written sentence: Spanish and Portuguese speakers use porque for both "why" and "because," and many learners write the answer to a question as a full sentence. In speech it's fine; in the paragraph, join it.
  • so = "very": "I was so tired so I slept." Show the two meanings: so tired (very) vs , so I slept (result).
  • Comma splices and run-ons: Russian, Polish and German put commas by grammar rules that differ from English. Keep to the three comma habits on the board; don't teach more at A2.
  • /ð/ in although: "alzo," "altho" with /d/ or /z/ (many L1s). Tongue between the teeth, voice on. Don't stop fluency work for it.

Closing the Module

End of C19: Module 6 wrap-up

Recap on slide 18: If it rains, I'll stay home → the friend who lives next door → I was tired, so I went to bed. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Review it in a C20 or C21 warmer.

Collect the paragraphs (or photos of them) at C20. Write one kind comment and underline every correct linker. Keep them: in C25 (the Module 8 review project, "My Life Story & Future Plans") learners re-use these linkers, first conditionals and relative clauses. The C19 paragraph is a useful "before" sample for their progress.

Watch for in Module 7: travel, shopping and health tasks are full of chances to join ideas: "If you turn left, you'll see the bank." "It's the store where I buy my medicine." "I'd like the soup, but not the salad." Praise joined sentences whenever you hear them.

The final assessment (C26) covers all eight modules. From this module it tests: the first conditional (no will after if, will / might, if / when), who / which / that / where and joining, and the five linkers with because / so and although / but. In any writing task from now on, look for joined sentences, not only correct ones. Today's paragraph is the first step.
