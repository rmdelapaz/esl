Module 5 — Telling Stories

Three classes that turn a list of past events into a real story. Learners freeze a past moment with the past continuous ("At eight I was cooking"), mix it with the past simple to show a background and a sudden event ("I was walking home when it started to rain"), and look back at old habits with used to ("I used to walk to work, but now I drive"). The module starts right after Progress Test 1, so C14 opens by handing the tests back in a warm, low-stress way.

3 lessons Lessons 5.1–5.3 Classes C14–C16 Weeks 7–8 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs and groups) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-5-telling-stories.html. Print materials/module-5/classroom-materials.html before C14. It holds the flashcards, the Alibi Game mysteries and planning sheets, the two picture stories for sequencing, the "Something happened!" story cards, the Then & Now character cards, the listening scripts and the Story Road board game.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear every model sentence and dialogue again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons use some British forms: film, having a shower, travelling, flat, cinema, colour, Shall we…?, and football for the sport Americans call soccer. Teach movie, taking a shower, traveling, apartment, movie theater, color, Should we…? / Let's…, and accept the British forms when learners use them.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

A moment → a story → a life. In C14 learners freeze one moment: what was happening at 8 p.m.? In C15 something happens in the middle of that moment: the long line (past continuous) meets the dot (past simple), and a story is born. In C16 learners zoom out to whole periods of life: what used to be true, and what is true now. Past-tense storytelling is the spine of A2. Module 1 gave learners the past simple; this module gives their stories a background, a surprise and a "before and after." Module 8 will bring it all together in the life-story project.

Trauma-informed: the past can be a hard place

This whole module asks about the past: last night, "something that happened," how life used to be. For some adults the past holds war, loss, detention, or a home they cannot go back to. Three rules for every personal task in this module:

  • Invented is always fine. Say it out loud every time: "Your real life, or a character card, or make it up." Choosing a card is normal, not a sign that something is wrong.
  • Never make the home country the topic. The online lesson 5.3 suggests "your hometown." In class, used to is about childhood, a job you used to have, or a character card. A learner may choose to talk about their hometown, but you never ask for it.
  • The Alibi Game is a silly mystery, not an interrogation. Questions like "Where were you at 8 p.m.?" can feel like a police interview to someone who has lived through one. So the "crime" is a missing birthday cake, the roles are "detectives" and "cake suspects," the alibi is invented together, and anyone can choose to be a detective instead of a suspect. Never ask a learner where they really were last night in front of the class; in pair drills, invented answers are welcome.

Before the Module

  • Mark Progress Test 1 before C14. Write a short, positive comment on every paper: one thing they did well + one thing to practice. Collect 4–5 common errors from different papers for the C14 board (no names). Note which learners scored below about 60% so you can offer a quiet 1:1 chat at the break.
  • Print from the materials sheet: the flashcards (one set for the board); the Alibi Game mystery cards, suspect planning sheets and detective notes (one set per group of 4); the picture-story cards, Story A and Story B (one set of each per group of 3); the "Something happened!" story cards (one A/B set per pair); the Then & Now character cards (keep them on a side table where anyone can take one); and the Story Road board (one per group of 3–4) with a die and small counters (coins work).
  • Bring a small bell, a whistle, or just clap for the "Freeze!" warmer in C15.
  • Optional for C16: one "then" photo of yourself (or a drawing), such as you as a child, with long hair, in an old uniform. It makes the used to lead-in personal and warm. A character from the slides works just as well.

If you run long

The test return must stay at 12 minutes. Cut the dictation (Script 5.1-B) and Ex 5 (set it as homework). If the Alibi Game is short on time, do one round only and skip the role swap. Never cut the Alibi Game: it is where was/were + -ing becomes real questions and answers.

Returning Progress Test 1 (12 min) — keep it warm

1. Celebrate first (3 min). Before any paper comes out, write "We can…" on the board and list three things the whole class did well (for example: tell a story in the past · compare things · talk about plans with "going to"). Say it out loud.

Four modules! You can talk about the past, compare things, make plans, and give advice, in English. That's a lot. Give yourselves a hand.

2. Return privately (2 min). Hand papers back face-down or in folders. Never read scores aloud and never compare learners. Everyone gets a written comment: one strength + one thing to practice.

3. "Let's fix it!" (5 min). Show 4 anonymous errors from the tests, for example: Yesterday I goed to the market. · Did you went to the party? · She is more tall than her brother. · I have visited Mexico last year. (Use your own learners' errors if you can: You must to see a doctor. · How much apples do we need? are also common.) Pairs fix them. Take answers. Praise the fixing, not the mistake-free papers.

4. One goal (2 min). Each learner writes one goal in the workbook box ("My goal after Progress Test 1") or on a sticky note: "I want to practice ___. I will ___." Give two examples: "I want to practice irregular verbs. I will say five verbs every morning." / "I want to practice listening. I will use the 🔊 buttons at home."

A test is a map. It tells us where we are. It doesn't tell us who we are.

Some adults carry hard memories of school and tests. Keep your voice light and move on quickly. Offer a 1:1 chat at the break to anyone who struggled (below about 60%), and plan catch-up with the Module 1–4 online lessons. Don't let the test return grow past 12 minutes. The best encouragement is a good, successful lesson straight after it, and today's grammar is kind: learners already know was/were (Lesson 1.1) and -ing (present continuous).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The past continuous describes an action in progress at a moment in the past. It was not finished at that moment; it was "in the middle." Use the camera image: the past simple tells the whole event; the past continuous is a photo taken in the middle of it.

"At eight o'clock I was cooking dinner." At eight, did I start cooking? (No, I started before.) At eight, was the cooking finished? (No.) So at eight I was… (in the middle of cooking).
"I cooked dinner." Is it finished? (Yes.) "I was cooking dinner at eight." Do we know if I finished? (No, we only know it was happening at eight.)
"While I was reading, she was cooking." How many actions? (Two.) One after the other, or at the same time? (At the same time.)

Form. Learners already have both parts. Say: "You know was/were from Lesson 1.1, and -ing from 'I'm cooking.' Put them together."

PositiveI / he / she / it + was + verb-ing
you / we / they + were + verb-ing		She was studying. · They were playing soccer.

State verbs. know, like, want, need, love are not actions you can "photograph," so they stay in the past simple: I knew the answer (not "I was knowing"). Keep this light; one example on the board is enough at A2.

Pronunciation. In a sentence, was and were are weak and fast: /wəz/, /wər/. "I was COOKing" sounds like "I wuz COOKing." The stress is on the -ing verb. In short answers they are strong: "Yes, I WAS." /wɑz/ · "No, they WEREN'T." /wɜrnt/. Wasn't has two syllables (/ˈwʌz.ənt/); weren't has one. The -ing ending is /ɪŋ/: no hard /g/ at the end, and not /ɪn/.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the model sentence: "dinner" → "cooking dinner" → "was cooking dinner" → "I was cooking dinner" → "At eight o'clock I was cooking dinner." Choral ×3 with weak was, then half the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Substitution drill (hold up a flashcard, then say a subject): T: "They… 📺" → S: "They were watching TV." T: "She… (read)" → S: "She was reading." T: "We… (bus stop)" → S: "We were waiting for the bus." Speed up when they are ready.
  3. Negative transformation: T: "At midnight I was dancing." (shake head) → S: "At midnight I wasn't dancing." Then with they: "They weren't…"
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "What were you doing at 7 a.m. yesterday?" S2: "I was having breakfast." (real or invented) S2 → S3 with a new time. Then yes/no: "Were you sleeping at 6 a.m.?" — "Yes, I was. / No, I wasn't."
The Alibi Game — how to run it (22 min)
  • Set-up (2 min). Read a mystery card aloud with a dramatic voice: "Last night, between 7 and 9 p.m., someone ate the birthday cake in the school kitchen. Only crumbs! Two people were in the building. Were they together all evening, like they say?" Make groups of 4: 2 suspects + 2 detectives. Anyone may choose to be a detective. It's a game; the evening is invented.
  • Plan (5 min). The two suspects sit together with the suspect planning sheet and invent their shared evening: where they were and what they were doing at 7:00, 7:30, 8:00, 8:30 and 9:00, plus details (what was the weather doing? what were you eating? what was on TV?). The two detectives use the question cards to write 6–8 questions on their notes sheet: "What were you doing at 7:30? What was your friend wearing?"
  • Interview (8 min). Suspect 1 stays; Suspect 2 goes to another group's table (or waits by the door with their back turned) for 4 minutes. The detectives ask their questions and write short notes. Swap: Suspect 2 comes back and answers the same questions. Suspects must not hear each other.
  • Verdict (3 min). Detectives compare the two sets of answers: ✓ same, ✗ different. 3 or more ✗ = "You ate the cake!" Then they report to the class with the past continuous: "At eight, Ana said they were watching TV, but Omar said they were eating pizza!"
  • Swap roles (4 min) with a new mystery card if there is time, or save the second round for the C15 warmer.

Large class: run it once as a whole class first. Two volunteer suspects leave the room to plan, and the whole class writes detective questions. Then everyone plays in groups. Small class (4–7): one group of suspects and everyone else are detectives.

ICQs before the Alibi Game
Is it a real crime? (No! It's a game.) · Is the evening real or invented? (Invented.) · Suspects: do you plan together or alone? (Together.) · Detectives: when you ask the questions, is the other suspect listening? (No!) · What is the question? ("What were you doing at…?") · How many differences for "You ate the cake!"? (Three.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Put the action flashcards on the table: learners point to a picture and say the sentence ("At eight we were watching TV"). On the planning sheet, they draw a small picture or copy a phrase from the flashcards for each time. In Ex 2–4, do the first 3 items only. In the game, the detective role is lighter: two strong questions, asked again and again, are a success.

Stretch

Suspects add details with while: "While we were eating, the neighbor was playing music." Detectives ask follow-up questions: "Where were you sitting? What color was the cake?" Early finishers write the alibi as a short paragraph that sets the scene: "It was a rainy evening. We were…"

L1 watch-outs
  • "I cooking dinner" (no was): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Arabic, Russian and many other learners whose L1 has no "be" in this position. Point at the two parts on the board: was + -ing. Two parts, every time.
  • "They was watching": from all L1s, and it is also heard in some spoken English dialects. Recast: "They were." The rule is the same as Lesson 1.1.
  • Past continuous for habits ("When I was a child, I was going to the beach every summer"): Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian and Russian speakers map their imperfect tense to the past continuous. Accept it today; say "Great! Thursday we learn a better way: used to."
  • State verbs in the continuous ("I was knowing," "I was wanting"): common for Hindi, Urdu, Punjabi, Tagalog and some African-language speakers. Keep it light: I knew / I wanted.
  • The -ing ending: Spanish and Portuguese speakers may say /ɪn/ ("cookin'"); Arabic, Polish and some Chinese speakers add a hard /g/ ("cooking-g"); Vietnamese speakers may drop the final sound. Model /ɪŋ/ with the mouth open and the tongue at the back.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Two actions, two roles. The background action was already in progress (long line → past continuous). The interrupting action happened in the middle of it (a dot → past simple). The background started first.

"I was sleeping when the phone rang." Which started first, the sleeping or the phone? (The sleeping.) Was the phone call long or short? (Short, sudden.) Did the phone stop my sleeping? (Yes, probably!)
"While she was cooking, the doorbell rang." Was she cooking before the doorbell? (Yes.) Which word goes with the long action? (While.)
"When the movie ended, we went home." Was anything in progress? (No.) One thing, then another? (Yes.) So both verbs are… (past simple).

ExampleWhile I was walking home, ……when it started to rain.

Punctuation. When the sentence starts with When or While, put a comma after the first part: "While she was reading, the light went out." No comma in the middle: "The light went out while she was reading."

A note on "when." At A2, teach the clear pattern: when + short action. Learners will also hear when + background ("When I was living in Chicago, …"). If a strong learner asks, say: "Yes, that's correct too. When can mean 'at that time.'" Don't mark it wrong.

Pronunciation. Storytelling is about rhythm. The background clause is often said a little slower; the event is stressed and sudden: "I was walking HOME… when it STARTED to RAIN!" Encourage a small pause before when and a bigger voice on the event. Suddenly is stressed on the first syllable: SUD-den-ly. Irregular past vowels: rang /æ/, fell /ɛ/, began /æ/, broke /oʊ/.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "to rain" → "it started to rain" → "when it started to rain" → "I was walking home when it started to rain." Choral ×3, then individuals, with the pause and the "sudden" voice.
  2. Flip it: T: "I was walking home when it started to rain." → S: "While I was walking home, it started to rain." T: "She was cooking when the phone rang." → S: "While she was cooking, the phone rang."
  3. Long or short? (hand signals): say a phrase; learners stretch their arms wide (long = past continuous) or clap once (short = past simple): "was sleeping" (arms) · "the phone rang" (clap) · "were driving" (arms) · "fell" (clap) · "was raining" (arms) · "knocked" (clap).
  4. Chain: S1: "I was watching TV when…" S2: "…the lights went out." S2 starts a new one: "While I was eating, …" S3 finishes it.
Picture story — how to run it
  • Groups of 3, one set of 8 Story A cards, shuffled, picture up. Each card has a picture and short verb cues only (for example: walk in the park · sun / shine).
  • Round 1 (listen): read Script 5.2-A at a natural pace. Groups put the cards in order. Read again, a little slower; groups fix their order and write 1–8 in the small box in pencil.
  • Round 2 (retell): each learner in turn picks up the next card and says the sentence. The group helps. At least one when and one while sentence per learner.
  • Round 3 (memory): turn two cards face down; retell the whole story again. Stronger groups turn all cards down.
  • Stretch: Story B (Omar's Kitchen, 6 cards) has no script. Groups agree the order by logic and tell it. Two orders can work if the group can explain them.

Answer (Story A): 1 walk in the park (park) → 2 read on a bench (read) → 3 start to rain (umbrella) → 4 run to the bus stop (🏃) → 5 wait for the bus, a man arrive (bus stop) → 6 old friend Marco! (happy) → 7 drink coffee, talk (coffee) → 8 sun shine again, happy (sunny). Model retell: Lena was walking in the park. While she was reading on a bench, it started to rain. She ran to the bus stop. While she was waiting for the bus, a man arrived. It was her old friend Marco! They went to a café. While they were drinking coffee, they talked for two hours. When they left, the sun was shining again.

ICQs before "Something happened!"
Does your story need to be true? (No. True, invented, or a story card.) · How do you start? (Set the scene: "I was… The sun was…") · Which tense for the sudden event? (Past simple.) · What does B do while A is talking? (Listens and reacts: "Oh no!") · What does B ask at the end? ("And then what happened?")
Differentiation
Literacy support

The picture cards carry the story, so pre-literate learners can take full part in the sequencing. In the retell, they may say two simple sentences instead of one joined one ("She was reading. It started to rain.") and the group adds "when." In the freer task, use a story card; the pictures and cue words are enough. Ex 5 can be 3 sentences.

Stretch

Use story words: suddenly, then, after that, in the end. Add a feeling (I was so nervous!). Set the scene with two past-continuous sentences before the event. Try a story with two interruptions. Tell Story B without the cards. In Ex 5, write 8 sentences.

L1 watch-outs
  • Both verbs in the continuous ("I was walking home when it was starting to rain"): common everywhere, especially Hindi/Urdu and Tagalog speakers, who use the continuous a lot. Use the long line and the dot: "Starting is a dot. Dots are past simple."
  • "During I was sleeping…" (Spanish durante, French pendant, Portuguese durante, Russian во время). During + a noun (during the movie); while + subject + verb (while I was sleeping). This is the distractor in Quiz Q2.
  • No tense difference at all ("I walk home, it start to rain"): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Malay have no verb endings; time words carry the meaning. Praise the story and recast just one verb at a time.
  • Good news for Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian speakers: their imperfect vs. preterite (estaba caminando / empezó) maps well onto this lesson. Tell them! It builds confidence.
  • Irregular past forms under pressure ("the phone ringed," "I falled"): from all L1s. Keep the irregular list on the board and point to it; don't stop the story.

used to — Life in the Past

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use used to + base verb for past habits (I used to walk to work) and past states (She used to have long hair) that are not true now
  • make the negative didn't use to and the question Did you use to…? with the spelling change (no -d), and answer Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Used to + base verb tells two things at once: (1) it was true again and again (a habit) or for a long time (a state) in the past, and (2) it is not true now.

"I used to walk to work." Did I walk to work one time or many times? (Many times.) Do I walk to work now? (No.) Is this about the past, the present, or the future? (The past.)
"She used to have long hair." Is her hair long now? (No.) Was it long for one day or a long time? (A long time.)
"Yesterday I used to see a movie." Right or wrong? (Wrong.) Why? (Yesterday is one time.) So… ("Yesterday I saw a movie.")
"I use to go to the gym." (said about now) Is used to for now? (No, only the past.) For now we say… ("I usually go to the gym.")

Form.

PositiveI / you / he / she / we / they + used to + base verbI used to live in a small town. · He used to smoke.
Negativedidn't use to + base verbWe didn't use to have a car.
QuestionDid + subject + use to + base verb…?Did you use to play soccer?
Short answerYes, I did. / No, I didn't.(not "Yes, I used to" in drills; it's fine in conversation)
Then vs nowused to …, but now …I used to walk to work, but now I drive.

Why no -d? Did already carries the past, just like I didn't go (not "didn't went"). Learners know this rule from Lesson 1.3; connect it.

Pronunciation. Used to and use to sound the same: /ˈjuːstə/, "YOO-stuh." The s is /s/ (not /z/ like the verb "use" /juːz/), and the d disappears in speech. That is why the spelling mistake is so common: learners can't hear the difference. Drill the sound, then show the spelling rule in writing. Stress falls on the main verb: "I used to WALK to work, but now I DRIVE." Contrastive stress on then/now makes the comparison clear.

Drill script
  1. Sound first: "YOO-stuh" ×3 choral. Then back-chain: "to work" → "walk to work" → "used to walk to work" → "I used to walk to work." Choral ×3, then individuals.
  2. Then → but now: T shows a then/now flashcard pair (bus / car): S: "I used to take the bus, but now I drive." More cues: long hair / short hair · play soccer / watch soccer · drink coffee / drink tea.
  3. Negative: T: "I liked coffee." (shake head, point to THEN) → S: "I didn't use to like coffee." Write use on the board and cross out the d with a flourish.
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "Did you use to play soccer?" S2: "Yes, I did. / No, I didn't." S2 → S3 with a new verb (play the guitar, swim, sing, have a bike). Any answer can be invented.
Then & Now — how to run it
  • Three doors, one choice. Write them on the board: 🧒 My childhood · 💼 A job I used to have · 🎭 A character card. Say: "Choose any door. You don't need to explain." Point at the card table. Take a card yourself for the model, so the choice looks normal.
  • Model with a character (Captain Rosa, Set E): "I used to be a sailor. I used to live on a ship. I didn't use to have a phone. But now I'm a chef, and I live in a small apartment."
  • Chart (5 min): learners write 4 THEN notes and 3 NOW notes in the workbook chart. Notes, not full sentences.
  • Pairs (8 min): A talks from the notes (6+ sentences, one didn't use to, one but now). B listens and asks 2 "Did you use to…?" questions. B writes one sentence about A: "Rosa used to live on a ship." Swap.
  • Two truths and a lie (4 min): new partner. Say three used to sentences; one is false. The partner asks one question, then guesses: "I think you didn't use to play the guitar!"

Why not "my hometown"? The online lesson's project offers "your hometown years ago." For many refugees and newcomers, the hometown is a place of loss or danger. It stays a learner's free choice inside "My childhood," never the class topic. If a learner starts to share something painful, listen, thank them simply, and gently offer to continue with a character card. Do not ask follow-up questions about it in front of the class.

ICQs before Then & Now
How many topics can you choose? (One.) · Do you have to talk about your real life? (No. A character card is fine.) · Do you explain your choice? (No.) · How many "used to" notes? (Four.) · What does your partner ask? ("Did you use to…?") · After did, do we write "used" or "use"? ("Use.")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the then-and-now flashcards and the character cards: the pictures show THEN and NOW, so learners can point and speak. On the chart, they may draw. Target: 3 used to sentences and 1 but now. Skip the spelling rule in writing (Ex 3) and focus on saying "YOO-stuh" and the base verb.

Stretch

Add there used to be… ("There used to be a bakery on my street"), time phrases (when I was young, years ago, these days, nowadays), and one sentence about a past state vs. a past habit. Compare with the past simple: "I lived there for two years" vs. "I used to live there." Write 8 sentences for the journal.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I use to go to the gym" meaning a present habit: Spanish suelo, Portuguese costumo, Italian di solito learners (and Tagalog speakers) use "use to" for "usually." CCQ: "Do you go to the gym now?" If yes: "I usually go to the gym."
  • "I used to lived" / "I used to living": from all L1s. Point at the board: used to + base verb. The past is already in used.
  • "Did you used to…?" / "I didn't used to…": very common, even for native speakers in writing. It matters in writing only; don't correct it in speech (you can't hear it).
  • "Before I play soccer" for "I used to play soccer": Chinese yǐqián, Tagalog dati, Vietnamese trước đây, Korean yejeone put a time word in front of a plain verb. Praise the meaning: "Yes! Before… so: I used to play soccer."
  • "I was used to play" (Arabic, Russian, French speakers who know be used to): Be used to is a different phrase (B1). Today: no was.
  • Past continuous for habits (from C14: "When I was a child I was going to the beach every summer"): now show the better tool: "I used to go to the beach every summer."
