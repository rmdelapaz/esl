Instructor Kit · Teacher's Guide

Module 4 — Experiences & Advice

Three classes that close the first half of A2. Learners meet the present perfect for life experiences and learn when to switch back to the past simple. Then they use have to / must / should to explain rules and give advice. Last, they put all of Modules 1–4 together in one task: telling a travel story. You score that story with a speaking checklist that feeds Progress Test 1 (C13).

3 lessons Lessons 4.1–4.3 Classes C10–C12 Weeks 5–6 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-4-experiences-advice.html (slides 3–8 for C10, 9–13 for C11, 14–18 for C12). Print materials/module-4/classroom-materials.html before C10. It holds the flashcards, the Find Someone Who game, the experience identity cards, the rules information gap, the problem-page cards, the travel role-play cards, the trip cards, the listening scripts and the C12 speaking checklist.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons sometimes show British forms (practise, centre, holiday, films, travellers, the crew were). Teach practice, downtown / the center, vacation, movies, and accept the British forms when learners use them. Mustn't is much less common in American speech than in British English: see the note in 4.2.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

What I've done → what we have to do → where I went. In C10 learners open a topic with the present perfect ("Have you ever…?") and then switch to the past simple for the details ("When did you go?"). That switch is the most important idea in the module; it comes back in every class. In C11 they explain rules and give advice. In C12 they tell a whole travel story that uses every A2 tense so far: past (Module 1), comparing and quantities (Module 2), future (Module 3) and the present perfect (Module 4). The story is the module's review project and your speaking evidence for Progress Test 1.

Trauma-informed: experiences, journeys and rules can be personal

"Have you ever…?" and "Tell me about a trip" can touch painful memories: a hospital stay, a lost job, a long journey to a new country, time in a camp. Never ask about migration journeys, and never make a learner explain one. In every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a card (experience identity cards in C10, trip cards in C12), or something imagined. Teach "Pass!" and "I'd rather not say" on the first day of the module, and model using them yourself. In C11, keep rules and advice everyday (work, school, the pool, the library, health, sleep). If a learner raises an immigration, legal, housing-court or money problem, don't give advice: say "That's important. Let's talk after class," and refer them to your program's caseworker or a legal-aid service.

If you run long

Cut Script 4.1-B (use it as the C11 warmer instead) and shorten the controlled practice to Ex 3 and Ex 4 only. Never cut the follow-up question in the mingle. Without it, learners practice only the present perfect and never make the switch, and the switch is the point of the lesson.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The present perfect talks about experience in my life up to now, when the exact time is not important and we don't say it. The past simple talks about one finished time that we say or know. In real talk, English speakers open a topic with the present perfect and then explain it with the past simple. Point at the board: box first, then the ✕.

"I've been to Mexico." Did I go to Mexico? (Yes.) Do we know when? (No.) Is the time important here? (No, only the experience.)
"I went to Mexico in 2019." Do we know when? (Yes, 2019.) Is it finished? (Yes.) So present perfect or past simple? (Past simple.)
"Have you ever eaten sushi?" Am I asking about yesterday? (No.) About what time? (Any time in your life.)
"I've never broken a bone." Did I break a bone last year? (No.) In my whole life? (No, never.) Do I need "not"? (No! "never" is already negative.)
"She's gone to the store." Is she here now? (No.) "She's been to the store." Is she here now? (Yes. She went and came back.)

Form.

PositiveI / you / we / they have ('ve) + participle · he / she / it has ('s) + participleI've been to Canada. She's tried Thai food.
Negativehaven't / hasn't + participle · have / has + never + participleHe hasn't seen it. I've never eaten octopus.
QuestionHave / Has + subject + ever + participle…?Have you ever ridden a horse?
Short answerYes, I have. / No, I haven't. / No, never.(not "Yes, I did." not "Yes, I have been.")
The switchDetails with a time → past simpleWhen did you go? — I went in 2019. It was great.

Participles. Regular verbs: the same as the -ed past (visited, tried, worked). Many irregulars are also the same as the past (met, made, had, read, lost, won). Teach the "different" group as a set: been, gone, seen, done, eaten, drunk, taken, written, broken, ridden, flown, swum, sung.

Pronunciation. Contractions are normal in speech: I've /aɪv/, she's /ʃiːz/, haven't /ˈhævənt/. In questions have is weak: "Have you ever…?" sounds like /həv jə ˈɛvər/. Model it slowly, then fast. In short answers have is strong: "Yes, I HAVE." The stress in the question goes on the participle and the key noun: "Have you ever RIDden a HORSE?" Been in American English is /bɪn/ (like "bin") in fast speech; both /bɪn/ and /biːn/ are fine.

Drill script
  1. Participle rhythm: T: "see" → S: "saw – seen." T: "eat" → S: "ate – eaten." Choral, then individuals. Clap on the participle.
  2. Back-chain the question: "a horse?" → "ridden a horse?" → "ever ridden a horse?" → "Have you ever ridden a horse?" Choral ×3, then 4–5 individuals.
  3. Picture prompt drill (point at a flashcard): T: 🏔️ → S1: "Have you ever climbed a mountain?" → S2: "Yes, I have. / No, never."
  4. The switch (the key drill): T: "Yes, I have!" → S: "When did you…?" T: "Mexico. Yes, I have." → S: "When did you go?" → T: "I went in 2019." Then open pairs across the room, then closed pairs for 1 minute.
  5. Fix the time word: T: "I've been to Paris last year." (look confused) → S: "I went to Paris last year!" Repeat with 3–4 sentences.
Find Someone Who — how to run it
  • One sheet per learner. Show the example row on the slide: "Have you ever ridden a horse?" "Yes, I have." "Where did you ride it?" "In Morocco." → write Amir · Morocco, age 10.
  • One name per box. Ask a person one or two questions, then move on. A "No, never" is fine: say "OK, thanks!" and ask someone else.
  • Teach two safe phrases before you start: "Pass!" and "I'd rather not say." Anyone may use them, for any reason. Learners with an identity card answer as their card person.
  • Walk around and listen for the switch. When you hear "Yes, I have. I have been there in 2018," just point at the board ✕.
  • Stretch: learners ask a second follow-up ("Who did you go with? How was it?"). Early finishers look for someone who has done the "bonus" row.
ICQs before the mingle
Do you sit or walk? (Walk.) · First question: "Have you…" or "Did you…"? ("Have you ever…?") · The answer is "Yes." What do you ask next? (A past question: When did you…? Where did you…?) · How many names in one box? (One.) · Do you have to answer every question? (No. You can say "Pass!")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Give a Find Someone Who sheet with the pictures only (flashcard minis) and 6 rows, not 12. They write a name or just the first letter, and ask one follow-up only: "When?" Aim for four participles they can say without reading (been, seen, eaten, done). In Ex 3, read each sentence aloud and let them circle what sounds right.

Stretch

Add a second follow-up and an opinion ("It was the best food I've ever eaten"). Use been vs gone about people in the class ("Maria has gone to the office"). Early finishers write six interview turns for Ex 6 with no book. Introduce once / twice / three times ("I've been there twice").

L1 watch-outs
  • "I have been there last year" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, German, Dutch speakers). Their perfect tense can take a past time word (Spanish he estado in Spain, French j'ai visité… l'année dernière, German ich bin… gewesen). This is the error of the module. Point at the ✕ every time.
  • "Did you ever…?" / "Yes, I did": learners who have heard American speakers. "Did you ever…?" is real informal American English, so don't call it "wrong." But teach Have you ever…? as the classroom and test form.
  • "I am already eat" / "I finish" for experience: Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Malay mark completion with a particle (le, rồi, sudah) and have no helper verb. Point at have + participle: two parts, always.
  • "She has went" / "I have saw" (all learners): the past form after have. Return to the three-column table.
  • "I haven't never…": Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French and Russian use double negatives (no he ido nunca). One negative only: I've never been or I haven't been.
  • Contractions disappear: Arabic, Russian and Japanese speakers often drop 've in speech ("I been there"). Tap your lips: "I've."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Have to = a rule or a need, usually from outside (the boss, the school, the doctor). Must = a strong rule, very common on signs and written rules. Don't have to = not necessary: your choice. Mustn't = not allowed. Should = a good idea (advice), not a rule.

"You don't have to pay. It's free." Can I pay if I want? (Yes. It's OK.) Do I need to pay? (No.) Is it my choice? (Yes.)
"You mustn't smoke here." Is it my choice? (No.) Is it OK to smoke? (No. It's not allowed.) So is "don't have to" the same as "mustn't"? (No! Very different.)
"You should see a doctor." Is it a rule? (No.) Is it a good idea? (Yes.) Who decides? (You do.)
"She has to work on Saturday." Does she want to? (Maybe not!) Does she need to? (Yes. It's her job.)
American English: mustn't vs can't

Americans rarely say mustn't in conversation. They say can't or you're not allowed to: "You can't park here." Teach mustn't so learners understand signs, forms and British speakers, but model can't as the everyday spoken form and accept both. Also accept have got to / gotta if learners know it.

Pronunciation. In speech have to is /ˈhæftə/ ("hafta") and has to is /ˈhæstə/ ("hasta"): the v becomes f. Model it, but accept the careful form. Mustn't /ˈmʌsənt/: the first t is silent. Should /ʃʊd/: the l is silent, like could and would. In advice, stress the main verb: "You should REST." In don't have to, stress DON'T to show the meaning: "You DON'T have to pay."

Drill script
  1. Traffic-light drill: hold up a color card and a picture. 🟢 + badge → S: "You have to wear a badge." 🔴 + phone → S: "You mustn't / can't use your phone." 🟡 + ticket (FREE) → S: "You don't have to buy a ticket."
  2. He/she transformation: T: "I have to work late." → S: "She has to work late." T: "I don't have to cook." → S: "He doesn't have to cook."
  3. Question chain: S1 → S2: "Do you have to get up early?" S2: "Yes, I do. / No, I don't." S2 → S3 with a new verb.
  4. Advice round: T mimes a problem (tired, cold, lost, late). Learners call out: "You should…" "You shouldn't…" Then one learner mimes for the class.
  5. Kill the "to": T: "You must to go." → S (stamp once): "You must go!" Four quick items.
The problem page — how to run it
  • Groups of three: one reader, two advisers. Cards face down. The reader reads the problem aloud as their own ("Dear Ask Ana… I'm always tired at work").
  • Each adviser gives one should / shouldn't sentence and, if possible, one have to / don't have to fact ("You don't have to buy a gym membership. You should walk after dinner.").
  • The reader answers: "Good idea! / Hmm, I can't do that because…" and picks the best advice. Rotate roles.
  • All problems are everyday ones (sleep, a new job, a noisy neighbor, a child's school, learning English, a broken phone). If a group invents a legal or immigration problem, gently steer them: "Let's choose an everyday problem today."
  • Stretch: advisers must soften (Maybe you should… / I think you should…) and give a reason with because.
ICQs before the information gap and the problem page
Information gap: Can you show your card? (No.) Who asks the questions first? (A, the new person.) What is the question? ("Do I have to…?") B says "No, you don't." Is it OK to do it? (Yes, it's your choice.)
Problem page: Who reads the card? (The reader.) How many pieces of advice from each adviser? (One, or two.) Which word do you use for advice: "should" or "must"? (Should.) Is it a real problem from your life? (No, it's on the card.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the traffic-light cards as the main tool: learners hold up 🟢 🟡 🔴 for each rule you read. In the problem page, read the problem card to them; they give advice from a bank of 6 picture phrases (see a doctor, go to bed early, drink water, ask your boss, take a break, call a friend) with "You should…" Target: 3 correct sentences.

Stretch

Compare rules: "In my country you don't have to…, but here you have to…" (keep it everyday: shops, school, tipping). Add had to for the past ("When I was a child, I had to walk to school"). As advisers, give two options and a reason: "You could… or you should… because…"

L1 watch-outs
  • "You must to go" / "You should to rest": Spanish tener que / deber, French devoir, Arabic lāzim an, Russian nado / dolzhen. Learners copy the to from have to. Stamp on the "to."
  • "You don't have to smoke" meaning "don't smoke": in German (du musst nicht = you don't have to) and many other languages the negative of "must" means "not necessary," while in others it means "not allowed." Both directions cause trouble. Use the traffic lights every time.
  • "She have to work" / "She has to works": the -s goes on has, once. Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai speakers often drop it; others add it twice.
  • "Do you must…?" / "You mustn't to…": learners treat must like a normal verb. Recast with the natural question: "Do you have to…?"
  • Politeness and directness: in many cultures giving strong advice is kind; in American English "You must…" to a friend can sound bossy. Teach Maybe you should… / I think you should… as friendly, not weak.

4.3 · C12

Review Project — Telling a Travel Story

90 min
A task-based review, not a new-grammar class

There is no new grammar today. The class is built around one real task: tell a travel story so that a listener can follow it and enjoy it. Learners plan with a five-step frame, rehearse with a partner, then tell the story to a small group while you listen with the speaking checklist. Language work comes from the task: short focus-on-form moments when you hear a gap, not long presentations. The checklist results feed C13 (Progress Test 1): they are your evidence of spoken narrative across Modules 1–4, and they tell you who goes to which catch-up station.

Objectives — learners can…
  • use travel phrases at a station, at a hotel and when asking for help, politely (Excuse me… please)
  • choose the right tense for each part of a story: was/were for the scene, past simple for events, comparatives and quantifiers for description, present perfect for experience, going to / will for the next trip
  • join sentences with first, then, after that, but, so, because, in the end
  • tell a real or imagined travel story in 5 steps (3–5 minutes) and answer a partner's questions
Materials

Hearing every story

In 20 minutes you can score about 6–8 learners live. Choose one of these for the rest, and tell the class which one before the story circles: (a) learners record the story on their phone in the story-circle stage and play it for you in the C13 catch-up time; (b) the rest tell their story to you in the C13 catch-up stations; (c) in a large class, score the checklist in two halves across C12 and C13. Anyone who prefers to tell the story only to you (not a group) may do so. That is a normal choice, not a problem.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review focus)

Meaning: each tense has a job in the story. Show the story road on slide 16 and ask which step each sentence belongs to.

"The hotel was small, but it was clean." Which step? (① the scene.) Why "was"? (It's the past; it describes.)
"I've never seen the ocean before." Is this about one day or my whole life? (My whole life, up to now.) Can I add "last year"? (No! Then: "I saw the ocean last year.")
"Next year I'm going to visit Greece." Past or future? (Future.) Is it a plan? (Yes. Going to = a plan.)
"We arrived late, so we went to bed." Why did we go to bed? (Because we arrived late.) Which word shows the result? (So.)

Form: the review table.

① Scenewas / were · there was / there wereIt was spring. The streets were busy.
② What I didpast simple: regular -ed + irregularWe arrived at night and took a taxi.
③ Compare-er than · the most · a lot of / some / a fewThe beach was quieter than the city. There were a lot of tourists.
④ Experiencepresent perfect: I've never… (before) · the best… I've ever…I've never eaten fish like that. It was the best meal I've ever had.
⑤ Next timegoing to (plan) · will (decision / prediction)Next year I'm going to go back. I think I'll stay longer.
Gluefirst · then · after that · but · so · because · in the endFirst we…, then… In the end…

Pronunciation. Two things matter most for a listener: the -ed ending (/t/ walked, /d/ stayed, /ɪd/ visited: only /ɪd/ adds a beat) and pausing between the five steps. Teach "chunk and pause": say a linking word, pause, then the next part. Luggage /ˈlʌɡɪdʒ/: two syllables. Souvenir /ˌsuːvəˈnɪr/: stress at the end.

Drill script
  1. Polite chunks: T: "Where's the check-in?" → S: "Excuse me, where's the check-in, please?" Four phrases, choral then individuals, rising-then-falling polite intonation.
  2. Tense switch drill: T holds up a step number and a verb. "① be / hot" → S: "It was hot." "④ eat / octopus / never" → S: "I've never eaten octopus." "⑤ visit / Peru" → S: "I'm going to visit Peru."
  3. Link it: T says two short sentences: "We were tired. We went to bed." → S: "We were tired, so we went to bed." Then with because and but.
  4. Best… ever: T: "good / trip" → S: "It was the best trip I've ever had." "beautiful / place" → "the most beautiful place I've ever seen."
The story-planning frame — how to run it
  • Show your own filled-in frame first (a real or invented trip). Notes only: 3–6 words per box. Full sentences on paper become reading aloud.
  • Any trip is a trip: a day at the beach, a visit to family in another town, a school trip, a work trip, a vacation, a dream trip, or a trip card. Say it out loud so no one feels their trip is "too small."
  • Step ④ is the hardest. Give three sentence starters: I've never… before. · It was the best… I've ever… · I've been to… but…
  • Step ⑤ can be a dream: "One day I'm going to…" is fine.
  • Early finishers add a travel-phrase moment: "At the hotel I said, 'Is breakfast included?'"
The speaking checklist — how to score it

Five criteria, 0–3 points each, /15. Score what you hear in the whole performance, including answers to questions. The full grid and descriptors are in the classroom materials (Set H).

CriterionWhat 3 points sounds like
1 · Task: the five stepsAll five steps are there, in a sensible order; the listener knows where, when, what happened, one experience and a plan.
2 · Past formswas/were and past simple (regular + irregular) are mostly correct; -ed endings are heard.
3 · Tense switchingPresent perfect for experience with no time; past simple when a time is given; going to / will for the future. No "I have been there last year."
4 · Linking & organizationUses 3+ different linking words; the story flows; not a list of facts.
5 · Fluency, pronunciation & interactionKeeps going with short pauses; easy to understand; answers a partner's question with a full sentence.

Feeding C13. Record every learner's five scores on the class grid. In C13, send learners with 0–1 on criterion 3 to the present perfect / past simple catch-up station, 0–1 on criterion 2 to the past-forms station (Module 1), and 0–1 on criterion 4 to the linking-words station. If Progress Test 1 has its own speaking task, keep this grid anyway: it is your evidence of spoken narrative for Modules 1–4, and it lets you give each learner one kind, specific next step.

ICQs before planning and the story circles
Planning: Do you write full sentences or notes? (Notes.) Does it have to be a real trip? (No. Real, a day trip, a card, or a dream.) How many steps? (Five.) Which step uses "I've never…"? (Step four.)
Story circles: Can you read your notes? (Only a quick look.) What do the listeners do? (Listen, then ask one question.) Do you have to tell your story to the group? (No. You can tell it only to the teacher.) How long? (About three or four minutes.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the picture version of the frame: learners draw a small picture in each box instead of notes (a sun for the scene, a plane, a plate of food). Give a trip card with photos. Target: steps ①, ②, ⑤ with 2 sentences each, plus one "I've never…" sentence. Score them on the same checklist; a clear, short story can earn full marks on criteria 1, 4 and 5.

Stretch

Add a problem on the trip and the advice someone gave ("I lost my bag. The man at the desk said I should call the airline."). Use two superlatives and a travel-phrase quote. Tell the story in 3 minutes with no notes. Listeners ask two follow-up questions, not one.

L1 watch-outs
  • Present tense for the whole story ("Last year I go to Italy. I see the sea."): Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Malay have no past endings; time words do the work. Prompt with the step: "① — was or is?"
  • "I have been there last year" is back (Spanish, French, Italian, German, Portuguese). One finger on the ✕ from C10 is enough.
  • "a luggage" / "luggages" / "informations": many languages count these nouns (Spanish equipajes, French des bagages). Teach some luggage / two bags.
  • "More better" / "the most biggest" (Module 2 errors return under pressure). Note them for feedback; don't stop the story.
  • "I went to home" / "We arrived to the hotel" (Spanish llegar a, Portuguese chegar a): arrive in a city, arrive at a hotel, go home.
  • -ed not heard (Vietnamese and Cantonese drop final consonants; Spanish speakers may add a vowel: "walk-ed"). Score what a patient listener understands.

Closing the Module

End of C12: Module 4 wrap-up and the halfway point

Recap on slide 18: I've been to… → I went there in… · You have to… / You don't have to… / You should… · Last year I went… Next year I'm going to… Learners tick the three "I can…" lists. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you. Use those notes, and your speaking checklist, to plan the C13 catch-up stations.

Celebrate the halfway point. Learners have finished half of A2. Ask each person to say one thing they can do now that they couldn't do in Week 1. Keep it short and warm.

Progress Test 1 (C13) covers Modules 1–4. From this module it can test: past participles, present perfect with ever / never, short answers, been vs gone, present perfect vs past simple with time words, have to / has to / don't have to, must / mustn't, should / shouldn't + base verb, and travel phrases. The C12 speaking checklist gives you the spoken evidence.

Watch for in Module 5: the past simple comes back in the past continuous (5.1–5.2) and used to (5.3). The present perfect returns in 8.1 (for, since, just, already, yet), so keep a "Have you ever…?" question in a warmer every week until then.
