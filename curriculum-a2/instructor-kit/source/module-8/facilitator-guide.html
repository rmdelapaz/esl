Module 8 — Experiences, Plans & Review

The last three classes of A2. In C23 learners connect the past to now with the present perfect and for, since, just, already, yet. In C24 they join two verbs together (I enjoy cooking · I want to cook). In C25 there is no new grammar: learners plan and tell My Life Story & Future Plans, using every A2 tense. You observe them with a checklist that feeds the C26 final speaking assessment.

3 lessons Lessons 8.1–8.3 Classes C23–C25 Weeks 12–13 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. C23 and C24 follow the usual shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs and groups) → delayed error correction → homework. C25 is a task-based class: pre-task review → planning → rehearsal → task → report and language focus.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-8-review.html. Print materials/module-8/classroom-materials.html before C23. It holds the flashcards, the for/since sort cards, the "How long have you…?" timeline sheet, the to-do list information gap, the verb-pattern sort cards, the Verb Pattern Race board game, the life-story cards, the listening scripts and the teacher observation checklist.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear every model sentence and dialogue again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons for this module use some British forms: neighbourhood, flat, films, at the weekend, memorise, move house. Teach neighborhood, apartment, movies, on the weekend, memorize, move, and accept the British forms when learners use them. Also: Americans often say I just ate / Did you eat yet? in speech. Teach the present perfect (I've just eaten, Have you eaten yet?) as the safe form, and accept the past simple with just / already / yet as correct American English.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Up to now → what I like and want → my whole story. C23 is about the bridge between the past and today (I've lived here for three years). C24 adds the language of hobbies, wishes and plans (I enjoy…, I want to…, I've decided to…). C25 puts all of A2 on one timeline: where I come from (past simple, past continuous, used to) → up to now (present perfect) → what's next (going to, will, first conditional). The C23 and C24 freer tasks are the first two parts of the C25 story, so learners arrive at C25 with material ready.

Trauma-informed: life stories are very personal

"Where did you grow up?" and "How long have you lived here?" can touch on war, migration, loss, family separation or immigration status. Never ask why someone left a country, or when they arrived, unless they offer it. In every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real story, a partly fictional story (change or skip any part: a real past with an imagined future, or start the story "up to now"), or a fully fictional story (a life-story card from the classroom materials, or a character you invent). Say it every time, so that choosing a card is normal. Timelines can be about anything: a phone, a hobby, a friendship, a favorite team.

Present Perfect — for, since, just, already, yet

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use for + a length of time (for three years) and since + a start point (since 2021, since Monday, since I was a child)
  • ask and answer How long have you lived / worked / known…?
  • use just (a moment ago) and already (done, maybe early) before the past participle
  • use yet at the end of questions and negatives: Have you paid yet? I haven't paid yet.
  • choose the present perfect (time up to now) or the past simple (finished time: yesterday, in 2019, ago)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

for / since — meaning. Both answer "How long?" about a situation that started in the past and is still true now. for + the length (the whole line). since + the start (the dot). Learners met the present perfect for experiences in 4.1 (C10), so the form is review. The new part is the "still true now" meaning.

"I've lived here for five years." Do I live here now? (Yes.) When did I start? (Five years ago.) Is it finished? (No.)
"since 2021." Is 2021 a length of time, or a start point? (A start point.) "three years": length or start point? (Length.) So: for three years, since 2021.
"I lived in Texas for two years." Do I live in Texas now? (No.) Finished? (Yes.) So which tense? (Past simple.)

just / already / yet — meaning. just = a very short time ago. already = it's done, often earlier than expected. yet = up to now; only in questions and negatives; we expect it to happen.

"I've just eaten." A long time ago or a minute ago? (A minute ago.) Am I hungry? (No!)
"I've already paid the bill." Is the bill paid? (Yes.) Did I pay early? (Yes, maybe earlier than you thought.)
"I haven't called her yet." Did I call her? (No.) Am I going to call her? (Yes, probably.) Where is yet? (At the end.)

Form.

How long?How long have / has + subject + past participle?How long have you lived here? — For six years. / Since 2020.
for+ a length of timefor ten minutes · for two weeks · for a long time
since+ a start point (a day, a date, an event)since Monday · since 9 o'clock · since I was a child
just / alreadyhave / has + just / already + past participleShe's just arrived. · I've already eaten.
yetquestion / negative … + yet (at the end)Have you finished yet? · I haven't finished yet.
vs past simplefinished time words: yesterday, last week, in 2019, agoI ate at seven. (not "I've eaten at seven")

Pronunciation. Contractions are normal in speech: I've /aɪv/, she's /ʃiːz/, haven't /ˈhævənt/. Point out that she's lived = she has lived (not she is); the past participle tells you. for is weak in a sentence: /fər/ ("f'r five years"). In How long have you…? the stress is on LONG and the main verb: "How LONG have you LIVED here?"; have you is fast, /əvjə/. yet /jɛt/ is not jet /dʒɛt/: smile for the /j/.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "…here." → "…lived here." → "How long have you lived here?" Choral ×3, then half the room, then individuals. Answer chorally: "For five years." / "Since 2021."
  2. for or since? T says a time phrase, learners say the whole phrase: T: "three years" → S: "for three years." T: "Monday" → S: "since Monday." T: "I was a child" → S: "since I was a child." Speed up.
  3. Mime drill (just): T sits down, stands up, closes a book → learners: "You've just sat down." "You've just closed the book."
  4. Transformation (yet): T: "I've called her." (shake head) → S: "I haven't called her yet." T: "You've paid?" → S: "Have you paid yet?"
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 → S2 across the room: "How long have you had your phone?" S2: "For a year." Then all pairs, 1 minute.
"How long have you…?" timelines — how to run it
  • Each learner writes 4–5 start points on the arrow on their sheet: a year, a month, a day or an event (2021 · last May · I was 20 · Monday) with a word or picture: this city · my job · my phone · my best friend · English class.
  • Choice: real life, a new identity timeline card, or "light" topics only (a phone, shoes, a hobby, a favorite TV show). Nobody has to write when they came to the country.
  • Pairs swap sheets. B looks at A's timeline and asks: "How long have you had your phone?" A: "Since 2024. / For two years." B writes the answer on A's sheet.
  • Swap roles. Stretch: ask a follow-up question in the past simple ("Where did you buy it?"). This is the present perfect → past simple move that native speakers make all the time.
ICQs before the timelines and the information gap
Timelines: Do you have to write your real life? (No. A card, or easy things like your phone.) What is the question? ("How long have you…?") What two words can you use in the answer? (For and since.) Who writes the answer? (My partner.)
To-do list: Can you show your card? (No.) What question do you ask? ("Have you… yet?") Where is "yet"? (At the end.) If the item has "5 minutes ago," what word do you use? (Just.) If it has an ✗? ("No, I haven't… yet.")
Differentiation
Literacy support

Timelines can be all numbers and pictures. Give a for / since strip to hold: for + 🔢 (a number of days / years) · since + 📅 (a date, a day). In the information gap, learners say only "Yes, I have. / No, I haven't." plus just or already; the partner points and ticks. Target: 4 sentences in the freer task.

Stretch

Add a past-simple follow-up to every "How long" answer ("When did you start?"). Use since + a clause: since I started my job. In the information gap, explain: "I haven't changed the money yet because the bank is closed." Try still: "I still haven't called my mom."

L1 watch-outs
  • "I live here since 2020" / "I am living here for five years" (Spanish vivo aquí desde…, Portuguese, French depuis, German seit, Italian, Russian, Polish: the present tense does this job). Very common and very fossilized. Draw the arrow reaching NOW and recast: "You've lived here since 2020."
  • "since three years" (French depuis and German seit cover both for and since; Spanish desde hace). Ask the quick test: "Can you count it?"
  • "I've lived here for five years ago" (Spanish hace, Portuguese há, Arabic mundhu, Tagalog na…ang nakalipas). ago goes with the past simple only.
  • "I already eat" / "I finish already" (Chinese yǐjīng … le, Vietnamese đã / rồi, Thai láew, Tagalog na: no tense change, and the "already" word often comes at the end). Accept I already ate (American); correct I already eat.
  • "I didn't eat yet" / "I still not eat" (Spanish todavía no, German noch nicht, Japanese mada, Korean ajik). I didn't eat yet is heard in American speech; teach I haven't eaten yet. Japanese and Korean speakers may use yet for "still" ("I yet live here"): contrast still (positive) and yet (negative).
  • Irregular past participles (all learners): I've ate, I've went, I've saw. Keep a participle list on the wall: eaten, gone/been, seen, done, bought, paid, known, had, found.
  • yet → "jet" (Spanish, Portuguese in some regions, Arabic).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Two verbs together: the first verb is the main one, and the second verb changes its shape. The first verb decides. There is no perfect rule, so learners learn the common verbs in two groups. A helpful hook: many -ing verbs talk about feelings and habits (enjoy, don't mind, keep); many to verbs point forward to a goal (want, plan, hope, decide).

"I enjoy cooking." Do I like it? (Yes.) Is it a habit or one time? (A habit, in general.)
"I don't mind waiting." Is waiting a problem for me? (No. It's OK.) Do I love it? (No, but it's OK.)
"He keeps forgetting his keys." One time, or again and again? (Again and again.) Is he happy about it? (No!)
"She finished cooking." Is she cooking now? (No. It's done.)
"I've decided to take a class." Is it an idea, or a decision? (A decision.) Past or future action? (The class is in the future.)
"I'd like to go home." = "I want to go home"? (Yes, but more polite.) "I'd like going home": OK? (No! would like + to.)

Form.

verb + -ingenjoy · don't mind · finish · keep · suggestI enjoy reading. · They suggested going to the beach.
verb + to + base verbwant · need · would like · decide · hope · plan · promise · learn · tryWe decided to stay home. · I'm learning to drive.
bothlike · love · hateI love cooking. = I love to cook.
would likealways + toI'd like to visit my grandparents.
negativethe first verb changes; the pattern staysI don't enjoy running. · She doesn't want to wait.

Pronunciation. -ing is /ɪŋ/: the tongue goes back, and there is no hard /g/ at the end (not "sing-g"). to is weak, /tə/: "I want tə go." Learners will hear wanna and gonna in movies; that is fine to understand, but write want to. I'd like to links: /aɪd ˈlaɪk tə/. don't mind: stress on MIND.

Drill script
  1. Substitution (-ing): T: "I enjoy… 🏊" (swim flashcard) → S: "I enjoy swimming." T: "dance" → "I enjoy dancing." T: "hike" → "I enjoy hiking." Then change the first verb: T: "don't mind" → S: "I don't mind hiking."
  2. Substitution (to): T: "I want… travel" → S: "I want to travel." T: "hope" → "I hope to travel." T: "would like" → "I'd like to travel."
  3. First verb decides (fast): T says a first verb + a second verb: "enjoy / cook" → S: "I enjoy cooking." "decide / move" → "I decided to move." "keep / forget" → "I keep forgetting." Learners who make a mistake just try again: no explanation.
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "What do you enjoy doing on the weekend?" S2 answers, then asks S3: "What do you want to do this year?"… around the room.
Verb Pattern Race — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one board, one die, one counter each. Everyone starts on START. The two-column list stays open in the workbook (the "referee").
  • Roll and move. Read the square and make a true sentence (or a sentence for your new identity): hope… next year → I hope to find a better job next year.
  • The group listens to the second verb. If it's wrong, the group helps fix it, and the player goes back 1 square.
  • ★ squares: ask the player on your left the question on the square. They answer with the right pattern. Arrow squares: go forward or back.
  • The first player to FINISH wins, but let groups keep playing until you stop the game. No dice? Use the dice-free variant printed under the board (count on fingers, or use a phone dice app).
ICQs before the board game and the interview
Game: Do you say a sentence or just the verb? (A sentence.) A true sentence? (Yes, or for your card person.) Your sentence is wrong. What happens? (Go back one square.) What do you do on a star square? (Ask the person on my left.)
Interview: Do you write full sentences? (No, just notes.) What do you ask first? ("What do you enjoy doing?") After "want," what comes? (To.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Focus on four verbs only: enjoy, don't mind (+ -ing) and want, would like (+ to). Give them a color card: green = -ing, blue = to; color the board game squares the same way before class. In the game, a picture flashcard can be the second verb (point to swim: "I enjoy swimming"). Target: 4 correct sentences.

Stretch

Add a reason or time: "I've decided to learn to drive because the bus is slow." Try two patterns in one sentence: "I want to learn to swim." Add -ing after a preposition (from the online FAQ): good at cooking, interested in learning. In the interview, ask a follow-up with the present perfect: "Have you ever tried…?"

L1 watch-outs
  • "I enjoy to read" / "I suggest to go" / "She finished to eat" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Russian, Polish: the second verb is an infinitive after almost every verb). This is the most common error in the class. Keep pointing to the -ing column.
  • "I want go" / "I enjoy cook" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Tagalog: the verb doesn't change shape). Tap the second verb: "What is missing?"
  • "I would like go" (German ich möchte gehen, Dutch: no to after the modal). Drill I'd like to as one chunk.
  • "I want that I go" / "I want that you come" (Arabic urīdu an…, Spanish quiero que…, Persian, Turkish). Show I want to go and I want you to come (stretch only).
  • -ing pronunciation: "sing-g" with a hard /g/ (Arabic, Cantonese, some Spanish speakers) or "-een" / "-in" (Spanish, Portuguese, Tagalog). Model the /ŋ/ with the tongue back and the mouth open.
  • Spelling: "swiming," "danceing," "makeing" (all learners). Use the board rules.

8.3 · C25

Review Project — My Life Story & Future Plans

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • understand the main points of a "getting to know you" interview about a life story
  • put the A2 tenses on one timeline: past simple, past continuous, used to · present perfect with for / since / just / already / yet · going to, will, first conditional
  • plan a life story in three parts (where I come from · up to now · what's next) with a planning frame
  • tell a 10+ sentence life story (real, partly fictional or fully fictional) to a small group, and answer their questions
  • listen to a classmate's story and ask one follow-up question
Materials
  • Workbook 8.3 (A2 tense toolkit, Exercises 1–4, story-planning frame, story circle, final A2 journal)
  • Slides 14–19 · Life-story cards (4 fictional people; on a side table) · time-signal word cards (for the board timeline) · story-circle question cards · Teacher observation checklist (C25 → C26) on a clipboard
  • Listening scripts 8.3-A (Maya's interview) and 8.3-B (the teacher's model life story)
  • Learners' own notes from C23 (timeline sheet) and C24 (hobbies & plans interview)

Left — PAST (where I come from): I grew up in… · I used to… · When I was young, I was living / working… · In 2019 I moved / started…

Center — UP TO NOW: I've lived here for… / since… · I've worked / studied… · I've just… · I've already… · I haven't… yet.

Right — FUTURE (what's next): I'm going to… (a plan) · I think I'll… (a prediction) · If I…, I'll… · I want to / I'd like to / I hope to…

Corner: Your story = real · partly real · or a story card. All are good. · C26 = final class 🎉

If you run long

Protect planning, rehearsal and the story circles. Cut in this order: (1) shorten the warmer to 4 minutes; (2) do Ex 3 as homework (keep Ex 4); (3) skip the pairs reading of Maya's dialogue. Never cut Looking Ahead. Learners must leave knowing exactly what C26 looks like.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review)

No new grammar. The job today is choosing the right tense for each part of the story. Use the time words as signals: when I was young, in 2019, used to → past · for, since, just, already, yet → up to now · next year, one day, if… → future. Keep CCQs short and fast.

"I used to play football every day." Do I play every day now? (No.) Was it a habit? (Yes.) (Module 5.3)
"I was living with my family then." Was it a long situation, or one quick moment? (A situation, in progress.) (Module 5.1)
"I've lived here for six years." Do I live here now? (Yes.) Past simple or present perfect? (Present perfect.)
"I'm going to study nursing." Decided or not decided? (Decided, a plan.) "I think I'll move to the city." A plan or a guess? (A guess, a prediction.) (Module 3)
"If I pass the exam, I'll celebrate." Did I pass? (Not yet. It's possible.) "If I will pass"? (No! If + present.) (Module 6.1)
Pastpast simple · past continuous · used to + base verbI grew up in a small town. · I was working on a farm. · I used to walk to school.
Up to nowpresent perfect + for / since / just / already / yetI've lived here since 2022. · I've just started a new job.
Futuregoing to (plan) · will (prediction) · If + present, will + verb · want / hope / would like + toI'm going to apply. · I think I'll stay. · If I get the job, I'll stay.

Pronunciation for storytelling. Pause between the three parts (it helps the listener). Stress the time words: "When I was YOUNG…", "NEXT year…". Link used to /ˈjuːstə/ (it sounds like "yoos-ta," not "yoozd-too"). Contracted I've, I'll, I'm sound natural, but full forms are fine in a story.

Drill script (quick, choral → individual)
  1. Time-word trigger: T says a time phrase, learners say the tense and one sentence: "When I was a child…" → "I used to…" · "for three years" → "I've… for three years." · "next year" → "I'm going to…".
  2. Back-chain the bridge line: "…since 2022." → "…lived here since 2022." → "I've lived here since 2022." Then learners change the start point to their own (or their card's).
  3. If-chain: T: "If I pass the exam…" → S1: "…I'll celebrate." S1: "If I celebrate…" → S2: "…I'll eat cake." Keep it fun and fast.
The task — story circles (groups of 3)

Story choices (say them out loud): (1) Real story. (2) Partly fictional: change places, dates or people, skip any part, or start with "up to now." Many learners choose a real present and an imagined, happy future. (3) Fully fictional: a life-story card, or invent a character (a famous person, a character from a movie). Nobody explains their choice.

Round: speaker tells the story in three parts (about 2 minutes; glancing at key words is fine, reading full sentences is not). Listener 1 asks a question about the past, listener 2 about the future (story-circle question cards help). Listeners tick "I heard: past · up to now · future." Then the next speaker. Clap or ring a bell every 5 minutes to keep the circles moving.

ICQs: Does it have to be your real life? (No. Real, partly real, or a card.) How many parts? (Three: past, up to now, future.) Do you read your frame? (No, only look at the key words.) Listeners, what do you do? (Listen, tick, and ask one question.) How long do you talk? (About two minutes.)

Observe for (checklist items 1–8): past forms, used to / past continuous, for / since, just / already / yet, going to / will, a first conditional, verb patterns, and answering a follow-up question.

The observation checklist → C26

Print it from the classroom materials. One row per learner, 8 can-do items, three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help, or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You won't see every learner do everything. That's fine: listen to each learner for at least one full story. You can add marks from the rehearsal stage. After class, use the sheet to:

  • Plan each C26 speaking interview. The C26 interview asks about the same three parts. For learners with ○ on item 3 or 5, start the interview with the part they told best, to build confidence. Learners who used a story card may use the same card in C26.
  • Choose the C26 role-play. The A2 final speaking check includes one Module 7 role-play (travel and directions, eating out or shopping, or a health appointment). Note which one suits each learner.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become the 10-minute review before the assessment starts.
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who didn't finish their story gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is friendly. It's the same as today. I'll help you."
  • Don't grade C25. It's practice. Tell learners so. The checklist is for your planning, not their record.
Differentiation
Literacy support

The story frame can be three pictures: learners draw (or use flashcards for) one scene per part and talk from the pictures. The life-story cards have picture symbols and short lines. Target: 6 sentences (2 per part), with at least one for / since and one going to. Pair a lower-literacy learner with a supportive partner in the rehearsal, not in the task.

Stretch

15+ sentences. Join ideas with Module 6: because, so, although, relative clauses (the town where I grew up), and a when / while sentence (Module 5.2: While I was working in a café, I met…). Listeners ask two follow-up questions, one with How long…?. Tell the story in the third person about a partner (he / she -s).

L1 watch-outs
  • "When I had 20 years…" (Spanish tenía 20 años, Portuguese, French, Italian: age with "have") comes back in life stories. Recast: When I was 20…
  • "I am living here since…" (see C23): the most common error in part 2. Recast it every time.
  • "I used to played" / "I use to" (all learners). The spoken /ˈjuːstə/ hides the d; point to the board: used to + base verb.
  • "If I will get the job…" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, German and Russian speakers often put a future in the if part). Board: If + present, will + verb.
  • No tense change at all (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Malay: time words carry the meaning). The story is still understandable, so praise the time words, then pick one tense to fix per learner.
  • Dropped be in going to (Arabic, Russian, Chinese): I going to open a shop. Tap the 'm.
  • Missing articles in place descriptions (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean): I grew up in small town. Accept in fluency; note it for the feedback stage.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C25: Module 8 and A2 wrap-up

Recap on slide 19: I've lived here since… → I enjoy… / I want to… → my whole story. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook and look back at the "I can…" lists from Module 1. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you, for the C26 warm-up.

The C26 final assessment covers the whole A2 course. From this module it checks: for / since, How long have you…?, just / already / yet, present perfect vs past simple, verb + -ing / verb + to, would like to, and telling a short life story across the three time frames.

Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, a short, friendly final assessment: a one-to-one (or pair) speaking check with you, about 6–8 minutes. You ask a few questions about their life (the same three parts as today: past, up to now, future) and do one short role-play from Module 7 (directions, a café or store, or a doctor's appointment). There are also short listening, reading and writing tasks in the kit's A2 final assessment. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next step, the B1 course, Independent English.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You talk with me for a few minutes, like today. You can tell the same story: real, partly real, or your card. No surprises: it's everything we practiced. Then we celebrate!"

Reduce anxiety: show A2 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you say 'I've lived here for three years, and next year I'm going to…'?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that the life-story recording is great practice for the interview.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great, and it recycles Module 7. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite volunteers to tell their "future plans" part to the whole group as a "graduation speech."

Teacher reflection (after C25)

Look at your checklist and answer three questions: Which two can-dos need a C26 warm-up? Which learners need a quiet word of encouragement? Which role-play and which starting question will each learner get in C26? Write your answers on the back of the checklist.
