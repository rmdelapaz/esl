Module 2 — Describing & Comparing

Module 1 gave learners the past for telling stories. Module 2 gives them the words for choosing and describing, which they need every day for shopping, cooking and talking about habits. In three classes they compare things (cheaper than, the best), talk about amounts (some, any, much, many, a few, a little), and say how often and how they do things (usually, twice a week, carefully, well).

3 lessons Lessons 2.1–2.3 Classes C4–C6 Weeks 2–3 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class has the same shape: warmer (review of the last class) → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs and groups) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-2-comparing.html. Print materials/module-2/classroom-materials.html before C4. It holds the flashcards, the countable/uncountable sort cards, the compare two cities / three phones information gap, the class superlatives quiz, the kitchen-cupboard information gap, the How often? survey, the adverb mime game cards, the new identity habit cards and all listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear the model sentences and dialogues again at home.
  • A2 recycles A1. Learners met some / any with food (A1 7.1), adverbs of frequency (A1 6.1) and the present continuous (A1 4.2). Treat those parts as quick review + extension. Comparatives, superlatives, much / many / a few / a little and adverbs of manner are new.
  • American English throughout. The online lessons and this kit both use American English (apartment, fall, the movies, movie, five dollars, practice, farther). Accept British forms when learners use them (flat, autumn, the cinema, film, five pounds, further).
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

Which one? → How much? → How often and how? In C4 learners compare things and choose (a city, a phone, a restaurant). In C5 they talk about amounts in a kitchen and on a shopping list. In C6 they describe actions: how often they do them and how they do them. All three are "describing" language. Each class ends with a real choice or a real description: which phone to buy, what to buy for dinner, how my week goes.

Trauma-informed: compare things, not people's bodies or lives

Never compare classmates' height, weight, age, looks, money or homes. The lesson has "My brother is taller than me" as a model, and that is fine for a family member the learner chooses to talk about, but all class games use things, cities, products and neutral class facts (who knows the most languages, who got up earliest today). "My home town vs. this city" can be painful for refugees and people who could not choose to leave. Always offer the city fact cards or a dream city instead. Some learners may have little food at home: in C5, the kitchen task uses the picture cupboard cards or an imagined kitchen, and nobody has to describe their own fridge. Say the choices out loud every time: your real life, a card, or an imagined one.

Before the Module

  • Print the picture flashcards (one set for the board: adjectives, food, adverbs). Card stock helps.
  • For C4: one compare-cities information gap page per pair (cut A from B), plus the three-phones Set 2 for fast pairs. One superlatives quiz answer sheet per team of 3–4.
  • For C5: one set of countable/uncountable sort cards per group of 3; one kitchen-cupboard information gap page per pair. Optional realia: a bag with a bottle of water, a packet of rice, two apples, some tea bags.
  • For C6: one How often? survey per learner; one set of mime cards (actions + adverbs) per group of 4–5.
  • Put the new identity habit cards and city fact cards on a side table where anyone can take one without asking.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A comparative compares two things. A superlative picks the number one from a group of three or more.

"The bus is cheaper than the train." How many things? (Two.) Which one costs less money? (The bus.) Is the train cheap? (Maybe, but the bus is cheaper.)
"This is the cheapest phone in the shop." How many phones are in the shop? (Many: three or more.) Is there a phone cheaper than this one? (No.) So it is number…? (One.)
"Tom is as tall as his father." Is Tom taller? (No.) Is his father taller? (No.) So they are…? (The same.)

Form. Count the syllables (clap them).

1 syllableadjective + -er + than · the + adjective + -estcheap → cheaper than → the cheapest
2+ syllablesmore + adjective + than · the most + adjectiveexpensive → more expensive than → the most expensive
2 syllables + -yy → -ier / -iesthappy → happier → the happiest · easy → easier
ends in -e+ -r / -stnice → nicer → the nicest
vowel + consonantdouble the last letterbig → bigger · hot → hotter · thin → thinner
irregularlearn themgood → better → the best · bad → worse → the worst · far → farther/further → the farthest/furthest

Pronunciation. The -er and than are weak: "CHEAP-er-thn" /ˈtʃiːpɚ ðən/. Model the natural chunk: cheaper than · bigger than · better than. The stress stays on the adjective: more exPENsive, the most BEAUtiful. The before a superlative is usually /ðə/: "thuh CHEAPest". The th in than / the is voiced /ð/: tongue between the teeth, and buzz. Accept "dan" early on; don't stop fluency for it.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "than the train" → "cheaper than the train" → "The bus is cheaper than the train." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals.
  2. Prompt drill with flashcards (hold up two): T: "Bus, taxi… cheap." → S: "The bus is cheaper than the taxi." T: "Summer, winter… hot." → S: "Summer is hotter than winter." T: "Hotel, hostel… expensive." → S: "The hotel is more expensive than the hostel."
  3. Three-form chain: T: "big" → S1: "bigger" → S2: "the biggest". Go fast around the room. Put good and bad in often.
  4. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1: "Which is faster, a bus or a plane?" → S2: "A plane is faster than a bus." Then all pairs, 1 minute, with the flashcards.
Compare two cities — how to run it
  • Pairs sit face to face. A and B do not show their cards. Each card has 8 facts, and half the numbers are missing (?).
  • Pre-teach the questions on slide 9: How many people live in…? How much is a bus ticket? How hot is it in summer? How old is…? How far is the airport? (How much is only for prices today. C5 will teach it for amounts.)
  • Round 1 (6 min): ask, answer, and write the numbers. Round 2 (4 min): write 4 comparatives together: "Greenfield is smaller than Port Mara." Round 3 (2 min): choose a city for a family, a student or a retired person, and say why.
  • Set 2 (three phones) needs superlatives: "The Zeta is the cheapest. The Nova has the best camera." Give it to fast pairs, or use it in C5's warmer.

Full facts (for monitoring): Port Mara: 850,000 people · apartment $1,400 a month · bus ticket $2.50 · 84°F (29°C) in summer · 300 years old · 12 parks · airport 22 miles (35 km) · 3 universities. Greenfield: 120,000 people · $950 · $1.75 · 75°F (24°C) · 150 years old · 20 parks · airport 56 miles (90 km) · 1 university. Full answers and model sentences are in the answer key.

ICQs before the city information gap
Do you show your card? (No.) · Do you ask questions or read? (Ask.) · Which question is for money? ("How much…?") · What do you write after you ask? (The number.) · How many comparisons do you write together? (Four.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Use the flashcards and the board table as a "look-up" wall. In Ex 1, do only the first 5 adjectives. In the info gap, the learner only asks and writes the numbers; the partner writes the sentences. Four spoken comparisons with than is a success. Clap the syllables together for the -er / more choice.

Stretch

Add much before comparatives ("much cheaper," "much more expensive") and use not as … as: "Greenfield is not as expensive as Port Mara." In the quiz, write the extra sentence for every answer. Early finishers do Set 2 and give a spoken "sales pitch" for one phone.

L1 watch-outs
  • "more big" / "more cheaper" (Spanish más grande, Portuguese mais, French plus, Tagalog mas malaki, Italian più): these languages use one "more" word for every adjective. Clap the syllables and point at the ❌ on the board.
  • "bigger that / bigger of" (Spanish que, Portuguese do que, Italian di): model than many times. And "the biggest of the world" → in the world (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian).
  • "He is tall than me" / "He than me tall" (Chinese 比 bǐ, Japanese yori, Korean boda): the adjective doesn't change in these languages. Look for the missing -er.
  • "It is most expensive" / "biggest city" (no the): Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and many other speakers drop the article. Keep pointing at the.
  • than / then spelling mix-up (all learners, and many native speakers). "dan / zan" for /ð/ (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, French, Russian). Let the sound go in fluency work.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Countable = you can say "one, two, three." Uncountable = liquids, powders, materials and ideas: no "one, two." A lot of = a big amount. A few / a little = a small amount, but enough.

"Rice." Can I say "one rice, two rices"? (No.) So it is…? (Uncountable.) How do I count it? ("A bag of rice, two bags of rice.")
"How many eggs?" Can I count eggs? (Yes.) "How much milk?" Can I count milk? (No.) Which word goes with countable: much or many? (Many.)
"I have a little money." Do I have a lot? (No.) Do I have zero? (No. I have some, not much.) "A few friends": can I count them? (Yes.)
"Would you like some tea?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Why not "any"? (It's an offer. We think the answer is yes.)

Form.

Countable (plural)Uncountable
(+) big amounta lot of eggsa lot of rice
(−) / (?)not many eggs · How many eggs?not much rice · How much rice?
small amounta few eggsa little rice
some / anysome eggs · any eggs?some rice · any rice?
zeroThere aren't any eggs.There isn't any rice.

Pronunciation. a lot of is one fast chunk: "a-LOT-uh" /əˈlɑːɾəv/, with an American flap t (like a quick d). Some is usually weak /səm/: "I have s'm milk." A few eggs links: "a-fyoo-weggs." A little: "a-LID-l" (American flap t). Stress the question word in "How MUCH?" / "How MAny?". Check the /s/ or /z/ at the end of plurals: eggs, apples, tomatoes.

Drill script
  1. C or U? T holds up a flashcard: "Rice." → S (together): "Uncountable! How much rice?" T: "Eggs." → S: "Countable! How many eggs?" Speed up.
  2. Big or small? T: "Eggs, big amount." → S: "a lot of eggs." T: "Milk, small amount." → S: "a little milk." T: "Tomatoes, small." → S: "a few tomatoes."
  3. Transformation: T: "There's some cheese." (shake head) → S: "There isn't any cheese." (question face) → S: "Is there any cheese?"
  4. Open pairs: S1: "How much water is there?" (points at a bottle) → S2: "There's a little water." Then closed pairs with the sort cards, 1 minute.
Kitchen-cupboard information gap — how to run it
  • A and B sit face to face and do not show the cards. Both cards show the same 16 kitchen items. On A's card, 8 items have an amount and 8 have a ?. B's card is the opposite.
  • Model on slide 14 with a strong learner: "Is there any milk?" → "Yes, there's a little milk." · "How many eggs are there?" → "There are a lot of eggs. Twelve!" · "Are there any bananas?" → "No, there aren't any."
  • Learners write the amount under each "?" item (a number, a word or ✗). Then they compare cards to check.
  • Part 2: choose one meal (pancakes, chicken and rice, or a salad) from the recipe box and write what you need to buy. Report: "For the pancakes, we need some milk and a few eggs. We have a lot of sugar."
ICQs before the kitchen information gap
Do you show your card? (No.) · For eggs, do you ask "How much" or "How many"? (How many.) · For rice? (How much.) · What do you write? (The amount.) · Is it your real kitchen? (No, it's the card kitchen.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

The sort cards have pictures: learners sort physically and say "How many… / How much…?" Color-code: countable cards and many / a few in one color, uncountable and much / a little in another. In the info gap, the learner answers with a number or a hand sign (a lot / a little) and the partner writes. In the workbook, do Ex 1, 2 and 5 only.

Stretch

Add containers to the shopping list ("two bottles of milk, a kilo of rice"). Teach not enough / too much receptively. Early finishers write the dialogue for a phone call: "Can you buy some…? How many…?" Ask them for any in a negative with no: "There's no milk" = "There isn't any milk."

L1 watch-outs
  • "a bread," "breads," "informations," "furnitures," "moneys," "advices" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Russian, Arabic): these words are countable in many languages. Keep a "surprise U" list on the board all class.
  • "How much eggs?" (Chinese duōshǎo 多少, Japanese ikutsu / dore kurai, Vietnamese bao nhiêu, Tagalog ilan / magkano are sorted differently): check with "Can you count it?"
  • "I have much friends / much money" (Spanish mucho, Portuguese muito): correct to a lot of in positives.
  • "There is milk?" / "Have milk?" with no any or no verb (Chinese, Vietnamese, Russian, Tagalog "May gatas?"). Recast: "Is there any milk?"
  • Missing plural -s: "three egg, a few tomato" (Vietnamese, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai, where plurals aren't marked). Listen for the final /s/ /z/.
  • "I don't have no money" (Spanish no tengo nada, Portuguese, Russian double negatives). One negative: "I don't have any money."

Adverbs of Frequency & Manner

90 min
Objectives — learners can…
  • use always, usually, often, sometimes, hardly ever, never in the right place: before the main verb, after be
  • ask How often…? and answer with once a week, twice a month, three times a day, every morning
  • make adverbs of manner with -ly (quickly, carefully, easily, simply) and put them after the verb or verb + object
  • use well, fast, hard and choose good (adjective) or well (adverb)
  • describe their week and how they do things in 6+ sentences
Materials
  • Workbook 2.3 (New words, Grammar box, Exercises 1–6, dialogue, "My habits")

Left: a line from 100% to 0%: always · usually · often · sometimes · hardly ever · never. Under it: I always drink coffee. (before the main verb) · I am always tired. (after be)

Do the survey with only 2 classmates (5 min). Skip "In the manner of the word" and play only the card version. Never cut the mime game: it makes the manner adverbs memorable, and it is the class's favorite part.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Frequency — meaning. How often = how many times. Point at the percentage line.

"I hardly ever watch TV." Do I watch TV a lot? (No.) Never? (Not never. Maybe one time in a month.) Is it closer to "sometimes" or to "never"? (Never.)
"She is usually late." What is the verb? ("is.") Where is "usually": before or after "is"? (After.) "She usually gets up early." Before or after "gets"? (Before.)

Manner — meaning. A manner adverb answers how? An adjective describes a thing or person; an adverb describes an action.

"She drives carefully." Is she fast and dangerous? (No.) Does she look at the road? (Yes.) Does "carefully" tell us about her or about her driving? (Her driving.)
"He's a good cook." "He cooks well." Same meaning? (Yes.) Which word goes with the noun "cook"? (Good.) Which word goes with the verb "cooks"? (Well.)
"I work hard." Do I work a lot, with effort? (Yes.) "I hardly work." Do I work a lot? (No! Almost not.)

Form.

main verbsubject + adverb + verbShe usually walks to work.
besubject + be + adverbThey are never late.
helper verbafter the first helperI can never remember. · Do you usually walk?
How often?once / twice / three times + a + period (at the end)I go to the gym twice a week.
manneradjective + -ly (after the verb or verb + object)He speaks English slowly. ❌ He speaks slowly English.
spelling-y → -ily · -le → -lyeasy → easily · happy → happily · simple → simply · terrible → terribly
irregulargood → well · fast → fast · hard → hardShe sings well. He runs fast. They work hard.

Pronunciation. The stress stays on the adjective part: CAREfully, QUICKly, HAPpily, EAsily. Usually has 3–4 syllables: "YOO-zhoo-uh-lee" /ˈjuːʒuəli/: drill it slowly. Often: most Americans say "OFF-en" with a silent t; "off-ten" is also correct. Once = "wunts" /wʌns/ (the spelling surprises learners). Hardly ever links: "hard-lee-YEV-er."

Drill script
  1. Frequency cards: hold up a % card. T: "Drink coffee… 100%." → S: "I always drink coffee." T: "Be late… 0%." → S: "I'm never late." Mix be and main verbs so learners have to think about the position.
  2. Move the adverb: T: "She walks to work. (usually)" → S: "She usually walks to work." T: "They are tired. (often)" → S: "They are often tired."
  3. How often? chain: S1: "How often do you cook?" → S2: "Three times a week. How often do you exercise?" → S3…
  4. Adjective → adverb with mime: T: "quick" (mime running) → S: "quickly." T: "careful" → "carefully." T: "good" → "well!" Then full sentences: T: "She's a careful driver." → S: "She drives carefully."
Adverb mime game — how to run it
  • Card version (groups of 4–5): put the 15 action cards and the 12 adverb cards face down in two piles. S1 takes one of each and mimes. The group guesses: "You're drinking coffee… happily!" The first learner to say both the action and the adverb keeps the cards. Then that learner mimes next.
  • Ask for the full sentence before the point counts: "You're driving a car carefully." A guess with the adjective ("You're driving careful") = no point until someone fixes it.
  • "In the manner of the word" (whole class): one volunteer goes outside the door. The class chooses an adverb card (e.g. loudly). The volunteer comes back and gives commands: "Anna, open the door in the manner of the word." "Everybody, say hello in the manner of the word." The class does it. The volunteer guesses the adverb (3 guesses).
  • Consent first: nobody has to mime alone. A learner can mime with a partner, or be the "guesser" only. Leave out angrily if someone in the group looks uncomfortable.
ICQs before the mime game
How many cards do you take? (Two: one action, one adverb.) · Do you speak when you mime? (No!) · What does the group say? ("You're …ing …ly!") · Do you have to stand up? (No. You can mime at your desk.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

The frequency cards use percentages and icons (●●●●, ●●○○, ○○○○). Learners hold up a card to answer "How often…?" before they say the word. The action cards have pictures. In the workbook, do Ex 1 (first 3 items), Ex 2 and Ex 3. In the project, 4 sentences is a success.

Stretch

Add very / really / quite ("She speaks really quickly"). Start sentences with Sometimes / Usually for emphasis, but never with always / never. Early finishers write a short "My week" paragraph with 3 frequency and 3 manner adverbs, then underline and check each position.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I drink always coffee" / "She speaks very well English" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, German, Dutch put the adverb after the verb, and let it go between the verb and the object). Model: verb + object stay together.
  • "I don't never cook" (Spanish no… nunca, Portuguese não… nunca, Russian никогда не, Italian): the double negative is correct in these languages. In English, never is enough.
  • "one time a week / two times a day" (Spanish una vez, Portuguese uma vez, Tagalog isang beses, Chinese 一次): understandable. Teach once / twice as the natural form.
  • "She sings good" (German gut, Dutch, and in very informal American speech too). Correct gently: "She sings well." Mention that people sometimes say "It's going good," but well is standard.
  • "fastly" / "hardly" for effort (all learners). "I work hardly" means the opposite of what they want!
  • Verb with no -s after the adverb: "She usually walk to work" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, and anyone focused on the new word). Recycle the A1 he/she -s.
