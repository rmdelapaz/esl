Module 1 — Talking About the Past

The first three classes of A2. Learners meet a new group and show you what they can already do in English. Then they learn the past simple: first was / were, then regular -ed verbs and their three sounds, and last the common irregular verbs with didn't and Did…?. By C3 they can ask a classmate "What did you do last weekend?" and tell a short true story.

3 lessons Lessons 1.1–1.3 Classes C1–C3 Weeks 1–2 90 min per class CEFR A2

How to Use This Guide

  • One lesson block = one 90-minute class. Every class follows the same shape: warmer → lead-in → Meaning / Form / Pronunciation with concept-checking questions (CCQs) → drill (choral, then individual) → controlled practice (workbook) → freer practice (pairs, groups, mingle) → feedback with delayed error correction → homework.
  • The stage plan is your clock. Each one adds up to 90 minutes. The numbers in the Time column are minutes.
  • Present from slides/module-1-the-past.html. Print materials/module-1/classroom-materials.html before C1. It holds the flashcards, the welcome question cards and the A1 diagnostic grid, the new identity cards, the "Where were they?" information gap, the -ed sound sort cards, the picture-story cards, the irregular-verb memory game, the weekend mingle sheet and all the listening scripts.
  • The online lessons are the learners' homework and review. Each one has 🔊 Listen buttons, so learners can hear every model sentence again at home.
  • American English is the classroom standard. The online lessons sometimes use British forms (on holiday, flat, film, shop, practise, memorise) and British phonetic symbols (/wɜːkt/, /wɒtʃt/). Teach vacation, apartment, movie, store, and accept the British forms when learners use them. Both are correct English.
  • Dashed boxes = lines to say out loud, CCQs and ICQs (instruction-checking questions). Green boxes = scripts you read aloud. Amber = watch-outs.
The through-line

How was it? → What did I do? → What did you do? In C1 learners describe where they were and how it was (was / were: no action verbs yet). In C2 they add actions with regular -ed verbs, positive sentences only, and learn to say the ending three ways. In C3 they add the irregular verbs and the question and negative with did. Now they can have a real conversation about the weekend. Every class ends with the same freer task in a bigger form: tell someone about a real past day (yours, or a "new identity" card).

Trauma-informed: the past is personal

"What did you do last weekend?" is an easy question for most people. But for some adults, the recent past holds hard things: a lost job, a hospital visit, a court date, a family far away. Questions like "Where were you last year?" or "When did you come here?" can be painful for newcomers and refugees. Never ask about migration, the home country or "last year" in a whole-class task. Keep personal questions to yesterday and last weekend. For every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real life, a new identity card, or an imagined day ("your dream weekend"). Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal.

If you run long

The welcome mingle is the most important part of C1. Do not cut it: the group needs it, and you need the diagnostic. Cut instead: the dictation (Script 1.1-B) and Ex 4 go to homework, and the information gap becomes a 5-minute task with 3 people only. Never skip the personal "How was your weekend?" question at the end.

Running the diagnostic — what to listen for

The mingle is a friendly first conversation, not a test. Learners must never feel watched. Hold the grid on a clipboard, stand near a pair for 30–60 seconds, and join in sometimes ("Oh, can you cook rice? I can't!"). Mark each learner ✓ (easy and accurate) · ~ (understands, some errors) · ✗ (can't produce it yet) in three columns:

  • BE: I'm / she's / they're, Are you…? Listen for a dropped be ("I tired," "She teacher") and "I have 30 years."
  • PRESENT SIMPLE: I work / I don't work, Do you…?, and he/she -s in the report at the end ("Ana likes tea"). The third-person -s is the hardest A1 point; ~ is normal.
  • CAN: I can / I can't + base verb, Can you…? Listen for "I can to swim" and "I can swimming."

What to do with the results: Mostly ✓ and ~ = the learner is ready for A2. Many ~ for BE = go slowly today, because was/were is built on am/is/are. Many ✗ in two or three columns = check the placement privately (the toolkit's oral placement interview takes 5 minutes) and give extra support from Day 1. If half the class drops the he/she -s, spend 3 minutes of the C2 warmer on it. Never share the grid with learners. Keep it; compare it with the Progress Test in C13.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Was / were is the past of am / is / are. We use it for where people were, how things were, and what places were like, in a finished time (yesterday, last week).

"I was tired last night." Now or before? (Before.) Am I tired now? (We don't know. Maybe not.) Which word shows the past? (Was.)
"They were at the park." One person or more? (More.) So was or were? (Were.)
"You were right, Ana." How many people is "you" here? (One.) Was or were? (Were: you is always were.)
"Were you at home? — No, I wasn't." Was she at home? (No.) Do we say "Did you were…?" (No! Be never needs did.)

Form. Put the A1 forms next to the new ones so learners see the pattern:

PositiveI / he / she / it + was · you / we / they + wereShe was at home. We were happy.
Negativewasn't (was not) · weren't (were not)He wasn't at school. They weren't ready.
QuestionWas / Were + subject…?Were you late? Was it cold?
Short answerYes, I was. / No, I wasn't.(never "Yes, I's")
Wh-Wh- word + was / were + subjectHow was your weekend? Where were you?
PlaceThere was + one / uncountable · There were + pluralThere was a café. There were many people.

Time words. last + a time word (last Monday, last week, last night); a length of time + ago (two days ago, an hour ago); in + a year (in 2020). No "the": last week, not "the last week."

Pronunciation. In positive sentences was and were are weak and fast: /wəz/, /wər/: "I w'z TIRED." Stress goes on the important word. In short answers they are strong: "Yes, I WAS." /wʌz/. wasn't has two syllables /ˈwʌznt/; weren't has one /wɝnt/ (it sounds like "went" with an r). Questions with How was…? fall at the end; yes/no questions (Were you…?) usually rise.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain: "last night" → "tired last night" → "I was tired last night." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Keep was weak.
  2. Subject substitution (point at people in the room): T: "I… at home." S: "I was at home." T: "They…" S: "They were at home." T: "Ana…" S: "Ana was at home." T: "You…" S: "You were at home." Speed up.
  3. Negative transformation: T: "He was at work." (shake head) → S: "He wasn't at work." T: "We were late." → S: "We weren't late."
  4. Question and short answer, open pairs: S1 → S2: "Were you at home last night?" S2: "Yes, I was. / No, I wasn't." S2 → S3: "Was it cold yesterday?"… around the room. Then: "How was your weekend?" → "It was great / OK / terrible."
ICQs before the mingle and the information gap
Welcome mingle: How many cards do you take? (Three, one of each color.) How many questions do you ask each person? (One.) What do you do after the question? (Swap cards and find a new partner.) Can you use a new identity? (Yes.)
Information gap: Can you show your paper? (No.) What do you ask? ("Where was… on Saturday? How was it?") Do you write or draw? (Write.) What do you say if you don't understand? ("Can you say that again, please?")
Differentiation
Literacy support

In the mingle, read the question card to the learner once and let them keep one card only. In the grammar stage, color-code: was in blue with I/he/she/it, were in red with you/we/they, and let them copy the table. In the information gap, they can point to a place picture (flashcards) instead of writing. Dictation 1.1-B: sentences 1–2 only.

Stretch

Ask two follow-up questions in the mingle (the small text on each card). In the freer task, add There was / There were and a reason: "It was great because there weren't many people." Try When I was a child, I was… (only if they choose to). Early finishers write 5 sentences for Ex 5 with no help.

L1 watch-outs
  • "You was / They was": all learners, and common in some spoken English too. Keep pointing at the two columns. You is always were, even for one person.
  • Dropping be: "Yesterday I tired." / "The party very good." (Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai and others have no linking verb here.) Recast: "You were tired."
  • No past form at all: "Yesterday I am at home." (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian and Tagalog speakers often show the past with a time word only.) Praise the time word, then add the verb: "Yesterday I was…"
  • "Did you were…?" / "I didn't was…": learners who know a little about did (Spanish, Portuguese and Arabic speakers often). Be never takes did; put was / were first.
  • "Ago" in the wrong place: "ago two days" (Spanish hace dos días, Portuguese há dois dias, Arabic, Turkish and others). "The last week" (Spanish la semana pasada, French la semaine dernière). Drill the chunks: two days ago · last week.
  • "It had many people" / "Had a party" for "There were…" (Portuguese tinha, Spanish había, Tagalog may, Chinese yǒu, Vietnamese có). The same "have = there is" transfer as in A1.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The past simple is for a finished action at a finished time: yesterday, last week, two days ago. It is often a story: this, then this, then this.

"I cooked dinner last night." Now or in the past? (Past.) Is it finished? (Yes.) Which part of the verb shows the past? (-ed.)
"She worked yesterday." Do we add -s for she? (No.) So: I worked, she worked, they worked… All the same? (Yes!)
"worked." How many beats? (One.) "wanted." How many beats? (Two.) Why? (After t, we add a syllable.)

Form — the four spelling rules.

Most verbs+ -edwork → worked · play → played · watch → watched
Ends in -e+ -dlike → liked · live → lived · arrive → arrived
Consonant + yy → -iedstudy → studied · try → tried (but vowel + y: play → played, stay → stayed)
1 syllable: vowel + consonantdouble + -edstop → stopped · plan → planned (never double w, x, y: showed, fixed, played)

Pronunciation — the three sounds. Spelling and sound are two different steps.

/ɪd/after a t or d sound: one extra syllablewanted · needed · started · visited · decided
/t/after a voiceless sound (p, k, s, sh, ch, f): no buzzworked · watched · stopped · danced · finished · liked
/d/after a voiced sound (vowels, b, g, l, m, n, r, v, z): buzzplayed · called · cleaned · lived · arrived · studied

The /t/ vs /d/ difference is small, and nobody misunderstands it. What matters for being understood: (1) say an ending at all, and (2) don't add a syllable except after t/d ("work-ed" sounds wrong). In fast speech the -ed before a consonant almost disappears (walked to → "walk-to"), so the time word carries the past. Point this out when learners listen: "Listen for yesterday, too."

Drill script
  1. Base → past (flashcards): T shows cook: "cook" → S: "cooked." Choral, then individuals. Hold up fingers for the sound (1, 2, 3) as you go.
  2. Sound groups: drill each column as a chain: "worked, watched, cooked, walked" (/t/) · "played, called, cleaned, stayed" (/d/) · "wanted, needed, started, visited" (/ɪd/, clap the extra beat).
  3. Minimal contrast: T: "I work every day." (point forward) / "I worked yesterday." (point back). Learners say which one: "Now or past?" Then they produce both.
  4. Chain story: S1: "Yesterday I cleaned the kitchen." S2: "Yesterday I cleaned the kitchen and I called my mother." S3 adds one more… Stop at 5 and start a new chain.
The -ed sound sort — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one set of 24 word cards + 3 header cards (/t/ 🤫 · /d/ 🐝 · /ɪd/ 👏). Cards face down in a pile.
  • Turn by turn: take a card, say it out loud with a hand on the throat, put it under a header. Others agree or say "Say it again!" The group may move a card only after saying it again together.
  • When the pile is empty, each learner reads one column aloud. Then turn over the answer strip and check. Talk about the mistakes: "Why is danced /t/?" (The c sounds like s: no buzz.)
  • Round 2 (stretch): each learner takes 3 cards and makes one true sentence about yesterday with each.
ICQs before the sort and the picture story
Sort: Do you read the word quietly or say it out loud? (Out loud.) How many columns? (Three.) What do you say if you are not sure? ("Say it again!")
Picture story: Can your partner see your card? (No.) Who talks first? (A.) What does B do? (Listens and numbers the pictures.) Do you say "clean" or "cleaned"? (Cleaned: it's the past.)
Differentiation
Literacy support

Pictures do the work: in the sort, the teacher or a partner reads each card and the learner decides the sound with fingers. Focus on 8 verbs only (worked, cooked, walked, played, called, cleaned, wanted, visited). Spelling rules are for recognition; copying the verbs is enough. Freer task: 4 sentences with picture cards is a success.

Stretch

Add the doubling rule for longer words for recognition only (preferred, traveled; British travelled). In the freer task, use 8+ verbs, a time for each, and after that / later. Retell the partner's day in the third person to a new partner: "Ali worked, then he…"

L1 watch-outs
  • "Work-ed", "play-ed" (an extra syllable on every verb): learners who read English "as it is written," e.g. Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Swahili speakers. Clap the beats: worked = 1, wanted = 2.
  • No ending at all: "Yesterday I walk to the park." (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Korean, Japanese; Vietnamese speakers also drop final consonants in general.) Make the ending visible: put your hand out and "catch" the -t. Accept progress, not perfection.
  • An extra vowel after the ending: "worked-o", "play-do" (Japanese, Korean, Brazilian Portuguese: words don't end in a consonant cluster). Model the "quick" ending and let it be soft.
  • l / r: played / prayed, collected / corrected (Japanese, Korean, some Chinese speakers). Only correct it when the meaning changes.
  • Spelling transfer: "studyed", "plaied", "stoped", "visitted". Point to the rule on the board, not the learner.
  • "He cookeds" / "She workeds": learners who over-apply the present -s from A1. "Good memory! But in the past: no -s for anyone."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Same meaning as 1.2: a finished action in the past. Irregular verbs only change the positive form. In negatives and questions, every verb is the same: did / didn't + base verb.

"I didn't go to the party." Did I go? (No.) Which word shows the past? (Didn't.) So do we need "went"? (No: go.)
"Did you see the game?" Is it a question about now or the past? (The past.) The answer is yes. What do you say? ("Yes, I did." not "Yes, I saw.")
"Were you at home?" / "Did you stay home?" Which one uses the verb be? (Were you…) Can we say "Did you were…"? (No! Be never uses did.)

Form.

Positiveregular: verb + -ed · irregular: learn the formI played. / I went.
Negativesubject + didn't + base verbShe didn't have breakfast. We didn't watch TV.
QuestionDid + subject + base verb…?Did you go out? Did she eat lunch?
Short answerYes, I did. / No, I didn't.(don't repeat the verb)
Wh-Wh- + did + subject + base verb…?What did you do? Where did they go? Who did you meet?

Pronunciation. Did you joins in natural speech: /dɪdʒə/ ("Didja go?"). What did you sounds like "Whadja" /ˈwʌdʒə/. Learners should understand this; they can say it slowly. didn't = /ˈdɪdnt/: the final t is often silent, so the listener hears "didn'". Stress the main verb and the negative: "I DIDN'T GO." Short answers are strong: "Yes, I DID." Irregular vowel sounds to drill: saw /sɔ/, bought, thought /ɔt/ (the gh is silent), said /sɛd/ (not "sayed"), read /rɛd/, ate /eɪt/.

  5. be or did? T: "at home" → S: "Were you at home?" T: "stay home" → S: "Did you stay home?" T: "tired" → "Were you tired?" T: "sleep well" → "Did you sleep well?"

No match → turn them back.
  • Most pairs at the end wins. Then each learner turns their pairs into negatives: "I didn't buy a cat."
  • Easier: play face up (a matching race) with 8 pairs. Harder: add the 4 bonus pairs, and the sentence must include a time word.

The through-line

Which one? → How much? → How often and how? In C4 learners compare things and choose (a city, a phone, a restaurant). In C5 they talk about amounts in a kitchen and on a shopping list. In C6 they describe actions: how often they do them and how they do them. All three are "describing" language. Each class ends with a real choice or a real description: which phone to buy, what to buy for dinner, how my week goes.

Trauma-informed: compare things, not people's bodies or lives

Never compare classmates' height, weight, age, looks, money or homes. The lesson has "My brother is taller than me" as a model, and that is fine for a family member the learner chooses to talk about, but all class games use things, cities, products and neutral class facts (who knows the most languages, who got up earliest today). Some learners may have little food at home: in C5, the kitchen task uses the picture cupboard cards or an imagined kitchen, and nobody has to describe their own fridge. Say the choices out loud every time: your real life, a card, or an imagined one.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A comparative compares two things. A superlative picks the number one from a group of three or more.

"The bus is cheaper than the train." How many things? (Two.) Which one costs less money? (The bus.) Is the train cheap? (Maybe, but the bus is cheaper.)
"This is the cheapest phone in the shop." How many phones are in the shop? (Many: three or more.) Is there a phone cheaper than this one? (No.) So it is number…? (One.)
"Tom is as tall as his father." Is Tom taller? (No.) Is his father taller? (No.) So they are…? (The same.)

Form. Count the syllables (clap them).

Pronunciation. The -er and than are weak: "CHEAP-er-thn" /ˈtʃiːpɚ ðən/. Model the natural chunk: cheaper than · bigger than · better than. Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Countable = you can say "one, two, three." Uncountable = liquids, powders, materials and ideas: no "one, two." A lot of = a big amount. A few / a little = a small amount, but enough.

"Rice." Can I say "one rice, two rices"? (No.) So it is…? (Uncountable.) How do I count it? ("A bag of rice, two bags of rice.")
"How many eggs?" Can I count eggs? (Yes.) "How much milk?" Can I count milk? (No.) Which word goes with countable: much or many? (Many.)
"I have a little money." Do I have a lot? (No.) Do I have zero? (No. I have some, not much.) "A few friends": can I count them? (Yes.)
"Would you like some tea?" Is this a question? (Yes.) Why not "any"? (It's an offer. We think the answer is yes.)

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Frequency — meaning. How often = how many times. Point at the percentage line.

"I hardly ever watch TV." Do I watch TV a lot? (No.) Never? (Not never. Maybe one time in a month.) Is it closer to "sometimes" or to "never"? (Never.)
"She is usually late." What is the verb? ("is.") Where is "usually": before or after "is"? (After.) "She usually gets up early." Before or after "gets"? (Before.)

Manner — meaning. A manner adverb answers how? An adjective describes a thing or person; an adverb describes an action.

"She drives carefully." Is she fast and dangerous? (No.) Does she look at the road? (Yes.) Does "carefully" tell us about her or about her driving? (Her driving.)
"He's a good cook." "He cooks well." Same meaning? (Yes.) Which word goes with the noun "cook"? (Good.) Which word goes with the verb "cooks"? (Well.)
"I work hard." Do I work a lot, with effort? (Yes.) "I hardly work." Do I work a lot? (No! Almost not.)

Adverb mime game — how to run it
  • Card version (groups of 4–5): put the 15 action cards and the 12 adverb cards face down in two piles. S1 takes one of each and mimes. The group guesses: "You're drinking coffee… happily!" The first learner to say both the action and the adverb keeps the cards. Then that learner mimes next.
  • Ask for the full sentence before the point counts: "You're driving a car carefully." A guess with the adjective ("You're driving careful") = no point until someone fixes it.
  • "In the manner of the word" (whole class): one volunteer goes outside the door. The class chooses an adverb card (e.g. loudly). The volunteer comes back and gives commands: "Anna, open the door in the manner of the word." "Everybody, say hello in the manner of the word." The class does it. The volunteer guesses the adverb (3 guesses).
  • Consent first: nobody has to mime alone. A learner can mime with a partner, or be the "guesser" only. Leave out angrily if someone in the group looks uncomfortable.

The through-line

My plans → my guesses → our plans. In C7 learners talk about their own plans and intentions (I'm going to…) and predict from what they can see (It's going to rain). In C8 they predict what they think or are not sure about (will / won't / might) and make decisions on the spot (I'll get it!). In C9 they make plans with another person: they suggest, accept or decline politely, find a free time in two diaries, and confirm the arrangement (So we're meeting on Thursday at seven.). The end of every class is the same freer task in a new form: share real (or imagined) plans with a partner.

Trauma-informed: the future is not easy for everyone

For some adult learners the future is uncertain or painful: a visa or asylum decision, a job search, family far away, temporary housing. Never ask "Are you going to stay in this country?", "Where will you live next year?" or "When will you see your family?" Keep tasks to small, everyday plans (this weekend, tonight, next week) and to light predictions (the weather, sports, the class, technology). For every personal task offer three choices out loud: your real plans, a "future identity" card (in the classroom materials), or a dream / imagined future. Anyone can say "Pass!" in the prediction game. Say it every time, so choosing a card is normal and not a sign that something is wrong.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning: prediction / opinion. What I think will happen. No plan, and often no evidence in front of me.

"I think our team will win." Do I know? (No, I think.) Is it a plan? (No.) Is it the future? (Yes.) · "It won't rain tomorrow." Rain tomorrow: yes or no? (No.) Am I sure? (Yes, quite sure.)

Crystal Ball game — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one card deck face down, one die. Put the certainty cards (won't / might / will) in the middle as a reminder.
  • Player 1 takes a card ("the weather this weekend," "this class next month," "phones in 20 years") and rolls: 1–2 = won't, 3–4 = might, 5–6 = will. They make a prediction with that word and add a reason: "Phones won't have buttons in 20 years, because everything is touch now."
  • The group reacts with one phrase: "I agree!" · "Maybe." · "I don't think so. I think…" The player who reacts best (clear, polite, with a reason) keeps the card. Most cards wins.
  • "You" cards are about the person on your left, and must be kind and light ("You'll have a great weekend." "You might find a great recipe."). Any player can say "Pass!" and take a new card.
  • Some learners are not comfortable with fortune-telling for cultural or religious reasons. The Weather Forecaster deck uses the same rules with weather maps and needs no crystal ball. Offer the choice without comment.

  • Step 1: find a free time. Learners ask about a slot: "Are you doing anything on Monday afternoon?" The partner reads the arrangement: "Yes, I'm going to the dentist." Ask for the next slot or suggest: "How about Tuesday evening?"
  • Step 2: when both are free, suggest an activity from the ideas box (two ways: "Let's… / Why don't we…?"). The partner accepts or says a polite no to the first idea and suggests another.
  • Step 3: agree a time and place ("Shall we meet at seven? At the station?") and confirm: "So we're meeting on … at … See you then!"
  • Answer: the only free time for both is Thursday evening. Pairs who finish fast use the blank diaries (round 2).

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The present perfect talks about experience in my life up to now, when the exact time is not important and we don't say it. The past simple talks about one finished time that we say or know. In real talk, English speakers open a topic with the present perfect and then explain it with the past simple. Point at the board: box first, then the ✕.

"I've been to Mexico." Did I go to Mexico? (Yes.) Do we know when? (No.) Is the time important here? (No, only the experience.)
"I went to Mexico in 2019." Do we know when? (Yes, 2019.) Is it finished? (Yes.) So present perfect or past simple? (Past simple.)
"Have you ever eaten sushi?" Am I asking about yesterday? (No.) About what time? (Any time in your life.)
"I've never broken a bone." Did I break a bone last year? (No.) In my whole life? (No, never.) Do I need "not"? (No! "never" is already negative.)
"She's gone to the store." Is she here now? (No.) "She's been to the store." Is she here now? (Yes. She went and came back.)

Been in American English is /bɪn/ (like "bin") in fast speech; both /bɪn/ and /biːn/ are fine.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Have to = a rule or a need, usually from outside (the boss, the school, the doctor). Must = a strong rule, very common on signs and written rules. Don't have to = not necessary: your choice. Mustn't = not allowed. Should = a good idea (advice), not a rule.

"You don't have to pay. It's free." Can I pay if I want? (Yes. It's OK.) Do I need to pay? (No.) Is it my choice? (Yes.)
"You mustn't smoke here." Is it my choice? (No.) Is it OK to smoke? (No. It's not allowed.) So is "don't have to" the same as "mustn't"? (No! Very different.)
"You should see a doctor." Is it a rule? (No.) Is it a good idea? (Yes.) Who decides? (You do.)
"She has to work on Saturday." Does she want to? (Maybe not!) Does she need to? (Yes. It's her job.)
American English: mustn't vs can't

Americans rarely say mustn't in conversation. They say can't or you're not allowed to: "You can't park here." Teach mustn't so learners understand signs, forms and British speakers, but model can't as the everyday spoken form and accept both. Also accept have got to / gotta if learners know it.

  • The reader answers: "Good idea! / Hmm, I can't do that because…" and picks the best advice. Rotate roles.
  • All problems are everyday ones (sleep, a new job, a noisy neighbor, a child's school, learning English, a broken phone). If a group invents a legal or immigration problem, gently steer them: "Let's choose an everyday problem today."
  • Stretch: advisers must soften (Maybe you should… / I think you should…) and give a reason with because.

L1 watch-outs
  • "You must to go" / "You should to rest": Spanish tener que / deber, French devoir, Arabic lāzim an, Russian nado / dolzhen. Learners copy the to from have to. Stamp on the "to."
  • "You don't have to smoke" meaning "don't smoke": in German (du musst nicht = you don't have to) and many other languages the negative of "must" means "not necessary," while in others it means "not allowed." Both directions cause trouble. Use the traffic lights every time.
  • "She have to work" / "She has to works": the -s goes on has, once. Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai speakers often drop it; others add it twice.
  • "Do you must…?" / "You mustn't to…": learners treat must like a normal verb. Recast with the natural question: "Do you have to…?"
  • Politeness and directness: in many cultures giving strong advice is kind; in American English "You must…" to a friend can sound bossy. Teach Maybe you should… / I think you should… as friendly, not weak.

Meaning: each tense has a job in the story. Show the story road on slide 16 and ask which step each sentence belongs to.

"The hotel was small, but it was clean." Which step? (① the scene.) Why "was"? (It's the past; it describes.)
"I've never seen the ocean before." Is this about one day or my whole life? (My whole life, up to now.) Can I add "last year"? (No! Then: "I saw the ocean last year.")

Pronunciation. Two things matter most for a listener: the -ed ending (/t/ walked, /d/ stayed, /ɪd/ visited: only /ɪd/ adds a beat) and pausing between the five steps. Drill script
  1. Polite chunks: T: "Where's the check-in?" → S: "Excuse me, where's the check-in, please?" Four phrases, choral then individuals, rising-then-falling polite intonation.
  2. Tense switch drill: T holds up a step number and a verb. "① be / hot" → S: "It was hot." "④ eat / octopus / never" → S: "I've never eaten octopus." "⑤ visit / Peru" → S: "I'm going to visit Peru."
  3. Link it: T says two short sentences: "We were tired. We went to bed." → S: "We were tired, so we went to bed." Then with because and but.
  4. Best… ever: T: "good / trip" → S: "It was the best trip I've ever had." "beautiful / place" → "the most beautiful place I've ever seen."
The story-planning frame — how to run it
  • Show your own filled-in frame first (a real or invented trip). Notes only: 3–6 words per box. Full sentences on paper become reading aloud.
  • Any trip is a trip: a day at the beach, a visit to family in another town, a school trip, a work trip, a vacation, a dream trip, or a trip card. Say it out loud so no one feels their trip is "too small."
  • Step ④ is the hardest. Give three sentence starters: I've never… before. · It was the best… I've ever… · I've been to… but…
  • Step ⑤ can be a dream: "One day I'm going to…" is fine.
  • Early finishers add a travel-phrase moment: "At the hotel I said, 'Is breakfast included?'"

The through-line

A moment → a story → a life. In C14 learners freeze one moment: what was happening at 8 p.m.? In C15 something happens in the middle of that moment: the long line (past continuous) meets the dot (past simple), and a story is born. In C16 learners zoom out to whole periods of life: what used to be true, and what is true now. Past-tense storytelling is the spine of A2. Module 1 gave learners the past simple; this module gives their stories a background, a surprise and a "before and after." Module 8 will bring it all together in the life-story project.

Trauma-informed: the past can be a hard place

This whole module asks about the past: last night, "something that happened," how life used to be. For some adults the past holds war, loss, detention, or a home they cannot go back to. Three rules for every personal task in this module:

  • Invented is always fine. Say it out loud every time: "Your real life, or a character card, or make it up." Choosing a card is normal, not a sign that something is wrong.
  • Never make the home country the topic. The online lesson 5.3 suggests "your hometown." In class, used to is about childhood, a job you used to have, or a character card. A learner may choose to talk about their hometown, but you never ask for it.
  • The Alibi Game is a silly mystery, not an interrogation. Questions like "Where were you at 8 p.m.?" can feel like a police interview to someone who has lived through one. So the "crime" is a missing birthday cake, the roles are "detectives" and "cake suspects," the alibi is invented together, and anyone can choose to be a detective instead of a suspect. Never ask a learner where they really were last night in front of the class; in pair drills, invented answers are welcome.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The past continuous describes an action in progress at a moment in the past. It was not finished at that moment; it was "in the middle." Use the camera image: the past simple tells the whole event; the past continuous is a photo taken in the middle of it.

"At eight o'clock I was cooking dinner." At eight, did I start cooking? (No, I started before.) At eight, was the cooking finished? (No.) So at eight I was… (in the middle of cooking).
"I cooked dinner." Is it finished? (Yes.) "I was cooking dinner at eight." Do we know if I finished? (No, we only know it was happening at eight.)

The Alibi Game — how to run it (22 min)
  • Set-up (2 min). Read a mystery card aloud with a dramatic voice: "Last night, between 7 and 9 p.m., someone ate the birthday cake in the school kitchen. Only crumbs! Two people were in the building. Were they together all evening, like they say?" Make groups of 4: 2 suspects + 2 detectives. Anyone may choose to be a detective. It's a game; the evening is invented.
  • Plan (5 min). The two suspects sit together with the suspect planning sheet and invent their shared evening: where they were and what they were doing at 7:00, 7:30, 8:00, 8:30 and 9:00, plus details (what was the weather doing? what were you eating? what was on TV?). The two detectives use the question cards to write 6–8 questions on their notes sheet: "What were you doing at 7:30? What was your friend wearing?"
  • Interview (8 min). Suspect 1 stays; Suspect 2 goes to another group's table (or waits by the door with their back turned) for 4 minutes. The detectives ask their questions and write short notes. Swap: Suspect 2 comes back and answers the same questions. Suspects must not hear each other.
  • Verdict (3 min). Detectives compare the two sets of answers: ✓ same, ✗ different. 3 or more ✗ = "You ate the cake!" Then they report to the class with the past continuous: "At eight, Ana said they were watching TV, but Omar said they were eating pizza!"
  • Swap roles (4 min) with a new mystery card if there is time, or save the second round for the C15 warmer.

Large class: run it once as a whole class first. Two volunteer suspects leave the room to plan, and the whole class writes detective questions. Then everyone plays in groups. Small class (4–7): one group of suspects and everyone else are detectives.

  • State verbs in the continuous ("I was knowing," "I was wanting"): common for Hindi, Urdu, Punjabi, Tagalog and some African-language speakers. Keep it light: I knew / I wanted.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Two actions, two roles. The background action was already in progress (long line → past continuous). The interrupting action happened in the middle of it (a dot → past simple). The background started first.

"I was sleeping when the phone rang." Which started first, the sleeping or the phone? (The sleeping.) Was the phone call long or short? (Short, sudden.) Did the phone stop my sleeping? (Yes, probably!)
"While she was cooking, the doorbell rang." Was she cooking before the doorbell? (Yes.) Which word goes with the long action? (While.)
"When the movie ended, we went home." Was anything in progress? (No.) One thing, then another? (Yes.) So both verbs are… (past simple).

Punctuation. When the sentence starts with When or While, put a comma after the first part: "While she was reading, the light went out." No comma in the middle: "The light went out while she was reading."

A note on "when." At A2, teach the clear pattern: when + short action. Learners will also hear when + background ("When I was living in Chicago, …"). If a strong learner asks, say: "Yes, that's correct too. When can mean 'at that time.'" Don't mark it wrong.

Pronunciation. Storytelling is about rhythm. The background clause is often said a little slower; the event is stressed and sudden: "I was walking HOME… when it STARTED to RAIN!" Encourage a small pause before when and a bigger voice on the event. Suddenly is stressed on the first syllable: SUD-den-ly. Irregular past vowels: rang /æ/, fell /ɛ/, began /æ/, broke /oʊ/.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Used to + base verb tells two things at once: (1) it was true again and again (a habit) or for a long time (a state) in the past, and (2) it is not true now.

"I used to walk to work." Did I walk to work one time or many times? (Many times.) Do I walk to work now? (No.) Is this about the past, the present, or the future? (The past.)
"She used to have long hair." Is her hair long now? (No.) Was it long for one day or a long time? (A long time.)
"Yesterday I used to see a movie." Right or wrong? (Wrong.) Why? (Yesterday is one time.) So… ("Yesterday I saw a movie.")
"I use to go to the gym." (said about now) Is used to for now? (No, only the past.) For now we say… ("I usually go to the gym.")

Then vs nowused to …, but now …I used to walk to work, but now I drive.

Why no -d? Did already carries the past, just like I didn't go (not "didn't went"). Learners know this rule from Lesson 1.3; connect it.

Pronunciation. Used to and use to sound the same: /ˈjuːstə/, "YOO-stuh." The s is /s/ (not /z/ like the verb "use" /juːz/), and the d disappears in speech. That is why the spelling mistake is so common: learners can't hear the difference. Drill the sound, then show the spelling rule in writing. Stress falls on the main verb: "I used to WALK to work, but now I DRIVE." Contrastive stress on then/now makes the comparison clear.

  3. Negative: T: "I liked coffee." (shake head, point to THEN) → S: "I didn't use to like coffee." Write use on the board and cross out the d with a flourish.
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "Did you use to play soccer?" S2: "Yes, I did. / No, I didn't." S2 → S3 with a new verb (play the guitar, swim, sing, have a bike). Any answer can be invented.

  • "Before I play soccer" for "I used to play soccer": Chinese yǐqián, Tagalog dati, Vietnamese trước đây, Korean yejeone put a time word in front of a plain verb. Praise the meaning: "Yes! Before… so: I used to play soccer."
  • "I was used to play" (Arabic, Russian, French speakers who know be used to): Be used to is a different phrase (B1). Today: no was.
  • Past continuous for habits (from C14: "When I was a child I was going to the beach every summer"): now show the better tool: "I used to go to the beach every summer."

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. The first conditional talks about a real possibility in the present or future and its result. The condition may happen or may not. It is not a dream (that is the second conditional, B1).

"If it rains, we'll stay home." Is it raining now? (No / we don't know.) Is rain possible? (Yes.) Past, or future? (Future.) If it rains, what happens? (We stay home.)
"If it rains, we might stay home." Are we sure we'll stay home? (No. Maybe.) "If you don't hurry, you won't catch the bus." Will you catch it? (No.)
"When I get home, I'll call you." Will I get home? (Yes, of course.) "If I get home early, I'll call you." Will I get home early? (Maybe. We don't know.)

Form. Two boxes on the board; the if-box is a "no will zone."

If a learner says a general truth like "If you heat ice, it melts" (present in both halves), accept it, but don't teach it as a new structure today: it is the zero conditional (B1).

Pronunciation. Use the contractions in speech: I'll /aɪl/, you'll /jʊl/, we'll /wil/ (like "wheel"), it'll /ˈɪtəl/. Many learners drop the /l/ completely, so "I'll call" becomes "I call." Make the "dark l" clear: tongue up, a little "ul" sound. won't /woʊnt/ vs want /wɑnt/: this pair confuses listeners. Drill a minimal pair: "I won't go" (no) / "I want to go" (yes). Intonation: the if-clause rises and pauses at the comma, and the result falls: "If it RAINS ↗ | we'll stay HOME ↘." The comma in writing = the pause in speech.

Drill script
  1. Back-chain the model: "stay home" → "we'll stay home" → "If it rains, | we'll stay home." Choral ×3, half the room, then 4–5 individuals. Show the rise-pause-fall with your hand.
  2. Picture substitution: hold up a flashcard (☀️, 🌧️, 🚌) and a result card. T: "sunny… park" → S: "If it's sunny, I'll go to the park." T: "late… miss the bus" → S: "If I'm late, I'll miss the bus."
  3. Certainty drill: T says the sentence and shows a gesture: thumbs up = will, shrug = might, thumbs down = won't. T: "If I have time… cook dinner" (shrug) → S: "If I have time, I might cook dinner."
  4. Clause flip: T: "If you study, you'll pass." → S: "You'll pass if you study." Write one flip on the board and ask: "Comma?" (No.)
  5. Open pairs → closed pairs: S1 asks across the room: "What will you do if it rains on Saturday?" S2: "If it rains, I'll…" Then all pairs for 1 minute with new weather words (cold, sunny, snowy).
The If-chain game — how to run it
  • Groups of 4–5 sit in a circle. Each group gets the starter cards face down and the twist cards face down in a second pile.
  • Round 1 (model): read Script 6.1-C. Show the change on the board: "we'll stay home" → "If we stay home, …" The result loses its 'll and moves into the if-box.
  • Round 2 (play): S1 turns a starter card and says it: "If it rains, I'll stay home." S2 takes the result and makes a new condition: "If I stay home, I'll watch a movie." S3: "If I watch a movie, I'll eat popcorn…" Go around twice.
  • Beep! If anyone says will in the if-half ("If I will stay home…"), the group says "Beep!" and the speaker says it again. That's the only rule the group polices.
  • Twists: every third turn, the speaker takes a twist card first: use might, use won't, start with when, put if in the middle, make it a warning, make it a promise.
  • Finish: when a chain gets back to the start ("…so I'll go outside, and it's raining!"), the group claps and turns a new starter. Stronger groups try to make a chain of 8 without a Beep.
  • Walk around with a notebook: write errors for the feedback stage. Don't stop the game to correct.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. A defining relative clause tells us which person, thing or place we mean. It works like a long adjective: a tall man → a man who is tall. Without it, the listener asks "Which one?"

"The man who lives next door is a doctor." Which man? (The man next door.) How many ideas are in the sentence? (Two: he lives next door; he's a doctor.) Is who for a person or a thing? (A person.)
"This is the phone which I bought yesterday." Is which for a person? (No, a thing.) "That's the café where we met." Is the café a person, a thing or a place? (A place.) So which word? (where.)
"the woman who she works at the bank" — OK? (No.) Why not? (who = she. We need only one.)
"That's the book I bought." Is a word missing? (Yes: that / which.) Is the sentence OK? (Yes.) "That's the man lives next door." OK? (No! A verb comes after, so keep who.)

Pronunciation. A defining clause is one tone unit, with no pause and no comma: "the-man-who-lives-next-DOOR." Drill it as one chunk. who /hu/ has a clear /h/ (French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and Russian speakers often drop or change it to /x/). which /wɪtʃ/ sounds the same as witch; don't worry about the "wh." where /wɛr/ vs were /wɜr/: many learners mix them up, so drill the pair once. that in a relative clause is weak: /ðət/.

Drill script
  1. Speed choice: T holds up a Define it! card; the class calls out the pronoun only: (nurse) "who!" · (airport) "where!" · (key) "which!" Get faster.
  2. Joining drill: T says two sentences; S joins. T: "I have a friend. She lives in Texas." → S: "I have a friend who lives in Texas." T: "This is the bus. It goes downtown." → S: "This is the bus which goes downtown." T: "That's the school. I studied there." → S: "That's the school where I studied."
  3. Back-chain the chunk: "next door" → "who lives next door" → "the man who lives next door" → "That's the man who lives next door." One breath, no pause.
  4. Which one? in open pairs: S1 points at something in the room: "Which one is your bag?" S2: "It's the bag which is under the chair." S2 → S3: "Who is Maria?" S3: "She's the woman who is sitting next to the door."
Define it! — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4. One set of word cards, face down. Learners take turns as the describer.
  • The describer takes the top card and looks at it privately. They start with the frame from the card's icon: 👤 "It's a person who…", 📍 "It's a place where…", 🧰 "It's a thing which / that…"
  • They must not say the word, point at the object, or mime. (Literacy-support learners may mime after one sentence.)
  • The first person to guess says the full sentence: "Is it a nurse? A nurse is a person who helps doctors." Then they keep the card. Most cards wins.
  • Stuck after 1 minute? The describer adds a second clue: "It's a thing that you use when it rains." If still stuck, the card goes to the bottom.
  • Stretch round: learners write their own card for a word from their life or job (a forklift, a food bank, a nail salon) and the group guesses.

  • Clause before the noun (Chinese, Japanese, Korean): "the lives-next-door man." Build the sentence left to right on the board: noun first, then the clause.
  • "the man which…" / "the book what I bought": Spanish, Portuguese, French and Italian use one word (que / che) for people and things, and Russian kotoryj works for both too. Learners pick which or what for everything. Offer that as the safe choice for people and things.
  • "the town that I grew up" / "the town where I grew up in" (Spanish, Portuguese, Tagalog, Russian): mixing that and where. Rule of thumb: place + new subject → where, and no extra preposition.
  • One linker for everything (Tagalog na / -ng, Indonesian yang, Chinese de): learners may use that everywhere, even for places. Accept that for people and things; correct it for places.
  • A comma before the clause ("the man, who lives next door"): German, Russian and Polish always put a comma before a relative clause. At A2, defining clauses have no comma.

Objectives — learners can…

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Each linker has one job. Teach the job, not a translation. The key pair is because (reason) and so (result): the same event from opposite ends.

"I was tired, so I went to bed." What came first? (I was tired.) What's the result? (I went to bed.) "I went to bed because I was tired." Why did I go to bed? (Because I was tired.) Same night, same story? (Yes.)
"Why did you stay home?" Can you answer "So it was raining"? (No.) "Because it was raining"? (Yes.) Which word answers "Why?" (because.)
"Although it rained, we went out." Did it rain? (Yes.) Did we go out? (Yes.) Is that a little surprising? (Yes.) "Although it rained, but we went out." OK? (No. One contrast word.)
"I like tea, but I prefer coffee." Two ideas the same, or different? (Different.) "I made tea and sat down." Different, or one more idea? (One more idea.)

Form.

andadd · usually no commaI made tea and sat down.
butcontrast · comma beforeI like tea, but I prefer coffee.
soresult · comma beforeI was tired, so I went to bed.
becausereason · usually no commaI went to bed because I was tired. / Because I was tired, I went to bed.
although / thoughsurprise contrast · comma after the first halfAlthough it rained, we went out.
✗never two linkers for one linkAlthough… but · Because… so

Pronunciation. because is usually weak: /bɪˈkəz/, stress on the second syllable. In fast speech learners will hear 'cause /kəz/; accept it in speaking, not in writing. although /ɔlˈðoʊ/: stress on -THOUGH, and the th is voiced. Learners mix though / through / thought, so write all three once and say them. Pausing: pause at the comma before but and so, and after the although half: "Although it RAINED ↗ | we went OUT ↘." and is weak /ən/: "tea-n-toast."

Drill script
  1. Linker cue: T says a first half and holds up a linker card; S finishes. T: "I was hungry…" (SO) → S: "I was hungry, so I made a sandwich." T: "I was hungry…" (BUT) → S: "…but I didn't eat." T: "I was hungry…" (BECAUSE) → S: "…because I didn't have breakfast."
  2. because ↔ so flip: T: "I stayed home because it was raining." → S: "It was raining, so I stayed home." Then reverse. Choral, then individuals.
  3. but → although: T: "The test was hard, but I passed." → S: "Although the test was hard, I passed." Tap your palm at the comma.
  4. Grow a sentence: start with "I went to the market." Each learner adds one linker + idea: "…and I bought some fruit" → "…but it was expensive" → "…so I bought only apples" → "…because I love apples!" The class repeats the whole sentence at the end.
Sentence builder → paragraph — how to run it
  • Step 1 · halves (4 min). Each group gets 12 sentence-half strips. The first half ends with a comma or nothing; the second half starts with the linker (except Although, which starts a first half). Groups match them and read the pairs aloud. There is only one perfect match per strip (answers in the Answer Key).

  • Step 4 · my paragraph (writing stage). Each learner writes their own paragraph on the same topic or a different one. They can copy the group's version, change it for their own life, or use the idea cards as a "new identity."

L1 watch-outs
  • "Although it rained, but we went out" / "Because I was tired, so I slept": Chinese (suīrán… dànshì, yīnwèi… suǒyǐ), Vietnamese (tuy… nhưng, vì… nên) and Korean use a pair of linkers. This is the #1 error of the lesson for these learners. Show two cards in one hand, then drop one.
  • "and… and… and…" chains (Arabic wa, and many learners with oral traditions): long sentences joined only with and. Praise the fluency, then ask "What's the link here: result or reason?"
  • "Because it was raining." alone as a written sentence: Spanish and Portuguese speakers use porque for both "why" and "because," and many learners write the answer to a question as a full sentence. In speech it's fine; in the paragraph, join it.
  • so = "very": "I was so tired so I slept." Show the two meanings: so tired (very) vs , so I slept (result).
  • Comma splices and run-ons: Russian, Polish and German put commas by grammar rules that differ from English. Keep to the three comma habits on the board; don't teach more at A2.
  • /ð/ in although: "alzo," "altho" with /d/ or /z/ (many L1s). Tongue between the teeth, voice on. Don't stop fluency work for it.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning — asking. "How do I get to…?" asks for the route. "Is there a… near here?" asks if the place exists nearby (the speaker doesn't know). "Which way is…?" asks only for the direction.

Meaning — giving. Directions use the imperative: the base verb, no subject. It is not rude here. It is normal and friendly for directions and instructions.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

too — meaning. too = more than is OK: a problem. very = strong, but not a problem.

"This jacket is too expensive." Can I buy it? (No.) Is it a problem? (Yes.) "This jacket is very expensive." Can I maybe buy it? (Yes, maybe.)
"This tea is too good." Is that a compliment? (No! It sounds strange.) So we say…? ("very good.")

enough — meaning. enough = the amount you need: OK. not… enough = less than you need: a problem.

"The jeans aren't long enough." Are they too long or too short? (Too short.) Is it a problem? (Yes.) "Is there enough time?" Do we need more time? (Maybe. That's the question.)

Complaint language — meaning. I'm afraid… here does not mean "I'm scared." It is a soft way to start bad news.

"I'm afraid there's a problem with my soup." Am I scared? (No.) Am I angry? (Maybe a little, but I'm polite.) Is it good news or bad news? (Bad news, said softly.)

Form.

too + adjectivea problem: more than OKIt's too expensive. These shoes are too small.

adjective + enoughOK: as much as you needIt's big enough. It isn't long enough.
enough + nounOK / not OK amountWe don't have enough chairs.
ComplainExcuse me. I'm afraid there's a problem with + noun. + Could I / Could you…?I'm afraid there's a problem with the check. Could you check it, please?
Staff answerI'm (so) sorry about that. + I'll + verbI'm so sorry. I'll bring you a new one right away.

Pronunciation. enough /ɪˈnʌf/: the gh sounds like f, stress on the second syllable. too is stressed and long in a complaint: "It's TOO small." Contractions sound friendly: I'd like /aɪd/, I'll have /aɪl/, I'm afraid. Intonation matters more than grammar here: say the complaint twice, once flat and angry, once warm with a small smile. Learners vote: which one gets a new soup faster? Stress in reFUND (noun and verb), exCHANGE, reCEIPT /rɪˈsiːt/ (the p is silent).

  2. too / enough transformation (hold up a flashcard): T: "The shoes are small." → S: "They're too small." / "They aren't big enough." T: "The coffee is hot." → S: "It's too hot." T: "chairs… not" → S: "We don't have enough chairs."

Cultural note: tipping and "the check"

In U.S. sit-down restaurants the tip is usually not included; people add about 15–20% for the server. The online lesson's "Is service included?" is more British/European. At a counter or fast-food place, a tip is optional. Present this as information, not a rule to judge: tipping customs differ a lot around the world, and many learners find them confusing. Servers in the class can explain it from their own experience if they want to.

L1 watch-outs
  • "I want…" / "Give me the soup": direct requests are polite in Russian (Дайте…), Arabic, Polish, Chinese and many other languages, but sound rude in English. Treat it as a register issue, not a grammar mistake: "Good English, but in a restaurant we say 'Could I have…?'"
  • "It's too good / too beautiful!" as a compliment: Chinese 太好了 (tài hǎo le), Spanish demasiado (informal), and Tagalog speakers sometimes use "too" to mean "very." The CCQ "Is it a problem?" fixes it.
  • "It's enough big": Spanish bastante grande, Portuguese grande o suficiente/bastante grande, French assez grand, Russian достаточно большой put the word before the adjective. Clap the order: big — ENOUGH.
  • "too much people": Spanish mucha gente and Portuguese muita gente are singular/uncountable. People is plural in English: too many people.
  • "The account, please": Spanish la cuenta, Portuguese a conta, Italian il conto. Teach the check (US) and the bill (UK, also understood in the U.S.).
  • "I'm afraid" taken literally ("Why are you afraid?"). Explain once with the CCQ; it is a very common softener at work.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Symptoms — meaning. I've got and I have mean the same here (both are fine in the U.S.). hurt is a verb; ache lives inside nouns (headache); sore is an adjective (a sore throat).

"It hurts when I swallow." Does it hurt all the time? (No, only when I swallow.) Show me "swallow." (Learners mime.)

How long — meaning. Draw a time line: Monday ● — — — ● today.

"I've had a cough since Monday." When did it start? (On Monday.) Do I have it now? (Yes.) Is "Monday" a point or a length of time? (A point.) So: since or for? (Since.)
"…for three days." Is "three days" a point or a length? (A length.) Can I say "since three days"? (No!)

Advice — meaning. All three are advice. The difference is strength.

"You should rest." Is it a good idea? (Yes.) Is it an order? (No.) "You'd better see a doctor." Stronger or softer? (Stronger.) Is there a problem if you don't? (Maybe, yes.) "Why don't you drink some tea?" Is it really a question about "why"? (No. It's a friendly idea.)

The through-line

Up to now → what I like and want → my whole story. C23 is about the bridge between the past and today (I've lived here for three years). C24 adds the language of hobbies, wishes and plans (I enjoy…, I want to…, I've decided to…). C25 puts all of A2 on one timeline: where I come from (past simple, past continuous, used to) → up to now (present perfect) → what's next (going to, will, first conditional). The C23 and C24 freer tasks are the first two parts of the C25 story, so learners arrive at C25 with material ready.

Trauma-informed: life stories are very personal

"Where did you grow up?" and "How long have you lived here?" can touch on war, migration, loss, family separation or immigration status. Never ask why someone left a country, or when they arrived, unless they offer it. In every personal task, offer three choices out loud: your real story, a partly fictional story (change or skip any part: a real past with an imagined future, or start the story "up to now"), or a fully fictional story (a life-story card from the classroom materials, or a character you invent). Say it every time, so that choosing a card is normal. Timelines can be about anything: a phone, a hobby, a friendship, a favorite team.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

for / since — meaning. Both answer "How long?" about a situation that started in the past and is still true now. for + the length (the whole line). since + the start (the dot). Learners met the present perfect for experiences in 4.1 (C10), so the form is review. The new part is the "still true now" meaning.

"I've lived here for five years." Do I live here now? (Yes.) When did I start? (Five years ago.) Is it finished? (No.)
"since 2021." Is 2021 a length of time, or a start point? (A start point.) "three years": length or start point? (Length.) So: for three years, since 2021.
"I lived in Texas for two years." Do I live in Texas now? (No.) Finished? (Yes.) So which tense? (Past simple.)

just / already / yet — meaning. just = a very short time ago. already = it's done, often earlier than expected. yet = up to now; only in questions and negatives; we expect it to happen.

"I've just eaten." A long time ago or a minute ago? (A minute ago.) Am I hungry? (No!)
"I've already paid the bill." Is the bill paid? (Yes.) Did I pay early? (Yes, maybe earlier than you thought.)
"I haven't called her yet." Did I call her? (No.) Am I going to call her? (Yes, probably.) Where is yet? (At the end.)

Pronunciation. Contractions are normal in speech: I've /aɪv/, she's /ʃiːz/, haven't /ˈhævənt/. Point out that she's lived = she has lived (not she is); the past participle tells you. for is weak in a sentence: /fər/ ("f'r five years"). L1 watch-outs

  • "since three years" (French depuis and German seit cover both for and since; Spanish desde hace). Ask the quick test: "Can you count it?"

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation

Meaning. Two verbs together: the first verb is the main one, and the second verb changes its shape. The first verb decides. There is no perfect rule, so learners learn the common verbs in two groups. A helpful hook: many -ing verbs talk about feelings and habits (enjoy, don't mind, keep); many to verbs point forward to a goal (want, plan, hope, decide).

"I enjoy cooking." Do I like it? (Yes.) Is it a habit or one time? (A habit, in general.)
"I don't mind waiting." Is waiting a problem for me? (No. It's OK.) Do I love it? (No, but it's OK.)
"He keeps forgetting his keys." One time, or again and again? (Again and again.) Is he happy about it? (No!)
"She finished cooking." Is she cooking now? (No. It's done.)
"I've decided to take a class." Is it an idea, or a decision? (A decision.) Past or future action? (The class is in the future.)
"I'd like to go home." = "I want to go home"? (Yes, but more polite.) "I'd like going home": OK? (No! would like + to.)

verb + -ingenjoy · don't mind · finish · keep · suggestI enjoy reading. · They suggested going to the beach.

Pronunciation. -ing is /ɪŋ/: the tongue goes back, and there is no hard /g/ at the end (not "sing-g"). to is weak, /tə/: "I want tə go." Learners will hear wanna and gonna in movies; that is fine to understand, but write want to. I'd like to links: /aɪd ˈlaɪk tə/. don't mind: stress on MIND.

Drill script
  1. Substitution (-ing): T: "I enjoy… 🏊" (swim flashcard) → S: "I enjoy swimming." T: "dance" → "I enjoy dancing." T: "hike" → "I enjoy hiking." Then change the first verb: T: "don't mind" → S: "I don't mind hiking."
  2. Substitution (to): T: "I want… travel" → S: "I want to travel." T: "hope" → "I hope to travel." T: "would like" → "I'd like to travel."
  3. First verb decides (fast): T says a first verb + a second verb: "enjoy / cook" → S: "I enjoy cooking." "decide / move" → "I decided to move." "keep / forget" → "I keep forgetting." Learners who make a mistake just try again: no explanation.
  4. Question chain: S1 → S2: "What do you enjoy doing on the weekend?" S2 answers, then asks S3: "What do you want to do this year?"… around the room.
Verb Pattern Race — how to run it
  • Groups of 3–4, one board, one die, one counter each. Everyone starts on START. The two-column list stays open in the workbook (the "referee").
  • Roll and move. Read the square and make a true sentence (or a sentence for your new identity): hope… next year → I hope to find a better job next year.
  • The group listens to the second verb. If it's wrong, the group helps fix it, and the player goes back 1 square.
  • ★ squares: ask the player on your left the question on the square. They answer with the right pattern. Arrow squares: go forward or back.
  • The first player to FINISH wins, but let groups keep playing until you stop the game. No dice? Use the dice-free variant printed under the board (count on fingers, or use a phone dice app).

  • "I want go" / "I enjoy cook" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Tagalog: the verb doesn't change shape). Tap the second verb: "What is missing?"

  • "I want that I go" / "I want that you come" (Arabic urīdu an…, Spanish quiero que…, Persian, Turkish). Show I want to go and I want you to come (stretch only).

If you run long

Protect planning, rehearsal and the story circles. Cut in this order: (1) shorten the warmer to 4 minutes; (2) do Ex 3 as homework (keep Ex 4); (3) skip the pairs reading of Maya's dialogue. Never cut Looking Ahead. Learners must leave knowing exactly what C26 looks like.

Meaning · Form · Pronunciation (review)

No new grammar. The job today is choosing the right tense for each part of the story. Use the time words as signals: when I was young, in 2019, used to → past · for, since, just, already, yet → up to now · next year, one day, if… → future. Keep CCQs short and fast.

"I used to play football every day." Do I play every day now? (No.) Was it a habit? (Yes.) (Module 5.3)
"I was living with my family then." Was it a long situation, or one quick moment? (A situation, in progress.) (Module 5.1)
"I've lived here for six years." Do I live here now? (Yes.) Past simple or present perfect? (Present perfect.)
"I'm going to study nursing." Decided or not decided? (Decided, a plan.) "I think I'll move to the city." A plan or a guess? (A guess, a prediction.) (Module 3)
"If I pass the exam, I'll celebrate." Did I pass? (Not yet. It's possible.) "If I will pass"? (No! If + present.) (Module 6.1)
Pastpast simple · past continuous · used to + base verbI grew up in a small town. · I was working on a farm. · I used to walk to school.
Up to nowpresent perfect + for / since / just / already / yetI've lived here since 2022. · I've just started a new job.
Futuregoing to (plan) · will (prediction) · If + present, will + verb · want / hope / would like + toI'm going to apply. · I think I'll stay. · If I get the job, I'll stay.

Pronunciation for storytelling. Pause between the three parts (it helps the listener). Stress the time words: "When I was YOUNG…", "NEXT year…". Link used to /ˈjuːstə/ (it sounds like "yoos-ta," not "yoozd-too"). Contracted I've, I'll, I'm sound natural, but full forms are fine in a story.

Drill script (quick, choral → individual)
  1. Time-word trigger: T says a time phrase, learners say the tense and one sentence: "When I was a child…" → "I used to…" · "for three years" → "I've… for three years." · "next year" → "I'm going to…".
  2. Back-chain the bridge line: "…since 2022." → "…lived here since 2022." → "I've lived here since 2022." Then learners change the start point to their own (or their card's).
  3. If-chain: T: "If I pass the exam…" → S1: "…I'll celebrate." S1: "If I celebrate…" → S2: "…I'll eat cake." Keep it fun and fast.
The task — story circles (groups of 3)

Story choices (say them out loud): (1) Real story. (2) Partly fictional: change places, dates or people, skip any part, or start with "up to now." Many learners choose a real present and an imagined, happy future. (3) Fully fictional: a life-story card, or invent a character (a famous person, a character from a movie). Nobody explains their choice.

Round: speaker tells the story in three parts (about 2 minutes; glancing at key words is fine, reading full sentences is not). Listener 1 asks a question about the past, listener 2 about the future (story-circle question cards help). Listeners tick "I heard: past · up to now · future." Then the next speaker. Clap or ring a bell every 5 minutes to keep the circles moving.

ICQs: Does it have to be your real life? (No. Real, partly real, or a card.) How many parts? (Three: past, up to now, future.) Do you read your frame? (No, only look at the key words.) Listeners, what do you do? (Listen, tick, and ask one question.) How long do you talk? (About two minutes.)

Observe for (checklist items 1–8): past forms, used to / past continuous, for / since, just / already / yet, going to / will, a first conditional, verb patterns, and answering a follow-up question.

The observation checklist → C26

Print it from the classroom materials. One row per learner, 8 can-do items, three marks: ✓ did it clearly · ~ did it with help, or errors that affect meaning · ○ not yet / not observed. You won't see every learner do everything. That's fine: listen to each learner for at least one full story. You can add marks from the rehearsal stage. After class, use the sheet to:

  • Plan each C26 speaking interview. The C26 interview asks about the same three parts. For learners with ○ on item 3 or 5, start the interview with the part they told best, to build confidence. Learners who used a story card may use the same card in C26.
  • Choose the C26 role-play. The A2 final speaking check includes one Module 7 role-play (travel and directions, eating out or shopping, or a health appointment). Note which one suits each learner.
  • Plan the C26 warm-up: the two items with the most ~ / ○ across the class become the 10-minute review before the assessment starts.
  • Spot who needs reassurance: anyone who didn't finish their story gets a quiet word at the end of C25: "Next class is friendly. It's the same as today. I'll help you."
  • Don't grade C25. It's practice. Tell learners so. The checklist is for your planning, not their record.
Differentiation
Literacy support

The story frame can be three pictures: learners draw (or use flashcards for) one scene per part and talk from the pictures. The life-story cards have picture symbols and short lines. Target: 6 sentences (2 per part), with at least one for / since and one going to. Pair a lower-literacy learner with a supportive partner in the rehearsal, not in the task.

Stretch

15+ sentences. Join ideas with Module 6: because, so, although, relative clauses (the town where I grew up), and a when / while sentence (Module 5.2: While I was working in a café, I met…). Listeners ask two follow-up questions, one with How long…?. Tell the story in the third person about a partner (he / she -s).

L1 watch-outs
  • "When I had 20 years…" (Spanish tenía 20 años, Portuguese, French, Italian: age with "have") comes back in life stories. Recast: When I was 20…
  • "I am living here since…" (see C23): the most common error in part 2. Recast it every time.
  • "I used to played" / "I use to" (all learners). The spoken /ˈjuːstə/ hides the d; point to the board: used to + base verb.
  • "If I will get the job…" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, German and Russian speakers often put a future in the if part). Board: If + present, will + verb.
  • No tense change at all (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Malay: time words carry the meaning). The story is still understandable, so praise the time words, then pick one tense to fix per learner.
  • Dropped be in going to (Arabic, Russian, Chinese): I going to open a shop. Tap the 'm.
  • Missing articles in place descriptions (Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean): I grew up in small town. Accept in fluency; note it for the feedback stage.

Closing the Module and the Course

End of C25: Module 8 and A2 wrap-up

Recap on slide 19: I've lived here since… → I enjoy… / I want to… → my whole story. Learners tick the three "I can…" lists in the workbook and look back at the "I can…" lists from Module 1. Anything still unticked goes on a sticky note for you, for the C26 warm-up.

The C26 final assessment covers the whole A2 course. From this module it checks: for / since, How long have you…?, just / already / yet, present perfect vs past simple, verb + -ing / verb + to, would like to, and telling a short life story across the three time frames.

Looking Ahead — C26: Final Assessment & Celebration

What to tell learners at the end of C25 (slide 18)

C26 has two parts. First, a short, friendly final assessment: a one-to-one (or pair) speaking check with you, about 6–8 minutes. You ask a few questions about their life (the same three parts as today: past, up to now, future) and do one short role-play from Module 7 (directions, a café or store, or a doctor's appointment). There are also short listening, reading and writing tasks in the kit's A2 final assessment. Then a celebration: certificates, a look back at what everyone can do now, and the next step, the B1 course, Independent English.

Say it simply: "Next class is our last class. You talk with me for a few minutes, like today. You can tell the same story: real, partly real, or your card. No surprises: it's everything we practiced. Then we celebrate!"

Reduce anxiety: show A2 lesson 1.1 on screen and ask: "Week 1: could you say 'I've lived here for three years, and next year I'm going to…'?" Let them hear how far they've come. Remind them that the online lessons stay open for review, and that the life-story recording is great practice for the interview.

Celebration planning: if learners want to bring food to share in C26, great, and it recycles Module 7. Ask everyone to label their dish with its main ingredients (allergies, halal, vegetarian). Nobody should feel they have to bring anything. Invite volunteers to tell their "future plans" part to the whole group as a "graduation speech."

Teacher reflection (after C25)

Look at your checklist and answer three questions: Which two can-dos need a C26 warm-up? Which learners need a quiet word of encouragement? Which role-play and which starting question will each learner get in C26? Write your answers on the back of the checklist.
