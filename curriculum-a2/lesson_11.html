✅ Lesson 4.2: Have to / Must / Should — Obligation & Advice

Life is full of rules and choices. Some things you must do, some things you have to do, and some things you really should do — but don't have to. In this lesson you'll learn how to talk about rules, obligations, and good advice, so you can explain how things work and help others make good decisions.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use have to / has to for obligation and rules, and don't have to for no obligation
  • Use must / mustn't for strong rules and prohibition
  • Use should / shouldn't to give advice
  • Choose the right word for the situation — and always follow it with the base verb

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Give advice and explain rules — write or say 6+ sentences about the rules in your job, school, or country, plus advice for a visitor.

Rules, Obligations & Advice

Every day you deal with three kinds of "shoulds and musts":

  • Obligations — things you have to do because of a rule ("I have to wear a uniform").
  • Prohibitions — things you're not allowed to do ("You mustn't smoke here").
  • Advice — good ideas, but your choice ("You should see a doctor").

English has a small set of words for exactly these situations: have to, must, and should. Once you can use them, you can explain how your job works, warn a friend, and give kind advice. Let's build them one at a time. 🛠️

🧠 Mindset

Don't try to memorize every difference at once. Learn each word with one clear example sentence you can picture — "I have to wear a uniform," "You mustn't smoke here," "You should rest." When you can see the situation, the grammar sticks. You don't have to be perfect today — you just have to start. 🌱

Have to / Has to

Use have to + base verb to talk about an obligation — usually a rule that comes from outside you (your boss, the law, your school). For he / she / it, use has to.

I have to wear a uniform at work. She has to work on Saturdays. We have to pay taxes.

Questions and negatives use the helper do / does — just like ordinary present-simple verbs:

FormI / you / we / theyhe / she / it
PositiveI have to go.He has to go.
NegativeI don't have to go.She doesn't have to go.
QuestionDo you have to go?Does he have to go?

✅ Pro Tip

After have to / has to, always use the base verb (go, work, wear) — never add -s or -ing. The -s already lives on has: "She has to work," not "has to works." ✅

I have to work late. She has to study tonight. Do I have to book a ticket? Does he have to wear a tie? We have to be quiet in the library.

Don't Have To ≠ Mustn't

This is the most important idea in the whole lesson, so read it twice. Don't have to means it is NOT necessary — you have a choice. It does not mean "don't do it."

⚠️ Key Difference

  • "You don't have to come." = It's optional. Come if you want — no problem either way. 🙂
  • "You mustn't come." = It's prohibited. Do NOT come. 🚫

Two very different meanings! Mixing them up can cause real confusion, so keep them separate.

SentenceMeaning
You don't have to pay. It's free.No obligation — paying is not necessary.
You mustn't park here.Prohibition — parking is not allowed.
She doesn't have to work tomorrow.She can choose to rest — it's her day off.
He mustn't be late again.Being late is forbidden — a strong rule.

A simple way to remember: don't have to = free choice, mustn't = red light 🔴.

Must / Mustn't

Use must + base verb for a strong obligation — something very important. Use mustn't (must not) for a prohibition — something you are not allowed to do. Good news: must is the same for every person — no -s, no do/does.

MeaningFormExample
strong obligationmust + base verbYou must stop at a red light.
prohibitionmustn't + base verbYou mustn't smoke here.
same for all personsI / you / he / she / we / they mustHe must finish today.

💡 Register note: must vs have to

Must is very common in written rules and signs: "Passengers must wear a seatbelt," "Staff must wash their hands." In everyday speech, most people use have to for obligation: "I have to wear a seatbelt." Both are correct — have to just sounds a little more relaxed and natural in conversation.

You must show your passport. You mustn't touch the paintings. We must be quiet. You mustn't use your phone here.

Should / Shouldn't (Advice)

Use should + base verb to give advice — to say something is a good idea. Use shouldn't (should not) to say something is a bad idea. Like must, should is the same for every person and takes no do/does.

MeaningFormExample
good idea (advice)should + base verbYou should see a doctor.
bad idea (advice against)shouldn't + base verbYou shouldn't eat so much sugar.
asking for adviceShould I / we…?What should I do?

To ask for advice, put should at the front:

Should I take a jacket? What should I do? Where should we eat? You should try the local food. You shouldn't worry so much.

Here's advice in a natural conversation. B has just moved to a new city:

A: I'm not feeling well, and I start my new job tomorrow.

B: Oh no. You should drink some water and go to bed early. You shouldn't stay up late.

A: Good idea. Do I have to wear a suit? I'm not sure of the rules.

B: You don't have to wear a suit — it's a relaxed office. But you mustn't be late on your first day!

A: Got it. Should I bring anything?

B: You should bring your ID. And you have to sign a form at reception — everyone does.

✅ Softening advice

Direct "You should…" is friendly and normal, but to sound gentler you can add maybe or I think: "Maybe you should rest," "I think you should call her." This is common and polite in everyday English. 🙂

Putting It All Together

Here's the whole lesson in one table. Keep it close — it answers "which word do I need?"

WordMeaningExample
have tonecessary (a rule / obligation)I have to wear a uniform.
don't have toNOT necessary (free choice)You don't have to come.
mustnecessary / very strongYou must stop at a red light.
mustn'tprohibited (don't do it)You mustn't smoke here.
shouldadvice (a good idea)You should see a doctor.
shouldn'tadvice against (a bad idea)You shouldn't eat so much sugar.

⚠️ One rule for all of them

Every one of these words is followed by the base verb — no to after must or should, and never -ing. Say "You must go" ✅ — not "You must to go" ❌. (Only have to keeps its little to, because to is part of that phrase.)

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: have to / don't have to / mustn't

Objective: Choose the best option for each situation.

  1. It's a work rule: everyone wears a badge. → You wear a badge. (have to / mustn't)
  2. The museum is free today. → You pay. (don't have to / mustn't)
  3. The sign says "No phones." → You use your phone. (don't have to / mustn't)
  4. It's Saturday — her day off. → She work today. (doesn't have to / mustn't)
  5. The road rule: stop at a red light. → You stop. (have to / don't have to)
✅ Answer

1. have to · 2. don't have to · 3. mustn't · 4. doesn't have to · 5. have to

🏋️ Exercise 2: Give advice with should / shouldn't

Objective: Read the problem and give one piece of advice. (Answers are examples — your idea may differ!)

  1. "I'm very tired." →
  2. "I want to learn English faster." →
  3. "I ate too much and now I feel sick." →
  4. "I'm always late for work." →
✅ Answer (example)

1. You should go to bed early. / You shouldn't drink coffee now.
2. You should practise every day. / You shouldn't be afraid of mistakes.
3. You should drink some water and rest. / You shouldn't eat so fast next time.
4. You should set an alarm. / You shouldn't stay up so late.

🏋️ Exercise 3: Fix the mistake

Objective: Each sentence has an error with must to or should to. Correct it.

  1. You must to show your ticket. ❌
  2. She should to see a doctor. ❌
  3. We must to be quiet in the library. ❌
  4. They should to book a table. ❌
✅ Answer

1. You must show your ticket.
2. She should see a doctor.
3. We must be quiet in the library.
4. They should book a table.
(Remove "to" — must and should take the base verb directly.)

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Use has to for he / she / it — the -s goes on has, not on the main verb.
  • Use do / does for questions and negatives with have to: "Do you have to…?", "She doesn't have to…".
  • Keep don't have to (optional) and mustn't (forbidden) clearly apart.
  • Use should for friendly advice; add maybe or I think to soften it.

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "You must to go." ❌ → "You must go." ✅ (no to after must/should).
  • "She have to work." ❌ → "She has to work." ✅.
  • "You don't have to smoke here" (when you mean it's forbidden) ❌ → "You mustn't smoke here." ✅.
  • "Do you must…?" ❌ → "Must you…?" or, more naturally, "Do you have to…?" ✅ (must takes no do).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • have to / has to = obligation (a rule); questions & negatives use do / does.
  • don't have to = NOT necessary (free choice) — not the same as mustn't!
  • must = strong obligation; mustn't = prohibition (don't do it).
  • should / shouldn't = advice (good idea / bad idea).
  • All are followed by the base verb — no to after must/should.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now do something very grown-up in English: explain how the world around you works. You can state a rule, warn about what's forbidden, tell someone what's optional, and give kind, useful advice. These are the words of bosses, guidebooks, doctors, and good friends — and now they're yours. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

What's the real difference between "must" and "have to"?

They're very close for obligation. Must is common in written rules and signs and can feel a bit stronger or more personal; have to is what most people say in everyday speech. In conversation, you can safely use have to for almost any obligation.

So "don't have to" and "mustn't" are opposites?

Almost! Don't have to = you can do it or not (optional). Mustn't = you are not allowed to do it (forbidden). They are not interchangeable — mixing them up changes the meaning completely.

Is "should" a strong command?

No — should is advice, not an order. It means "it's a good idea." For a strong rule, use must or have to. To give advice more gently, add maybe or I think: "Maybe you should rest."
