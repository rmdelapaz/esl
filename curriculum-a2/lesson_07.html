📅 Lesson 3.1: "Going to" — Plans & Intentions

You can talk about the past and the present. Now let's open the last door: the future. In this lesson you'll learn be going to. It's the everyday way English speakers talk about their plans, their intentions, and what they can see coming. Ready to make some plans? 🗓️

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Form be going to + base verb to talk about future plans
  • Make negatives (I'm not going to…) and questions (Are you going to…?)
  • Use future time expressions like tomorrow, next week, and this weekend
  • Talk about plans and make predictions from evidence you can see

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45–55 minutes

🎯 Project: Describe your plans for next weekend (or this year) in 6+ sentences using going to — spoken to a partner or recorded on your phone.

Opening the Future

In A1 you learned the verb to be — am, is, are. Keep it ready, because be going to is built right on top of it. If you can say "I am happy," you can already say "I am going to travel." 🎒

We use going to for the future all the time in daily life: "I'm going to call you later." "She's going to start a new job." "We're going to have dinner at eight." It's how you share your plans. And when you can share plans, you can invite people, plan your week, and dream out loud. 🌟

🧠 Mindset

Think about what just happened: you can now talk about the past (was/were, -ed), the present (am/is/are, present simple), and — from today — the future. All three tenses are open to you. That's the whole timeline of a conversation in your hands. If future forms still feel hard, add the magic word: "I can't talk about my plans easily… yet." 🌱

Going To: The Form

The pattern never changes: subject + am / is / are + going to + base verb. The base verb is the plain dictionary form — go, eat, study, travel — with no ending.

I am going to travel. · She is going to study. · They are going to move house.

In real speech, we almost always use the short forms (contractions). Here is the verb for every person:

PersonFull formShort form (natural)Example
II am going toI'm going toI'm going to cook dinner.
youyou are going toyou're going toYou're going to love it.
he / she / ithe/she is going toshe's going toShe's going to study tonight.
wewe are going towe're going toWe're going to travel in July.
theythey are going tothey're going toThey're going to visit us.

⚠️ Watch Out: use the BASE verb

After going to, use the plain base verb — not the -ing form and not another to. Say "going to eat," "going to study," and yes, even "going to go" is correct (the second go is the base verb). ✅ "I'm going to go home." · ❌ "I'm going to going home." · ❌ "I'm going to to go home."

Two Uses: Plans & Predictions

We use going to for two close jobs. Both are about the future. The difference is why you know.

Use 1 — Plans & intentions (decided before now)

You already made this decision in your head. You have a plan:

"I'm going to visit my family next week." · "We're going to paint the kitchen this weekend." · "He's going to learn to drive."

Use 2 — Predictions from evidence (you can see it coming)

You look at the situation right now and predict what will happen because the signs are clear:

"Look at those dark clouds — it's going to rain!" · "The bus is full; we're going to be late." · "Careful — you're going to drop that!"

💡 How to tell the two uses apart

Ask yourself: Did I decide this, or do I just see it happening?

  • Plan = a decision in your mind. "I'm going to study Spanish." (I chose this.)
  • Prediction = evidence in front of you. "She's going to fall!" (I can see it.)

Good news: the grammar is exactly the same for both. You only choose the meaning — the form never changes. 🎉

Negatives & Questions

To make a negative, add not after am/is/are — just like A1.

PersonNegative (short form)Example
II'm not going toI'm not going to work tomorrow.
he / she / itisn't going toHe isn't going to come.
you / we / theyaren't going toThey aren't going to wait.

To make a question, put am/is/are at the front, then the subject, then going to + base verb.

QuestionShort answer (yes)Short answer (no)
Are you going to come?Yes, I am.No, I'm not.
Is she going to stay?Yes, she is.No, she isn't.
Are they going to help?Yes, they are.No, they aren't.
⚠️ Important Note: In a short yes answer, don't contract the verb. Say "Yes, I am." — never "Yes, I'm." The no answer does contract: "No, I'm not."

You can also ask for information with Wh- words:

What are you going to do? Where are you going to go? When is she going to arrive? Who are they going to invite? How are we going to get there?

Future Time Expressions

Plans usually come with a word that tells us when. These words point to the future — when you see or say one, use going to.

  • tomorrow
  • tonight
  • next week
  • next month
  • next year
  • this weekend
  • in the summer
  • in two days
  • soon

💡 next vs in

Use next before a time word for the future: "next Monday," "next summer." Use in to count forward from now: "in two days," "in an hour." We don't say "next two days" or "in Monday." A time expression usually goes at the end of the sentence: "I'm going to call you tonight."

Talking About Plans

Here is a small phrase bank to start conversations. Mix and match these openers:

What are you going to do…? I'm going to… We're going to… Are you going to…? She's going to… I'm not going to…

Now let's hear two friends make weekend plans:

A: What are you going to do this weekend?

B: I'm going to visit my sister in the city. We're going to see a film on Saturday.

A: Nice! Are you going to stay the night?

B: Yes, I am. I'm not going to drive back so late. What about you?

A: I'm going to relax at home. Look at the forecast, though — it's going to rain all Sunday!

B: Ha! Then it's a good day to stay in. Are you going to cook something special?

A: Maybe. I'm going to try a new soup recipe. 🍲

✅ Register Note

In fast, friendly speech, "going to" often sounds like "gonna" — "I'm gonna call you." That's normal and natural to hear. But when you write, always use the full going to. Keep gonna for casual talk only.

Practice & Project

🏋️ Exercise 1: Build a "going to" sentence

Objective: Make a full future sentence from the prompt. Use the correct short form.

  1. (I / cook / dinner / tonight)
  2. (She / study / next week)
  3. (We / travel / in the summer)
  4. (They / move house / soon)
  5. (He / start / a new job / tomorrow)
✅ Answer

1. I'm going to cook dinner tonight.
2. She's going to study next week.
3. We're going to travel in the summer.
4. They're going to move house soon.
5. He's going to start a new job tomorrow.

🏋️ Exercise 2: Make it negative, then a question

Objective: Change each sentence two ways.

Example: She's going to work tomorrow. → She isn't going to work tomorrow. → Is she going to work tomorrow?

  1. They're going to help us.
  2. You're going to call me tonight.
✅ Answer

1. They aren't going to help us. / Are they going to help us?
2. You aren't going to call me tonight. / Are you going to call me tonight?

🏋️ Exercise 3: Plan or evidence-prediction?

Objective: Read each sentence. Is it a plan (a decision made before now) or a prediction from evidence (you can see it coming)?

  1. "Watch out — that glass is going to fall!"
  2. "I'm going to learn to play the guitar this year."
  3. "The sky is grey and windy. It's going to storm."
  4. "We're going to get married in September."
✅ Answer

1. Prediction (you can see the glass) · 2. Plan (a decision) · 3. Prediction (evidence: grey, windy sky) · 4. Plan (a decision)

🎯 Your Project: My Future Plans

Speak out loud — to a partner, in class, or into your phone's recorder. Describe your plans for next weekend or this year.

  1. (3 min) Say 6 or more sentences about your plans using going to: what you're going to do, where you're going to go, and who you're going to see. Add a time expression to each (this weekend, tomorrow, in the summer).
  2. (2 min) Include at least one negative ("I'm not going to…") and one prediction from evidence ("It's going to be busy, so…").
  3. (2 min) Ask a partner (or imagine one): "What are you going to do this weekend?" and listen to their answer.
  4. (1 min) If you recorded yourself, listen back. Did you use going to + base verb every time?

Tips & Common Mistakes

✅ Do's

  • Match the person to be: I'm, you're/we're/they're, he's/she's/it's going to.
  • Always put the base verb after going to — go, eat, study, be.
  • Use short forms when you speak (I'm going to…) — they sound natural and quick.
  • Add a future time expression to make the plan clear (tomorrow, next week, this weekend).

❌ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out

  • "I going to travel." ❌ → "I'm going to travel." ✅ (don't drop the am/is/are).
  • "She's going to studying." ❌ → "She's going to study." ✅ (base verb, not -ing).
  • "They going to to eat." ❌ → "They're going to eat." ✅ (one to, then the base verb).
  • "Is you going to come?" ❌ → "Are you going to come?" ✅ (you takes are).

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Form: subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb.
  • Two uses: plans/intentions (decided before now) and predictions from evidence.
  • Negatives: I'm not / isn't / aren't going to; questions put am/is/are at the front.
  • After going to, always use the base verb — never -ing, never a second to.
  • Signal the future with time words: tomorrow, next week, this weekend, soon.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You just opened the future. With going to, you can share your plans, invite people, and even predict what's coming from the signs around you. Add this to your past and present, and you now have the whole timeline — you can talk about your life in all three directions. That's a real A2 milestone. 🎉

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is "going to go" really correct? It looks strange.

Yes! The first part going to shows the future. The second go is the base verb (the action). "I'm going to go to the beach" is perfectly correct. Many people make it shorter in speech: "I'm going to the beach," but the full form is fine.

What's the difference between a plan and a prediction?

A plan is a decision you made in your head ("I'm going to study tonight"). A prediction is what you expect because of evidence you can see ("Look at those clouds — it's going to rain"). The grammar is the same. Only the reason is different.

Can I use "going to" for both "I" and "they"?

Yes — you just change the be verb. I am going to, he/she/it is going to, you/we/they are going to. The going to + base verb part stays the same.
